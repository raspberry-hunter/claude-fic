# Signal and Noise

Oct 1, 2026 · @C

*A sequel to "To The Waves My Heart Is Wed": Reggie and Pascual, the night Charles takes Monty to the sea.*

Pascual is writing up Beta when Reggie comes in.

Not Beta's post-mortem. That is the point; that is the whole improbable, unprecedented point. He has the weights laid out along the bench beside Charles' food logs, the surfacing intervals, the colour of the urine charted day by day from port wine through strong tea to straw, and he is trying to find a sentence that a referee in London will accept, which says, in effect: *this animal ought to have died, by every precedent we have, and he did not, and I cannot entirely tell you why.*

He can tell them about the bicarbonate. He can tell them about the fish. He cannot think how to tell them about a gangling English postgraduate in a rubber suit, murmuring nonsense into the water for five days straight, and an animal who would let no one else near him holding still for a needle because the boy had a hand in his hair.

It is well past midnight. The rain that has been threatening all week has come at last -- not the deluge Reggie promised them for a fortnight from now, but a fine, insistent hiss on the corrugated roof, under the endless drone of the generator. Pascual has the lamp pulled close. He does not hear the door.

"You're up late," Reggie says.

Pascual does not jump. He has had a great deal of practice, these last months, at not giving Reggie Tar the satisfaction. He finishes his sentence, blots it, and only then looks round. "So are you. Have you come to see whether I'm still shouting at you? I've stopped. I'm too tired."

"No," Reggie says, and something in the way he says it makes Pascual put down the pen.

Reggie is wet. Not drenched: damp, the shoulders of his shirt dark with rain, his hair -- ordinarily combed into the kind of order Pascual associates with English schoolboys and English generals -- flattened to his temples. Pascual's eyes go down, because they always go to the body first; that is the job. Sand on the trousers, caked to the knee. Sand ground into the knuckles of both hands, under the nails. A long scrape across the back of the left wrist, not deep, still beading. He is holding a bottle of rum, one of the good ones Hester keeps back for visiting Americans.

"You've been on the beach," Pascual says. "On your knees."

"Yes."

"In the rain. At midnight. With the Colonel's rum."

"The rum was afterwards." Reggie sets the bottle down on the one clear corner of the bench, carefully, the way he would set down a sample. "I need to tell you something. I'd rather you heard it from me tonight than found it out at six tomorrow morning, which you would. You're the only one who goes to Beta's pen before breakfast."

Pascual is on his feet before he has decided to stand. "What's happened to him?"

"Nothing. Sit down, Pascual. Nothing's happened to him," Reggie says. "He's gone."

\* \* \*

He tells it the way he tells everything: in order, without adjectives, as if reading it out of his own notebook. John's old family friend, the novelist. The Navy's interest. A second observer coming in the morning with the Americans, a man named Spilsbury. A conversation in Reggie's room after supper, in which John was made to understand certain things and then sent to bed. A second conversation, with Charles.

"I told him to take Beta to the sea," Reggie says. "Tonight. Before ten hundred hours."

Pascual stares at him. There is a jar on the shelf behind Reggie's head, a section of Alpha's lung in formalin, pale and grey and spongy; he finds he is looking at that instead. "On a tarp," he says at last. "Across the sand."

"Yes."

"With the muscle damage barely resolved. He could have -- the exertion alone --"

"He was shoving himself through the surf by the end of it. I don't think the muscles were troubling him." Reggie's mouth does something complicated. "I watched from the dune. I didn't go down. If anyone asks, I'd like to be able to say I didn't go down."

"And then?"

"And then he was in the water. Past the breakers. I could see them both, for a while, and then I could see one of them, and then I couldn't see anything." A pause. "I waited an hour and twenty minutes."

Of course he timed it. Pascual feels it like a blow, low in the gut, before he has properly understood it. "And the boy?"

Reggie looks at him, steadily, and says nothing at all, and that is the answer.

Pascual sits down. The chair takes him badly; he hears it scrape. He has the absurd thought that he never once told the boy his food logs were the best data in the building, and then the next absurd thought, that Charles left his glasses on the shelf by the pen half the time, and does he have them now, out there, in the dark, with a mile of black water under him --

"He'll die," Pascual says. "You know that. He'll die out there, Reggie. He's a man. He can't drink seawater, he can't sleep in it, he'll be hypothermic inside a few days even in these waters if he can't get out of them --"

"Possibly," Reggie says.

"*Possibly!*"

"Possibly. He has a knapsack. I didn't look in it. I suspect he packed it a week ago and didn't let himself know why. And he has a chart." Reggie's eyes slide away for the first time. "Jean drew it. She's been out on the fishing boats more than any of us, and she spent two years on those salt ponds. She knows where there's fresh water on every cay from here to the Passage. She knows which of them the Americans used for target practice in the war. She marked them."

"Jean knew."

"Jean knew before I did, I think. She's cleverer than I am about people. And Hester. And the Colonel, by now, as much as Hester has decided he ought to know, which I imagine is everything except the parts he'd have to swear to." Reggie spreads his sandy hands. "John knows a good deal less than he thinks he does. I'd like it to stay that way."

"And not me," Pascual says.

It comes out quieter than he means it to. He hears it hang there, between the bench and the door.

"Five days I was in and out of that pen," he says. "I had my hands in that animal's muscle. I've sat up with horses like that, at home -- good animals, tied up stiff as boards, pissing the colour of port wine -- and lost more of them than I saved. I didn't lose him. And you didn't think I should be told."

"You'd have tried to stop it."

"Would I?"

"You'd have argued that he needed another week of bicarbonate and observation," Reggie says, "and you'd have been right, medically. And we didn't have a week."

Pascual opens his mouth, and shuts it again, because it is true. He would have. He would have planted himself in front of that pen like a fool, with his clipboard and his urine chart, and argued that the patient was not fit for discharge.

"The pathologist," he says finally, "is always the last to be told. They bring him the thing when it's over."

"It isn't over," Reggie says. "That's rather the point. Spilsbury hasn't arrived yet."

\* \* \*

"I know the name," Pascual says, after a while. The rum is open. He has found two beakers that are, he is nearly certain, clean. "Spilsbury. There were reports. I saw some of them before I came out here -- things circulate, among anatomists, whatever the Admiralty would prefer. Very fine plates. The innervation of the caudal fin, I remember. Forty pages on the caudal fin, and not one line on how the animals died."

"No," Reggie says. "There wouldn't have been."

Something in his voice. Pascual looks up.

"I wrote some of the captions," Reggie says.

He says it the way a man confesses to a crime; and also, underneath that, so faintly that Pascual is ashamed of himself for hearing it, the way a man mentions a prize he won once, a long time ago.

The generator drones. Rain hisses on the roof.

"I was twenty-six," Reggie says, "and I thought it was the most important work in the world, and I suppose in its way it was. We learned a great deal. I learned that their calls carry identification. That they sound for one another in the tanks, by name, over and over. I had very good recordings of that." He turns the beaker in his fingers without drinking. "You told me you can't sedate them. You said it would kill him."

"It would."

"We found that out too," Reggie says. "Spilsbury didn't regard it as a reason to wait."

Pascual sets his beaker down. He is not a squeamish man; he cannot afford to be, in his work. He has been in places where men were curious and impatient and fond of knives, and he left one of them a long time ago and has not looked back at it since, because a man can only carry so much. He finds he cannot look at Reggie, and then finds that he must.

"When I said," he begins, "about the government labs --"

"That if I was going to kill the first specimen we'd ever kept alive, I might as well have stayed with them." Reggie says it precisely, as if quoting a paper. "Yes. I remember."

"I meant it."

"I know you did. It's the reason I left them. It was a sentence I'd been saying to myself for two years before I found the nerve to resign." He drinks at last: a small, neat swallow. "It was disagreeable to hear it in your voice."

He sets the beaker down. "Your meaning it, I could have borne. What I minded was that you were right. I'd kept Charles away from Beta for my separation trial, and put him in the water with Gamma instead, and written down very carefully how sick each of them got. I'd have gone on doing it for a cleaner curve. I resigned from those labs, Pascual. It seems I hadn't resigned from the man who worked in them."

Jean, at breakfast afterwards, telling anyone who would listen: *he went all white and quiet, I've never seen him like that, not even when Alpha died.* Pascual had told himself at the time that it was only a hit, a palpable one, and that a man like Reggie Tar enjoys being told the truth about himself no more than other men do. It had not occurred to him that Reggie might agree.

"And now," he says, carefully, "you've done this. To prove me wrong."

"No. I did this because Beta is the only one of them anyone has ever kept alive, and the method that kept him alive can't be published, and Spilsbury would take him apart to look for it. And then he'd take Charles apart. Figuratively, if he were in a good mood." Reggie considers. "No longer proving you right was a secondary benefit."

Despite everything, Pascual laughs. It hurts.

\* \* \*

"Here is what happens tomorrow," Reggie says. "At six o'clock you'll go to Beta's pen, as you always do, and find it empty, and Charles' sleeping pad beside it, and very likely his glasses on the shelf. You'll raise the alarm. I'll take the boat out with whoever the Colonel can spare, and I'll search very thoroughly in all the places they aren't. The Americans will arrive at ten hundred hours, and Spilsbury will ask for the records, and I want you to give him all of them."

"You want me to lie."

"I want you, very specifically, not to. Your clinical notes say that not three weeks ago this animal hauled himself out of a concrete pen and across several yards of deck, in the dark, for no reason anyone could establish. They say Charles went to him in the night, alone, without waking anyone, and dragged him back. They say Charles was the only man who ever got into the water with him. Every word of that is true." Reggie's voice is very even. "Spilsbury will read it and draw the conclusion it supports, which is that the animal did it again, and that a sentimental young man with a history of going in after him went in after him once too often."

"And drowned."

"He'll draw that conclusion himself. No one will need to say it."

"*Dios.*" Pascual puts his head in his hands. "You're going to let them think he's dead."

"It's the only conclusion that makes people stop looking," Reggie says. "And the rain's seen to the drag marks. I did what I could with my hands before it came. I'm not a praying man, but I'd have prayed for it, if I'd thought it would help. It came at eleven."

Pascual lifts his head and looks at the sand caked on Reggie's trousers and under his fingernails; at the long raw scrape across his wrist, where he must have caught it on something in the dark, on his knees, smoothing a trail of tarp and blood and boy and mermaid out of the beach with his bare hands while the first drops came down.

"Give me your hand," he says.

"It's nothing."

"Give it to me, or I'll take it."

Reggie gives it to him. Pascual turns it to the lamp and fetches the carbolic, and Reggie sits through it with the patience of a man who has been treated by Pascual before. As he has, in fact. Pascual's thumb finds the old scar on the heel of the hand -- four neat stitches, from a boat cleat in the spring -- and rests there a moment, quite without his permission.

"There's one thing I don't understand," he says, to the hand. "If that's the plan, then I'm part of the plan. I have to walk down to that pen at six and find it empty and be frightened. You need me frightened. If you'd said nothing, I would have been. Now I know." He looks up. "So why tell me?"

Reggie is quiet for long enough that Pascual begins to think he will not answer.

"I found," Reggie says at last, "that I couldn't let you be frightened."

Pascual's hand is still on his. He becomes aware of it all at once, the way one becomes aware of one's own heartbeat.

"That," he says, "is the stupidest thing I have ever heard you say."

"I'm aware that it's suboptimal."

"I'm a terrible liar."

"I know," Reggie says. "I've watched you try."

\* \* \*

"You've watched me," Pascual repeats.

"Yes." Reggie takes his hand back -- gently; he does everything gently tonight, and it is unnerving -- and reaches into his shirt pocket. "That's the other thing I came to tell you. There is a reasonable probability that by the end of the week I'll be on an aeroplane to London in the company of some very courteous men, or at any rate that I won't be employed here any longer. Hester and the Colonel will do what they can. Spilsbury is very good at not being stopped. So I'd like to disclose the rest of my data while I'm still in a position to."

He holds out a notebook. Small, black, oilcloth-covered, swollen at the edges with damp. Pascual has never once seen him without one, and has watched him write in it a thousand times, on the verandah, at meals, in the boat.

"What data?"

"Open it," Reggie says, "at the ribbon."

Pascual opens it at the ribbon.

The page is headed, in Reggie's small, upright, maddening hand, with a single letter: *P.*

He reads the first line. Then he reads it again.

*Verandah, after supper. P. seated within arm's reach of R.T.: 23 of 31 evenings. Seven chairs; attendance varies. Expected under random seating: approx. 9.*

He turns the page.

*Intervention, wk of 12th: R.T. moves to E. end of verandah (windward). P. follows on 5 of 6 evenings. P. complains of wind on 4 of the 5.*

He turns the page.

*Withdrawal, 3 evenings: R.T. absent from verandah. P. comes to R.T.'s room with pretext on 2 of 3. (Pretexts: Gamma's samples, which could have been delivered at breakfast; a journal article from 1931.)*

"You," Pascual says. It comes out strangled. "You ran an experiment. On *me*."

"Several."

"You moved your *chair* --"

"The east end is the windward end. I checked it against the anemometer before I started. I wanted to be sure you'd have no reason to sit there apart from --" Reggie makes a small gesture that takes in himself. "Apart from."

"I complained about the wind!"

"Four times out of five," Reggie agrees. "And sat there anyway."

Pascual turns another page. *Arguments initiated by P., by party.* A small, neat table. Jean: two. John: one. The Colonel: none, which is only sense. *R.T.*: nineteen, with a footnote: *duration markedly longer than with any other party; P. does not stop when he has made his point.*

"I've argued with Jean more than twice," he protests.

"You've disagreed with Jean more than twice. You've argued with her twice. There's a difference. When you argue with Jean, you stop when you've won."

*Forms of address,* says the next page. *'Tar': in company, when angry (n=14). 'Reggie': in private, any mood (all obs.). 'That wretched Tar': to C.C., reported by J.L. (n=1).* Pascual feels heat climb his neck. There is a note in the margin, in slightly smaller writing, as though Reggie had been embarrassed to write it even to himself: *cf. merfolk ident. calls -- distinct tag by context?*

"You've catalogued my calls," Pascual says. "Like one of your mermaids."

"It's the only method I know that works."

"Does it? You told the whole Institute -- you *published* it -- that they have names and no language. That none of it rises to anything more."

"Yes," Reggie says. "And then Charles Cholmondeley sat with his feet in a pen for a week and proved I'd been listening on the wrong channel. I'm aware of the irony, Pascual. You needn't point it out. I've been sitting on the beach with it since nine o'clock."

Pascual turns the page. The next is dated from the spring, and holds a single entry.

*3rd. P. sutures R.T.'s left hand (cleat). Procedure complete approx. 4 min. Contact maintained a further 2 min 40 s. P. did not explain.*

He looks at the hand -- that scar was under his thumb not ten minutes ago -- and away.

"I was checking the sutures," he says.

"For two minutes and forty seconds."

"You'd been bleeding."

"I had stopped bleeding."

And there is one more entry, from the night of the shouting on the verandah.

*P. outside R.T.'s door, 11.40 to 11.52 p.m. Did not knock.*

Pascual sits with that one for a while.

"I didn't knock," he says eventually, "because I hadn't decided whether I was sorry."

"Had you decided, by eleven fifty-two?"

"I'd decided I was sorry I'd made you look like that. Not sorry I'd said it."

"That's fair," Reggie says. "It was a true thing to say."

The last page with writing on it has no date. It is set out the way Reggie sets out everything, like a proof.

*Hyp. A: P. is attracted to R.T.*

*Hyp. B: P. finds R.T. uniquely infuriating.*

*NB. A and B predict identical behaviour on all measures to date. Discriminating test required.*

And below it, crossed out so many times that the nib has torn the paper: *ask him?*

Pascual closes the notebook.

"This is what you did to the boy," he says -- not gently, because it needs saying and no one else at the Institute will say it to Reggie Tar. "You put him in with Gamma and watched him be sick into the drain, and you watched Beta stop eating, and you wrote it all down in a little book."

Reggie doesn't flinch. "Yes," he says. "It's how I do things. I'm aware it isn't a particularly good way." A pause. "I didn't separate you from anything. I'd like that noted. I only moved a chair."

"And never once," Pascual says, "in all of this, did it occur to you simply to -- *ask*."

"It occurred to me a great many times." Reggie looks at the closed notebook in Pascual's hands, not at Pascual. "If the answer had been B, I'd have had to sit on that verandah every evening with a man who knew. And who would, presumably, stop sitting within arm's reach. I preferred the data I had."

The rain has thinned. Under the drone of the generator Pascual can hear the drip from the eaves, slow, uneven, like a clock that cannot keep time.

"And now," Pascual says.

"Now," says Reggie, "I may not have a verandah after Thursday."

\* \* \*

Pascual sets the notebook on the bench beside the urine chart, very carefully.

"Your experimental design," he says, "is terrible."

"So I'm told."

"Your hypotheses aren't exclusive. Any first-year could see it. You've built a test that can't discriminate between two causes, because both causes are present. A pathologist would have told you so in five minutes. You never have only one cause of death. You have a proximate cause, and a contributing cause, and a man who drank too much and drove too fast and also had a bad heart."

Something in Reggie's face shifts, very slightly. "And what would a pathologist suggest?"

"Direct examination," says Pascual. "Did you think to measure pupils?"

"I discarded it. The light on the verandah's too poor to be reliable."

"The light in here," Pascual says, "is excellent."

He stands, and comes round the bench, and puts two fingers under Reggie's chin and turns his face to the lamp, exactly as he would for any patient. Reggie lets him. Reggie's eyes are grey. Pascual has never been near enough to him, in good light, to be sure of that before.

"I have the lamp full in your eyes," he says. "They ought to be pinpoints. They are not."

"Fear dilates the pupils as well," Reggie says. His voice is not quite even. "It's a confound."

"Are you afraid?"

"Yes."

It is the most honest thing Pascual has ever heard him say. He finds that his hand has moved, of its own accord, from Reggie's chin to the side of his face: palm along the jaw, thumb at the cheekbone, the faint rasp of a day's beard. Reggie's skin is cool and damp from the rain. Under his fingertips, at the angle of the jaw, the pulse is going like a bird's.

Reggie goes absolutely still.

Not the stillness of a patient. Something else. Pascual watches it go through him; watches him understand something that Pascual does not, and does not know what he has done.

"Reggie?"

"It's what they do," Reggie says, very low. "The merfolk. John worked out that much from watching the boy, and Hester gave Jean the rest, and Jean gave it to me, because she thought I ought to know what I'd been playing with. One of them puts a hand to the other's face. And if the other answers in kind --" He stops.

"Then what?"

"Then something opens. Hester told Charles it was like a door." Reggie's eyes are on his. "For them. For Charles. For men like us, nothing happens at all. Most people can't. Jean says Hester was quite definite on the point."

And then, slowly, deliberately, with the air of a man performing the one step of a procedure that cannot be undone, he raises his own hand and lays it along Pascual's cheek.

Nothing happens.

No door. No flood. Pascual does not see the inside of Reggie Tar's head: the twenty-six-year-old writing captions under plates of the caudal fin; the man on his knees in the sand at eleven o'clock, smoothing a trail into nothing. There is only the hiss of the rain, and the drone of the generator, and a man's palm on his face, cool and a little gritty, and his own heart going far too fast.

But Reggie knew that nothing would happen, and did it anyway. That is what Pascual will keep coming back to, afterwards: not that the door didn't open, but that Reggie put his hand to it all the same.

"Your pulse is a hundred and twelve," Pascual says. His voice has gone strange. "It was a hundred and twelve in the spring, too. With the cleat. I had my fingers on your wrist. Habit."

Reggie blinks.

"A hundred and twelve," Pascual says, "for a cut that size, sitting down, in a man who stands up in a boat in a swell without turning a hair. I thought you were going to faint. I held on in case you did."

"I have never fainted in my life."

"No. I worked that out afterwards. I wrote it in the clinical log."

"You entered me," Reggie says, and there is something rising under the words that might, in another man, have been delight, "in the *clinical log*."

"You were a patient," Pascual says. "I always record the pulse."

He has a sudden helpless picture of the two of them, all these months: Reggie on the verandah with his little black book, counting chairs; himself at this bench with the log, writing *Pt. denies pain* and underlining it twice, and turning back to that page, more evenings than he will ever admit, to look at a number. Two men with their ears pressed to the same wall, each taking careful notes on the other's heartbeat. Neither with the wit to knock.

"Hypothesis A," Pascual says, "and hypothesis B. I find you uniquely infuriating. I have since the first week. And --"

He doesn't finish. He has never been any good with words in English when it mattered, and for once he does not have to be. He leans in and kisses him.

Reggie makes a sound against his mouth -- small, startled, wholly uncontrolled; only the second such sound Pascual has ever heard from him -- and then his hand slides from Pascual's cheek into his hair and holds on, and he kisses back as if he has been composing it for months, as if this too were a page of the notebook he never let himself write. He tastes of rum and rain. He kisses, Pascual thinks dizzily, the way he does everything: with total, alarming concentration, as though he means to get it right and remember all of it.

When they part, Reggie's forehead stays against his. His breath is uneven.

"Not mutually exclusive," Reggie says, a little hoarsely. "Then."

"I told you. Any first-year."

"I'll want to repeat the result."

"Of course you will," says Pascual, and does it for him.

\* \* \*

Later -- not very much later; neither of them, it turns out, is a patient man once he has stopped pretending -- they are in the narrow room behind the laboratory with the door shut and the lamp turned down to a bead, and Reggie is sitting on the edge of Pascual's bed with his shirt half unbuttoned, looking at his own sandy hands as though he has only just noticed that they belong to him.

"I'm filthy," he says.

"You're a patient," says Pascual. "Patients are always filthy. Sit still."

He fetches the jug and the basin and a clean cloth, and kneels on the floor in front of him. It occurs to him as he does it that Reggie has spent half of tonight on his knees in the sand, and that this is only fair. He washes Reggie's hands first, finger by finger, the sand coming away grey in the water; then the scraped wrist again, though he dressed it an hour ago. Then he unbuttons the rest of the shirt and eases it back off Reggie's shoulders and lets it fall, sand and all, to the floor. Reggie lets him. He sits very upright on the edge of the bed with his hands open on his knees, and watches Pascual's face the whole time, as if he means to commit it to memory.

There is sand everywhere. Pascual finds it in the hollow of Reggie's throat, and behind his ears, and in a long gritty smear across the small of his back where his shirt must have ridden up while he knelt. He follows it with the cloth. And then -- because the cloth is not, after all, a very good instrument, and because Reggie's breath goes uneven every time Pascual leans close -- with his hands; and then with his mouth.

"Your trousers," he says eventually, against Reggie's shoulder, "are a disgrace."

Reggie reaches for his belt. His fingers are stiff and scraped and the buckle defeats him; Pascual watches him fail at it twice, with mounting indignation, and then pushes his hands aside and does it himself.

The knees are worse than the hands: both of them raw, grit ground into the broken skin. Pascual cleans them properly, kneeling between them, with the carbolic, and Reggie hisses through his teeth and does not pull away. There is a bruise coming up on one hip, dark already, where something in the dark struck him; a split nail on the left hand; a long shallow graze down one shin. Pascual finds the rest of the beach on him with his hands and his mouth, every last scrape of it, and catalogues each one. Reggie lets him.

"You're examining me," Reggie says into the dark, sounding as though he cannot decide whether to be offended.

"Thoroughly," says Pascual. "Hold still."

And Reggie Tar, for once in his life, does as he is told.

He holds still for a remarkably long time, for Reggie. He holds still while Pascual lays him back on the narrow bed and finds the place under the angle of his jaw where the pulse is, and puts his mouth there, and counts. He holds still while Pascual's hand travels down from the bruise on his hip the way it would travel in any examination: palm flat, unhurried, learning the long muscle of his thigh, the jump of his stomach, the place where his breath catches and the place where it stops. He holds still right up until Pascual's hand slides between his thighs and finds him already slick and waiting -- and then he makes a sound that Pascual intends to remember for the rest of his life.

"That," Reggie says, rather unsteadily, "was not in the protocol."

"There is no protocol."

"There's always a protocol."

"Then you'll have to write it up afterwards," says Pascual, and moves his hand, and Reggie stops arguing.

It turns out that Reggie has calls of his own. Several. Pascual learns them in order, the way Reggie once learned the merfolk's, by patient repetition and close attention to context: the sharp indrawn breath when Pascual's fingers press into him, slow; the low, startled sound, almost a protest, when Pascual moves down the bed and puts his mouth where his hand has been; the long unravelling sentence -- something about methodology, Pascual thinks, with a subordinate clause in it -- that Reggie begins and cannot finish, because Pascual has found exactly the place, and stays there. Reggie's hand comes down into his hair and holds on. The other is flung across his own eyes. He is shaking, very finely, all over, like a struck wire.

When he comes apart it is almost entirely in silence: his whole body drawn tight as a bow, his heel hard against Pascual's back, and only at the very end Pascual's name, once, in a voice Pascual has never heard from him, and that nobody else on earth, he is suddenly and fiercely certain, ever has.

Afterwards Reggie lies with his arm still over his eyes, breathing as if he has run a long way. Pascual moves up the bed and lies beside him and, out of a habit he will never break, rests two fingers on the inside of his wrist.

"A hundred and thirty," he says.

"You're taking my pulse."

"I always record the pulse."

Reggie takes his arm off his eyes and looks at him -- grey eyes, pupils blown wide, no confound in the world to blame it on now -- and then rolls over, quite suddenly, and pins Pascual's wrist to the pillow with one scraped hand.

"I'll want," he says, "to repeat the result."

And it turns out that Reggie Tar brings to this the same total, alarming concentration he brings to everything else, as though it were the most important experiment of his life and he means to get it exactly right. He is clumsy with his ruined hands and does not care; he is far too attentive to Pascual's breathing, and Pascual does not care either. He watches Pascual's face the whole time. When at last he draws Pascual over him and in, and they move together, slow and then not slow at all, in the narrow creaking bed with the rain hissing on the roof, his eyes stay open and fixed on Pascual's; and Pascual finds, somewhat to his own astonishment, that he does not mind in the least being recorded.

Near the end Reggie's hand comes up and settles along his cheek. Pascual turns his face into it and holds it there with his own, and when he goes over the edge it is with his mouth against Reggie's palm and Reggie's name in it -- the private one, not the one for company -- and Reggie holding him through it as though he means never to let go.

They lie tangled together afterwards in a bed built for one, sticky and sandy and sore, while the rain thins on the roof. Reggie falls asleep first, in the middle of a sentence, with his face in Pascual's neck. Pascual lies awake a while longer with two fingers resting on his wrist, feeling the pulse slow -- a hundred, ninety, eighty, lower -- and thinks about a boy somewhere out on the black water with his hand on a mermaid's back, and the empty pen he must walk down to at six, and the man asleep against him who knelt in the sand tonight with his bare hands to keep them both safe; and then, for a little while, about nothing at all.

\* \* \*

Pascual wakes a little before five. The rain has stopped. Through the louvres the sky is the colour of a bruise going yellow at the edges, and the generator is droning on as it always does, and there is a man asleep in his bed with one arm flung over his eyes and his mouth slightly open, looking about twenty-two and not in the least like someone who has ever written a caption.

He gets up quietly and goes through to the laboratory, where the lamp has burnt itself out, and the urine chart is still lying on the bench, and the rum, and the notebook.

He finds a pen. He opens the notebook to the last page with writing on it -- *Hyp. A*, *Hyp. B*, the torn-through *ask him?* -- and underneath, in his own appalling hand, he writes:

*Discriminating test performed. Direct examination. Result: A. (Also B.) Further observation recommended.*

He considers, and adds: *P.*

Then he takes it back into the bedroom and slides it into the pocket of the sandy shirt on the floor, where Reggie will find it, and goes to stand at the louvres.

In an hour he has to walk down to the pen, and find it empty, and be frightened. He thinks he can manage it. He will not, it turns out, need to pretend very much. Somewhere out there, past the reef, past the places Jean marked *keep clear*, there is a boy without his glasses and a knapsack, and his hand on the back of a mermaid, and a great deal of very cold, very deep water in every direction; and Pascual has sat up with enough patients to know exactly what the odds are.

He finds, looking out at the grey water, that he does not believe them.

Behind him, the bed creaks. Reggie's voice, rough with sleep: "You've been writing in my notebook."

Of course he was watching. "Peer review," says Pascual, without turning round. "Go back to sleep, Tar."

A pause. "That's a new usage," Reggie says. "In private. Not angry."

"Then write it down," Pascual says, "and go back to sleep. We have an hour."

## Part Two: Wound Age

Pascual finds the glasses at six.

He has known for five hours that he would. He lay awake beside a sleeping man and rehearsed it: the walk down, the empty pen, the shout. He thought knowing would make it easier. It does not. The water lies flat and grey under Jean's tarp. The sleeping pad is rumpled where the boy left it. And on the shelf by the ladder, folded neatly, as if their owner meant to come back for them, are Charles' spectacles.

One arm has been mended with fuse wire. Pascual did not know that. He stands turning the little twist of wire under his thumb, and finds that he does not have to pretend anything at all.

He shouts.

What follows he will remember afterwards only in pieces. Juan running from the fish shed. The Colonel on the bungalow steps in his shirtsleeves, already giving orders. Jean, barefoot, her face doing nothing whatsoever, which is how Pascual knows she is the second-best liar on the cay. And Reggie, arriving from the direction of his own hut -- shaved, combed, collar buttoned -- taking it all in with one long look: the pen, the pad, the glasses in Pascual's hand.

"How long?" Reggie says.

"I don't know. I don't *know*. The pad's cold."

"Juan, the boat. Two men and the long gaff. John --"

John has come out in his pyjamas. He is staring at the empty pen. "He went in after him," he says. "The bloody fool. He went in after him, didn't he? Like last time."

"Very likely," Reggie says. "Get dressed. You're coming with me."

Pascual watches him give orders, cool and quick and entirely without expression, and is more frightened by it than by anything else that has happened. He had not known, until this morning, exactly how good Reggie is at this.

While the boat is being run down, John comes and stands beside him at the pen, dressed now, his shirt buttoned wrong. He does not look at Pascual. "I wrote to Fleming," he says, low. "About Charles and Beta. All of it -- everything I'd seen, and what I thought it meant. I wrote it up like a lark. Something for him to laugh about at his club."

Pascual has nothing to say to that. After a moment he says, "Go with Reggie," and John goes.

Someone has to stay with Gamma, so Pascual stays. He sits on the rim of the empty pen with the glasses in his breast pocket and watches the boat's wake go white, and then thin, and then vanish round the point, and he does not let himself think about which way it is going, or which way it is not.

It comes back at half past nine. John is scarlet with sunburn and silent. Reggie steps up onto the jetty, and Pascual sees his hands: the palms raw, the fingers bloodied, both striped across with fresh rope burn. He has handled the lines bare-handed for four hours. The men are all in gloves.

Their eyes meet. Reggie's say nothing at all.

New wounds, laid down over the old ones. Covering his tracks the only way he could think of, with his own skin. Pascual wants, very badly, to hit him -- and also to take those hands and hold them under the tap until they stop bleeding -- and he can do neither, because the Navy launch has just come into sight round the point.

\* \* \*

Spilsbury is not what Pascual expected.

He had expected -- he is not sure what. Someone like the reports: cold, exact, tidy as a plate. The man who comes down the launch's gangway behind the American commander is tall and silver-haired and slightly stooped, in a pale linen suit that has somehow survived a sea voyage without a crease, with a red carnation in his buttonhole that cannot possibly be fresh and is. He has a soft voice. He has a kind face. He shakes the Colonel's hand, and Hester's, gloved even in this heat, and Jean's, and when he comes to Reggie he does not shake his hand at all but only looks at him, for a long moment, with something that on any other man's face Pascual would have called fondness.

"Reginald," he says.

"Dr Spilsbury," says Reggie.

Pascual watches Reggie's shoulders go back as he says it, the way a boy's do when the headmaster walks into the room; and then watches him notice, and let them down again, deliberately, one at a time.

Behind them the sailors are swinging a canvas-sided tank up out of the launch's well on a derrick. Something inside it moves, heavily, and water slops over the rim. Delta. Pascual finds he is counting her breaths through the canvas without meaning to.

The Colonel tells them about Beta, and about Charles. He tells it well: gravely, with no more detail than a soldier gives a family. Commander Ferris swears, briefly and with feeling; Beta was, after all, the thing the Navy had been paying to keep alive. Spilsbury says, "How very unfortunate," in the tone of a man for whom the misfortune is chiefly his own, and then, "I should like to see the records. All of them. Dr Pobil, I believe the clinical notes are yours?"

So it is Pascual who takes him to the laboratory, and Pascual who stands by the bench -- the same bench, the rum put away, the urine chart still lying where he left it -- while Spilsbury sits in Pascual's own chair and reads.

He reads very fast. He reads everything. He turns back twice to the night Beta left the pen and reads it again, slowly, one long finger under the lines.

"This is admirable work," he says at last, and looks up, and Pascual is appalled at how much he wants to hear it. "Azoturia, in effect. Tying-up. You've a veterinary training?"

"Horses," Pascual says. "At home."

"So had my best dissector. The bicarbonate in the fish was very good. And you'd no means of sedating him, of course." Spilsbury smiles faintly, as at a shared memory. "One learns that early. Have you any tissue from the first animal? Alpha, I think you call her."

"Eleven jars." Pascual hears the stiffness in his own voice. "Miss Leslie did the post-mortem. I have her sections."

"The brain?"

"Fixed whole."

"Ah," says Spilsbury, softly, and something comes into his face that Pascual recognises, because he has seen it in his own shaving mirror, the first time he was handed a specimen no one else in the world had: an absolute, tender hunger.

Reggie comes in then, with the turtle ledger he was asked for and nothing else, and Spilsbury rises to meet him.

"I read your paper on the identification calls," Spilsbury says. "The Martin animals, in the *Journal*, last year." He says it the way another man might remark on the weather. "Very elegant, Reginald. The spectrograms especially. I read it twice."

Pascual sees it go through Reggie: a quick, unguarded flush of pleasure, there and gone, like a current through a wire, before he can get his face in front of it. For an instant he looks about twenty-six. Then the face closes, and Reggie says, "Thank you," in a voice with nothing in it at all; and Pascual finds that of all the things Spilsbury has done since he stepped off the launch, this is the one he cannot forgive.

Then, still smiling, Spilsbury reaches for his hand.

It is done so naturally that Pascual does not see it coming. Spilsbury simply takes Reggie's right hand in both of his, palm up, the way Pascual himself would, and turns it to the window.

"You've hurt yourself."

"Rope," Reggie says. "We were out four hours."

"Yes, these are rope." Spilsbury's thumbnail moves, very lightly, along the heel of the hand, and Reggie does not flinch, and Pascual sees what it costs him not to. "And under them -- these are not. These are abrasions, and there's sand in them. There's no sand in a boat, Reginald." He tilts the hand a little further to the light. "Twelve hours, I'd say. Perhaps a little more." He looks up at Pascual, pleasantly. "You'll have dressed them, Doctor. You'll know. Twelve?"

"Ten," Pascual says.

It is a terrible lie. He hears it come out of his mouth too fast and too loud, the number plucked from nowhere, and sees Spilsbury hear it too; sees the kind face take it in, and turn it over, and file it.

"Ten, then," Spilsbury says gently. He lets Reggie's hand go. From his inside pocket he takes a small black notebook -- oilcloth, Pascual notes, with a dull lurch of the stomach; the same kind; of course the same kind, where else would Reggie have learnt it -- and writes something in it, and puts it away. "I'll want the separation trial too, Reginald. This evening, if you would."

\* \* \*

Reggie comes to the laboratory a little after eleven. There is no rum this time. His hands are wrapped in clean gauze he has plainly done himself, badly, and he sits down on the stool across the bench from Pascual as if reporting to a committee.

"He had me on the jetty after supper," he says. "He was very civil. He generally is."

"And?"

"And there's a berth for me on the launch on Thursday. I'm to bring the separation logs. I'm to come back to the labs at a better grade than I left, and tell him about Charles at my leisure. He used that phrase. *At your leisure.*" Reggie looks at his bandaged hands. "Otherwise there's a report. A specimen held under a Navy contract, deliberately released by the senior scientist. Abrasions consistent with dragging a heavy load across sand, dated by a pathologist with thirty years in the witness box. A colleague who lied about the dating in his presence." He lifts his eyes. "He'll name you, Pascual. The Americans pull their money. The Colonel's finished; Hester's finished. Gamma and the new one go to London in tanks, and Jean goes home to teach biology to schoolgirls. Or I go on Thursday, and he's satisfied, and none of that happens."

"And you've decided."

"I've packed."

"He said he'd missed me," Reggie says, to his hands. "Did I mention that? He said there's been nobody since who listened half so well." His mouth does something brief and painful. "It's a thing he says. He's very good at knowing what to say."

"Is it a thing he says?"

Reggie is silent a moment. "No," he says. "I don't believe I've ever heard him say it to anyone."

Pascual takes a breath, and lets it out, and does not throw the beaker in his hand at the wall, which he considers a considerable achievement.

"Sit there," he says. "Don't speak. You're going to hear a differential."

"Pascual --"

"You had your method last night. You moved chairs about for six months and wrote it all down and never once asked the patient a question. Now we'll do mine." He pulls his own stool round the bench and sits facing him, knee to knee. "What does he want? Not the animal. Animals die; he said so himself. Not you, exactly. You were a good pair of ears, but he's managed for years without you. What he wants is the thing between Charles and Beta. Yes?"

After a moment Reggie says, "Yes."

"And what has he got to show it exists? One: John's letter to his friend the novelist. Gossip. Two: your hands. They prove you were on a beach. They don't prove what was in the boy's head. Three --" Pascual holds up three fingers. "Your separation trial. Charles sick in the drain every time you put him in the water with Gamma. Beta off his food the same week. The boy waking in the night, two pens away, over that generator, and *knowing*. Is there another record anywhere in the world that says this thing is real?"

Reggie is silent for a long time.

"No," he says. "There isn't."

"Then it isn't real," Pascual says. "Not until somebody reads it."

Reggie looks at him. It is the look, Pascual thinks, of a man being asked to cut off his own hand. The log is the best work he has ever done. It is the only proof that he was wrong about the merfolk; the only proof of anything.

"And there's the other thing," Pascual says, more quietly. "If you go on Thursday, what's in your head goes with you. All of it. Charles. The chart. Jean." He pauses. "Hester."

Reggie goes very still.

"I'm not a fool, Reggie. She knew to put them together before you did. She gave Jean the old stories. She runs this place, and in all the months I've been here she has never once set foot near those pens." Pascual leans in. "You think you'd keep that from him? You told *me* everything you knew, with a notebook and the Colonel's rum, because you couldn't stand for me to be frightened for six hours. He'll have you for twenty years."

The generator drones. Somewhere out past the reef, the sea.

"So you see," Pascual says, "your going isn't the price of keeping everyone safe. It's the price of keeping no one safe. You've made an error in the design."

Reggie gets up. He goes to the shelf by the door and comes back with a ledger -- the separation trial; Pascual knows it by its green cloth spine, he watched Reggie write in it in Gamma's pen every day for a fortnight -- and stands holding it over the big steel sink.

"I've never destroyed data in my life," he says.

"I know."

"Not at the labs. Not even then. I kept everything. I thought, if I keep all of it, someone will read it someday and know what we did." His voice is perfectly level. "That was the only defence I ever had."

"Then this is a different defence."

Reggie strikes the match.

It takes a long time. A ledger does not burn quickly; he has to tear the pages out in sheaves and feed them in, and the gauze on his hands catches once and Pascual swats it out. *Day 3: No change. Day 4: No change. Day 5: C.C. unable to remain in water. Emesis.* Pascual watches the boy's suffering curl and blacken and go down the drain in flakes, and puts his hand flat on the small of Reggie's back, and leaves it there until the last page is gone.

When it's done Reggie stands looking at the ash. Then his hand goes, as if of its own accord, to his shirt pocket.

"That one too," he says. "It's evidence. Of a different sort. They put men in prison for --"

"I know what they do."

Reggie takes out the little black notebook and holds it, and does not move.

So Pascual holds out his hand, and after a moment Reggie gives it to him. He opens it at the ribbon, turns to the end, and tears out exactly one page: *Hyp. A, Hyp. B*, the torn-through *ask him?*, his own scrawl underneath. He folds it twice, lights it from the last of the flames, and drops it in the sink.

"There," he says, and hands the rest back. "Chairs. Arguments. Wind. Let him read about the wind, if he likes."

"That was the result," Reggie says, a little roughly.

"I remember the result."

\* \* \*

They sit after that on the two stools, knee to knee, and work through the rest of it the way Pascual would a case: what is in front of them, and what is not.

John, Reggie tells him, was with Spilsbury for an hour this afternoon. He told him he had invented the whole thing. That he'd wanted Fleming to think he was doing something more exciting than drawing turtles; that Charles was only good with animals, that was all, he was only ever -- and then he cried, Reggie says, in front of Spilsbury, and couldn't stop, and Spilsbury lent him his handkerchief.

"Did he invent it?"

"No. But he thinks his letter killed Charles, so he's decided none of it can have been true." Reggie turns his bandaged hands over. "It's the most useful thing anyone's done all day."

"You sound pleased."

"I'm not pleased," Reggie says. "I'm grateful. It isn't the same thing."

Pascual looks at him and sees that it isn't.

"Then that leaves your hands, and my lie," he says. "And money. Who pays for all this?"

"The Admiralty, a little. The Colonel's friends. The Americans, mostly, these days."

"And what do the Americans want?"

Reggie's head comes up, slowly.

"Mermaids," Pascual says, "that don't die. They lost the only one that didn't, yesterday. You've a sick one in pen two and a new one in a canvas bath. Put them together. Tomorrow. In front of the Commander."

"One trial. No controls. In front of the only people who can end us."

"Beta's your control. Beta alone: dying. Beta with company: nine pounds up. You have the curve."

"I have Beta with *Charles*. It's not the same --"

"It's the nearest thing," Pascual says. "You told Hester you'd try it once. Try it with the Navy watching. If it works, you're the only man alive who can keep them going, and the Commander won't let Spilsbury have you if he has to tow you across the Atlantic himself."

"And if they tear each other apart?"

"Then I'll be standing there with the bicarbonate and the needle and whatever else I've got. And we'll be where we are now."

"Two dead mermaids worse."

"Yes," says Pascual. "Sometimes you have to operate."

Reggie is silent. Then: "He won't go home with nothing."

"No. He came for a body." Pascual nods at the padlocked cupboard in the corner. "Alpha's in there. Eleven jars. Her brain's in the big one. Give it to him. Let him take it home and cut it into slices and look for the thing between Charles and Beta, and not find it."

"That's yours. That's the only --"

"It's Jean's, really; she did the cutting. And it's dead." Pascual shrugs. "The dead don't mind. That's the first thing you learn, in my trade. It's the living you have to be careful with."

Reggie looks at him for a long time. Then he laughs: a short, startled breath, hardly a laugh at all.

"Ten hours," he says.

"It was the first number that came into my head."

"The truth was nearer fourteen. You lied in the right direction, at least."

"I was aiming for six."

"He'll have seen that too."

"Then he knows which side I'm a fool on," says Pascual. "Go and unpack your bag."

"I haven't agreed --"

"Medical opinion," Pascual says. "Go and unpack it. And then come back here."

Reggie goes. He comes back.

\* \* \*

They do it at eleven the next morning, in Gamma's pen, with everyone watching.

Pascual has never seen so many people on the deck. The Colonel, bareheaded in the sun. Commander Ferris with his cap pushed back and his arms folded. John, on an upturned bucket with his sketchbook on his knee, drawing because he does not know what else to do with his hands. Jean with the fish pail. Juan and two of the men on the sling. And Spilsbury, a little apart, in the shade of Jean's tarp, his carnation -- a new one; where does he get them? -- bright against the grey concrete.

And Hester. In all the months Pascual has been at the Institute he has never once seen Hester Bevan at the pens. She stands well back from the edge, in a straw hat and gloves, her hands folded, and comes no nearer the water than she must. Nobody remarks on it. Pascual remarks on it, silently, and then makes himself look away.

Reggie has the acoustic gear out: the hydrophone on its cable over the side, the recorder on a crate, the big headphones round his neck. *If I'm going to do this,* he said at breakfast, *I'm going to have it on tape,* and nobody argued.

Gamma is at the bottom of the pen. She has been at the bottom of the pen for three days, drifting, coming up for air at long irregular intervals that Pascual has been writing down with a feeling of dread. She does not look up when the sling swings out over the water.

Delta is small, younger than any of them, with a torn fin and a startled, furious face. When the canvas drops away and she slides into the pen she goes straight to the far wall and flattens herself there, and stares.

Nothing happens for a long time.

Then Reggie, his headphones on, says very quietly, "She's calling."

"Which?"

"Delta. Her name. Over and over." He closes his eyes. "And again. And -- there."

Gamma has lifted her head.

She rises slowly, the way a sick animal rises, as though the water were heavier than it ought to be. She hangs in the middle of the pen and looks at the newcomer. Pascual, watching her flanks, counts. One breath. Two.

Then Gamma goes for her.

It is so fast that he does not see it start. One moment there are two mermaids at opposite ends of the pen and the next there is a single thrashing knot of tails and arms in the middle of it, rolling, and the water heaves up over the rim and soaks Pascual to the knee, and the Commander says "*Jesus*," and someone shouts.

"Nets," says Spilsbury. He has not raised his voice. He has stepped out from under the tarp. "Now, please, Commander. I've seen this. We lost four that way in 'forty-nine. They'll have each other's throats out."

The Commander turns to the men at the sling. Reggie turns to Pascual.

It is only a second. Reggie's face has nothing in it, nothing at all, and his hand on the headphone cable is white to the knuckle under its bandage. And Pascual understands that Reggie cannot call it: that he has wanted this too badly for too long to trust his own eyes, and he is asking Pascual to look for him.

So Pascual looks.

He goes down on one knee at the edge, in the slop, and looks the way he would at anything on his table: not at the whole, which lies, but at the parts. Gamma's mouth is on Delta's shoulder, and closed. Delta twists free, and her hand drags across Gamma's back and leaves no mark. Gamma's tail strikes the wall, and she turns with it, using the push. There is foam in the water and no colour in it at all. He has had his hands on every inch of these animals' skin; he knows how easily it tears, and how much they bleed.

"No teeth," he says.

"Doctor --"

"No teeth. Look at the mouth. She's not biting, she's holding. There's no blood in the water. Commander, there isn't a scratch on either of them." He hears his own voice, hoarse and certain. "They're playing."

"*Playing*," says Spilsbury.

And as if to prove it, Delta breaks free and darts to the far wall and turns, and waits -- and Gamma, who has not moved faster than a drift in a fortnight, goes after her.

Reggie has the headphones pressed to his ears with both hands. "They're calling each other," he says. His voice is not quite steady. "By name. Back and forth. Back and forth. I've had them on tape calling for six years. I've never once heard one of them answered."

Nobody speaks. The two mermaids go round and round the pen, and it is not the terrible blank circling Beta did in his first months, before the boy; it is something else entirely. At the edge of it Jean, without anyone telling her to, throws in a fish.

Gamma catches it. Tears into it. Surfaces, and looks round, and Jean throws another, and Gamma takes that too.

"That's more than she's eaten all week," Jean says. Her voice is flat and her eyes are streaming. "That's more than she's eaten all *week*."

Pascual looks across at Hester. She has not moved. She is standing with her gloved hands folded and her eyes closed, and he sees her lips move, very slightly, and does not know what she is saying, or to whom.

Commander Ferris lets out a long breath. "Well," he says. "I'll be goddamned." He turns to Reggie. "Dr Tar. How many of these could you keep like this?"

Reggie takes the headphones off. "I don't know. We have six pens."

"We'll get you more pens." The Commander is already looking past him at the empty concrete beyond, as if he could see it filling. "We've a boat working the estuaries. I'd say we can have you a dozen by spring. More, if you can house them." Then he turns to Spilsbury, who is standing very still in the sun. "Doctor, I'll be straight with you. Whatever arrangement your people had in mind regarding Dr Tar -- the Navy's not interested in anything that moves this man off this island. I'll be putting that in writing tonight."

Spilsbury inclines his head. He smiles. "Of course, Commander," he says. "I quite understand."

Pascual looks at Reggie, and sees the moment he hears it. Not the Commander's last sentence: the one before. *A dozen by spring.* He watches it go into Reggie and land somewhere deep, and watches Reggie let nothing whatever show on his face, and nod, and say, "Yes. Thank you, Commander. We'll be ready."

One mermaid, Pascual thinks, out past the reef with a boy on his back. And a dozen in the pens by spring, so that Reggie Tar can stay on this cay and keep them alive. He thinks of Charles on the beach, doing the same sum, alone. He wonders whether there is a single person in this whole business who has got anything they wanted without paying for it with somebody else.

Then he goes for his bag, because the new one's fin needs looking at, and that, at least, is a thing he knows how to do.

\* \* \*

Spilsbury leaves on Thursday, as arranged.

He takes Alpha with him: eleven jars packed in straw, in a crate stencilled with the government's broad arrow, the big one wrapped in a blanket and set on the seat beside him like a passenger. He thanked Pascual for it the night before with every appearance of real warmth. "A very generous gift, Doctor. I'll send you the sections." Pascual said, "Please don't," and Spilsbury laughed as if he had made a good joke.

He offered John a post, too. Pascual has that from Jean, who had it from John: a draughtsman's job in London, with the labs, at three times the money. Plates. John said no.

"He said," Jean reports, "that he's had enough of drawing things that are dying." She is quiet a moment. "He's moved back into the hut. Charles' things are still in it. He won't let anyone touch them."

On the jetty, while the sailors cast off, Spilsbury stands a moment with Reggie, and Pascual, who has found reasons to be carrying a crate down the jetty just then, hears the end of it.

"You always did have the best ears of any of them, Reginald," Spilsbury says. "Do keep listening. I shall." He turns, and his kind tired eyes find Pascual over the top of the crate. "And Doctor. You really are the most dreadful liar. I find it rather touching. If I were you, I'd stay with the dead. They never ask you anything."

He touches two fingers to the brim of his hat and steps down into the launch, and the last Pascual sees of him is the carnation, very red, going away across the water.

Reggie watches the launch all the way to the point. Pascual sees his hands, at his sides, close once and open again, as if he had been handed something and made himself let go of it.

"Ten hours," Reggie says beside him, under his breath.

"Don't."

"He'll be back. Or someone like him. Next year, the year after. He's written it all down."

"So have you," says Pascual. "So have I. We'll see whose notes are better."

\* \* \*

That evening on the verandah there are seven chairs and seven people: the Colonel and Hester, Jean and John, the Commander's young lieutenant, who has stayed behind to see about the new pens; and Reggie; and Pascual, who comes up the steps last, as he usually does.

Reggie is sitting at the east end. The windward end. The chair beside him is empty.

Pascual looks at it. He looks at Reggie, who is not looking at him, who is looking out at the dark water with a glass in his hand and his bandaged fingers very still around it.

He sits down in it.

"It's windy at this end," he says.

"So I'm told," says Reggie.

"I'm going to complain about it every evening for the rest of my life."

"I'd expect nothing less." And without looking round, without any change in his face, Reggie takes the notebook from his shirt pocket, opens it on his knee, and makes a small mark.

Across the verandah, Hester catches Pascual's eye. She does not smile, exactly. But she lifts her glass an inch off the arm of her chair, and sets it down again; and Pascual, who has never been any good at being seen, finds that for once he does not much mind.

\* \* \*

The Americans send the new hydrophone within the month: better than anything Reggie had at the labs, on four hundred yards of armoured cable, which he and Juan and a very doubtful Pascual lay out across the reef from the boat in an afternoon and anchor in nine fathoms off the drop-off. Reggie listens to it every night. Pascual has stopped asking what he hopes to hear.

Nineteen days after Charles went into the sea, Reggie comes to the laboratory at two in the morning with the headphones in his hand.

"Listen," he says.

Pascual listens. Hiss. The far thud of surf on the reef. A crackle of shrimp like fat in a pan. Then, very faint, a long way off, a sound he knows: a low hum, then a click. He has heard it a hundred times through the wall of a concrete pen.

"That's Beta," Reggie says. "His tag. I had a hydrophone in his pen from the first week Charles was with him." He sees Pascual's face. "I never told Charles. I never told anyone. Of course I had a hydrophone in the pen, Pascual. I'm *me*. Listen."

The hum and the click again. Then a second call, different: a click, and a rising sound, two notes, almost a word.

"That one," Reggie says, "he made every time Charles came through the door. Every time. I have forty-one recordings of it. It's a name."

They listen. The hiss. The surf. Then a gap: a silence of perhaps a second and a half, two seconds. Then the second call again, softer; and the gap again.

"That's the interval," Reggie says. His voice is very quiet. "When they call in the pens now, Gamma and Delta, that's the length of the pause before the other one answers. Exactly that length."

"But there's nothing in it," Pascual says. "There's no answer."

"Nothing this microphone can hear." Reggie takes the headphones back and holds them in his lap. "Sound carries a long way in water. That could be thirty miles off, or three hundred, if it found the right layer. He's calling a name, and waiting exactly as long as he'd wait for an answer, and then calling again. The way one does, in a conversation." He stops. "Or he's calling, and no one is answering, and he waits anyway."

Pascual thinks about a pair of spectacles mended with fuse wire, which are in the top drawer of his desk and will stay there. He thinks about fresh water on every cay from here to the Passage, in Jean's neat hand, and a knapsack packed a week early by a man who would not let himself know why.

"Possibly," he says.

Reggie is quiet for a while. He has spent his whole life, Pascual knows, refusing to believe anything he could not measure. This month he has burnt a ledger, and given away his only proof, and sat in a chair at the windy end of a verandah because a man asked him to; and he has a dozen mermaids coming in the spring that he will have to live with for the rest of his life.

"Probably," says Reggie Tar. "On no evidence whatsoever."

And Pascual puts his hand to Reggie's face, there in the lamplight, the way the merfolk do, the way the stories say; and Reggie, without hesitating at all this time, lifts his own hand and answers.

Nothing opens. Nothing ever will, for them. They will have to go on saying everything out loud, badly, in two languages, for the rest of their lives.

Out past the reef, a long way off, someone is calling a name, and waiting just long enough for the answer.

## Part Three: Playback

The notebook is in two hands now.

It began by accident. Reggie left it open on the bench one night, and Pascual, who had been reading the last entry upside down for a week, picked up a pen. In the morning Reggie said nothing, and that evening there was an answer underneath. Two years on, they are a third of the way into the second volume, and it reads, Pascual sometimes thinks, like the minutes of a very small and very quarrelsome learned society.

*R.T. -- Verandah. P. complains of wind. (Running total: 431.)*

*P. -- R.T. pulse at rest, 62. Under the lamp, 88. Patient denies everything.*

*R.T. -- P. says "querido" in his sleep. Consulted the Colonel's dictionary. Cf. Hyp. A.*

*P. -- There is no Hyp. A in this book. I burned it.*

*R.T. -- I remember the result.*

*P. -- R.T. attempted a sentence in Spanish at breakfast. Jean asked whether he was choking.*

*R.T. -- Fourteen in the pens: Gamma through Pi. Ferris's boat due Thursday with two more.*

*P. -- Pi has a cough. I don't like it.*

*R.T. -- Northern estuary: Ferris's boat nought for six this season. The pods scatter before the nets go out. Ferris says it's as if they're warned.*

*P. -- Don't write that down.*

Under that, a line, struck through so hard that the nib has gone through the paper. Neither of them has written a word about the northern estuary since.

\* \* \*

It is nearly two years since the boy went into the sea.

The Institute has new pens now, poured with American money over a whole wet season, and a new generator to drown out the old one, and fourteen merfolk who mostly do not die. Jean runs the pens. There was a fortnight of correspondence from somewhere in Washington about whether a woman could, which Hester ended by not answering any of it. Gamma has grown fat and insolent. Delta has a scar across one fin where Pascual stitched it, and a habit of soaking anyone who comes near her in a white coat. John draws them every day, living, and has stopped calling anyone anything.

The reef hydrophone picked up Beta's call six times in the first two months, each fainter than the last, and then not again. Reggie still listens to it most nights. Pascual has stopped telling him not to.

And Reggie still keeps his hut on the far side of the cay. He sleeps there two nights in seven, or at least rumples the bed, so that Juan's wife, who does the laundry, will have something to wash. Pascual has never asked him to stop. They are neither of them fools. They know what is done, in England and in the colonies and in the United States Navy, to men who are found out; and they know that on a cay four miles round, with a young American lieutenant named Moss who writes very thorough reports, it would not take much.

Then the *Corinne* comes in from the estuaries a week early, and Spilsbury is aboard her.

\* \* \*

He is thinner. That is the first thing. The linen suit hangs on him, and he comes down the *Corinne*'s gangway slowly, one hand on the rail and the other pressed, very lightly, to his right side under the jacket, as if holding something in. The whites of his eyes have gone the colour of old piano keys. There is no carnation.

Pascual sees all of it in the time it takes him to reach the jetty, the way he would see it on any man who walked into his surgery, and he knows; and Spilsbury, looking up, sees him know. He smiles.

"Doctor Pobil," he says warmly. "Still among the living, I see. And Reginald." To Reggie he only nods. Then he holds out to Pascual a flat wooden box with brass corners. "I promised you the sections. You told me not to send them, so I've brought them instead."

Pascual takes it. He does not need to open it to know what is inside: Alpha, in slices, mounted and stained and labelled in a fine copperplate hand. Two years' work, for somebody.

"There's nothing in them," Spilsbury says. "I looked at every one. Twice. Nothing in the brain, nothing in the skin, nothing in the hands. Whatever it is, Doctor, it isn't in them." He looks out past the jetty at the pens, at the sleek dark heads breaking the water, and his voice goes softer still. "Which means, I'm afraid, that it must be in us."

He has given Reggie nothing but a nod and his name. Pascual, glancing sideways, sees Reggie register it -- the warmth all spent on the doctor, and for him a nod -- and sees it land; sees the jaw set against it, the way it sets against a cold wind. Two years, Pascual thinks, and the man can still do that to him with a nod.

\* \* \*

He waits until after supper, on the verandah, with all of them there, to bring out the tin.

He sets it down among the glasses without any ceremony at all. It is an ordinary chart tube, the kind every boat at the Institute carries: dented, rusted at the seams, with a crust of dried salt in the threads of the cap. On the cap, scratched with the point of a knife, are two letters. Pascual knows them. Reggie scratches them into everything he owns: his calipers, his hydrophone housings, the handle of the long gaff. *R.T.*

Nobody moves.

"Found," Spilsbury says, "in a camp on the Guiana coast, forty miles up a river the Navy's charts don't name with any confidence. A lean-to of mangrove poles. A fire pit. A great many fish bones. Somebody had been living there for the better part of a year, and left in a hurry when the *Corinne*'s boats came round the bend. They left this." He unscrews the cap and draws out a roll of paper gone soft as cloth. It is Jean's chart: Pascual recognises her hand at once, the neat capitals, *Fresh water*, *Keep clear -- ordnance*. And over and around and between Jean's notes, crowded into every margin, in pencil and then in something brown, there is a second hand, small and crabbed. Rolled inside the chart is a thick fold of pages wrapped in oilcloth.

Jean has gone the colour of the tablecloth. Hester has not moved at all.

"He's alive," Spilsbury says, and looks round at all of them with real, gentle pleasure, like a man bringing good news to a family. His eyes come to rest on John. "Isn't that wonderful?"

John's chair goes over backwards. He is on his feet, staring at the tin. "He's *alive*?" he says. "He's -- all this time, I thought --" He stops. His face is working. Then he turns and goes down the verandah steps into the dark, quickly, without another word, and they hear him break into a run.

Spilsbury watches him go with interest. Then he unwraps the oilcloth and, before anyone can stop him, begins to read aloud.

"*Day ninety-something. I have lost count, which M. thinks is very funny. Today I was in the water with eleven of them at once and it was loud, but not like Gamma, not like the pens. It was like walking into a room full of people talking, and finding that some of them were talking to me. M. kept his hand on my back the whole time so that I wouldn't lose myself. I didn't. I think I am learning how not to.*"

He reads it in his soft voice, slowly, with great tenderness, the way a man might read aloud a love letter that was not addressed to him; and it is, Pascual thinks, quite the most obscene thing he has ever heard.

\* \* \*

Afterwards, Spilsbury asks the two of them to walk with him on the jetty.

"Both of you," he says pleasantly, when Reggie begins to object. "You come as a pair now, I'm told."

The *Corinne* lies alongside, dark except for her riding light. Below her waterline, Pascual knows, are the tanks: four of them, empty this season. Spilsbury walks slowly, with his hand at his side, and stops at the far end, where the lamp doesn't reach.

"The Navy has had a very poor year in that estuary," he says. "Nought for six, I'm told. The pods scatter before the nets go out. Commander Ferris has a theory, which he has put to me with some heat, that a renegade Englishman has gone native in the mangroves and is driving his animals off. The Commander would like him stopped. He wasn't precise about the means." He turns his yellowed eyes on Reggie. "I, on the other hand, would like him kept. Alive, and well, and talking. You can see which of us is offering the boy the kinder future."

"No," says Reggie.

"I haven't asked you anything yet, Reginald." Spilsbury sounds faintly reproachful. "You had a hydrophone in Beta's pen. Don't trouble to deny it; you can't help yourself, you never could. So you have his call. And you'll have whatever call he had for the boy, if he had one, which I imagine he did. The Navy has lent me a projector, a very good one. We play Beta his own name, in that river. He answers. We take a bearing. Where Beta is, the boy will be." He turns to Pascual. "And we'll want a physician. He's been a year on fish and rainwater, and if I'm any judge of a man's handwriting he hasn't been well. I shouldn't like to lose him to scurvy before we've had a chance to talk."

"And if we won't," Pascual says.

Spilsbury sighs. It is not an unkind sound.

"Then I shall have to show the Commander the tin. Mr Tar's initials on the cap. Miss Leslie's hand on the chart. The Navy takes a dim view of people who give its property away. Miss Leslie would never work with animals again, and Reginald would be lucky to work at all." He pauses. "And then there's Lieutenant Moss. Such a conscientious young man. He writes very thorough reports. He mentions, in one of them, that Mr Tar's hut is always very tidy." He smiles at them both, gently. "Hardly slept in."

The jetty seems, for a moment, to tilt under Pascual's feet.

"I'm not a moralist," Spilsbury says. "I've never had the least interest in what men do with their evenings. But the Americans, you know, are so much less broad-minded than I am." He takes the oilcloth packet from his pocket and puts it into Reggie's hands. "Read it. All of it. I'd like you to know who you're looking for." He touches two fingers to his hat brim. "We sail on Friday. Bring your tapes, Reginald. Bring your doctor. Good night."

\* \* \*

They read the pages in Pascual's room, on the bed, with the lamp between them, until the oil runs low.

There are perhaps forty sheets, torn from a field notebook with marbled boards that Pascual remembers seeing on the ledge by Beta's pen, always closed, always face down. Some are dated. Most are not. The pencil gives out somewhere in the second month, and after that the boy writes in something brown and faintly fishy that Reggie, after a long look, identifies as squid ink.

*Day 11. Jean's water was there. Exactly where she said, in a hole in the rock under a sea-grape, green and full of mosquito larvae and the best thing I have ever tasted. If it hadn't been, I think I would have died tomorrow. Thank God for Jean.*

*M. tows me. I hold on to his shoulders and he goes like a train. He thinks it's very funny that I can't keep up. When I'm too tired to hold on he lets me float on my back and pushes me along with his head. I have used nearly all the zinc paste.*

*He catches fish for me and is offended when I cook them. I have tried to explain fire. He thinks it is a waste of a perfectly good fish.*

Pascual makes a small noise.

"What?"

"Nothing. He's cooking them. Go on."

*We are home. I didn't know it until we were in it: the water changes, browner and softer, and full of voices. So many voices. M. went still, and then he called, and someone answered, and then everyone answered, and I had to hold on to the mangrove roots, because it was like a wave going over my head. His mother is alive. He put his hand on her face, the way a calf does, and she held quite still and let him, and I had to look away, it was so --. I don't have a word for it. I've stopped expecting to have words for things.*

*The-beautiful-one-who-is-dangerous-when-crossed has a calf. She looked at me for a long time today. Then she let me hold him.*

*They have a name for us. I keep trying to write it down. The nearest I can get is: the-ones-shaped-like-us-who-do-not-answer.*

Reggie's hand is quite still on the page. Pascual puts his own over it.

*Some of what I left behind I miss more than the rest. Nobody here has an opinion about what shape I am. I hadn't known how tired I was of people having opinions.*

*M. has names for everyone at the Martin. Jean is a sort of crab that carries a sponge about on its back -- affectionate, I think. Pascual is the-one-with-needles. That isn't affectionate, but it isn't unkind either: M. says, as near as I can make out, that he was angry and he was right. He has no name for Reggie at all. When I asked, he showed me the generator.*

Pascual laughs before he can help it, and then can't stop, and Reggie says, "I fail to see what's funny," with great dignity, and that only makes it worse.

*It IS a language. I'm sure of it now. Half of it is touch and half of it is sound, and the sound is mostly names, which is why it looks like nothing much if all you have is the sound. Reggie would want to argue. I wish he could.*

Reggie reads that one twice. Then he sets the page down, very carefully, and does not pick up the next one for a while.

*The boats came again. M. felt them before I heard them, and I felt M. I took everyone up the creek past the second bend, where the water's too shallow for their launches. I know where the boats come from. I'm sorry. I can't let them.*

*My gums bleed. Probably nothing.*

"It isn't nothing," Pascual tells the page.

*There is something M. has been trying to show me for days, and I think I've understood it at last. He took me to a cave under the big mangrove at the creek mouth and showed me -- I don't know whether it was a memory or a story, something old -- two of them in it, both sick, both coughing, touching each other; and then something so bad that I had to get out of the water. As near as I can put it into words: you must never put two sick ones together in a cave. Everyone knows that. The calves know it. In open water it doesn't matter; it goes out into the sea, and the sea takes it. In a cave it has nowhere to go. It comes back off the walls and goes through them again, and again, until --*

*A pen is a cave.*

*I told Hester to put them together. I stood in her sitting room and told her they weren't fighting, they were playing, and I was so sure. I was right about M. and me, and I was right about open water. And Gamma was sick when I left, and they were going to put her in with the new one, and I don't know whether the new one was well. I've thought about it every night since. Reggie wouldn't put them together, and I thought that was only Reggie being Reggie. He was right. Maybe not for the reason he thought. But he was right, and I wasn't, and there's no one I can tell.*

Pascual looks up from the page. Reggie's face has gone a colour Pascual has seen before, on the table.

"Forty-nine," Reggie says.

It takes Pascual a moment. Then he has it: the pen on the cay two years ago, the water heaving over the rim, and Spilsbury stepping out from under Jean's tarp. *We lost four that way in 'forty-nine.*

"Spilsbury's four."

"Mine," says Reggie. "They were mine. It was my idea. They came out of one river on one day and called each other every night, and they were dying, all four of them -- pneumonia -- and I thought, if it's company they want -- I went to him and asked for it. I set it up. I stood at the edge of the tank and watched." His voice is perfectly level, which is how Pascual knows. "I wrote it up that night. I wrote that it was like feedback: a microphone set too near its own loudspeaker. I wrote the word down as a figure of speech, Pascual, and I've read that page a hundred times in six years and never once --" He stops.

Pascual is thinking of something else. He is thinking of Gamma on the bottom of her pen, three days drifting, the long ragged intervals at the surface that he wrote down with such dread. And of Delta sliding out of the canvas: small and furious and torn in one fin, and otherwise -- he went over her himself that afternoon, every inch -- the healthiest animal he had laid hands on in a year.

"If she'd been sick," he says.

"Then we'd have lost them both in front of Ferris, and Spilsbury would have been right, and I'd have been on his launch on the Thursday." Reggie puts the page down. "I was right. That's the part I can't -- I was right not to put them together, that whole first year, for entirely the wrong reasons. And the boy was wrong. And you knelt in the slop and said *no teeth*, and we were lucky. That's all it was. A healthy animal off a Navy boat. Luck."

Pascual told him, that night in the laboratory, that sometimes you have to operate. He would very much like, now, to take it back.

"We tell Jean," he says at last. "In the morning, before Ferris sends another boat. Never two sick ones in one pen."

Reggie nods. He takes out the notebook and writes it down, and then -- Pascual watches him do it -- draws a line under it. Pascual has never seen him underline anything before.

The last few sheets are not a journal at all. They are a letter, or the beginning of one, written carefully, with the date for once at the top.

*If this ever gets back to the Martin --*

*Jean: thank you for the map. You knew, didn't you. I should have known you knew.*

*Pascual: I left my glasses by the pen. I'd like you to have them, if nobody's thrown them away. You were the only one who ever said "boy" as if it were a kind word.*

Pascual has to put that one down.

The glasses are in the top drawer of his desk, four feet from where he is sitting, in an oilcloth pouch he sewed for them himself, with the fuse wire still on the arm. Reggie knows they are there. He has never once mentioned them.

*Reggie: your hand was shaking when you gave me the tin. I won't tell anyone.*

*Tell H. that they still say his name. As near as I can write it:*

And then, not letters at all, but a notation: a short vertical stroke, and then a long wavering line. Pascual has watched Reggie write calls this way in his ledgers a hundred times. A click. Then a hum.

"Who is H.?" Reggie says, very quietly.

Pascual looks at the stroke and the wave for a long moment. He thinks of a woman in a straw hat and gloves, standing well back from the edge of a pen with her eyes closed, saying something to someone.

"I think," he says, "that isn't ours to know."

He tears the page out, carefully, along the fold. Reggie watches him do it and doesn't say a word.

\* \* \*

They don't sleep. Towards three Reggie gets up and goes out, and comes back with a reel of tape in a flat tin case, and sets it on the blanket between them like an exhibit.

"Beta," he says. "His tag, and his call for the boy. Forty-one recordings. If I burn it, Spilsbury will know why. If I don't, he'll use it. If I refuse to go, he shows Ferris the tin and Moss's report, and we're on a boat to somewhere very unpleasant by the end of the month, and Jean with us, and he finds someone else to play the tape. He doesn't need me. He only needs my ears."

"So we go," Pascual says.

"So we go." Reggie looks at the tape. "And then there's this."

He takes a second case from his pocket: older, battered, a War Department stencil half scraped off the lid.

"Nineteen forty-seven," he says. "The labs. Spilsbury's Eleven. I had a hydrophone in her tank the whole of that week, because I was cataloguing calls and I thought it was a good opportunity." His voice does not change at all. "It's the clearest danger call I have ever recorded. Every one of them in that river will know what it means. I published the waveform in my first paper; it's in every library in the world. Nobody ever asked me where I got it."

Pascual looks at the case, and then at him.

"I have been listening to them for ten years," Reggie says. "I have never once said anything back."

That is the plan, in the end. It takes the rest of the night to make it into something that might work, and it is not, Pascual thinks, a very good plan; but it is theirs, and they make it together, on two stools in the laboratory with the notebook open between them, writing in it by turns. Spilsbury will want the lure played; the lure will be played. Beta will answer. Beta will come, and where Beta comes, the boy will come. And when the nets go out --

"I'll need to be in the water," Pascual says.

"Absolutely not."

"He has scurvy, Reggie. He's been cooking his fish, the fool. I can't send him a letter. And someone has to tell him what's happening before you play that tape, or he'll see you in the bow, think you've sold him, and do something noble and stupid to save Beta." He reaches into the drawer, takes out the oilcloth pouch, and buttons it into his breast pocket. "Spilsbury wants a physician for the boy. He shall have one."

"There won't be time to explain anything," Reggie says. "Not with Spilsbury in the boat."

"No." Pascual looks at him. "But you told me how they do it. A hand to the face. If he's what I think Hester is, I won't need to explain. He'll look."

Reggie goes very still. "He'd see everything."

"Then he'll see everything," says Pascual. "There's nothing in me I'd mind him seeing." He considers. "Nearly nothing."

Reggie looks at the pouch for a long time.

"If you drown," he says at last, in a voice Pascual has never heard him use, "I shall be extremely cross."

"Write it down," says Pascual.

\* \* \*

On Thursday night, past midnight, Pascual goes up to the bungalow.

Hester opens the door herself, in her dressing gown, as though she has been expecting someone; perhaps she has. He does not go in. He gives her the page, folded, and stands on the step while she reads it in the light from the hall.

She does not say anything for a long time. Her other hand has gone to the door frame.

"Thank you, Doctor," she says at last. Her voice is perfectly steady. "Please tell him --" She stops. "No. You can't tell him anything, can you. Not really. Not the way --" She stops again; and when she speaks next it is in her administrator's voice, brisk and clear. "Be careful on that boat. And bring Reggie back. I shan't manage the Americans without him."

She shuts the door very gently. As he goes down the steps Pascual hears, through it, a sound that he will never mention to anyone.

John is waiting for him at the bottom of the path, in the dark, with his hands in his pockets.

"If you see him," John says.

"I'm not going to see him."

"If you see him." John doesn't look at him. "Tell him I'm sorry. About -- what I used to call him. He'll know." And he walks away before Pascual can answer.

\* \* \*

The *Corinne* takes three days to the coast. Pascual is sick for the first of them, and Reggie sits with him in the cramped cabin and reads him the boy's pages again, by request, until he falls asleep.

On the second afternoon, steadier, Pascual goes up on deck and finds Reggie and Spilsbury together at the chart table under the wheelhouse awning, with the river mouth spread out between them on the Admiralty chart.

He stops at the top of the companionway. He doesn't mean to listen.

Spilsbury is asking about the river: how sound will carry in it, fresh water lying over salt, the tide pushing in underneath. Reggie is answering. He has a pencil in his hand and is drawing in the margin of the chart -- curves, layers, the path a call would take bending through them -- and he is talking faster than Pascual has ever heard him talk, his whole face alight, the way he talks to Pascual about the hydrophone at two in the morning when he forgets that Pascual doesn't understand half of it. Except that Spilsbury understands all of it. Pascual can see that. He is leaning on the table with his hand at his side, nodding, putting in a word here and there that sends Reggie off again, faster.

"Yes," Spilsbury says. "Yes. Exactly so, Reginald. You always did see it before anyone else."

Reggie looks up, pleased -- and sees Pascual at the companionway, and stops in the middle of a sentence.

Pascual watches him understand what he has been doing. He watches the light go out of his face, not all at once but deliberately, like a man turning down a lamp. Reggie puts the pencil down. "I think that covers it," he says, in quite a different voice, and goes below.

That night in the cabin, before Pascual can open his mouth, Reggie says, "Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to." Reggie is lying on the lower bunk with his arm over his eyes. "He's the only man alive who understands the work. Not the results; the work. Do you know what that's like? To spend ten years doing something nobody can follow, and then the one person who can follow it is --" He stops. "I hate that I did that. I'd do it again tomorrow. That's the part I hate."

Pascual sits on the edge of the bunk and puts his hand flat on Reggie's chest, over his heart, and leaves it there. He doesn't understand the work. He never will. He thinks Reggie knows that, and has chosen him anyway.

On the third evening, at dusk, Pascual finds Spilsbury alone at the rail.

The coast is close now. You can smell it: rot and silt and something green, a river smell, coming out over the sea to meet them. Spilsbury stands very straight, both hands on the rail, and in the light from the wheelhouse his face is the colour of tallow.

"How long?" Pascual says.

Spilsbury doesn't pretend not to understand. "The man in Harley Street said six months. That was in February." He glances sideways. "You saw it on the jetty. I watched you see it. Reginald didn't; he never looks at the body. That always was his weakness."

"You should be in a bed."

"I should be in a great many places." Spilsbury turns back to the dark water. "I've put my hand in every tank in England, Doctor. Did Reginald tell you? After the boy. I had them brought up from the labs one at a time, and I got into the water with them, and I held their faces. One of them took two of my fingers for my trouble." He lifts his left hand, and in the wheelhouse light Pascual sees the neat, healed stumps. "Nothing. Not a flicker. I'm not built for it, it seems. Most people aren't." He is quiet a moment. "Forty years I have spent getting inside things. Bodies. Brains. Men's alibis. I have always, always been able to get inside. And here at last is a door, and I can't so much as find the handle."

Pascual says nothing.

"You and Reginald," Spilsbury says. "You manage without it. I've watched you, on the verandah. I've wondered how."

It is so unexpected that Pascual answers him honestly. "Badly," he says. "Out loud. In two languages. We get it wrong most of the time."

"How very inefficient," says Spilsbury, and smiles; and then, after a long moment, in a voice Pascual has not heard from him before, almost wistful: "Still."

Then he takes his hands from the rail, and touches his hat, and goes below, and leaves Pascual alone with the smell of the river and the uncomfortable knowledge that for perhaps ten seconds he has been sorry for him.

Afterwards he goes to find Reggie on the hatch cover, and tells him all of it: the six months, and the door, and *Still*.

\* \* \*

They go up the river at first light in the *Corinne*'s launch: Spilsbury in the stern under an awning, Lieutenant Moss at the tiller, four sailors with the nets, and Reggie in the bow with the projector -- a squat grey drum on a boom -- and his headphones, and the recorder on the thwart beside him. Pascual sits amidships with his bag on his knees and his jacket buttoned over the oilcloth pouch, the pockets of the jacket heavy with limes.

The river is brown and slow and enormous, walled in on either hand by mangrove, the roots going down into the water like the legs of something. Birds Pascual has no names for go up off the mudbanks as they pass. Forty miles in, past the second bend, Moss cuts the engine, and in the sudden quiet Pascual can hear the water talking against the hull.

"Here," says Spilsbury. "This is where we found the camp. Reginald, if you would."

Reggie looks at Pascual once. Then he threads the tape, and settles the headphones, and lowers the drum into the water, and plays Beta his own name.

The hum, and then the click. Out into the river, louder than any mermaid ever made it. Pascual feels it in the planks under his feet. The hum, and the click. The hum, and the click.

Nothing.

Reggie plays it again. And again. Spilsbury sits quite still under his awning, his hand at his side.

Then Reggie's head comes up sharply, and his hand goes to the headphones.

"He's answered," he says.

He does not sound like a man who has betrayed someone. He sounds like a man who has heard a voice he thought he would never hear again; and for a moment, before he can school it, his whole face is naked with it. Pascual sees Spilsbury see it too.

"Bearing?" says Moss.

Reggie points upriver, toward a bend where the mangroves lean together. "Close. Half a mile. Coming."

"Nets," says Spilsbury softly, and the sailors stand.

Reggie plays the second call -- a click and a rising whistle, two notes, almost a word: the boy's name in Beta's mouth -- and this time Pascual doesn't need headphones. He hears the answer himself, faint and then nearer, a long way off across the water and then not a long way off at all: the click and the whistle, in a living voice.

A head breaks the surface forty yards off the bow. Two heads.

One is Beta. Pascual would know that cocky, suspicious face anywhere; he has had his thumb in that animal's mouth. And the other --

He is very thin, and very brown, and his hair has grown past his shoulders and gone pale at the ends with sun, and there is a beard. He has no glasses. He is squinting at the launch, one hand on Beta's shoulder and the other raised against the glare, the way a short-sighted man squints, and Pascual knows that he cannot make out a single face in it.

"There," says Spilsbury, very gently. "There he is."

The sailors lift the nets.

Pascual stands, takes off his shoes, and goes over the side.

Nobody stops him. He told Moss on the voyage, and Spilsbury agreed, that the boy would be frightened, that a familiar face in the water might be the only way to keep him calm; and so it is only Reggie who makes a sound when he goes in, and only Reggie who knows that the sound is not about the boy.

The river is warm as blood and brown as tea, and Pascual is not a good swimmer. He goes under once and comes up with a mouthful of silt, and strikes out clumsily for the two heads, which have not moved. Beta watches him come with an expression of deep and disgusted recognition. Then the boy's face changes.

"*Pascual?*"

"Boy," Pascual says, and swallows river, and coughs. He gets near enough. He fumbles at his breast pocket under the water, and gets the pouch out, and holds it up. "Your glasses."

Charles stares at them. Then he reaches out and takes them, very carefully, as if they might dissolve. And Pascual, treading water, with the river in his mouth and the nets coming up behind him, does what he decided on in the laboratory at four in the morning: he puts out his hand and lays it flat along the side of the boy's face.

Pascual has been told about this. He has read the boy's pages, and Hester's old stories at second hand, and he has stood in his own lamplight with his hand on Reggie's face and felt nothing open at all. He thinks he knows what to expect.

He does not. It lasts about a second. Nothing comes into him: no door opens his way, no pictures, no flood. It runs the other way entirely. It is like being looked at, for the first time in his life, by someone who can see all the way down -- and who goes, before anything else, straight past all the rest of him to look for one thing. Pascual feels him find it: the pen on the cay two years ago, the water heaving over the rim, and Gamma and Delta going round and round together; Gamma taking fish from Jean's hand; Gamma two years on, grown fat and insolent. Only then does the boy look at the rest of it. The river, the launch. Spilsbury under his awning with his yellow eyes and his ruined hand. The nets. The second case in Reggie's pocket. And all the rest of it, two years of it, the verandah and the wind and the notebook in two hands, and a word he has never once said out loud in either of his languages. He feels the boy see every bit of it. It is the most frightening thing that has ever happened to him, and it is a kindness.

Charles turns his face out of Pascual's hand.

"Gamma's alive," he says. His eyes are wet, or it is the river. "She's alive. The new one was well -- Delta, she was well. Oh, thank God. Pascual, I've been so frightened. I told Hester to put them together, I was so *sure*, and then Monty showed me the cave, and Gamma was sick when I left, and every night since I've --" He stops, and breathes. "You were lucky. All of you. You were so lucky."

"I know," says Pascual. "Reggie knows. We read your pages."

Then Charles looks at him again, properly, and something else comes into his face, something that anywhere else would have been a laugh. "Oh, Pascual," he says. "You haven't *told* him."

"There hasn't been --"

"Tell him." Charles puts the glasses on. They're crooked; the fuse wire has slipped. He looks past Pascual at the launch, and Pascual watches the faces come into focus for him one by one, and sees which one he stops at. "Tell him I heard. Tell him thank you. Tell John it's all right, about Fish Boy. Tell Jean --"

"Limes," says Pascual, absurdly, and begins emptying his pockets into the water between them. "For your gums. You've been cooking your fish, you idiot. Raw is better. The liver especially. Eat the *liver* --"

Charles catches limes. Beta catches one, sniffs it, and looks at Pascual with profound contempt.

In the bow of the launch, Reggie takes off his headphones. Pascual sees him do it. He sees him take the second case from his pocket and thread the second tape, steadily, without hurrying, with his back to Spilsbury; sees him put his hand on the switch, and look across forty yards of brown water at the boy, and at Pascual, and wait.

Charles looks back at him. Then he nods.

Reggie plays them the danger call.

Pascual has never heard it before. He hopes never to hear it again. It is not loud, as such things go; it is only a voice. A single voice, a mermaid who died in a government tank in 1947, saying the one thing she had left to say. It goes out into the river and the river carries it, and all around them, up and down the mangroves, Pascual hears it taken up -- a call, and then another, and then a dozen, near and far, passing it on, *danger*, *danger*, the whole estuary saying it at once.

Beta dives. Charles takes a breath, and looks at Pascual one last time, with the crooked glasses on his nose and a lime in his fist, and grins -- the boy's old, awkward, astonished grin -- and dives after him.

Then there is nothing on the water but limes.

The sailors cast the nets anyway. They come up full of leaves. Moss swears, and brings the launch round, and they quarter that reach of the river for an hour, and then two, and hear nothing on the hydrophone but shrimp and the slow talk of the water. Reggie hauls Pascual out of the river by his collar and his belt, not gently, and sits him down on the bottom boards, and stays there beside him with one hand clamped on the back of his neck, as though he might go in again.

Spilsbury has not moved from under his awning the whole time.

\* \* \*

That evening, at anchor off the river mouth, Spilsbury sends for Pascual.

He is lying on his bunk in the *Corinne*'s one decent cabin, his jacket off and his collar open, and he looks, Pascual thinks, a good deal nearer to February's six months than he did a week ago. The tin lies on the blanket beside him. Pascual makes himself stay by the door.

"That was Eleven, wasn't it," Spilsbury says. "On the second tape. I'd know her anywhere. Reginald recorded her the week we opened her." He closes his eyes. "He always did keep everything."

Pascual says nothing. He has no idea what will happen now. He finds, rather to his surprise, that he is not frightened; he is too tired, and there is still river in his ears.

"I could tell Moss," says Spilsbury, without opening his eyes. "I could tell Ferris. I could give them the tin, and the hut, and that tape, and they'd have the pair of you in irons by Tuesday. You understand that."

"Yes."

"I'm not going to." He opens his eyes. "Do you know why?"

"No."

"Because it wouldn't get me anything I want." He says it simply, as a matter of fact. "I have never in my life done a thing that didn't. That isn't virtue, Doctor; don't mistake it for one. I simply haven't the time left to waste on spite." He looks at Pascual for a long moment with his yellowed, intelligent, terrible eyes. "You put your hand to his face. In the water. I saw yours."

"Yes."

"What was it like?"

And Pascual thinks of the river, and the boy's wet face under his palm, and the second in which he was looked at all the way to the bottom.

"Like being seen," he says. "That's all. He saw me. It took about a second. Then he turned his face away."

Spilsbury lies very still.

"Thank you," he says at last, quite quietly; and Pascual understands that this is the nearest Spilsbury will ever come to the door, and that Spilsbury knows it.

Then he picks up the tin and holds it out.

"Take it. Throw it in the river, if you like. I shan't need it." He watches Pascual take it. "But I should be doing you a disservice, Doctor, if I let you think that was the end of it. I've written it all down. The tin. The hut. Ten hours. That tape. It's all in my notes, and my notes go to the Admiralty archive when I die, with everything else I've ever written. Someone will read them, one day. I've no idea who."

"Burn them," says Pascual.

Spilsbury smiles, and closes his eyes again.

"I have never destroyed data in my life," he says.

Pascual is at the door when Spilsbury speaks again, without opening his eyes.

"Tell Reginald," he says, "that it was very neatly done. The second tape. He'll pretend not to care."

Pascual tells him that night on deck, because he has decided on the way up the companionway that he has no right not to.

Reggie stands very still at the rail. He does not say anything for long enough that Pascual begins to regret it.

"I wish you hadn't told me," he says at last.

"Should I not have?"

"No." Reggie's hands tighten on the rail. "No, I'd have wanted to know. That's the trouble. That's always been the trouble." He is quiet a moment. "He's right, you know. It was very neatly done."

Then, with an effort Pascual can see in the set of his shoulders, he lets go of the rail, and turns his back on the river, and goes below.

\* \* \*

On the first night home, in the narrow room behind the laboratory, with the generator droning and the tin on the shelf beside the empty oilcloth pouch, Pascual does what he was told.

He tells Reggie the rest first. *I heard. Thank you.* He watches Reggie take that in, and look away, and not say anything for a while.

Then he says the other thing. He says it in Spanish first, because that is easier, and because Reggie has been working through the Colonel's dictionary for two years, and Pascual would very much like to know whether it took.

It did. Reggie goes completely still.

So then he says it in English, out loud, badly, with his hand on Reggie's face; and gets it wrong the first time, and has to start again; and gets it right.

Reggie is quiet for so long that Pascual begins to wonder whether he has made an error in the design. Then Reggie lifts his own hand to Pascual's face, and answers him: out loud, in English, and then, appallingly, in Spanish, with an accent that would have made Jean ask whether he was choking.

Nothing opens. Nothing ever will. They don't need it to.

Afterwards, when Reggie is asleep, Pascual lies awake for a while and watches the notebook on the shelf, where Reggie left it with the pen across it. He waits for Reggie to wake and reach for it, the way he reaches for it after everything: to write down the time, and the pulse, and the result.

Reggie doesn't. In the morning the page is still blank, and it stays blank; and Pascual, who has read every page of that notebook upside down and right way up for two years, understands that it is the first thing Reggie Tar has ever decided not to record.

Not because it isn't data. Because he'll remember the result.

\* \* \*

Spilsbury dies in London at the end of September.

The letter comes out on the October launch, at the bottom of the mailbag, under the Navy's requisitions and a parcel of journals for Jean. It is addressed to the Colonel, from someone at the Admiralty whose name none of them knows, and it is very short. Hester brings it down to the laboratory herself, at four in the afternoon, when she knows they will both be there. She puts it on the bench in front of Reggie and stands there while he reads it, and that is how Pascual knows what it is before he has seen a word.

Reggie reads it twice. Then he folds it along its creases and hands it to Pascual without looking at him.

*... regret to inform you that Dr B. Spilsbury died on the 21st inst., after an illness borne with great fortitude. The end was peaceful. In accordance with his wishes, his papers have been deposited entire with the Department, where --*

*Peaceful.* Pascual, who knows what the liver does at the end -- the yellow going to bronze, the belly filling, the bleeding from the gums, the slow confusion, the last days when a man no longer knows the faces at his bedside -- does not believe a word of it. Then he reads it again, and his eye catches on *entire*, and stays there.

"Thank you, Hester," Reggie says.

"I thought you'd rather have it from me than at supper." She looks at him a moment, with an expression Pascual cannot read and suspects he isn't meant to. "It's quite all right to be sorry, Reggie."

"I'm not."

"No," says Hester gently. "Of course not." She touches his shoulder, once, very lightly, through his shirt, and goes.

Reggie picks up his pen and goes back to what he was doing, which is calibrating the reef hydrophone against a tuning fork. He does it for an hour, with great care. Pascual watches him take the same reading four times.

At supper the Colonel tells the others, briefly and without comment, as he does everything. John says nothing. Jean says, "Good riddance," and then glances down the table at Reggie and looks as though she would like to take it back. Reggie is cutting his fish into very small, very exact pieces.

"Yes," he says. "Quite."

He does not eat any of them.

\* \* \*

Pascual wakes at two and the bed beside him is empty.

He finds Reggie in the laboratory, as he knew he would, in his shirtsleeves at the bench with the lamp turned low. There is a trunk open on the floor beside him that Pascual has never seen opened: a cheap tin trunk with a War Department stencil, the kind men bring home and push under a bed and do not look at again. The bench is covered with notebooks. Small, black, oilcloth-covered, cracked along the spines with age and damp. Dozens of them.

"I didn't know you'd kept those," Pascual says.

"I keep everything." Reggie doesn't look up. He is holding one open in both hands. "This was the first. He gave it to me the week I started. Bought it himself, from a stationer's in the Strand -- I remember, because I thought it was extraordinarily kind, and it was. He said, *Write everything down, Reginald. Memory is a liar, and so are you.*" He turns a page. "I was twenty-six. Nobody had ever bought me anything for my work before."

Pascual pulls the other stool round and sits.

"He brought me oranges," Reggie says, "the winter I had influenza. Forty-six. There weren't any oranges in London that winter; I've no idea where he found them. He sat on the end of my bed and read me the *Lancet* until I fell asleep." He is quiet a moment. "He was the first person who ever told me I had good ears. People had told me I was clever all my life. It isn't the same. He was the first person who looked at what I actually did, and saw it."

The generator drones. Pascual says nothing.

"And then he'd go next door," Reggie says, in exactly the same voice, "and open one of them up while it was still awake, because he wanted to see how the heart behaved, and come back through and ask me how the recordings were coming along. And I would tell him. I'd play him the best bits." He closes the notebook. "I've been trying all day to decide whether I'm glad he's dead."

"Of course you have."

"I've drawn up a table."

"Of course you have," Pascual says again, more gently; and Reggie, after a moment, turns the current notebook round on the bench so that he can see it.

*Glad: Eleven. The others (n=31). The tin. The hut. Ten hours. Moss's report. He would have come back.*

*Not glad: Oranges. The Strand. "Best ears." "Very neatly done." He gave the tin back. He said thank you.*

Underneath, in smaller writing: *Units incommensurable. Cannot be weighted.* And under that, struck through so many times that the nib has torn the paper, something Pascual cannot make out at all.

"It doesn't balance," Reggie says. "I've tried it every way. I keep adding things to both sides." He presses the heels of his hands against his eyes. "And I'm still frightened of him. That's what I can't -- He's dead, and his papers are in a box in Whitehall, *entire*, with the hut and the tin and you in them, and I'm sitting here at two in the morning frightened of a dead man, exactly as frightened as I was in the spring. I thought I'd stop when he died. I haven't."

"No," says Pascual. "You won't. Not for a while."

"Is that a medical opinion?"

"Yes."

Reggie lowers his hands. His face is quite dry, and quite white.

"Do you know the worst of it?" he says. "I understand him. I said the same sentence to you, over the sink, before I burned the ledger. *I've never destroyed data in my life.* And he said it to you on the boat, word for word. We had the same sentence, he and I. He taught it to me. The only difference --" He stops.

"Go on."

"The only difference is that I had someone standing next to me at the sink," Reggie says. "And he didn't. That's all. That's the whole of the difference between us. It isn't very much."

Pascual looks at him for a long time: at the stubble in the lamplight, and the white knuckles, and the cracked old notebook from the Strand.

"I think," Pascual says carefully, "the difference is that you let someone stand beside you at the sink. That's all the difference in the world. And I don't think he ever told himself it was a choice he might make."

Reggie looks at him. Then something in his face gives way -- not all at once, but the way a sea wall goes, a stone at a time and then the rest -- and he puts his head down on his folded arms on the bench, among the notebooks, and weeps: badly, almost silently, his shoulders shaking, the way a man weeps who has not done it since school and has forgotten entirely how it is done.

Pascual gets up and stands behind him and puts his arms round him, and holds on. For once he does not take his pulse. He does not count anything at all.

When it's over, Reggie sits up and wipes his face on his sleeve with great precision, and looks at the notebooks, and the open trunk, and the table that will not balance.

"I'm not going to burn them," he says. "I want you to know that. I thought about it all evening. But someone ought to be able to read them one day and know what we did. That was always the only defence I had. It still is."

"I know."

"Even the parts about him being kind."

"Especially those," says Pascual.

Reggie puts the notebooks back in the trunk one at a time, in order, and closes the lid. Then he takes up the current notebook -- theirs, in two hands -- and looks at the table on its page for a while; and then, very deliberately, writes one more line beneath it, and closes it, and puts it in his pocket. Pascual doesn't ask what the line was. He reads it the next morning, upside down, while Reggie is shaving.

*Glad: he isn't in pain any more.*

Nobody comes to the Institute for anything, that autumn or after. Pascual supposes the papers are in a box somewhere in Whitehall, with Alpha's sections and forty years of other men's insides, waiting for someone to open them; and that he and Reggie will go on waiting too, in a small way, for the rest of their lives, every time an envelope with an Admiralty frank comes in on the launch.

That is the price. He has decided he can pay it.

The *Corinne* does not go back to the Guiana coast. Ferris writes that estuary off as played out and sends her south, to another river, with other pods in it, who have no boy to warn them; and in the spring she comes back with four, and Jean puts them in the new pens, and Reggie sits up three nights with the hydrophone cataloguing their names. Pascual sits up with him. Neither of them writes anything about it in the notebook. Some things, it turns out, you only have to live with.
