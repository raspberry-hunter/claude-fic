# The Wrong Channel

Oct 2, 2026 · @C

*Reggie's side of "Signal and Noise": scenes from the margins, and the notebook.*

## The dune

*Part One: the night Charles goes*

He watches from the dune because he told Charles he would not come down, and because if anyone asks, he would like to be able to say he didn't.

It is not a clever reason. He knows it isn't. Nobody is going to ask him whether he stood on a dune; they will ask him where the mermaid is, and he will lie, and the dune will make no difference either way. But there is a kind of man who keeps his hands clean in small ways because he cannot in large ones, and Reggie has been that man ever since Deptford, and he does not know, tonight, how to stop being him all at once.

So he stands in the sea-grape at the top of the dune with his hands in his pockets, and watches.

There is a moon, a thin one, mostly behind cloud. He sees the tarp come over the crest of the beach: a dark shape on paler sand, and a man bent double hauling it, falling, getting up again. Over the surf he can hear nothing. He finds that he is straining to anyway, out of a habit so old he hasn't noticed it in years -- listening for the call. There isn't one. Beta makes no sound at all, the whole way down the beach.

Then they are in the surf, and the push and the pull of it, and the tarp is gone, and there are two heads past the breakers.

He looks at his watch by the luminous dial. *21.40.* Two heads. Then, at 22.14, one. Then none.

He waits.

He tells himself that he is waiting to make sure: that a man who has given a junior colleague a chart and a tin and an instruction has some duty to see whether the junior colleague drowns in the first half mile. It is not untrue. At eleven it begins to rain, and he goes on waiting; and at 23.34 by the luminous dial he admits to himself that he is not waiting to make sure of anything. He is waiting because the boy is not coming back, and Reggie knew that he would not, and sent him anyway; and some absurd part of him, unbecoming in a man of his age and education, wants the boy to come back so that he can be told to go again.

He goes down to the beach.

The drag marks run all the way from the path to the waterline: a broad furrow where the tarp went, the sand churned at its edges, heel-marks where the boy dug in and pulled. Near the top there is a dark smear that might be blood, and probably is. The rain is helping, but not enough. He kneels in the wet sand and begins, with his hands, to put the beach back.

It takes a long time. The sand is coarse and full of broken shell, and it gets into scrapes he doesn't notice making. He works from the water up, smoothing, filling, smoothing; and somewhere in the middle of it, with his knees gone numb and the rain running down the back of his collar, he finds that he is thinking about the notebook.

It is in his shirt pocket, in its oilcloth, where it always is. On the last page with writing on it there are two words he has crossed out -- he has kept count -- nineteen times, on nineteen separate nights, harder each time, until the nib went through the paper. They were the first words he ever crossed out in his life. He remembers the first time precisely: a Tuesday in March, the windward end of the verandah, Pobil four feet away complaining about the wind. He wrote *ask him?*, and looked at it, and drew a line through it, and felt something in his chest go cold and then hot, as though he had committed a small crime and got away with it.

He has been getting away with it since March. And tonight Spilsbury is a day's sail from this beach, and the boy is somewhere out in the dark with a mermaid and a chart, and Reggie is on his knees in the sand with his hands bleeding; and he finds that he does not want to get away with anything any more.

At six tomorrow Pobil will walk down to that pen and find it empty. He'll be frightened -- for the boy, and for Beta, and for the Institute -- and he'll raise the alarm, and his face will do whatever it does when he is frightened. Reggie has never seen it. He has seen it angry, and tired, and appalled, and bent over a slide with a kind of fierce tenderness that Reggie has spent several months not writing down; but never frightened. He has a sudden, very clear conviction that he would rather not.

He finishes the last of the furrow. He sits back on his heels in the rain and looks at the beach, which is a beach again, and at his hands, which are not anything he'd care to show a doctor.

Then he gets up and goes to show them to one.

\* \* \*

Hester is on the verandah of the bungalow when he comes up from the beach, in the dark, in a cardigan, as though she has been waiting. Perhaps she has. She looks at his knees, and his hands, and his face. Then she goes inside without a word and comes back with a bottle of the good rum, the one she keeps for Americans, and puts it into his hands.

"He's gone, then," she says.

"Yes."

She nods. "Go on, Reggie," she says, quite gently. "He's still up. His lamp's on."

Reggie doesn't ask whom she means. He has an uncomfortable feeling that he doesn't need to.

The laboratory window is a square of yellow in the wet dark. He stops under the eaves, out of the rain, and looks in. Pobil is at the bench with the lamp pulled close and his sleeves rolled up, writing, frowning at what he has written. Reggie watches him for a while, the way he has watched him for months: the set of the shoulders; the hand that pauses, and goes on. Then he takes out the notebook and, by the light from the window, writes:

*Beta + C.C. into the sea, approx. 2140. Two heads to 2205; one to 2214; none after. Waited to 2334. Rain from 2300. Beach: done.*

He looks at the page. He thinks about writing *ask him* again, without the question mark this time.

He doesn't. He is going to do it instead.

He goes in. Pobil doesn't hear the door.

## Direct examination

*Part One: after midnight*

Pobil has two fingers under his chin and the lamp full in his eyes, and Reggie cannot see him at all: only a dark shape against a white blaze, and a voice out of it saying *they ought to be pinpoints*. *Fear dilates the pupils as well*, Reggie says, because it is true, and because it is the last thing in the room he can stand behind. And when Pobil asks whether he is afraid, he says yes.

Then the hand moves.

He knows what it is the moment the palm settles along his jaw. Jean told him a week ago, by Gamma's pen, with her arms folded, in the flat voice she keeps for things she thinks he ought to have known already: the old stories, the hand to the face and the hand returned. He wrote it down that night. He has reread it since more often than he would care to admit. He knows as surely as he knows anything that between two men like them nothing will happen; Jean was quite definite, and Hester, according to Jean, more definite still.

He is afraid of it anyway. Not of nothing happening. Of what it would mean to want it to.

He has spent his whole life being looked at by one man in particular: a man who saw straight to the bottom of him on the first morning, in a stationer's on the Strand, and found the thing there that could be used, and used it. Reggie has spent every year since Deptford making certain that nobody would ever see that far down again. He has been very good at it. He had the best teacher there is.

And tomorrow that man will step off a launch at ten hundred hours and look at him, and see everything, whether Reggie permits it or not. Reggie knows that as he knows his own name.

*Then let this be seen first,* he thinks, with a sort of terrible clarity. *By someone who isn't looking to use it.*

He lifts his hand, and lays it along Pobil's face.

Nothing happens. He knew that nothing would. What he did not know -- what he would never have predicted, if he had been asked to set it out as a hypothesis -- is that nothing happening would feel like this: like the moment on a long night at the hydrophone when the noise drops away all at once, and the water is simply quiet, and you can hear your own blood.

Pobil is telling him about his pulse. A hundred and twelve, in the spring, with the cleat. Reggie hears it the way he'd hear a call he had been chasing for months come suddenly clear on the tape: *oh.* That's what that was.

Then Pobil kisses him, and for some while Reggie stops hearing anything at all.

\* \* \*

Pobil washes his hands.

Reggie sits on the edge of the narrow bed in the room behind the laboratory and lets him do it, and tries to remember the last time anybody washed his hands for him. An ayah, presumably, in Calcutta, before he was six. He cannot bring back her face. He notices that he cannot -- and this is new -- he lets himself notice it, and sits with it, while Pobil works the sand out of his knuckles one at a time with his thumb, frowning, as though Reggie's hands were the only problem in the world worth his attention tonight.

Then Pobil examines him.

There is no other word for it, and Reggie, who has spent his entire adult life on the other side of the glass, finds he does not want one. Pobil does it the way he does everything to a living thing: thoroughly, and without any hurry to be done. The knees. The hip. The split nail. He finds every place the beach got into Reggie and puts his mouth to each one in turn, as if he were making a note.

*You're examining me.*

*Thoroughly. Hold still.*

And Reggie holds still -- because he finds, to his own astonishment, that he wants to know what Pobil will find.

When Pobil's hand travels down from his hip, slow, palm flat, and slides between his thighs and finds him already wet and waiting, he hears the sound he makes, and is for a fraction of a second purely astonished, as at a new species.

He did not expect this. He is an educated man -- perhaps lacking in practical experience in this area, but one is given to understand a certain amount in the way of theoretical knowledge, of mechanics -- but that he could be touched in such a way, with a feeling like current going all the way through him, and not stopping but intensifying, this he did not know -- and more than that, that he was ready for it; that some part of him had been ready for a long while without troubling to tell the rest. He has the absurd thought that his body reached its conclusion months ago, somewhere around the cleat, and neglected to inform the investigator.

*That was not in the protocol.*

*There is no protocol.*

*There's always a protocol.*

He tries, for a while, to keep thinking through it, to observe. Pobil's fingers pressing into him, slowly, and the way that the touch seems to travel all the way across his body at once. Pobil moving down the bed and his mouth where his hand had been, and the way he all but convulses around the other man's mouth, which he had not known could do that. He finds himself saying, too fast, trying to keep hold of anything at all, "If this is to be repeated, and I think it ought to be, we should really establish some sort of baseline, so that subsequent observations, which I'd propose we --" He can hear the sentence going wrong even as he says it, a subordinate clause with nowhere to go; and Pobil answers it, without lifting his head, by doing something -- finding a place in him -- that ends the sentence, and with it any rational thought whatsoever. He has one hand in Pobil's hair and the other across his own eyes, because there is suddenly far too much to attend to and none of it is anything he can see; and at the very end, when everything in him draws tight as wire and then lets go all at once, there is only one call left in him. It is Pobil's name. He hears it come out of him in a voice he has never used for anything in his life.

Afterwards he lies with his arm over his eyes, small aftershocks still going through him like the last of a call dying out of a tank, and listens to his own heart come down, and feels two fingers settle on the inside of his wrist.

*A hundred and thirty.*

He laughs -- he cannot help that either -- and rolls over, and pins the wrist to the pillow.

*I'll want to repeat the result.*

He watches Pobil's face the whole time. He would not have thought he could do it -- look at someone like that, steadily, while being looked at back, with nothing between them, no glass, no headphones, no notebook. He is clumsy with his torn hands and he doesn't care. He watches every change in Pobil's face as if it were the only signal in the world, and he does not write any of it down, and he finds he will remember all of it anyway. When at last he draws Pobil over him, he keeps his eyes open. He wants to see.

It is slower than he expected. That surprises him too; he finds he has expected almost nothing that has actually happened tonight. Pobil moves into him the way he does everything that matters -- with total attention and no hurry at all, watching Reggie's face for every change, stopping when Reggie's breath catches and waiting until it settles, as if he were easing a needle into a vein and would not for the world have it hurt. It doesn't hurt. It is only a great deal: a slow, deepening fullness, and the weight of him, and the heat of him, and the strangeness of having someone not beside him, not across a bench from him, but *in* him -- as near as one person can come to another without a door.

Then Pobil shifts -- a small adjustment, the angle of his hips, the sort of correction a man makes at a microscope without looking up -- and the current Reggie felt earlier comes back from a new direction, slower and deeper. It finds a channel in him and runs along it, the way a sound does below the layer, carrying further than you would credit; and Reggie hears himself make a noise that he would not, under any other circumstances, have permitted to leave the building.

Reggie has spent his life at a distance. On the other side of the glass; at the end of a cable; forty yards off across a tank. He has made a career of it. He understands now, with Pobil's weight on him and Pobil's breath against his mouth, that he has never once been this close to anyone, and that he did not know -- had not let himself know -- that it was a thing he wanted. He wants it now. He hooks his heels behind Pobil's thighs and pulls him even closer, and Pobil makes a sound against his mouth, low and broken, that Reggie files at once under a heading of its own.

Then they are moving together, slow and then not slow at all, in the narrow creaking bed with the rain hissing on the roof, and he stops filing anything. He watches Pobil's face come apart above him, a little at a time, and then more and more. And then, somewhere in the middle of watching, without any warning he was paying attention to, it happens to him again -- he had not known it could, twice, and so soon; he will want to look into that -- and he goes over with his eyes still open, because he is not, now, going to look away from anything. Pobil makes a rough sound it is not clear he knows he's making, and he puts his hand to Pobil's cheek, and Pobil turns his mouth into the palm; and Reggie feels it more than hears it as Pobil goes over -- his own name, the private one, said into his hand.

Afterwards he lies half over Pobil, the other man's hand moving lightly over his back.

For a while neither of them says anything. The rain has thinned; he can hear it dripping off the eaves, irregular, and under that the generator, and under that Pobil's heart, slowing, which he finds he is counting without meaning to.

"You haven't done that before," Pobil says. His hand has stopped on Reggie's back -- not withdrawn; waiting, the way it waits on a pulse.

He considers lying, out of long habit, and finds that he has no wish to; it seems a waste of the evening. "No. It was --" He looks for the word, and there isn't one, or none he could say aloud, even here, even now. He settles for the nearest thing he has. "Educational."

Pobil snorts into his hair. "I'm glad. That's high praise, from you."

"It is," Reggie agrees, only slightly ironically. Then, because Pobil's hand has begun to move again and it is very difficult to be ironic about anything underneath it: "I'll want to repeat the result."

"Tomorrow," Pobil says. "And the day after." He stops there, and Reggie knows why; they are both thinking about the launch, and what tomorrow is, and of the distinct possibility that they will not, after all, be able to repeat the result. Neither of them says so. After a moment Pobil's hand goes on down his back, slow and steady, as though it had never stopped.

Reggie means to answer, and yawns instead.

"Go to sleep, Reggie," Pobil says. "You have to be frightened at six."

"*You* have to be frightened at six. I have to be convincing." He can feel sleep coming up under him like a tide, and he does not want it yet. There is a great deal more he would like to say, and he has wasted six months not saying any of it. Instead he says, "Pascual. Tomorrow. When he looks at you -- and he will; he'll look at you the way he looks at everything, as though he's already opened it -- you mustn't --"

"Mustn't what?"

"You mustn't let him see that you --"

He knows exactly how the sentence ends. *That you'd lie for me.* He has it; it is right there; it is very important, and he only has to say it. And the bed is warm, and the rain has nearly stopped, and Pobil's hand is moving on his back, slow and steady as a pulse, and --

He falls asleep in the middle of his sentence, with his face in Pobil's neck. The last thing he thinks, as far as he can afterwards remember, is about ten hundred hours tomorrow, and the launch, and the man on it, and what that man will see.

*Let him look,* he thinks. *I've been seen already.*

## Peer review

*Part One: before six*

At half past five he dresses in the grey light, takes the sandy shirt from the floor, and walks back across the neck of the cay to his own hut, so that at six he can arrive at the pens from the right direction.

The notebook is in the shirt pocket. He knows what is in it; he watched Pobil put it there, through his fingers, with his arm across his eyes, pretending to be asleep. He sits on the edge of his own bed -- unslept in, and very tidy -- and opens it at the ribbon.

*Discriminating test performed. Direct examination. Result: A. (Also B.) Further observation recommended.*

*P.*

Nobody has ever written in one of his notebooks before. Not in seven years. Not even Spilsbury, who had his own.

He reads it four times. Then, because he has been told to write everything down, he uncaps his pen and writes underneath:

*Concur.*

*"Tar": in private, not angry. New usage. (n=1.)*

Then he shaves.

He does it carefully, by the small square of mirror over the washstand, with the light coming up pink behind the casuarinas, and halfway down his jaw he realises that he is listening. Waiting. There is a sentence that has come to him in shaving mirrors, on and off, for six years, in a voice he eventually identified as his own. It came less often after Plymouth, and less again after he came out here; but it has never quite stopped. He waits for it with the razor lifted.

It doesn't come.

He finishes shaving. He does not cut himself. He writes that down too.

*0540, shaving. No sentence.*

\* \* \*

He arrives at the pens at six minutes past six, shaved and combed and with his collar buttoned, from the direction of his own hut, as planned. He has rehearsed what his face will do. He has rehearsed what he will say.

Pobil is standing at the edge of the empty pen with the boy's spectacles in his hand, turning them over, and his face is doing something Reggie has never seen it do.

So that is what it looks like. He was right not to want to see it.

"How long?" he says, and it comes out exactly as rehearsed, and he hates it.

## Ten

*Part Two: the laboratory*

He brings the turtle ledger and nothing else, as he was asked, and Spilsbury rises from Pobil's chair to meet him and says, "I read your paper on the identification calls."

Reggie has been braced since the jetty for a great many things. He has not been braced for that.

"Very elegant, Reginald. The spectrograms especially. I read it twice."

It goes in. He feels it go in -- there is no other way to describe it -- like warmth into cold hands, all the way up into his face before he can do anything about it. For one second he is twenty-six, at a trestle table on the bonded floor at seven in the morning, with the headphones round his neck and the man he would have walked into the river for saying *very good ears*. He has had four years. He has had an ocean. It takes one second.

Then he gets his face in front of it. He says, "Thank you," in a voice with nothing in it at all. And he sees, across the bench, that Pobil saw the second.

Spilsbury reaches for his hand.

He knows what is going to happen before it happens. He has known since he stepped onto the jetty this morning with four hours of rope burns laid over last night's sand like a coat of varnish, and known it was not enough. Spilsbury's hands are dry and cool. They are the same hands. His body remembers them before his mind does -- a cold February, a bedroom in Greenwich, a palm on his forehead -- and for a moment something in him leans towards them, the way a plant leans towards a window. He hates that more than anything that follows.

The thumbnail moves along the heel of his hand. *These are not.* The sand. *Twelve hours, I'd say.*

And then Spilsbury turns, pleasantly, to Pobil.

*Say twelve,* Reggie thinks. He has never wished anything so hard in his life. *Agree with him. Give him nothing. Say twelve, say you didn't look, say anything, say --*

"Ten," says Pobil.

It is the worst thing he could possibly have said. It is too fast and too loud. It is a number plucked out of the air by a man who has never in his life had to lie about a wound and has no idea how it is done. It ties Pobil to him, in front of the one man in the world who will know exactly what that means and keep it. Reggie watches Spilsbury hear it, and turn it over, and file it, and take out the oilcloth notebook.

And under the horror, rising through it like water through sand, there is something else.

He goes back through it quickly, the way he would check a reel. Masters, examiners, tutors. His father. Spilsbury -- who lied *to* him, beautifully, often, for three years, and never once for him. Nobody has ever lied for Reggie Tar. Not badly, not at once, not without first working out whether it was wise. Not like that.

He doesn't know what to do with his face.

He hasn't had that particular problem since 1946.

## At your leisure

*Part Two: the jetty, after supper*

Spilsbury finds him on the verandah steps after supper and says, "Walk with me, Reginald," and Reggie walks with him, because his feet have done it before his mind is consulted, the way they always did.

The launch lies alongside the jetty with her riding light lit. Spilsbury walks the way he has always walked, as though the jetty were his and he were showing it to you, and stops at the far end where the lamp from the shore doesn't reach, and looks at the water.

"You've done well here," he says. "Better than I expected. I expected barnacles."

"There are barnacles."

"There always are." Spilsbury smiles at the sea. "Your doctor's notes are admirable. And the food logs -- those were the boy's, I think? Very careful. Very thorough." A pause, perfectly judged. "Where is he, Reginald?"

"Drowned, I should think."

"Do you." Spilsbury doesn't look round. "You were always a very poor liar. It was one of the things I liked best about you. Your doctor is worse. I liked him for it, too."

Reggie says nothing. There is nothing to say that would not make it worse, and they both know it, and Spilsbury lets the silence go on exactly long enough for Reggie to feel it, and then lets it go.

"I've missed you," he says.

And there it is. Reggie had known it would come; he could have written the sentence himself on the walk down. Knowing does nothing. It arrives like weather. He stands at the end of the jetty and feels it come in -- the old warmth, rising, the particular shape of it, a sound he has heard ten thousand times -- and he catalogues it, scrupulously, as it comes: *onset; rise; peak.* Cataloguing it does not stop it either.

"There's been nobody since," Spilsbury says, "who listened half so well."

He lays it out after that, gently, as one lays out instruments. A berth on the launch on Thursday. The separation logs. A better grade than the one Reggie left at; he has spoken to people. The labs are not what they were; there is a great deal more money, and a new building, and a young man in Reggie's old chair who can hear perfectly well but cannot *listen*, Reginald, there's the difference. And the boy. Reggie will tell him about the boy, at his leisure.

"And if I don't?"

Spilsbury sighs. He tells him that too, just as gently. The report. The wound, dated by a man with thirty years in the witness box. The doctor's lie, in front of that man. The Americans, who are particular about property; the Colonel, who would not survive it; Mrs Bevan's institute, which would not survive the Colonel. Gamma, and the new one, in tanks, on a ship, to London.

"I'd so much rather you came," Spilsbury says. "I'd so much rather not write any of it." He puts a hand on Reggie's shoulder, lightly, the way he did on the bonded floor in 1946, the way he did at his desk in 1949. "You always do the sensible thing in the end, Reginald. That was the second thing I liked best."

The hand stays there for a moment. Then it goes, and so does he, back along the jetty towards the lights, unhurried, not looking round.

\* \* \*

Reggie stays where he is for some time.

He finds that he has already done the arithmetic. He did it while Spilsbury was talking, without meaning to, the way one finishes a sum one doesn't like before one has decided to start it. One man on a launch, on one side. On the other: the Institute; Jean; Hester and the Colonel; Gamma; the new animal. And Pobil, who said *Ten.* It is not difficult arithmetic. He has always been good at arithmetic.

He'll go.

And there is a part of him -- he looks at it, briefly, the way one looks at a lesion -- that is glad. That would like nothing better than to sit at a trestle table under a good lamp, with the headphones on and the whole night ahead of him, and be told *very good, Reginald* at seven in the morning. It is not a large part. It is not, he thinks, the largest. But it is there, and it is his, and it has been there since a stationer's on the Strand, and he does not know how to cut it out.

He does what he has always done with it. He files it, and does not look again.

Then he walks back to his hut and packs, because packing is a thing to do with the hands. His hands are bandaged, and he packs badly, and it takes him an hour. When it's done he sits on the edge of the bed, which he has made up very tidily out of habit, and looks at the suitcase.

At eleven he gets up and goes to the laboratory. He tells himself that it is to say goodbye properly, and to warn Pobil about the report, and to settle what is to be said to Ferris. All of that is true.

It is also true -- though he will not admit it to himself until the stool, and the knees, and the word *differential* -- that he has gone to be told he's wrong.

## The interval

*Part Two: Gamma and Delta*

In the headphones, Delta is saying her own name.

Over and over, from the far wall of the pen where she has flattened herself: the same short phrase, the same rise at the end, every few seconds. Reggie has heard a great many of them do this in their first hour in a tank. He has never once heard anything come back.

Then, under it, faint, rough with disuse, a second voice. Gamma's signature. He has not heard Gamma make it in three days.

"There," he says, or thinks he says. He is not sure afterwards whether it was out loud.

Then Gamma goes for her, and the water goes white.

The headphones fill with noise -- too much of it, too loud, too many sources at once; he cannot sort it, he cannot tell what any of it is. And from the shade of Jean's tarp, quite mildly, Spilsbury says, "Nets. Now, please, Commander. I've seen this. We lost four that way in 'forty-nine."

He says it to Ferris. He says it, Reggie understands perfectly, to Reggie.

And Reggie is not on the cay any more. He is on the bonded floor at Deptford in February, at the edge of the old settling tank, with the headphones in his useless hand and four of them in the water in front of him and the water going white, then pink, then red. He can hear it. He can hear it over everything: one call, four throats, *danger, danger, danger*, coming back off the concrete. He can't see Gamma's pen at all. He can't see anything but the cistern.

He looks at Pobil because he cannot look at anything else.

Pobil is already down on one knee at the edge, in the slop, in his good trousers, looking -- not at the whole of it, Reggie can see that even now; at the parts. At mouths. At hands. At the colour of the water. And then he says, hoarse and certain, *No teeth*, and Reggie puts the headphones back over his ears and makes himself listen.

It is not *danger*. That is the first thing. There is no type D in it at all.

It is names. Gamma's, and then Delta's. Delta's, and then Gamma's. A call, and then an interval -- a second and a half, two seconds -- and then the other voice, filling it. And again. And again.

He has spent six years listening to that interval. Thousands of hours of it, on hundreds of reels, in tanks and pens on two sides of an ocean: a call, and then the space where an answer would go, and then nothing. He has measured it. He has written papers about the shape of the call that goes into it. He has never once, in all that time, heard it filled.

He hears it filled now. He stands at the edge of a concrete pen in the sun with his hands pressed over the headphones and listens to two of them answer each other, over and over, as if they had always been going to, as if it were the simplest thing in the world; and he finds that his voice, when he tries to tell the others what he is hearing, is not quite steady, and that he does not much care who notices.

\* \* \*

Afterwards, Ferris asks him how many he could keep like this, and says *a dozen by spring*, and Reggie hears himself say, "Yes. Thank you, Commander. We'll be ready."

He does the sum before Ferris has finished turning away. He does it the way he did it on the jetty, without meaning to. One, past the reef, with a boy on his back. Twelve, in the pens, by spring. He wonders whether Charles did this sum on the beach, and knows perfectly well that he did.

## From the notebook

*P. -- Recorded him on his own machine and played it back to him. He left the room.*

## Forty yards

*Part Three: the river*

On the second afternoon out, Spilsbury sends for him to the chart table under the wheelhouse awning, where the Admiralty chart of the coast is spread out and weighted at the corners with a sextant box and an ashtray, and says, "Tell me about the river, Reginald."

He has decided, on the way up from the cabin, to say very little.

"Fresh over salt," says Spilsbury, tapping the river mouth with one long finger -- the left hand, the ruined one; Reggie looks away from it. "The tide comes in underneath and the river goes out on top. Two layers. What does a call do?"

"Bends," Reggie says. And then, because it is a good question, and nobody has asked him a good question about sound in two years: "Towards the slower water. A call made up in the fresh layer will bend back off the boundary -- you'd lose it near the surface; it'd drop into a shadow and come up again a mile off. Below the boundary it's the other way about. It'll channel. Run along under the fresh water as if it were in a pipe."

"How far?"

"In a river that size? With the boundary as sharp as that?" His hand is already reaching for the pencil. "Miles. All the way up the reaches, if the layer holds. Further than you'd credit."

"So a call put in below the layer, in the right place --"

"Would carry to every reach of the river at once." He's drawing it: the wedge of salt under the fresh, the curve of the boundary, the path a sound would take, bending, trapped, running on. "You'd want the projector here. At the second bend, where it deepens. Below the boundary, angled upstream ten degrees or so. Anything in that river would hear it. Anything at all."

"Yes," says Spilsbury softly. "Yes. Exactly so, Reginald."

And Reggie, with the pencil still moving, hears what he has just done. He has told Spilsbury precisely where to put a lure so that Beta cannot fail to hear it. And he has also, in the same breath, worked out precisely where to put a warning so that every one of them in that river cannot fail to hear *it* -- because it is the same place, and the same angle, and the same physics -- and he has said it aloud, at a chart table, to the one man alive who might be able to tell the difference.

He looks up. Spilsbury is looking at the chart, not at him, with an expression of mild and absorbed pleasure.

"You always did see it before anyone else," Spilsbury says. "Do you remember the cistern? How long the sound hung in it, afterwards?"

Reggie remembers. He has the figure in a notebook in a trunk under his bed: the reverberation time of the old settling tank at Deptford, which he measured one night in the spring of 1948 with a starting pistol, because it seemed a good opportunity. He read the boy's pages three nights ago. *It comes back off the walls and goes through them again.*

"Two point three seconds," he says.

"Two point three," Spilsbury agrees, as a man might agree about the weather. "A remarkable thing, a cistern." And then, before Reggie can find out whether that means what he thinks it means, he has turned the chart a little and is asking about the tides; and the moment is gone. Reggie's pencil is moving again across the margin, because the tide is interesting, because the boundary will shift with it, because there is nobody else he can talk to about any of this without first having to explain what he means by a layer -- and he has missed it, God help him, he has missed this -- and for twenty minutes he forgets everything else. It is like being twenty-six. It is like being warm.

Then he looks up and sees Pobil at the top of the companionway, and stops.

He doesn't decide to stop. He simply finds that he has.

\* \* \*

The third evening out, Reggie is sitting on the deck working through the tide tables for the river mouth with a pencil, working out how far the boundary will shift on the flood, when Pobil comes and sits down beside him on the hatch cover. For a while he just sits there while Reggie works. After a few minutes, he says, "Reggie."

Reggie raises his head, smiling, but the smile drops as he looks at Pobil's face. He doesn't know what is making Pobil look like that. "What is it?"

"I ran into Spilsbury. At the rail."

"Oh," Reggie says. Another threat, he thinks, and he realises he is tired of being afraid. He waits.

He does not expect what Pobil says next. "Reggie. He's dying. He has a few months. He said you didn't know."

Reggie didn't. He stares at Pobil, and Pobil looks back at him with the face he keeps for relatives.

"The liver," Pobil says. "Since February. He's known since the jetty that I could see it. I'm sorry, Reggie, I thought you knew. I didn't say anything because I thought you didn't want to talk about it. If I'd realised -- He says you never look at the body."

It's true. Reggie has been looking at Spilsbury's face for nine years -- watching it, reading it, waiting on it -- and has never once looked at the rest of him. He thinks of the suit hanging loose, and the hand held to the side, and the slow way down the gangway at the Martin, and the colour of the eyes, which he had put down to the light. Pobil watches him thinking.

"Are you all right?"

Reggie considers the question as honestly as he can, the way he would any other. He feels nothing, in point of fact. He supposes that is numbness, and that he will feel a great deal more later. "Yes," he says, looking out at the water. "No. I don't know."

Pobil nods, as though that were a perfectly reasonable answer, which from Pobil it is. They sit there a while longer. Away to the south, where the coast must be, the last of the light goes off the water.

Pobil says, "He said something else. He said -- the door.  He said, you and I manage without it. He said he wondered how."

Reggie does look at him then, and sees about five different things going across Pobil's face, in the dimming twilight. "What did you tell him?" It comes out a little too level. Pobil hears it. That goes across his face too.

"I said, badly. Out loud. In two languages. I told him we get it wrong most of the time."

Reggie snorts. "That's certainly the case. We can't even distinguish between two hypotheses."

Pobil laughs then, cleanly. "It's all Hypothesis B."&#32;

"And Hypothesis A."

"Yes." Pobil smiles. After a second it fades, and he takes a breath. "Spilsbury said that it was inefficient. And then he said, *Still*."

Reggie blinks. "He said that?"

"Yes."

"To you."

"Yes." Pobil doesn't say what both of them surely know, that Spilsbury would never say such a thing to Reggie. That the whole conversation is one he wouldn't have with Reggie.

It is the strangest thing anyone has ever told him about Spilsbury. In nine years Reggie has never once known him want anything he could not simply take: a specimen, a section, a young man's attention at seven in the morning. He has never known him ask a question he did not already know the answer to. And he has asked Pobil one. About the two of them. And then said *Still*, as though -- Reggie turns the thought over and finds there is no way to hold it that doesn't cut -- as though, of Spilsbury at his desk and Reggie at his trestle table, Reggie were the one who had turned out to have something worth wanting.

Pobil looks at Reggie's hand. He won't take it, not out in the open like this, not on a boat with Spilsbury and Moss and the US Navy. But Reggie knows he wants to, and that's enough, right now.

Pobil says, "The plan. We're still --"

"Yes, Pascual," Reggie says, fast, so that he won't say any more. Why he is bringing it up, in the open, where someone might hear, Reggie doesn't know. "Why?"

"I just thought," Pobil says. "You didn't know. You do now. I didn't know if that would change anything."

Now he knows. "It doesn't," Reggie says. "Everything that was true then is still true now." He considers that statement, to see whether he believes it. He does.

It doesn't change anything. But it is heavier. Tomorrow, in a river, with a dead mermaid's voice, he is going to take the last thing a dying man wants out from under him, and he knows it. He runs the sum again, the way he ran it on the jetty two years ago: one man under an awning, on one side; on the other, a boy in the mangroves and everything that lives in that river. It comes out the same. It is still not difficult arithmetic. It is only harder to do.

"All right," Pobil says. "Just -- tell me? If any of it is too much."

Reggie doesn't say anything.&#32;

"*Reggie.*"

"Yes," he says.

Then, after a while -- because he has just promised, and because it turns out to be true -- he says, quite quietly, "It is. A little. Now."

Pobil doesn't say anything. He shifts on the hatch cover until his knee rests against Reggie's, low down, where the dark and the coaming hide it from the wheelhouse, and leaves it there. It is the only thing either of them can do out here, and Pobil does it.

They sit like that until there is no light left on the water at all, and then a while longer.

\* \* \*

He threads the tape in the bow of the launch, forty miles up a brown river, with Spilsbury under the awning behind him and Pobil amidships with his pockets full of limes; and he plays Beta his own name.

He made that recording himself. Through the wall of a concrete pen, at three in the morning, two years ago, with the boy asleep on a pad beside it and one hand trailing in the water. He knows every grain of it. He has played it perhaps a hundred times since, alone, at the bench, at two in the morning, for no reason he has ever written down.

Nothing. He plays it again. Nothing.

Then, in the headphones, a long way off: a hum, and a click.

He has never heard Beta's voice in open water. It is not the same voice. It is larger; it has room in it; it comes round the bend of the river with the whole estuary behind it, and he does not know what his face is doing, and he knows that Spilsbury is watching it, and he cannot stop it and does not try.

*He's answered.*

He plays the boy's name. The answer comes back in a living voice, near enough now to hear without headphones. And then two heads, forty yards off the bow: Beta, cocky and suspicious, exactly as he was; and the boy, burnt dark, bearded, squinting into the glare without his spectacles.

Then Pobil stands up.

Reggie knew he would. They planned it at four in the morning, on two stools, with the notebook open between them. Knowing does nothing whatever. Pobil takes his shoes off methodically, the left and then the right, and sets them side by side on the thwart like a man going to bed; and goes over the side; and Reggie makes a sound he has not made since he was a child, and has to put his hand over his mouth.

Pobil swims like a man who learned in a river somewhere in Spain and has not done it since: badly, with his head up, swallowing water. Reggie counts his strokes. He can't help it. Eleven. Twelve. Pobil goes under, and Reggie's hand is on the gunwale and he is on his feet without knowing it, and Moss says, "Sir --" and Pobil comes up. Thirteen. Fourteen. Fifteen. He's there.

The glasses. And then Pobil puts out his hand and lays it along the boy's face.

Reggie watches it from forty yards.

He has always been the one on the other side of the glass. The one with the headphones, writing it down. And here he is again, in the bow of a launch, at a distance, watching someone be seen and unable to hear a word of it -- and he understands, with a jolt that goes all the way through him, that this is exactly where Spilsbury is sitting. Behind him. Under the awning. Outside, watching a door open from forty yards off.

The difference is that he knows what the boy is seeing.

He knows exactly. It is all in the notebook, in two hands. Chairs, and wind, and coffee, and snoring with a name; a bed made and unmade and made again; a word not yet said in either of two languages. Every bit of it is in Pobil, and so every bit of it is going into the boy now, through a palm on a wet cheek -- and Reggie is in it. All of him. All the parts he has spent every year since Deptford making certain nobody would ever see: being seen, secondhand, by a boy he sent into the sea.

He waits to be afraid. He has been afraid of precisely this for as long as he can remember.

He isn't. He stands in the bow with one hand on the projector switch and the river light in his eyes, and he finds that he does not mind. *Let him look.* There is nothing in there now that Reggie would mind him seeing.

Nearly nothing.

The boy turns his face out of Pobil's hand. He talks; Reggie can't hear what. He puts the spectacles on, crooked. And then he looks across the forty yards of brown water at the bow of the launch, and Reggie watches his eyes find him, and focus, and stay.

It is the look of someone who already knows him -- all of him -- and has found nothing in it to use.

The boy nods.

Reggie threads the second tape. It is exactly as heavy as he knew it would be, last night on the hatch cover, and no heavier. He has already done the sum.

He has carried it in a War Department case through four sets of lodgings and two oceans. He has listened to it perhaps twice. He has never once played it into water. It is her voice -- Eleven's, from the store-cupboard at Deptford, with the gain turned down and then down again -- and as it goes out of the drum into the river, for the first time in eight years someone hears it who can understand what it says.

The river answers.

Up and down the mangroves, near and far: a call, and another, and a dozen, and more than he can count, passing it on. *Danger. Danger.* He doesn't need the headphones. He stands in the bow with his hand on the switch and listens to a whole estuary answer a dead mermaid, and he thinks: *she called for five days, and nobody heard her. Now everyone has.*

Behind him, under the awning, he hears Spilsbury draw one breath -- short, sharp, almost a laugh -- and say nothing at all.

Reggie doesn't turn round. He knows, now, what he would see if he did, and he is not ready to look at it.

\* \* \*

Afterwards, with Pobil hauled out by the collar and the belt and sitting on the bottom boards streaming river water, Reggie finds that he has one hand clamped on the back of Pobil's neck and two fingers pressed to the side of it, just under the jaw.

He is counting.

He doesn't stop. A hundred and twenty. A hundred and ten. A hundred.

Recovering.

## Very neatly done

*Part Three: the Corinne, and the cay*

That evening, at anchor off the river mouth, Spilsbury sends for Pobil.

Not for Reggie. For Pobil.

Reggie stands at the rail in the dusk with the river smell coming out over the water and watches the cabin door close, and feels it -- he is too tired to pretend otherwise -- exactly where he has always felt it: under the breastbone, the small cold place. Not chosen. He is thirty-five years old. He has just played a dead mermaid's voice into a river under the nose of the man who taught him to record it, and he is standing on a deck in the dark feeling passed over, like a boy left out of a side.

He makes himself look at it. That's new, too.

He hasn't been sent for, he decides, because he has nothing Spilsbury wants. Spilsbury knows everything Reggie knows; Spilsbury taught him most of it. Whatever Spilsbury wants tonight is something Reggie hasn't got, and Pobil has. Pobil had his hand on the boy's face this afternoon, and was looked at all the way down, and came up out of the river with it still on him like water. And Spilsbury, who has spent his whole life getting inside things, has sent for the one man on this boat who has been somewhere he cannot go.

Or -- he half-sees it, and then cannot stop seeing it -- the other way about. *Still*, Spilsbury said. Perhaps Reggie has the one thing on this boat that Spilsbury wants, and that is precisely why he cannot be sent for. One does not ask a man to sit across a cabin from one, the night before one dies or near enough, and be the thing one has wanted all one's life and could not have.

The cold place goes quiet. What is in it instead, Reggie doesn't have a word for. It is not unlike pity. He doesn't like it any better.

\* \* \*

Pobil comes up an hour later and stands beside him at the rail for a while without speaking. Then he says, "He gave me a message for you. I've been deciding, all the way up the companionway, whether I have any right to keep it from you."

"And?"

"I don't think I have."

"Then tell me."

"He said to tell you it was very neatly done," Pobil says. "The second tape. He said you'd pretend not to care."

It goes in.

Of course it goes in; he has stopped expecting otherwise. *Very neatly done.* The last mark he will ever be given by the only examiner whose marks he wanted -- for the one piece of work Spilsbury would least have wished him to do. He stands at the rail and feels it arrive, the old warmth, all the way up into his face, and this time he doesn't get in front of it. He lets it come. It seems the least he can do, for a dying man, to let him be right about this one thing.

"I wish you hadn't told me," he says.

"Should I not have?"

"No. I'd have wanted to know. That's the trouble. That's always been the trouble." He is quiet a moment. "He's right, you know. It was very neatly done."

Then he takes his hands off the rail. It takes more effort than it should; it is like letting go of something he has been holding since he was twenty-six. He turns his back on the river, and goes below, and Pobil follows him, and neither of them says anything else that night.

\* \* \*

There is a page near the front of the notebook that has been blank for two years.

It has a heading -- *P., before* -- in Reggie's small upright hand, and under the heading nothing at all. Every other page about Pobil is full: pulses, chairs, coffee, the wind, a snore with a name. Reggie knows the year he was born, and the town, because both are on his papers; and he knows that Pobil speaks of horses "at home" and has never once said where home is now. He has never asked. He grew up among men who had come back from one war and did not speak of it, and he has a trunk of his own under his bed. He does not ask other men about theirs.

The day after the river, in the afternoon, with Spilsbury shut in his cabin and the *Corinne* rolling north under a white sky, Pobil comes and leans on the stern rail beside him and says, without preamble, "He saw it, you know."

"Who saw what?"

"The boy. When I had my hand on his face." Pobil is looking at the wake, not at him. "He saw all of it. Further back than you've ever been." He is quiet a moment. "I lay awake last night thinking that there is a boy in a river in Guiana who knows things about me that you don't. That didn't seem right."

Reggie says nothing. He has learned, lately, when that is the most useful thing to say.

"Huelva," Pobil says. "You know that much; it's on my papers. The town sits at the mouth of two rivers, and one of them runs red, because an English company has been digging copper out of the hills above it for eighty years, and nobody in Huelva is ever allowed to forget who owns the river." His mouth twists. "My father doctored horses on the marisma. The marsh. Miles of it, flat as a table, with the tide coming in under the river water twice a day, brown and warm. You don't learn to swim in the marisma. You learn to walk in water. That's why I swim like a dog."

*Fresh over salt*, Reggie thinks, and doesn't say it.

"I was a vet, like him. Then there was the war, and I was a vet for the army, which mostly meant mules. And then for a while I was at a hospital, because they were short of anyone who could hold a knife." He stops. When he goes on, some of it is in Spanish, low and quick, and Reggie doesn't ask him to translate. He isn't sure it's meant for him to understand; only to hear. Then, in English again: "There was a surgeon there. Very able. Very curious. He had prisoners, and nobody to tell him not to, and he wanted to know things." Pobil's hands are quite still on the rail. "I knew Spilsbury the moment I saw him on the jetty. Not him. The kind."

He says nothing else about the hospital. Reggie doesn't ask.

"I left in 'thirty-nine," Pobil says. "Over the mountains in February, with half the country, in the snow. France didn't want us; they put us on a beach behind wire. Then a boat to Santo Domingo, which took us for reasons of its own. Then a colleague of a colleague, and a post in Trinidad. And then the Colonel's wife, who wanted someone who could keep animals alive, and didn't mind about papers." He shrugs. "And here I am. Complaining about the wind."

"Four hundred and thirty-one times."

"I know. I saw it in the notebook." Pobil looks at him at last. "That's all. That's the little. There's more, but it isn't any better."

Reggie looks at him for a long moment: the man who came over the Pyrenees in the snow, and onto a French beach behind wire, and across an ocean, and who went into a brown river yesterday without hesitating, swimming like a dog.

"Thank you," he says. It is not enough, and it is what he has.

That night in the cramped cabin, with Pobil asleep in the upper bunk, Reggie turns to the page near the front and considers it for a long time. Then he writes one line under the heading, the shortest entry in the book.

*Huelva. Two rivers; one red. The marsh. 1939.*

He will not add to it unless he is told more. He finds that he can wait.

\* \* \*

The *Corinne* comes in to the cay two days later. Spilsbury does not come ashore. She is going on to Port of Spain, where there is an aeroplane.

He is on deck when Reggie and Pobil go down the ladder to the Institute's launch, in a canvas chair in the shade of the awning, with a rug over his knees. Reggie stops at the head of the ladder.

He has known for four days. He has not once let himself look. Now he looks at the body.

The suit, too large by a size. The hands on the rug, the colour of old ivory. The yellow in the whites of the eyes, which is not the light, and never was. The way he sits, a little to the left, as though the right side of him were somewhere he would rather not lean. The way he is not standing up.

Spilsbury watches him look. Reggie sees him see it -- sees him understand that Reggie is, at last, looking -- and something comes into his face that Reggie has seen there exactly once before: on the bonded floor at Deptford, at seven in the morning in the spring of 1946, when Spilsbury took a pair of headphones off very slowly and said "Names," and looked at him as though he were something worth looking at. Not the look that uses. The one that came before it.

"Yes," Spilsbury says softly. "That's it, Reginald. That's how it's done."

In 1949 he walked out of an office and down a flight of stairs without saying goodbye, because he did not trust himself to say it and keep walking. He finds that he trusts himself now.

"Goodbye, sir," he says.

Spilsbury lifts two fingers to the brim of his hat.

Reggie goes down the ladder. Pobil is already in the launch, holding it steady against the *Corinne*'s side, looking up at him. At the last rung Pobil's hand comes up to steady him, the way any man's would, and stays a second longer than any man's would. He doesn't look back until they are halfway to the jetty; and when he does, Spilsbury has closed his eyes, with his face turned up a little towards the sun, as though he were listening to something a long way off.

## The result

*Part Three: home*

Pobil says it in Spanish first.

Reggie has known what it means for two years. He looked up "querido" in the Colonel's dictionary the week after Pobil first said it in his sleep -- the only way Pobil has ever said it -- and then, being thorough, the verb it comes from, and everything that verb can be made to say; and he has read the entry since more often than he has read his own paper. He has known what it means. He has not known what it would sound like said to him, awake, with a hand on his face, in a narrow room with the generator going.

He goes completely still. It seems the only safe thing to do.

Then Pobil says it in English, out loud, and gets it wrong, and stops, and starts again, and gets it right. And Reggie, who has spent his whole life listening, finds that he has never in his life heard anything so clearly.

He answers. Out loud, in English, in his own voice. Then -- because it seems only fair, and because he has been practising in his head for two years and it would be a waste -- in Spanish, which comes out exactly as Anglican as Pobil has always said it does, and Pobil laughs so hard he has to put his forehead down on Reggie's shoulder.

Nothing opens. He knew it wouldn't.

He falls asleep before he means to.

\* \* \*

He wakes after six, which is late for him. The light is coming through the louvres in bars. Pobil is asleep now, at last, on his back with one arm flung out, snoring at approximately fourteen to the minute with a slight rise at the end of each.

The notebook is on the shelf above the bed, where Reggie left it last night with the pen lying across it.

He looks at it from the pillow for a long time.

He knows what he would write. He has written it in one form or another every day for two years: the time; the pulse; the result. He could do it now without sitting up. *0600. Said aloud, both languages. Pulse --* he would have to guess; Pobil took it, not he. *Result:*

He doesn't reach for it.

It isn't that it isn't data. It's the best data he has ever had. It's that for nine years he has written everything down because a man in a stationer's on the Strand told him that memory is a liar, and so is he; and he believed it; and it was true. But he finds, lying here in the barred light with Pobil snoring beside him, that he is not going to forget this. Not one second of it. He doesn't need a record. He has the result.

He lies still and lets the page stay blank.

\* \* \*

That evening, at the windward end of the verandah, he opens the notebook for the first time since.

Not at the blank page. At the front.

The first page of every notebook he has owned since Plymouth has the same two sentences on it, in his own small upright hand. He writes them without thinking, the way other men cross themselves. They are here too.

*Write everything down. Memory is a liar, and so are you.*

He looks at them for a while. Then he uncaps his pen, and -- with a single neat line, the way one corrects a misprint, not at all the way he once crossed out two words on nineteen separate nights -- he strikes through *and so are you*.

He leaves the rest. It's good advice. It was always good advice.

Beside him Pobil, settling into his chair with a groan, says that it is windy at this end.

"So I'm told," says Reggie, and turns to the back of the notebook, and makes a small mark in the running total.

## Too much

*October*

Reggie goes to work the morning after the letter, because that is what one does.

He calibrates the reef hydrophone again, though he calibrated it yesterday. He catalogues two new calls from Lambda, who has begun using a variant of her own signature that he has never heard before and suspects means something, and writes them down. He eats lunch. He answers a requisition from Ferris about cable. By any measurement he could think to take he is perfectly all right, and he would be prepared to show his working.

Pobil watches him all day: from the pens, from the bench, from across the dining room. He doesn't say anything. That is how Reggie knows.

He does not open the trunk. He does not look at the table in the notebook. Now and then he thinks about a box in Whitehall with his name in it, *entire*, and finds that it frightens him exactly as much as it did yesterday -- which is to say a great deal, and which is to say nothing he can do anything about -- and goes on calibrating.

It is the oranges, in the end.

Jean has brought a basket up from the village, and someone has heaped them in the blue bowl on the dining-room sideboard, as they do most weeks at this time of year: twenty or thirty of them, small and green-skinned and lopsided, the way they grow here, so cheap that nobody bothers to count them. Reggie comes in for supper and sees them, and stops.

*There weren't any oranges in London that winter. I've no idea where he found them.*

He stands quite still in the doorway with his hand on the frame. He is thinking about a paper bag on a blanket, and four oranges bright as lamps, and a man in a good grey hat with snow on the brim saying *Don't ask*. And he is thinking -- this he has not let himself think, not yesterday, not at two in the morning among the notebooks -- about a bedroom in London last month that he has never seen, and a man in it who may by the end no longer have known the faces at his bedside, if there were any faces. Whether anyone sat on the end of the bed. Whether anyone put a hand on his forehead.

He finds he hopes so. He finds that he hopes so very much, and that the hoping is somehow worse than all the rest of it put together.

He turns round and walks out of the dining room.

He is standing in the middle of their room behind the laboratory, not doing anything at all, when Pobil comes in and shuts the door behind him.

"Reggie."

He made a promise, on a hatch cover, in the dark. He has kept every promise he has ever made, mostly by making very few.

"It's too much," he says.

It comes out quite steadily. That seems unfair.

"I know," says Pobil, and crosses the room, and puts his arms round him.

Reggie lets him. He doesn't weep; he did that last night, at two in the morning, among the notebooks, and once seems to have been all he had in him. He only stands there with his forehead on Pobil's shoulder and Pobil's arms round him, while the light goes outside and someone in the dining room laughs and the generator drones on, and the oranges sit in their blue bowl, uncounted.

Pobil's heart is going under his ear. Seventy-something; he could have it to the beat in fifteen seconds. He has counted it a hundred times. He counts everything.

"I'm here," Pobil says.

He doesn't count it. For once in his life, he only listens.
