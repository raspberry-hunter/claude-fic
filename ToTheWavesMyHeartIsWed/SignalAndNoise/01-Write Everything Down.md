# Write Everything Down

Oct 2, 2026 · @C

*A prequel to "Signal and Noise": Reggie at the Admiralty's station at Deptford, 1946–1951.*

## 1946

The stationer's is on the Strand, between a tobacconist's and a shop that sells surgical trusses, and Spilsbury takes him there on his first morning, before they have so much as looked at a tank.

"You'll want one of these," he says, and puts it into Reggie's hands: small, black, oilcloth-covered, the pages ruled in faint blue. He has paid before Reggie can find his wallet. "Write everything down, Reginald. Everything. The time. The temperature of the water. What you had for breakfast. What you think you heard. Memory is a liar." He smiles. He has a kind, tired face, and a red carnation in his buttonhole, and the eyes of a man who has looked into a great many people who were no longer using them. "And so are you. So am I. The notebook isn't."

Reggie is twenty-six. He has been given a good many things in his life -- a scholarship to Eton, another to Trinity, a reserved occupation and a desk at Portland for the duration, a commendation from someone at the Admiralty he never met -- but all of them were given on paper, by men who had read his marks. Nobody has ever before stood beside him in a shop and bought him something for his work. He says, "Thank you, sir," and puts the notebook in his breast pocket, where he will keep one of the same kind for the rest of his life.

\* \* \*

It was the shrimp that brought him to Spilsbury's notice.

At Portland, in the war, the hydrophone men had been driven half mad by a noise in the water off the Caribbean stations: a crackling like fat in a pan, loud enough to bury a U-boat a mile off. Reggie was twenty-two and had nothing better to do with his evenings. He sat up with the recordings for six weeks and worked out that it was shrimp -- snapping shrimp, millions of them, in the beds along the reefs, each one cocking a claw and letting it go. He wrote a report. Somebody read it.

There had been other sounds on those recordings too, under the shrimp. Hums. Clicks. Long rising whistles that were not whales and not porpoises and not anything in the manuals. Reggie had marked them in the margins, and been told, quite kindly, by a commander with a great deal of gold on his sleeve, to leave them alone.

Now, a year after the war, somebody in a room Reggie will never be told the location of has passed his shrimp report to Spilsbury with a note saying that the young man has an ear; and Spilsbury has written to him, by hand, on plain paper with no letterhead, to ask whether he would care to come and listen to something.

His father, who is in Bengal, writes to congratulate him on the appointment. The letter takes five weeks to arrive and is mostly about the monsoon. Reggie thinks it very decent of him to have written at all; he knows how busy his father is.

\* \* \*

The Station is a bonded warehouse on the river at Deptford, requisitioned in 1944 and never given back. From the outside it looks like every other warehouse on that stretch of the river: soot-black brick, a loading crane, a gate with a policeman at it who reads Reggie's pass twice. Inside, it is warm. That is the first thing he notices -- it is the first properly warm building he has been in since October -- and the second is the smell: salt, and coal smoke, and under them both something sweetish and organic that he will learn, in time, not to notice at all.

The water comes up from the Thames at high tide, through a filter house, and is heated by two coal boilers in the basement that eat more fuel in a week, Spilsbury says, than the whole of Deptford. "They're estuarine," he says. "Brackish water, and warm. The river gives us the first, and the Admiralty pays for the second, and we try not to tell the Treasury how much."

There are twelve tanks, concrete-lined, in two rows down the length of the old bonded floor, each with a number painted on its side in white. Four of them are occupied.

"Five," says Spilsbury, walking him along the row the way a man walks a guest along his herbaceous borders. "Seven. Eight. Nine. One to Four didn't survive the crossing; the voyage from Georgetown is very hard on them. Six we lost in March." He stops at Seven's tank. "Pneumonia, as it turned out. It's nearly always pneumonia. We do what we can."

Reggie looks down into the water.

He has seen photographs. He has seen, at Portland, one smudged print passed round a wardroom that might have been anything at all. He has not been prepared for this: for the long dark body turning at the bottom of the tank, and the pale upturned face, and the eyes, which find him at once and do not look away. Seven rises, slowly, and hangs just under the surface, and looks at him. Reggie has been looked at by a great many people -- masters, examiners, selection boards, senior officers -- and he knows the look of a man deciding what he is for. This is not that. He does not know what this is.

"Yes," Spilsbury says softly, at his elbow. "They do that."

At the far end of the floor there is a second room, tiled white, with a drain in the floor and a long table and a tank beside it, and a big, quiet Yorkshireman in a rubber apron washing his hands at the sink.

"Fenwick," says Spilsbury. "My dissector. He trained on horses; it's the best possible preparation. Fenwick, this is Mr Tar, who is going to find out what they're saying."

Fenwick dries his hands and shakes Reggie's. His hands are very large and very clean and quite steady. "Grand," he says.

That night, in his lodgings in Greenwich, Reggie opens the new notebook for the first time and writes:

*6 Apr. Deptford. Specimens alive: 4. Water 27°C. Fenwick: vet, Thirsk. S.: "They do that." Breakfast: none.*

He looks at it for a while. Then, because he has been told to write everything down, he adds: *Seven looked at me.*

## Names

He is given the night shift because nobody else wants it, and because, Spilsbury says, they are quieter at night. They are not. They are only quieter to people who aren't listening.

The Navy has lent him a hydrophone for each occupied tank, and a German tape machine brought back from Hamburg in the last weeks of the war, which records on a ribbon of brown plastic and is better than anything the British have. Reggie loves it at once and without reservation. He sits at a trestle table at the end of the bonded floor with the headphones on and the boilers ticking somewhere below him, and he listens, and writes everything down.

They are never silent. That is the first thing. All through the night, all four of them, there is a continual murmur in the water: hums, clicks, little runs of rising notes, a sound like a wet finger drawn round the rim of a glass. Most of it he cannot tell apart. He records it anyway, reel after reel, and at four in the morning, when the river outside is quiet, he plays it back and plays it back and draws the shapes of the sounds on squared paper until his eyes ache.

It is the end of April before he sees it.

*29 Apr. Spec. 7, 0200–0500. Most frequent call: hum-click-hum ("A"). Approx. 1/min, irregular.*

*Spec. 5, same period. Most frequent call: click-click-rising ("B").*

*Spec. 7 also produces B. Approx. every 40–90 s. Not produced by 7 at any other time of day.*

*No acoustic path between tanks 5 and 7. 5 cannot hear 7. 7 cannot hear 5.*

He sits looking at that for a long time, with the headphones round his neck and the cold of the floor coming up through the soles of his shoes.

Then he goes back through every reel he has made, from the first night, and does the same for all four of them. It takes eleven nights. By the end of it he has a table, and the table says what he thought it would say, and he still cannot quite believe it, and so he checks it again.

Each of them has a call that is theirs: the one they make most, the one that is like a signature. And each of them, in the small hours, makes other calls besides -- and the other calls are the signatures of the others. Seven makes Five's. Eight makes Seven's and Nine's. Nine makes a signature that belongs to no one in the building, over and over, every night, which Reggie cannot account for at all.

They are calling each other by name. Through concrete walls that will not carry a sound, every night, they are calling each other by name.

He shows Spilsbury at seven in the morning, when Spilsbury comes in fresh from his breakfast with a new carnation. He had meant to wait and write it up properly; he can't. He gives Spilsbury the headphones and plays him Seven, and then Five, and then Seven again, making Five's call into the dark; and watches Spilsbury's face as he listens.

Spilsbury takes the headphones off very slowly.

"Names," he says.

"I think so, sir. Identification calls, at any rate. I'd want a great deal more data before I --"

"Names." Spilsbury looks at him. Then he does something nobody has ever done to Reggie before: he takes him by both shoulders and holds him at arm's length and looks at him, properly, as though he were something worth looking at. "You have very good ears, Reginald," he says. "Very good ears indeed. I knew you would have."

Reggie walks home to Greenwich along the river in the first light, past the bombed wharves and the barges lying over on the mud, and finds that he cannot stop smiling, and does not know what to do with his face.

\* \* \*

Spilsbury is very interested in the names. He wants to know, he tells Reggie at lunch, where they live. "In the throat, or in the head? Is it the same apparatus for a name as for a cry of pain? Find me which calls they make most, Reginald, and when, and I'll find you what they make them with." He says it so warmly that Reggie, who has not slept, takes it for encouragement, and goes back to the trestle table that night with his heart full.

Five dies in the middle of May.

*18 May. Spec. 5 d. 0340. Pneumonia (per S.). To F. 0700.*

*19 May. Spec. 7, 0200–0500: calls B (Five's) at approx. 40–90 s. Rate unchanged.*

*26 May. Spec. 7 calls B. Rate unchanged.*

*9 June. Spec. 7 calls B. Rate unchanged.*

He writes *rate unchanged* every night until Seven dies, in September. On the night after that, for the first time since Reggie came to Deptford, there is no one in the building calling Five's name.

\* \* \*

In October a Navy transport brings three more from Georgetown, and Spilsbury numbers them Ten, Eleven and Twelve.

On Eleven's first night in the building, Reggie, at his trestle table, hears her signature; and it is the call that Nine has been making every night since April. The one that belonged to no one.

He sits very still. Then he switches to Nine's hydrophone and listens. Nine is making Eleven's call, as she always does. And Eleven, two tanks away, through concrete that will not carry a sound, is making Nine's.

*14 Oct. Spec. 11's signature = the unassigned call made by Spec. 9 since April. 11 produces 9's signature from first night.*

*Hyp.: 9 and 11 known to each other before capture. Same river? Same group?*

*Hyp.: 9 has been calling 11 for six months.*

He shows Spilsbury in the morning.

"How very touching," Spilsbury says, and means it. Then: "We'll keep them apart, of course. We don't want to confound the data."

## February 1947

The snow comes in the last week of January and does not stop.

By the second week of February the river is grey slush at the margins and the coal isn't getting through. The colliers are iced in at the Tyne; the railways are drifted over; a minister goes on the wireless to say that the electricity will be cut, across half the country, for five hours a day. The Station has its own boilers and its own priority, on paper, and in practice it has whatever the coal merchant at Deptford Bridge can be persuaded to sell it, which by the twelfth is nothing at all.

Reggie has been ill for a week by then. He doesn't notice at first; he is cold all the time anyway, and so is everyone. He goes on sitting at the trestle table through the nights in his overcoat and scarf, listening to the water cool. Then one morning he stands up to go home and the bonded floor tilts gently away from him, and Fenwick catches him by the arm, and he wakes in his own bed in Greenwich with no clear memory of getting there and a fever that his landlady, Mrs Pruett, takes one look at and sends for the doctor about.

Influenza. Ten days of it. He lies in the narrow bed under every blanket Mrs Pruett owns and two overcoats, with the window furred white on the inside, and sweats, and shivers, and dreams about the tanks.

On the fourth day, Spilsbury comes.

Reggie hears his voice on the stairs, and Mrs Pruett's, flustered; and then the door opens and there he is, in his overcoat and his good grey hat with snow on the brim, carrying a paper bag. He sets it down on the blanket. There are oranges in it. Four of them, bright as lamps.

Reggie stares at them. There have been no oranges in London since Christmas. There have barely been oranges since the war.

"Don't ask," Spilsbury says, pleased with himself, and sits down on the end of the bed.

He puts a hand on Reggie's forehead. It is cool and dry, and stays there a moment, judging -- a doctor's hand -- and Reggie, who cannot remember the last time anyone did that, Matron perhaps, at his prep school, or before that someone whose face he can no longer bring back, shuts his eyes; and does not think about it any further, because there is no reason to.

"You'll do," Spilsbury says. "A hundred and two, I should think. Mrs Pruett tells me you're not eating."

"The tanks," says Reggie. His voice comes out cracked. "The boilers. Is there coal?"

Spilsbury takes his hand away.

"Not since Tuesday," he says. "We had the electric heaters on the generator while the generator had fuel. It ran out on Tuesday night." He says it gently, the way one gives bad news to a patient. "Eight and Nine died before morning. Twelve on Wednesday. Eleven's holding. Fenwick's been sleeping on the floor beside her in his coat, feeding the stove with packing cases."

Nine.

Reggie lies very still under the overcoats and thinks of Eleven, two tanks along from Nine's, in the cold and the dark, making Nine's call at forty- to ninety-second intervals into water that will not carry it; and Nine no longer making Eleven's.

"Eleven will be calling her," he says. "Nine. She'll go on calling her."

"Will she?" Spilsbury looks interested. "How remarkable. You must make a note of it when you're back." He pats the blanket over Reggie's knee. "I've done the three of them already, I'm afraid; I couldn't wait for you. The cold keeps things beautifully. I've never had such sections." He reaches into his coat and takes out a folded copy of the *Lancet*. "Now. There's a perfectly fascinating paper in here on chilblains, which seems apposite. Shall I read it to you?"

He does. He reads all of it, and then the correspondence, and then an obituary of a man in Leeds, in his soft, even voice, while the light goes blue in the frosted window and Mrs Pruett brings up tea that neither of them drinks. Reggie lies and listens. He is thinking about Nine, and about the sections, and about Eleven calling in the dark; and somewhere underneath all that he is more comfortable than he can remember being in his life, and he falls asleep before the man in Leeds is buried, with one hand on the paper bag of oranges.

When he wakes it is dark and Spilsbury has gone. He finds his notebook on the chair beside the bed, where someone has put it within reach, and writes, with some difficulty:

*13 Feb. 8, 9, 12 d. (cold). S. came. Oranges (4). Read Lancet. Stayed till ten.*

He looks at the line for a long time. Then he adds: *Eleven.* And cannot, for the moment, think what to put after it.

## Eleven

In April, when the thaw has come and gone and the river runs high and brown with snowmelt, Spilsbury decides to look at the heart.

"The cold," he says at lunch, in the little office above the bonded floor that smells of formalin and his pipe. "Eight and Nine and Twelve died in a night, at a water temperature a man could swim in for an hour. There's something about the heart, Reginald. How it behaves when it's chilled. I can't learn it from the dead ones; the dead ones have stopped." He spreads marmalade on his toast. "I shall have to look at a live one."

Reggie, who has been thinking about something else, says, "You can't sedate them."

They both know this. They learned it in November, from Ten, and Spilsbury has not mentioned it since.

"No," Spilsbury agrees. "We can't." He looks at Reggie across the table with his kind, tired eyes. "It's a great pity, but there it is. Fenwick works quickly. I'd thought of Eleven; she's the strongest we have." He pauses. "You've a hydrophone in her tank."

"Yes, sir."

"Keep it there. We'll move her to the side tank by the table and run your line through. It'll be a good opportunity -- we'll want to know what she says." He smiles. "No one has ever made a recording under those conditions. No one in the world."

And Reggie hears himself say, "No, sir. No one has," and is aware, faintly, of a small cold place opening under his breastbone; and is aware too that his mind has already gone on ahead of him, to the cable run, and the gain, and whether the German machine will want a second reel.

He writes it down that night. *Hydrophone to stay in 11's tank for procedure. Good opportunity.* He reads it over, and does not change it.

\* \* \*

He is not in the white room. He is in the store-cupboard next to it, with the door shut, at a trestle table with the tape machine and his headphones and his notebook. Through the wall, now and then, he can hear Spilsbury's voice, low and even, and Fenwick's, and the sound of water. He does not listen to those. He listens to Eleven.

For the first hour she calls Nine.

Then she stops calling Nine, and begins the other thing.

He has heard it before, in pieces. He has a type D in his catalogue, fourteen poor examples, made when a tank was being drained or a specimen netted: a sharp falling sound with a rough edge, very loud, repeated. In his notes he has been calling it the alarm call, provisionally. He has never heard it like this. It is so clear and so strong that the needle on the German machine slams over and he has to turn the gain down, and then down again; and it does not stop. It goes on and on, one voice, the same call, at intervals of six or seven seconds, through the second hour and into the third.

There is no one to hear it. The nearest of the others is forty yards away through two concrete walls. Reggie is the only living thing in the world that can hear what Eleven is saying, and what she is saying is *danger*, and she is saying it to no one.

Sometime in the third hour, the door opens and Spilsbury looks in. His sleeves are rolled to the elbow and his forearms are wet, and there is no carnation in his buttonhole; he has taken it out and put it in a glass of water on the windowsill, Reggie sees afterwards, so that it won't wilt.

"How are the recordings coming along?"

"Very clean, sir," Reggie says. And then -- he will remember this for the rest of his life; he will remember exactly the angle of the light through the store-cupboard window, and the weight of the headphones in his hands as he lifts them off -- he says, "Would you like to hear?"

He plays him the best of it. Spilsbury listens with his head bent, his wet hands held a little away from his sides.

"Beautiful," he says softly. "Quite beautiful, Reginald. Well done." And goes back through the door.

It takes most of the week. Eleven calls *danger* for most of the week. Reggie records all of it. He changes reels, and labels them, and logs the counts: two hundred and fourteen on the first day, three hundred and some on the second. On the fifth night, at twenty to ten, the calling stops.

*Spec. 11 d. 2140. Type D (alarm) recorded: 1,206 instances. Clarity: best to date. Reels 31–44.*

He goes home to Greenwich and sleeps for eleven hours without dreaming.

In the morning, shaving, he looks at his own face in the glass over Mrs Pruett's washstand and hears himself say, quite clearly and out loud, as if it were somebody else: *You might as well have held the knife.*

He cuts himself. Not badly. He stands there with the blood running down his jaw into the lather, looking at the man in the glass, and the man in the glass looks back at him with no expression at all.

Then he dries his face, and finds the notebook, and -- because he has been told to write everything down -- writes:

*15 Apr. 0710, shaving. "You might as well have held the knife." Source unclear.*

## 1948

The sentence comes back.

Not every day. Often enough. Shaving, mostly; sometimes on the river path, or at the trestle table at four in the morning with a new reel turning. It is always the same sentence, and always in the same voice, which Reggie has eventually, reluctantly, identified as his own.

*3 June 47. Shaving. Same sentence.*

*22 Aug. River path. Same.*

*9 Nov. At the table, 0410. Same. (n=17)*

He goes on counting it, because he counts everything. He goes on counting the other things too. The Navy sends more specimens from Georgetown, in batches of three and four, and Spilsbury numbers them, and they die -- of pneumonia, mostly, and sometimes of other things. Reggie keeps a list in the back of his notebook, on a page by itself, and copies it into each new notebook as the old one fills. It has two columns: a number, and a date.

*Running total: 19.*

*Running total: 23.*

His paper on the identification calls grows too, in the evenings, in Mrs Pruett's front room by the gas fire. He has thirty-odd signatures catalogued now, and a hundred and forty-two instances of one specimen making another's, and he has begun -- carefully, with every caveat he can think of -- to wonder in writing whether there may be more to it than names. He does not show that part to anyone yet.

In March, Fenwick leaves.

He tells Reggie on the river stairs, in the rain, with his kitbag at his feet. He has taken a practice in the Dales, near where he was born: cattle and sheepdogs and the odd hunter, nothing that'll keep him up at night. He holds out his hand to shake, and Reggie sees that it is trembling, very slightly, all the time now, as it has been since Eleven.

"Spilsbury says you'll be back," Reggie says.

Fenwick looks at him for a while. "Aye," he says. "He would." He picks up the kitbag. "Don't wait till your hands go, lad." And he walks off up the stairs into the rain, and Reggie never sees him again.

Spilsbury takes it with perfect equanimity. By the end of the month he has a new dissector, a young man from Edinburgh with excellent hands and no conversation. "Fenwick was the best I ever had," he says at lunch, quite sincerely. "He'll come back. They nearly always do."

\* \* \*

In the summer, Reggie's father comes home from India.

The new government in Delhi has no further use for him and he has none for Bengal, and so he has retired, with Reggie's mother, to a house outside Cheltenham with a monkey-puzzle tree in the garden. In July he comes up to town for a week of cricket, and he and Reggie have lunch at his club in Pall Mall.

It is a very pleasant lunch. They have not seen each other in six years, and before that in four, and on the walk up from Charing Cross Reggie had wondered whether there would be anything to say. There turns out to be a great deal. His father has been to Lord's to see the Australians, and has views on Bradman, and on the new ball, and on the Labour government, and on the state of the club's claret since the war; and Reggie listens and agrees and now and then asks a question, and the time goes by very easily.

"And you're with the Admiralty, your mother tells me," his father says over the coffee. "Something hush-hush."

"I'm afraid I can't really talk about it, sir."

"No, no. Quite right." His father looks relieved, Reggie thinks, though possibly only because the coffee has come. "Good show."

On the club steps, in the white afternoon glare, they shake hands. "Well done, my boy," his father says -- which is what he has always said, at the end of every holiday Reggie can remember, for no reason in particular -- and puts on his hat, and walks off towards St James's to look for a taxi.

Reggie walks back to Deptford along the Embankment. He thinks it went off very well. They get on, he and his father; they always have. They hardly ever disagree about anything.

He thinks, for no reason he can name, of Spilsbury's hands on his shoulders on the bonded floor, two years ago -- *You have very good ears, Reginald* -- and then puts it out of his mind, because there's no reason to think about it at all.

*14 July. Lunch, Father, Pall Mall. Bradman. Claret. Went off well.*

## 1949

The four come in on the same ship in January -- Twenty-two, Twenty-four, Twenty-five and Twenty-seven -- out of the same river, according to the manifest, on the same day.

By the end of their first week Reggie has all four signatures, and by the end of the second he has the rest. Every night, from two until five, each of them calls the other three. Twenty-two calls Twenty-four and Twenty-five and Twenty-seven; Twenty-four calls Twenty-two and Twenty-five and Twenty-seven; round and round, like a round sung by four people in four separate rooms who cannot hear one another and keep on singing. He has never recorded anything like it. He has never had four from one group before.

They begin to fail in the second week, sooner than most. Twenty-five has the cough first -- wet and rattling, so loud on the hydrophone that Reggie can hear it between the calls -- and within a few days all four of them have it. Less feeding. Longer intervals at the surface. The young man from Edinburgh writes "early pneumonia, all four" in his ledger, without comment. They go on calling each other every night, between the coughing.

Reggie lies awake for two nights. Then he goes up to Spilsbury's office with his catalogue under his arm and says it.

"Put them together."

Spilsbury looks up from his slides.

"They're one group," Reggie says. "They came out of the same river on the same day, and they've called each other every night since they arrived. If the calls are what I think they are -- if it's company they --" He stops, and starts again, in the voice he would use to a committee. "Separation may be a factor in mortality. It has never been tested. We have four specimens from one group, all calling one another. It would be a clean experiment. And they're dying in any case."

Spilsbury looks at him for a long moment with his head a little on one side.

"Yes," he says. "Yes, it would. Do it, Reginald. The big tank, Thursday. I'll come down and watch."

\* \* \*

On Thursday morning they fill the big tank at the end of the floor -- the old settling tank, twenty feet across and eight deep, a concrete pit under the low roof of the bonded floor, more like a cistern than anything Reggie would call open water -- and warm it, and at nine o'clock the handlers carry them in one at a time in canvas slings and lower them in.

Reggie is at the edge with the hydrophone over and the headphones on and his notebook open on the rim. He has thought about this moment for two nights. He has not let himself think about what he expects to hear. He knows what he hopes to hear: Twenty-two's signature, and an answer.

For four minutes, nothing. The four of them hang at the four quarters of the tank, very still, looking at one another. Twenty-five coughs, and the sound goes round the concrete walls and comes back.

Then Twenty-four moves, and Twenty-two moves, and they meet in the middle, and touch --

And the water goes white.

Afterwards he will try to describe it in the notebook, and fail. It is not like anything he has seen animals do. It is as though, the instant they touched, something went through all four of them at once -- and then had nowhere to go, and came back off the concrete, and went through them again, louder each time, the way a microphone howls when it is set too near its own loudspeaker. None of them can bear it. They go for each other with their teeth and their hands. The headphones fill with a sound so loud that he tears them off, and then he doesn't need them; he can hear it in the air, one call, the rough falling one, from four throats at once. *Danger. Danger. Danger.* The water goes pink, and then red.

"Nets," says Spilsbury, quite calmly, from the gallery.

The handlers go in with the long nets and the waders. It takes them half an hour to get them apart, and they cannot sedate them, and Reggie stands at the edge of the tank with the useless headphones in his hand and watches the four of them fight the nets and the men and each other, and does nothing at all. Twenty-two is dead by noon. Twenty-seven by one. Twenty-four and Twenty-five, back in their own tanks, torn and separated, live into the small hours; and for those hours, from two until five, they call each other by name through the concrete, as they always have.

\* \* \*

Spilsbury comes down to the trestle table at six that evening, as Reggie is writing up.

"A clean negative," he says. He puts a hand on Reggie's shoulder and leaves it there. "Beautifully run, Reginald. You set it up perfectly and you didn't flinch, and now we know. They can't be kept together; they'll kill each other. Nobody need ever lose another specimen finding that out." His hand tightens a little. "Most men would have flinched from running it. You didn't. I'm very pleased with you."

And it goes through Reggie like warmth into cold hands: there, and spreading, before he can stop it, all the way up into his face.

Spilsbury goes. Reggie sits with the pen in his hand and feels it -- the pleasure, the glow of it, the being *pleased with* -- and slowly understands, with a kind of disbelief, what it is he is feeling, and when, and why.

Four of them. His idea. Two dead by lunch and two more, he already knows, by morning. And he is pleased, because Spilsbury is pleased with him.

He writes it down, because he writes everything down.

*3 Feb. 22, 24, 25, 27 into tank A, 0900. Contact 0904. Type D, all four. Onset resembled acoustic feedback (cf. microphone too near loudspeaker). 22 d. 1158. 27 d. 1306. Result: negative.*

*S.: "I'm very pleased with you."*

*R.T.: pleased.*

He looks at the last line for a long time. Then underneath it, in exactly the same small upright hand, he writes one more word.

*Resign.*

\* \* \*

Spilsbury takes the letter without surprise. He reads it twice at his desk under the green lamp, with his pipe going, and then puts it down and looks at Reggie over his half-moon spectacles.

"Fenwick gave me two years," he says. "You've given me three. I'd hoped for rather longer." He doesn't sound angry. He sounds, if anything, fond. "Where will you go?"

"Plymouth, sir. The Marine Biological Association have offered me a bench."

"Plymouth." Spilsbury smiles. "Barnacles."

"Mostly."

"You'll be bored to tears in six months." He taps out his pipe. "You've kept everything, I imagine. The notebooks. The reels."

"Yes, sir," says Reggie. "Everything."

He has rehearsed this part in the shaving glass for a week. He has thought about saying it with emphasis, or not at all. In the end he only says it, plainly; and Spilsbury looks at him a moment -- and then laughs, delighted, the way a man laughs when a pupil plays the move he would have played himself.

"Good," he says. "Good man. So have I." He takes a sheaf of typescript from his drawer, and Reggie sees with a jolt that it is his own: the paper on the identification calls, which he submitted for clearance a month ago and has heard nothing of since. "We'll both keep ours somewhere dry, shall we? You signed the Act, as I did; everything in them belongs to the King. I can't think of anyone who'd want to read them. But it's a comfort to know where they are."

He uncaps his pen.

"I've cleared this," he says, "with one change. You may publish. The calls, and the method, and the waveforms -- all the waveforms, Reginald; the alarm call especially. It's the finest thing you've done. Nothing about where, or how. Nothing about us." He turns to the last page, reads it, draws a single neat line through one sentence, writes something in the margin, and turns the page round so that Reggie can see.

Reggie had written: *These observations raise the possibility that the vocal repertoire of N. aestuarius constitutes a rudimentary language.*

In the margin, in Spilsbury's fine copperplate: *-- though none of it rises to the level of an actual language.*

"If they have a language," Spilsbury says gently, "someone will ask whether they're people. And then where would we all be?" He caps the pen. "Names and alarms, Reginald. That's quite remarkable enough for a first paper. Don't overreach."

Reggie looks at the line through his sentence.

"Yes, sir," he says.

Spilsbury gets up and comes round the desk and puts both hands on Reggie's shoulders, as he did once before, on the bonded floor, three years ago.

"I'm very proud of you," he says. "I want you to know that. You've the best ears I ever had, and you'll do great things; and when you're tired of barnacles you'll come back, because the good ones always do. The work calls them." He smiles, kind and tired. "Go on. Before I change my mind."

Reggie goes down the stairs, and through the bonded floor between the tanks, past the white numbers on their sides, without looking into any of them. He shows his pass to the policeman at the gate. He walks out along the river towards Greenwich in the February dusk, with the paper under his arm and the notebook in his pocket and Spilsbury's hands still warm on his shoulders through his coat, and the words going round and round in his head -- *proud of you, the best ears I ever had* -- like a tune he can't get rid of.

Nobody has ever said that to him before. Not once, in his life. He knows it the way he knows his own name, and it does not seem to him to mean anything in particular, except that it is very hard to keep walking.

He keeps walking. That is all the escape amounts to, in the end. He wants, more than he has ever wanted anything, to turn round and go back up those stairs; and he doesn't; and he goes on not doing it, one step and then the next, all the way to Greenwich.

## Plymouth, 1951

The paper comes out in the spring of 1950 and is, people tell him, rather a sensation. He is asked to speak at the Linnean, and declines. It is cited, and quarrelled with, and cited again; and every reprint request that comes to his bench at Plymouth has the same figure circled -- Figure 4, the alarm call, the cleanest trace of an animal signal anyone has ever published. Nobody asks where he got it. He had thought someone would.

He counts barnacles. He is bored to tears within six months, as predicted, and then, slowly, less so. In the evenings, in a cold room at the top of the laboratory with a view of the Sound in front and the bombed city behind, he takes out the old notebooks one by one and works through them page by page, every specimen he has a record of, from Five onward, and builds a table. Date of arrival. Date of death. Cause, where known.

It takes him most of a year. When it's done it says what he knew it would. Thirty-four animals. Thirty-two dead. Not one of the thirty-two lived as long as eighteen months. The other two were alive the morning he left Deptford; he has no way of finding out what became of them, and does not need one. He publishes it as a short note, dry as dust, with no names in it and no Station, and the only line in it that matters is the last row of the table, which reads *n=32*.

He keeps his own count privately, on the inside cover of his current notebook, the way he always has. It has a line for Eleven, by herself, and a line for the rest.

*The others: n=31.*

Spilsbury sends him a postcard, once, of Plymouth Hoe, with nothing written on the back but *Barnacles? -- B.S.* Reggie keeps it. He keeps everything.

\* \* \*

The letter comes in March of 1951, in a long cream envelope with a West Indian stamp.

*Dear Mr Tar,*

*I have read your paper on the identification calls of N. aestuarius, and your note on mortality in captivity, with very great attention.*

*My husband and I are establishing a small institute in the British West Indies for the study of the species. It is on a site rather a long way from where the animals actually live, I'm afraid -- but it was the best we could get, and the price was right. We have money for three years, a great deal of concrete, and at present nobody who can listen.*

*May I ask you a question that is not in your paper? When they call by name, in your recordings, are they calling to anyone in particular? And does anyone ever answer?*

*Yours sincerely,*

*Hester Bevan (Mrs)*

*The William Martin Institute for Marine Studies (in formation)*

Reggie reads it three times, standing at his bench with the barnacles drying under the lamp.

Nobody has ever asked him that. Not the referees, not the men at the Linnean, not the Navy, not the King. Spilsbury asked him once where the names *lived*. Nobody has ever asked him who they were for.

He writes back that evening on Association paper, and it is the shortest letter he has written in his life.

*Dear Mrs Bevan, Yes. No. I should be glad to come. R. Tar.*

His father writes from Cheltenham to say that he and Reggie's mother were interested to hear of the appointment, and that Reggie should take care with the sun.

\* \* \*

The day before he sails, he goes into a stationer's on the bombed ground behind Old Town Street -- a prefabricated hut with a hand-painted sign -- to buy a notebook for the voyage.

He means to buy something different. He means it quite sincerely, all the way to the counter. Then he finds that he is holding one: small, black, oilcloth-covered, the pages ruled in faint blue. The same kind. Of course the same kind. He pays for it with his own money and goes out into the thin spring sun, and on a bench on the Hoe, looking out over the Sound, he opens it to the first page and uncaps his pen.

He writes without thinking, in his small upright hand:

*Write everything down. Memory is a liar, and so are you.*

He looks at it for a long time. The second sentence isn't his. He knows it isn't. He thinks, quite seriously, about putting a line through it.

But he has never crossed anything out in his life.

Not yet.
