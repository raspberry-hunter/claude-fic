# Interested Party

He reads Tar for the first time on the eleventh of October and writes the card that evening.

The card says four things. The third line says *interested party: none*, which he means in the technical sense, the vetting sense — a man with no stake anywhere is a man you cannot predict and cannot hold, and Tar had none that could be found in two hours, and that is the entry. The fourth line says *do not put him on anything he can see the end of*, and he is rather pleased with it, and he is right about it.

He would like that on the record before anything else, because there is a version of all this in which he is a fool, and he is not going to have it. He was right about everything. Not nearly right. He has been doing this since 1931 and has read four thousand men and there is one error in the whole of the file, and it is not an error of reading.

What he saw was not brilliance. Brilliance is common and mostly a nuisance. He saw a man who does not require to be handled — you put a thing in front of him and it goes straight in, no arrangement necessary, no allowance for the fact that it's Tuesday or his mother's ill or he's got a view about the Minister. No friction in him at all. Like handing something to a machine that's been left running for years in an empty room, and the machine takes it and starts, and you understand that it has been on the whole time and nobody ever came.

—-

The four hours are twice a week and by the November he has moved two things off the Thursday to protect them. That goes down as a scheduling matter and he does not intend to say otherwise.

Here is what the four hours are, and he'll put it plainly because there's nobody to put it to.

He has spent his working life talking to people who are managing him. Every one. The ones who like him are managing him and the ones who loathe him are managing him and the difference is only in the finish, and he sees the whole of it, always, from about the second sentence, and has not been in a room where it wasn't happening in twenty years. Not a complaint. He's good at it, it's the job, he'd be bored to death without it.

Then there is a hut on a hill with a man in it who is doing none of that, who has never in his life done any of it and wouldn't know how to start, and who takes a bad design apart in front of him at eleven at night and says *no, that's wrong, and here is where*, and doesn't look up to see how it lands.

He goes back down the hill on those nights and he is — and he'll use the word, he isn't a coward about words, only about a few of them — he is happy. It's a physical fact and it takes about the length of the walk to go off.

He made no plan. He is a man who plans and he did not plan this. He moved two things off the Thursday and that was the whole of the operation in October and November, and if it had stayed there it would have been the best two months of his life and nothing else.

—-

On the fourth of December Tar writes his returns in a different hand and Spilsbury has them on his desk on the Friday morning.

Smaller. Pressure altered. The long stroke on the double-t gone short — the one ornamental mark the man makes, the only stroke in his writing that isn't required, and it stops between one day's sheet and the next.

He does not need to be careful about what that is. A state has arrived in a man who did not have one, on a particular afternoon, between the intake figures and the evening returns. He could stand that up in front of anybody.

What he does not have is the object.

And here it is, the one error in the file, and he can give the hour it was made in: eight o'clock on the morning of the sixth of December, at that desk, with the sheet in front of him and the fire not lit. There is nobody on this establishment. Forty staff, four of them women and all four married and two of them older than his mother. There is a hut on a hill and there is himself, twice a week, four hours, since October.

He reads it in his own favour.

That is the whole of the offence and he has never done it before or since. He has built a career on the fact that he does not do it — that he can read a room he is standing in, that he can take his own effect out of a signal the way you subtract a tare, and he has done it ten thousand times and has never once been wrong about a man who was trying to please him. And on the sixth of December he takes a genuine alteration in a genuine hand and he puts himself down as the cause of it, and he knows while he is doing it that he has not tested it, and he does not test it, because he does not want the other answer.

He is thirty-nine. It ought to be embarrassing. It is not embarrassing at all; it is the best six weeks he has ever had, and he'd have them again.

—-

He tries to confirm it for six weeks and cannot.

That is the part that would tell anybody with sense what was going on. He is the best reader in England and he cannot get a second point out of the man, because there is nothing to get: Tar is exactly the same in the December that he was in the October, exactly the same on the Thursday as on the Monday, and produces no signal of any kind about anything that is not work. It does not occur to him that this is itself the finding.

Somewhere in that six weeks he sits in the office and explains to Tar how the establishment gets people. He does it well. You find the thing, and you never do anything with it, you simply make sure he knows you have it. It's a good half hour and he means every word and it is the truest account of his trade he has ever given anybody.

He has since had occasion to think about the fact that he gave that lecture in the middle of the six weeks. He does not go any further with it than that.

The snow comes on the twenty-third of January and by then he has run out of ways to find out, and there is one left.

—-

He puts it to him on a Wednesday in February and he does it badly.

Not the words. He starts by trying to say what the four hours are and can't get it out, which has not happened since he was twenty-two, and he leaves the sentence three-quarters finished and goes on to the next thing, and the next thing is the offer.

He tells him it stood before December. He tells him he has a fair idea what happened in the December and is not going to have it confirmed, because a thing you have confirmed is a thing you've got, and he says it in the voice he uses for the true things and Tar takes it, because it sounds exactly like him and because it is exactly what he would say if it were true.

It is not true. There is no offer before December. The offer is the test and there is nothing else in it.

And Tar sits and thinks about it — actually thinks, you can watch him do it, you can nearly read the headings — and comes back inside four minutes and says *very well*.

Not yes. *Very well.* He has had that answer from men signing for equipment.

He decides it is a beginning. A man who has never done anything at all doesn't arrive with the vocabulary; four minutes is quick because it's reasoned, not because it's nothing; this is a matter of eight or nine months.

That's the error. Not the reading — the reading was correct. The error is that he had the answer in his hand at four minutes past and put it down, and he would not have allowed any other man alive to put it down in front of him.

—-

He has it properly inside a week and there is no drama about it.

He isn't going to describe how. The man is not concealing anything, because it has not entered his head that there is anything to conceal, and there is a difference between the way a person is with somebody and the way a person is with an arrangement, and it is not subtle, and Spilsbury could pick it up off a bad photograph.

So: not him. Fine. A test is not less good for coming out negative and he has said so to junior officers for fifteen years.

He'd give an account of the Thursday and the Friday if there were one. He was at his desk both days and the work went out on time and he can produce it.

Then he goes and finds out who.

He wants it understood that there was no struggle about this. He did not sit up over it. He has never in his life declined a piece of information he could have and he was not going to start on this one, and it took him about forty minutes with the ward returns on a Saturday: the hour and a quarter, four days in five; the folder shut on his knee; the name written out in full where every other man on that ward is a number. Sidney Alderton, S/11, thirty-eight, married, wife in Warminster, admitted the previous March.

He sat with that for a while.

And there is a word for what he had then and he had no trouble with it, then or since. He is not a coward about words. He has used this one about other men for twenty years, in rooms, to their faces, and been right every time, and he would have written it on a card about anybody in this building and gone on to the next file before lunch.

So he wrote it on a card. He is not going to be coy about that either. He took one that evening, the same size as the October one, and put the finding on it in his own hand, and the finding is *interested party*.

Technical sense. The same two words he had written about Tar on the eleventh of October, third line, and they mean a man who has acquired a stake: a man who cannot assess his own case, whose returns on the matter in hand are not admissible, who must come off it and be replaced by somebody with nothing in it. That is a finding of unfitness and it is the severest in the trade and it ends careers, and he wrote it about himself, unprompted, with nobody in the room and nobody ever going to be, and there is not a board in England that would have gone further than he went that evening at that desk on his own.

It is the correct classification and he would defend it now. A board wants to know what a man is disqualified from. That is what the card says. There is no more precise term in the language for the condition he was in and he had it down inside a minute.

The card is in the establishment safe and it is still there in 1967.

It is a good card. It is the best one he ever wrote and it is about himself and nobody will read it, and he has had it out perhaps a dozen times in twenty years and has never once found anything wrong with it.

—-

The submission for the next case goes to London on the fourteenth of February and it takes forty minutes and it is a good piece of work.

He is not going to pretend about the fourteenth of February. There is a version he has given to three separate people and it is the operational one and it would stand in front of any board that has ever sat, and it is not what happened at that desk.

What happened at that desk is that he wrote four criteria. Duration. Stability of the presentation. Absence of an alternative course. And capacity to give an account afterwards, which is genuinely new, which nobody has had before, which is the thing that turns this programme from a body of anecdote into a body of evidence and which he had been trying to get for two years.

All four are true criteria. All four are necessary. He would defend the fourth to his grave and it is the best single idea he has had in this trade.

And he wrote them in an order and with a weighting that produced one man, and he knew it at the second criterion, and he did not stop, and it went off on the fourteenth and there was never any difficulty about it in any forum and there never has been since.

That is what he is. That's the finding and it was available on the fourteenth of February 1947 and he had it and he put it away, and the putting away took under an hour, because he is very good at that and has been since about 1931.

The other thing he does is put Tar on the schedule and require the whole thirty-one items in his own hand.

The reason is good and it is the reason he'd give: the best assessor on the establishment is the best assessor on the establishment, and you do not take a worse measurement when a better one is available, and he has never left a good instrument in the drawer and has said so out loud to several people and meant it every time.

He is not going to say anything further about that one.

—-

The fourth of March is a Tuesday and it takes twenty-three minutes and he is not in the room, because he never is. He reads the reports.

The night before, Tar comes down the hill.

It isn't one of the nights, he hasn't been asked, and he stands at the door in the wet with fifty minutes of his own handwriting in a folder and the schedule complete, and Spilsbury opens the door and looks at him for about two seconds.

Both seconds are used. In the first he sees exactly what it is and who it is about. In the second he decides.

He stands back and lets him in and does not say one word about it then or ever, and if he were asked — he never is; there is nobody to ask him — he'd say that a man who wants a thing at ten o'clock at night before a hard morning is entitled to have it and not to be examined about it, and that examining him would have been the cruelty.

That's true as well. That is the difficulty with the whole of it. It is all true, every line, you could publish the lot.

—-

He is expecting it to stop and it does not stop.

Tar comes down the hill in the April as though the fourth of March had happened to somebody else, and in the May, and through the summer, and for thirteen months, and does not once refer to it and does not once alter.

For a fortnight he lets himself think it is forgiveness. He knows better at the time and does it anyway; it costs nothing and there is nobody watching. It goes at about the middle of April, off nothing much — the way the man puts his coat down — and after that there is no version left except the true one, which is that there is nothing in there at present to do the refusing with, and that a thing which is not being refused is not therefore being agreed to.

He does not stop it.

Here is what he tells himself, and he would stand by it now. The terms said it ends the day either party says so and no reason required. He wrote those terms and meant them and they are the only decent thing in the whole arrangement. He has taken two decisions on this man's behalf, the fourteenth of February and the schedule, and they are the two worst things he has done in his life, and he is not going to take a third. To end it would be to decide for him again. It is Tar's to take and Tar will take it when he is able.

That is true. It would stand in front of anybody.

It also happens to be the arrangement under which he goes on having what he wants, at no cost, for thirteen months. Both of those are the same sentence. He has never once found the place where they come apart.

—-

By the June there is a plate on the table twice a week.

The reason is that the man has stopped eating and is running on four hours and is producing more work than the establishment can process, and you do not run apparatus like that without maintenance, and he has costed the replacement and there is no replacement. That is correct and it is not the whole of it and he is not going to go into the rest.

He'll say this much, because it's procedure and not about him: he never mentions the plate and never lets the plate become a thing, and if half comes back he sends the same size the following week, because the fastest way to stop a man eating is to let him see he is being fed.

He sends the MO in the September with the ration figures put into his hand so it won't look like an order. Tar works out that he was sent, of course he does, and says nothing, which is what he does with everything.

Nineteen months, and the man asks him two questions. Both in the same quarter of an hour, in the September, with a plate on his knee.

The first is *how long have you known*, and he says he doesn't know anything, which is a lie of a size he has not attempted before or since, delivered about forty seconds after taking a reading off two pages of handwriting in front of him. Tar is not fooled for a moment and lets it go, and he watches him decide to let it go and has never worked out why.

The second is *why was he next*, and he gives the operational answer, which is true and complete and does not touch the question, and Tar says it is not the whole of it, and he says no, and does not answer.

That is everything either of them ever asks the other in nineteen months. He lied to the first and declined the second, and there was never a third.

—-

He reads the follow-up paper on the fifteenth of November standing up, all nine pages, and at section four he stops turning the pages.

Eleven lines. The specification for the subject.

He knows what it is by the fourth line. It's a man. It is a description of one person written out as a class, and there are two on that ward it fits, and Tar knew that while he was writing it and wrote it better because of it, and brought it down the hill and put it in his hands.

That is the point at which it goes.

Not guilt about February — he has had February since February, he wrote it on the fourteenth and knew what he was doing at the second criterion and has carried it about ever since in perfectly good order. This is a different reading and it comes off the paper in his hands.

He made this. In October there was a machine running in an empty room that had never done a dishonest thing because it had never occurred to it that there was another way to proceed, and it is now the fifteenth of November and that man has written a document that will consign somebody, and knows it, and has submitted it because he is constitutionally unable to withhold a finding.

It is the same operation as the fourteenth of February. The same man in the same kind of chair specifying a person and calling it a class.

The only difference between the two documents is that Tar's is honest.

And then the second thing, about four seconds behind the first, and it is not about February at all.

He knows what this man does. He has the return and he took it himself: put a thing in front of Tar that he can read to the bottom, and he reads it to the bottom, and then he acts on what it says, and there is no floor under that and no appeal from it. And what is now in front of him, in his own writing, on nine pages, is himself.

He is going to read it. Not tonight — tonight he is pleased with the design, and that is the worst single fact in the file. But he will, because he reads everything, and when he has read it he will go, and there is no operation in the world for stopping a man reading his own returns.

He tells him it's the best thing that has been written in this building, which is true. He says *don't go*, which he had not intended to say and which is not a thing he does, and which is not a request. It is a prediction with the tense wrong. Tar says he isn't going anywhere and does not know that he is lying.

Then he sits down at the desk and does not do anything for a while, which does not happen to him, and then he gets up because sitting has stopped being available, and he is sick in the downstairs lavatory and it is not the drink.

He stays there some time. He is forty years old, he has run this establishment four years, he has sent thirteen men into that annexe and read every report and slept, and he is sitting on the floor of his own lavatory and it will not stop. And what will not stop is not remorse — he'd know remorse, he's seen plenty, it's slow — it is the other thing, the one that arrives all at once when you have read your own returns and there is no error in them.

He came into an empty room and found something that had been running for years with nobody in it, and he has spent thirteen months in there, and it now writes papers like his.

And he has five months. He does not know it is five; he knows it is not many. He is going to spend them at that table twice a week and sometimes three, holding the whole of it, and there is not one thing to be done with the knowledge, because the only operation that would answer is to tell him, and telling him is the same as the reading, only faster.

He gets up about a quarter to two and puts it away.

The technique is that you do not argue with the thing, because arguing keeps it in the room, and you do not excuse it, because an excuse is an admission with a bow on it and it will come back for that. You take it and you put it where you do not go, and then you do not go there. It takes him under an hour and he does two hours on the animal returns afterwards and they are correct in the morning.

It does not come back. He has forty-one years and it does not come back, and there is one occasion in the whole of them — a guest room at a marine station in the Caribbean, the eleventh of March 1957, at four in the morning, an hour he has never in his life been awake at for that purpose — when it very nearly does, and he puts the light on and reads a co-housing paper until it is light.

—-

He drives him to the station himself on the thirtieth of April and does the ten miles without saying anything.

At Salisbury he pulls up short of the yard and gets the reason for the card, and then the other reason, which is *I found out what I would do if the work were good enough*, and he tells him that is the second thing he has said about himself in eighteen months and that he has been keeping count.

He has. Two. He could give the dates.

He says he isn't apologising, which is true and is the last useful thing he does. He offers a post in a year and is refused. And then he says *write to me*, and hears himself say it, and there is a version of him watching from about four feet away that cannot believe it.

*Not about the work. That is the whole of the request and I am perfectly aware of what it is.*

*No,* Tar says.

Nineteen months. One word.

He puts his hand out and it is taken, and he drives up the London road, and about two miles on he stops the car for a bit, and then goes on.

—-

He writes the card that afternoon and it is four lines and the fourth says *not to be approached again on the current programme; consider in eighteen months for anything with sea in it,* and he is right about that too.

He knows exactly what is in the way. He has known since about eleven o'clock on the fifteenth of November 1947 and there has not been an hour since when he did not, and that is the whole difference between him and every other man he has ever done this to. They wonder. He has never had the consolation of wondering about anything.

So he is not, on any of the nine occasions that follow, trying to get him. He'd like that on the record with the rest of it. He is a serious man and he does not run an operation nine times against a result he obtained himself at full cost.

What an establishment offer is, is a letter that will be answered.

He asked once, in a car outside Salisbury station, for the other kind, and was refused inside two seconds by a man who was entirely right to refuse him. And every spring for nineteen years he manufactures a reason to write that has a post in it, and a salary, and a programme, and it goes off, and something comes back with Tar's name at the bottom. Two sentences, mostly. Once one.

That is what he has. He built it himself out of the only material there was, and there is nobody alive he could explain it to who would not laugh.

The last is answered in five paragraphs.

He reads it twice in an office in Maryland. Five, from a man who wrote *health* on a card and made him ask twice for the rest: five paragraphs, entirely about the design, precise as anything he has ever been sent, containing nothing personal of any kind whatsoever, in a hand that has not altered in twelve years.

He does not offer again, and it is not because he is beaten. There is nowhere further to go. He asked for a letter in the April of 1948 and nineteen years later he was sent one, and it is the largest thing he has ever been given by anybody, and it is about a research programme, and it is the answer.

He puts it in the drawer. If anybody had been in the room they would have said he took it well.
