# Confounding

The costing he does in the first week of January and it comes out clean.

It is not a moral decision and he does not dress it as one. He goes through himself the way he goes through anybody, item by item, at the table in Hut 2 with the stove out, and the schedule is short because he is thirty and has arranged his life on the assumption that this audit would one day be required of him, without ever having said so.

There is his mother in Reading, who is seventy-one and knows nothing about anything he has done since 1939 and would not understand it if she were told. That is a handle and it is a small one and there is no operation available for reducing it further.

There is no property, no debt, no appointment he is attached to, nobody in the world whose name could be said to him across a desk.

And there is a notebook in the case under the bed with two lists in it.

He takes that one seriously and gives it eleven minutes. What he concludes is that a thing which has never been acted on and will never be acted on cannot be photographed; that there is no third party to it and never has been; that the second list is nine items about a man who does not know he is on it and will not be told; and that the material therefore has no value to anybody, because nothing can be got out of it and nothing can be threatened with it. He rules it not a handle. He is careful about it. He tests it twice.

The audit is correct. Every line of it is correct, and he does it the way he would do anybody's, and if it had come across his desk in another man's hand he would have passed it.

What he does not do, because there is no item on the schedule for it, is ask what a man in that condition will subsequently accept.

—-

He has, as it happens, a procedure.

He was at school between 1930 and 1935 in a house of forty boys, and in his second half two of them were the subject of an arrangement that everybody in the house knew about and that had no name in the house except the ones that were jokes. It went on for something over a year. It was dealt with in the end by the housemaster, who moved one of them to another house at half-term and produced a form of words for the change that was to do with the boy's mathematics, and who said, once, to the assembled house, in a voice like a man closing a window, that they would not be discussing it.

They did not discuss it. The whole apparatus worked exactly as designed. Reggie was fifteen and observed the entire operation from about six feet away and drew from it a conclusion he has never had cause to revise, which is that a full-sized English institution can operate a thing it has agreed not to have a word for, indefinitely, at no cost to itself.

He did not participate. Nobody asked him, and if anybody had asked him he would have said no, on the grounds that he had nothing to bring to it. He has never done anything of any kind with anybody at all, in thirty years, and he notes this occasionally in the way one notes a gap in a series: as a figure that is not surprising and not interesting and may or may not eventually mean something.

So he is not shocked, in the February, and the absence of the shock is not a moral position. It is a filing card, twelve years old, in good order, retrieved from where it has been sitting.

—-

The snow starts on the twenty-third of January and does not go for seven weeks.

The office in the house has the fire on one bar and the blackout curtains still up because nobody has taken them down, and by that stage of the winter the bottle is on the table before the papers are, which neither of them has remarked on.

They do four hours on the animal series and it is the best four hours of the month. Then Spilsbury caps the bottle, which he does not usually do, and sits back and says:

"I'm going to put a thing to you, and I want the whole of it heard before you answer, because if you answer the first sentence you'll answer it wrong and then we're both stuck with it."

"Go on."

"There is nobody else on this establishment I can do what we have just done with. There is nobody in London. I have sat here since October twice a week with a man who does not have to be waited for, and I should like to tell you what that is from this side of the table, and I find I can't put it, and I have never in my life been unable to put a thing I wanted."

Reggie says nothing.

"Right," Spilsbury says, and puts it.

He puts it flatly and without any decoration at all, in about four sentences, in the tone of a man raising a question of establishment transport: what he is suggesting, on what nights, on what terms, and that it stops the day either of them says so and that no reason will be required from whichever of them says it. He does not get up. He does not come round the table. It is the least ornamented speech Reggie has heard him make in fifteen months and he has heard him make a great many.

Then he says: "There's a second thing and it is not part of that, and I'd be obliged if you didn't join them up, because I've gone to some trouble to keep them apart. Something happened to you in the first week of December. I don't know what it was. I have a fair idea and I am not going to have it confirmed — I could get it out of you in this room in about forty minutes if I set myself at it, and we'd both know I'd done it, and I'm not going to. A thing I've had confirmed is a thing I've got. I've told you how I work and I told you because it was true, and the consequence of it being true is that I take some care about what I pick up." He puts the bottle an inch across the blotter and takes his hand off it. "The offer stands whichever way that goes. It stood before December. I'd have made it in the October if I'd thought you'd hear it."

"You'll want to think about it," Spilsbury says. "Take a fortnight."

Reggie goes to it the way he goes to everything.

The first objection is legal and he disposes of it in under a minute: he is standing in a building where the ordinary operation of that sort of thing has been suspended by instrument of the Crown for purposes he has typed out four hundred times, and there is no version of himself, at any age, who is going to be able to hold that particular line with a straight face in this particular corridor.

The second objection is that he does not want it, and this one he examines properly, because he suspects it of being the real one and it turns out not to be. What he finds when he goes down into it is that he does not want it and does not object to it, and that these are two different measures and he has been in the habit of treating them as one. He turns that over. It is a genuine finding and he is briefly interested in it in the way he would be interested in anything.

The third objection is the only one that matters, and it is the handle, and this is where he makes the error.

He makes it in about ninety seconds and it is beautifully constructed. A handle is a thing another party can take hold of. The party in question already holds his file, his undertaking, his employment and his access, and has held all four since the October; and a thing that is with the man who has all of it is not a new handle. It is the same handle. The count does not go up. It cannot go up, because there is nobody to tell who is not already the person you would be telling.

He checks it twice, as he checked the notebook, and it comes out the same both times, and it is wrong, and he will not see why it is wrong for six years.

He does not take the fortnight.

"Very well," he says.

The walk back up to Hut 2 is four minutes in ordinary weather. It takes eleven, because of the snow. He does the returns until two and in the morning he finds that he has done them correctly.

—-

It settles into the week the way everything at that establishment settles into the week, which is completely and without discussion.

Twice, mostly. Sometimes three. Always after the work and never instead of it — four hours of the thing itself first, every time, at the table with the fire on one bar, and neither of them ever proposes an alteration to that order, and the fact that the order is never altered is the fact Reggie holds on to and takes out and looks at on the walk back.

The effect is not the effect of the third glass. He is precise about this because there is no point in being anything else in his own head. It is longer, and it is cleaner, and it does not have to be paid for at six in the morning, and where the glass takes the edge off the arithmetic this stops it, in the way a hand stops a bell. He gets four hours afterwards and sometimes five. On the nights it happens he does not go down the list.

It is also, and he sets this down once, in February, and does not join it to anything, not unpleasant.

There are things he does not collect.

He never asks the man a question about himself. Not one, in fifteen months. He could — Spilsbury is candid by construction and would answer, and would rather enjoy answering, and Reggie is aware of both of those facts and declines to use them. His reasoning is that a thing you have been told is a thing you are keeping, and he has decided about keeping things. So he does not learn whether there was a wife, or is one, or what the man does in London, or where he learnt the trick with the card index, and he is asked about none of it in return.

And it is never mentioned in daylight. Not by either of them, not once, not by an inflection, not in fifteen months. Spilsbury is exactly the same man in the corridor at eleven in the morning that he was in October. He is the same man in front of Sister Renshaw and the same man on the telephone to London and the same man reading a bad return with his coat on. Reggie watches for the crack for about a month and then stops watching, because there is not going to be one, and the absence of it is the most frightening single fact he has about Alan Spilsbury: that the columns are genuinely separate, and that keeping them separate costs him nothing that can be measured.

It is the second thing on that establishment that is not called anything.

He does not put the two of them on the same sheet either.

—-

He writes the paper on S/11 in the last week of February by lamplight, in nine pages, and what it says is that the case is uninterpretable, and it is right, and Spilsbury reads it on the Thursday and comes and finds him in the hut.

He sits down on the end of the bed with his coat on and the paper in his hands.

Reggie takes the reading and there is nothing in it. Not a flicker. The man is where he was in October: gentle about it, entirely reasonable, wholly occupied with nine pages of good work, and there is no second thing anywhere in the room and there is not going to be.

"He goes to the top of it," Spilsbury says, and makes the case, and the case is correct, and Reggie sits with his hands on his knees and goes through it looking for the fault and there is no fault.

"Yes," he says.

"You'll be in for it. I want you in for it."

"Yes."

"Fourth of March. Bring the schedule down the day before and do the whole thirty-one; I want it complete and I want it in your hand." At the door he stops. "That's Tuesday. Nothing else on the Tuesday."

Which is the nearest thing to a reference either of them ever makes, and it is not one, because it is a scheduling remark.

—-

Alderton asks him on the Monday, sideways, with the sister's clock apart on the counterpane.

"Would you have it? If it was you."

"Of the thirteen before you," Reggie says, "nine are better than they were."

He does the thirty-one items that evening and they take fifty minutes and he writes it all in his own hand.

Then he goes down the hill.

It is not one of the nights. He has not been asked and there is no arrangement covering the Monday, and it is a quarter past ten and the snow has gone to slush and he goes down anyway, and stands at the door of the house, and Spilsbury opens it and looks at him for about two seconds and stands back and lets him in.

Nothing whatever is said about it. Not then and not afterwards and not at any subsequent point in either of their lives.

He is not able to state what it was for. That is the part he takes away. He has spent eleven years being able to state the purpose of any operation he has undertaken within about four seconds of being asked, and he goes up the hill at half past one with the purpose of this one entirely unavailable to him, and he notes the failure in the morning, crossing the yard to the annexe, and does not have time to do anything with it.

The fourth of March is a Tuesday and the road is still bad.

It is twenty-three minutes. He has the figure and he keeps it and it is the only thing he takes out of that room.

—-

They have him in the annexe until the end of the month and then he comes back on to the ward, and Reggie goes up on the Thursday with the schedule, and Alderton looks well, and does the thirty-one items pleasantly, and is better than he has ever been on item nineteen.

The tobacco tin comes out for the watch and goes back into the locker.

At half past six the tea goes on to Dando's locker in bed eleven and Alderton looks at it the way a man looks at a thing on a shelf in a shop, and then looks away, and at a quarter to eight Reggie takes it over himself and does it badly.

He gets the notebook out that night and goes down the list, and the absences are survivable and the ticks are not, and he gets as far as the eighth item and stops and goes outside without a coat and is sick.

Then he goes down the hill.

Spilsbury opens the door and looks at him and does not stand back this time. He takes him through into the office and puts him in the chair by the fire and goes away and comes back with a plate: bread, and the end of something, and a cup of tea he has made himself, which is not a thing that has happened before.

"Eat that."

"I don't want it."

"No." He sits down at the desk and turns a lamp round so it is not in Reggie's face, and picks up a pen, and does not use it. "You've been on that ward four days out of five this week. Your Wednesday returns are in a different hand from your Monday ones. Not worse — different. Smaller and the line goes up at the end. I've been reading your writing for eighteen months. Give me a year of it with the dates off and I'd sort it into days of the week and miss four."

Reggie sits with the plate on his knee.

"How long have you known," he says.

"I don't know anything." Spilsbury says it without any emphasis at all. "I've told you twice now and it's the truth and it's the only thing I've got that's any use to you. A thing I know is a thing I've got. I have taken care not to get this one and it has cost me something to take the care, because it would have been extremely easy and I am not by nature a man who leaves a good instrument in the drawer, and I have left this one in the drawer for fifteen months." He puts the pen down. "What I have is a handwriting series, and a man who sits an hour and a quarter on a ward with his folder shut. That's all. That would not survive a board and it would not survive you, and I'm aware of the difference between those two and so are you."

"That isn't an answer."

"No."

Reggie looks at the fire.

"Why was he next," he says.

"Because he could give an account afterwards. You wrote the paper that proved it and I have never had that on a ward before and I am not likely to get it again."

"Yes."

"That's the reason and it's a good one and it would stand in front of anybody."

"It is not the whole of it."

Spilsbury picks the pen up and puts it down again, which is a thing Reggie has not seen him do.

"No," he says. "But I don't answer that one. Not to a board, not to you, and not to myself at four in the morning. That last one's your habit and it has never been mine. I have not once in my life put a question of that sort to myself at any hour and I don't propose to start. Eat the bread, Reggie."

He eats the bread.

What he takes up the hill is a residual.

The reason he has been given is a good reason and it is not a sufficient one, and he establishes that on the Saturday, in the hut, with the ward records, in about forty minutes. The criterion is the capacity to give an account afterwards. He goes down the eleven and there are three men who meet it, two of them comfortably, and one of those two has been in one place or another since 1944 and has no wife and no visitors and nobody in the world who would ask a question about him afterwards, which on every operational ground there is makes him the better subject and not the worse.

So the stated reason is a reason and is not the reason. He has proved that much and he cannot get any further, because proving that a named variable does not account for an effect is not the same operation as producing the one that does.

He tries the appetite, which is the only category he has ever had for Alan Spilsbury that was any use, and it does not close it either: a man in the grip of a design takes whichever subject the design will fit and does not mind which, and this one minded.

He puts it where he keeps the things that are not currently determinable, and it stays there, and it is never any nearer.

He is looking for a variable. It sits across a table from him twice a week and there is no item on any schedule of his that would take a reading off it.

—-

By the summer he is not eating properly and nobody notices, because it is 1947 and the whole country is thin.

Except that there is a plate in the office twice a week, before the work, put down without a word and not remarked on afterwards, and it is there in the June and it is there in the August and it is there on nights when there is nothing else. It is never mentioned. It is never made into anything. It is simply on the table when he comes in, the way the bottle is, and if he leaves half of it the half is not commented on and the plate is the same size the following week.

The shaking starts in the August. It is worst about an hour after he stops working and it goes off if he eats, which he knows, and he takes it standing in the passage by the dispensary with his fingers on his own wrist and times it, because timing it is the only operation available.

And the machinery has never been better. He is producing more than the establishment can use. In the July he sees the thing in the twenty-two ward records that three people have been looking at for a year, crossing the yard, complete, and has to stand still in the sun.

The MO takes his pulse in the September, says a good deal that is entirely correct, offers him something to take at nights, and is refused.

"You're thirty."

"I'm aware of it."

"You're the colour of a candle and I'd not put you on a horse. Take three days."

It is a fortnight before Reggie works out that the MO was sent. It is not a difficult finding: the man had the ration figures, and the ration figures are not the MO's to have.

He sits with that for a while, in the hut, on a Sunday.

What he arrives at is that he is being maintained. That is the correct description and he holds himself to it. He is a piece of apparatus in a building that cannot replace him inside two years, and he is being kept fed and kept running and kept off the fourth glass by a man who has never in his life left a good instrument in the drawer, and it is being done well, and it is being done for the reason it looks as though it is being done for.

He examines whether this constitutes ill-usage and finds that it does not, on the grounds that it is precisely what he would do, and has done, with equipment he could not get another of.

And then, some way past the point at which the point has been made, he finds that this is not the whole of it. He cannot get any further than that. He sits at the table with his hands flat on it for a period he does not time, and the thing does not resolve into anything he can put in a sentence, and there is no item on any schedule of his for it, and eventually he gets the returns out and does four hours and it goes away.

—-

The follow-up paper he writes in the November and it is the best piece of work he does in that building or anywhere for fifteen years.

The design requires a subject. He specifies the subject in section four, in eleven lines, between about ten o'clock and half past eleven at night on the fourteenth, straight off, with no difficulty of any kind, and he is pleased with them in the specific way one is pleased when a hard thing has come out clean, and he sits back and reads them through with the pen still in his hand.

They are a description of a person.

Not of a class. There are two men on that ward who fit them and he has known which two since the fourth line, and he knew it at the time, in the sense that it was there, and he went on writing, and the writing was better because of it.

He takes it down the following night, because the alternative is to be a man who withholds a finding.

Spilsbury reads it standing up, all nine pages, and takes his time, and Reggie stands by the fire and watches him do it and can see the exact line at which he arrives at section four, because the man stops turning the page back and forth and goes very slightly still.

He finishes it. He squares it up on the table.

"That is the best thing that has been written in this building," he says, "and I include everything I have ever written myself, and I want you to know that I know what it cost, which is a thing I generally don't say because it's cheap and it doesn't help."

"It didn't cost anything. It came out in ninety minutes."

"Yes," Spilsbury says. "That's what I mean."

He stands with his hand on the paper for a moment.

"Do you know what I've got here," he says. "I have got a man who will write me that, and hand it to me, and not put a foot wrong in the doing of it, and walk back up that hill. I could run an establishment like this for twenty years on one of you. There has only ever been one of you and I've read four thousand files."

And then he says: "Don't go."

Reggie looks at him.

"I'm not going anywhere," he says.

"No," Spilsbury says, and puts the paper in the drawer. "Not yet."

—-

He writes it on Tuesday the sixth of April 1948 and it is two sentences.

There is no scene. He is careful about that and he is careful about it on grounds he could not have stated, and the arrangement ends on the same day and is not mentioned on that day or on any of the twenty-four that follow, and neither of them says a single word about it, and this is not a difficulty for either of them, and Reggie is unable afterwards to decide which of those two facts is the worse.

Spilsbury drives him to the station himself on the thirtieth and does the whole ten miles without saying anything at all.

At Salisbury he pulls up short of the yard and puts the brake on.

"Give me a reason I can put on the card."

"Health."

"I'll put health." He does not write anything. "Now give me the other one."

Reggie looks out of the windscreen at the wall of the goods yard for some time.

"I found out what I would do," he says, "if the work were good enough. It is a fact about me. It is not about you and it is not about the programme, and I would be obliged if you did not construct anything out of it."

"No." And then: "That is the second thing you have said to me in eighteen months that was about you. I have been keeping count."

"You would be."

"I would." He puts both hands on the wheel and looks at them. "I wrote a card in September of '46 that said, *do not put him on anything he can see the end of.* I have thought about that a good deal since about the middle of March last year, and I want it understood that I read my own instruction, and I put you on it, and I would do it again, because you were the best instrument I had and I have never in my life been able to leave a good instrument in the drawer. I am not apologising. I don't do that and you would not believe it."

"No."

"Come back in a year. Not to this. I'll have something else by then and you'll want it."

"No," Reggie says.

It is the first time. There will be nine more of them, and the last of them will be five paragraphs long and will be written in an office four thousand miles away with the sea outside the window, and neither man in this car has any means whatever of knowing that.

Spilsbury sits for a moment with his hands on the wheel.

"Write to me," he says.

Reggie does not answer.

"Not about the work. That is the whole of the request and I am perfectly aware of what it is and of the fact that I am not entitled to make it, and I have made it, and I would take it as a courtesy if you did not do me the kindness of pretending you did not hear it."

"No," Reggie says.

"No," Spilsbury says. "Well."

He puts out his hand and Reggie takes it, and then he gets the case out of the back and stands on the pavement and watches the car go up the London road, and finds that he would like to sit down, and finds that there is nowhere to sit down, and picks the case up and goes in and gets the twelve-forty.

Of the nine refusals that follow, over nineteen years, not one of them costs him anything at all. He notices this in about 1961, in an office, in the middle of dictating one of them, and does not stop dictating.

—-

In the inside pocket of his coat there is one sheet of establishment paper, folded in four, with nine items on it in his own hand and a rule under the ninth.

He puts it in the back of the notebook with the other one, on the train, somewhere before Andover.

Then he sits with the notebook open on his knee and does the thing he has come on this train to do, which is to establish whether there is a third.

There is not.

He goes through it properly, because a negative result is a result and he does not take them on impression. Fifteen months. Twice a week and sometimes three, and four hours of the best work of his life before each of them, and a plate on a table. There is not one item. There is nothing that man ever did that nobody had asked him to do; there is nothing Reggie noticed at the end of a day and wrote into the back of a working notebook without a heading and in the same hand as everything else. There is no first item, and therefore no list, and therefore no finding, and the whole of it comes to nothing at all.

Two is still a series. Three would be an established finding, and there is not going to be a third, and he settles that between Andover and Basingstoke with the fields going past, in about the time it takes to settle a thing that has already been decided, and he holds it for six years.

He is not able to establish, on that journey or on any subsequent one, whether the empty column is the best thing he knows about himself or the worst. He files it as not currently determinable. It is one of the four or five items he takes out and looks at, without warning and without occasion, for the remainder of his life, and he never does get it decided, and he is not, in the end, sure that it was ever the kind of question that has an answer at the bottom of it.
