# Historical Control

Spilsbury has been on the station five days and has been, throughout, extremely pleasant.

That is the part nobody who has not met the type will ever credit. He shook hands. He admired the pens and asked intelligent questions about the drainage. He was good with the Colonel and better with Mrs Bevan, and he told John his drawings were the best working record he had seen at any station in the hemisphere, which was true, and John has been insufferable about it for four days and will be for four more, and does not know and will never know what he did.

He is forty-seven and has not altered in any particular that Reggie can measure. Reggie has been measuring since the launch came in on the Saturday, because the man has been six years out of a room with him and there ought to be something, and there is nothing. It is the pleasantness of the corridor at eleven in the morning in the February of 1947, unamended, running at the same rate on the same fuel.

The Navy put the new animal ashore on Thursday, and Spilsbury had him in the small pen by the afternoon, and he has not eaten since.

---

What Reggie is afraid of is not in the food logs and he had it worked out on the pier while the man was still shaking hands.

Every other person on this station is reading a chief of station who is tired. One of them holds a complete set of observations, taken twice a week and sometimes three, over fifteen months, at close range, by the best assessor in England, of what Reggie Tar is like when there is a person he would be sorry to see put in a room.

He knows the set was taken. That is the difference between this week and any week he has had since 1948. He was told in the September of 1947, in an office, with a plate on his knee, by a man who said in the same quarter of an hour that he did not know anything and had forty seconds earlier read the month off two pages of handwriting — and Reggie let it go, and has let it go for seven years, and has never once been able to say why he let it go.

It was not written down. He is fairly certain it was not written down, and that is not the difficulty. It is not a document. It is a calibration, in the head of a man who reads people for the Crown, and there is no operation in the world for taking it off him.

And the difficulty this week is that he is not in the condition the calibration was taken in.

He has been in a much larger one since the night of the twenty-eighth of August, and the twenty-ninth did not improve it. Ten days. He can date it to the hour and he has the figure and the figure is a third of an interval he cannot account for, which he has never in his life produced before, and there is no reason on earth to suppose it does not show.

---

He does not go near Pascual for five days.

There is a good reason available and he uses it: the man has the new animal and the new animal will not feed, and there is nothing to be discussed until it does. That is true. It is also a lie of the kind he has spent eight years not needing to tell, and he told it to himself on the Sunday morning and has been running it since.

On the Monday they are within about ten feet of each other at the pens for two minutes with Jean present, and he conducts himself perfectly, and when he gets back to the office he finds he has to sit down for a moment, which has not happened.

And he knows — this is the part he cannot put anywhere — that a man who arranges not to be in a room with somebody for five days has told the whole of it to anybody watching, and that there is precisely one person on this station who watches.

---

On the second afternoon Spilsbury sat down opposite him with the food logs and the intake sheets and Charles's log, and the station returns underneath.

He took the returns first, which was not the order Reggie had prepared for.

He read two pages, in about the time it takes to read two pages, and then held the second one up a little, not far, in the way of a man checking a plate against the light.

"Your hand's altered."

"Has it."

"Smaller. Pressure's changed. And the long stroke on the double-t has gone short, which you have done since I have known you and which I have never seen on another man's paper in my life." He put the sheet down, face up, and squared it, and left his hand flat on it. "Recent. Inside the fortnight."

"I have had a bad fortnight. I lost a research student on the twenty-ninth."

"You did," Spilsbury said, and moved his hand off the sheet, and took up Charles's log.

Then he went through the lot at a rate that would have been a pleasure to watch in any other circumstance, and after about forty minutes he stopped with one finger on a column and asked, almost idly, "Where did your man go, anyway, Tar?"

"I don't know," Reggie said, and he was speaking the truth, and he saw Spilsbury register that he was speaking the truth.

"He wrote *no change* for four months on an animal that put on nine pounds."

"Yes."

"Every week. In the same hand. *No change.*"

"Yes."

"That's either a very stupid man or a very interesting one, Tar, and I've read his transfer papers and he's not stupid."

And this is where Reggie found out what he was going to do, since he had not known in advance and had refused to rehearse it.

"He kept a poor log," Reggie said, "and I did not check it."

Spilsbury looked at him.

"For four months. I had two specimens and four postgraduates and I was doing the acoustic work myself, and I read the food sheets, because the food sheets had figures in them, and I did not read the behavioural log, because it had nothing in it. That is the whole of it. If you want it in writing you may have it in writing."

There was a pause and he had not allowed for its length.

Four was what the sentence wanted and he had built it to take four. At six he understood that he had handed it to the one man in the hemisphere with a documented series on the exact question of whether Reggie Tar reads what is in front of him, and that he had known that when he built it and built it anyway, because it was the only lever in the room and it was in his hand.

At eight, Spilsbury's face changed.

"Well," he said. "Well, well." And then, kindly: "You have got slack, old man. It happens. It happens particularly to the clever ones, if you want my view — you stop looking at the dull half because you've decided in advance that it's dull." He squared the sheets and handed them back. "I shall have to say so."

"I imagine you will."

"You want a chief of station and a scientist and you've been trying to be both, and it's cost you an animal and a research student and it very nearly cost you a programme." He stood up. "Get the co-housing trial done and get me a paper out of it and we shall say no more about the rest."

At the door he stopped, with his hand on it, and did not turn round.

"Fourth of December, nineteen forty-six," he said.

Then he went out.

He was gone a good while before Reggie's hands began, and Reggie sat in the room with them flat on the table until they stopped, which took rather longer than a quarter of an hour.

---

It worked. That is the thing he keeps arriving back at, and he has been arriving back at it since Tuesday.

A slack man is of no use to anybody. There is nothing in a slack man to be got at, nothing to hold over him, no reason to put him in a room with a paper. Spilsbury came here to find out whether there was a phenomenon and whether there was a man who understood it, and he is going away with a tired administrator who does not read his own logs, and he will write that down, and the writing-down is the safest thing that has ever happened to Reggie Tar.

He was, for about two hours that afternoon, extremely pleased with himself.

Then there is the other thing, and the other thing has no bottom to it.

He was given a date. He was given nothing else — no clause, no verb, no request, no terms — and he is not going to be able to produce it in any forum, because a man who says four words at a door and leaves has said nothing at all, and Reggie knows precisely where that technique was explained to him and by whom and in what month.

What it means is not in dispute. It means: *I have seen your hand do this once before. I know what it was the last time. I have the date to the day and I have had it for eight years and I have never used it, and I am not using it now, and you will understand from this that I could.*

And underneath that, and worse, and also not in dispute: *there is somebody on this station.*

He is not able to establish anything beyond that and he has been at it since Thursday morning.

He cannot establish why the lie was taken. It was aimed at a man who had spent forty minutes that afternoon proving he could date an alteration in Reggie's handwriting inside a fortnight, and who therefore knows to a certainty that Reggie Tar does not fail to read things, and who accepted without a second question that its author had gone four months without opening a log. He has run it out four times and there is no version of Alan Spilsbury who believed it.

He cannot establish what was done with the four days between the returns and the door. He knows what he would have done with them.

He cannot establish whether he has been let off, or kept, or merely postponed, and the difference matters and he has no instrument for it.

And he cannot establish — this is the one he keeps coming back to at four in the morning, which is an hour he has been at rather a lot lately — what the man came away with about the fifty minutes he spent alone with Pascual on the Wednesday, and whether Reggie's five days of not being in a room with Pascual were the thing that told him, or whether he had it off two pages of handwriting on the Tuesday before either of them said a word, and was simply confirming it afterwards, in the ordinary way, the way you confirm anything.

He puts it away at about two o'clock on the Thursday and gets on with the returns, and it comes back, and does not resolve, and has not resolved by the Saturday.

The launch goes out on the Saturday at twenty past four and Spilsbury shakes his hand on the pier and says he'll want that paper by the spring.
