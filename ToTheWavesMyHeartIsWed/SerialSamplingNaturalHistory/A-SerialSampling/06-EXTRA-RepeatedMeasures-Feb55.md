# Repeated Measures

Delta began to eat on the eleventh of November, four days after they put Gamma in with her,
and has eaten every day since.

It is the best result the station has ever had. Spilsbury has written twice. The Colonel has
been to look at the pen on three separate occasions in a good hat, and Mrs Bevan has been
down every week since it started and stands at the rail for exactly eleven minutes and says
nothing to anybody, and Reggie has stopped trying to work out what she is doing there.

He has the food figures, the weight curve, the surfacing intervals, the whole of it, and it
is unimpeachable and it will publish, and the paper will say that co-housing conspecifics
produces a measurable improvement in intake and condition in the receiving animal, and that
is a true sentence and it is not what happened.

What happened is that on the seventh of November Delta came off the bottom for the first
time in four months and went to the other animal and put her hand flat on his face, and
stayed there, and Reggie was standing at the rail at the time with a stopwatch in his hand,
and did not start it.

He has been at that rail most nights since.

---

The typewriter is the difficulty.

He can take the data. Taking data is a mechanical operation and he could do it in his sleep
and very nearly does. It is the writing-up that cannot be borne, because the writing-up
requires him to construct, sentence by sentence, an account of a mechanism, and he knows the
mechanism, and every paragraph is built to route round the middle of it, and doing that for
two hours makes the whole apparatus start up and run in the wrong direction, and by ten
o'clock he is not thinking about Delta at all.

Whisky is no use on a working night. Whisky costs him the following morning and he has
mornings to get through, and he found in December that the dose was climbing, which is a
figure he recorded once and has not looked at again.

The other thing does not cost him the morning. It costs him nothing at all that he has been
able to measure.

So he has, since about the middle of December, been trying to establish under what conditions
it can be relied upon.

He has been unable to do so.

That is the whole of the trouble and he has laid it out properly and it will not come right.
On perhaps six occasions the effect has been complete: an hour or so of nothing usable
afterwards, and then sleep, and nothing whatever in the morning. On five others it has not
worked at all — he has been present throughout, entirely himself, taking the reading,
watching the man's face in the half-dark and cataloguing it; pleasant, in its way, and quite
useless. And he cannot find the variable. He has tried the hour. He has tried the day of the
week. He has tried going up at once after the typewriter and going up after a walk. He has
tried saying nothing at all when he arrives and he has tried the opposite.

Chiefly he has tried the opposite. He has found — this is the nearest thing to a finding he
has — that the effect is more reliable when he has been, in the phrase he uses in his own
head and has never once examined, *making a fuss of him* during the preceding day.

He has been varying that. Systematically. Since about the tenth of January.

---

Tonight the typewriter has been going since eight and it has been a bad one, and at half
past ten he goes up.

Pascual opens the door with his glasses pushed up into his hair and a journal in one hand
and looks at him, and puts the journal down.

It is very good. It is always very good; that is not a thing Reggie has ever pretended
about. There is a stretch near the beginning where Pascual takes his time in a way that is
frankly unreasonable and Reggie has to put his forehead against the man's shoulder and
simply wait, and there is another stretch afterwards where he is not, by any standard he
would accept in a laboratory, present.

But he is present at the beginning and he is present at the end, and somewhere in the middle
he becomes aware that he has been present throughout, and that this is the sixth failure,
and that if he can only establish what is different tonight he will have it.

So he starts counting.

He does it the way he has done it every time: his own pulse, against the thing he can hear,
which is the generator, because the generator is fifty cycles and reliable and a man who has
counted it for four years can count it in his head at any hour. It gives him a rate and a
duration and something to lay one night against another.

He is at ninety-odd and rising when Pascual — who has his hand round Reggie's wrist, and has
had it there for some while, and it had not occurred to Reggie to think about why — goes
entirely still.

There is a silence of perhaps three seconds.

Then Pascual takes his hand away and sits back on his heels and says, in a voice Reggie has
not heard from him before, "What are you counting."

---

"Get up," Pascual says. "Get up and put your shirt on. I am not doing this with you like
that."

He gets up. He puts his shirt on. His hands are not working properly and he has to do the
buttons twice.

"How long."

"I do not know what you —"

"*How long.*"

"Since the middle of December," Reggie says.

Pascual sits down on the chair and puts both hands over his face for a moment, and then takes
them away, and when he speaks he is perfectly level, and that is worse than any other
possible thing.

"I want to tell you what I have got," he says. "You will appreciate it. I have kept a record
too."

"Pascual —"

"Eleven weeks. It is always a night the typewriter has been going, and never a night it has
not, and there are no exceptions, and I looked. Three times in January you came in and did
not say one word to me, not one, and left before four. You have not stayed for breakfast
since November and you stayed for breakfast every time before that. You have stopped asking
me about Delta and you used to ask me every day and I liked it. On the fifteenth of January
you were — " and here his voice does something, and he stops, and starts again. "On the
fifteenth of January you were kind to me all day. In front of Jean. You brought me the
Aberdeen offprint because you thought I would want it, and I did want it, and I was very
happy, and then that night it did not work, whatever it is, and the next day you were exactly
as you were in October. And I sat with that for four days and I would not let myself make
anything of it, because I do not build a thing out of four observations."

"That is —"

"I have nineteen," Pascual says. "You had nine and a control. I have nineteen and no control,
because I am the control, and I have been the control since December, and you have been
running conditions on me."

---

The room is quite still.

Reggie hears himself say, "I have not lied to you."

"No," Pascual agrees. "That is true. That is entirely true and it is the worst thing you have
ever said to me."

And Reggie opens his mouth to explain the design — to explain that the object was never the
man, that the object was a state, that the state was the only thing standing between him and
a pen fifty yards away with two animals in it doing a thing he cannot write down, that he was
not using him, he was using an *effect* —

and gets three words into it, and stops, because he has heard the shape of it.

*I would not be the handle.*

He sits down on the end of the bed rather suddenly.

He has been examined by a great many people in his life and has always known, from the inside,
what was actually there to find, and there has never been anything to find, and he has been
proud of it in a way he would not have owned to. And in eleven weeks, with no coercion of any
kind, with nobody putting a paper in front of him, on his own initiative and in his own
handwriting, he has taken the only person who has ever been in the room with him and made him
into a mechanism, and worked out what to feed him to get the result, and varied it to be sure.

Not for the state. For himself. Because it was there and it was quiet and it saved him from
having to know a thing he already knows.

He says, "Oh."

And then, because it appears to be the only piece of information left in his possession that
is worth anything, "I did not know I was doing that."

"No," Pascual says. "I do not think you did."

---

He is not able to give a full account of the next half-hour.

He is aware of shaking, badly, in a way he has not done since Salisbury and has no
explanation for; of being extremely cold; of the fact that at some point he has been got round
the shoulders and hauled in and is being held there hard, with a hand at the back of his neck,
by a man he has spent eleven weeks running conditions on and who has every possible reason not
to be doing it.

He tries once to say something about that and is told, briefly and in Spanish, to be quiet.

Later, when he has stopped, Pascual says into the top of his head: "You are not a spy, Reggie.
You are a scientist. You did to me the exact thing you do to everything, which is the only
thing anybody ever taught you to do with something you want. That is not an excuse. It is a
diagnosis, and you can have it, and I want it understood that a diagnosis is not a
forgiveness."

"No."

"You will not do it again."

"No."

"And when the typewriter has been going and you cannot stop, you will not come up here for a
dose. You will come up here and say *the typewriter has been going and I cannot stop,* which
is nine words, and I have counted them for you, and then we will see."

Reggie says, "That is not a procedure."

"No," Pascual says. "It is not. You will have to manage."

---

They go down to the pen at about two.

It is the first time either of them has said it out loud — that he has been going down alone,
most nights, since November, and standing at that rail in the dark for an hour and coming
back and not mentioning it. Pascual takes this in without comment and puts on his boots.

The night watch is not a bad idea on its own merits. Nobody has ever taken observations on
these animals after midnight because there has never been anybody willing to do it, and Reggie
says this on the way down, twice, and Pascual lets him have it.

The tide is out and the pen is very quiet. Gamma is up at the far end. Delta is at the near end
against the wall, and after about ten minutes she comes up and puts her face at the rail below
them, which she has never done for anybody, and looks at the two of them for some time.

Reggie has the stopwatch. He does not start it.

Pascual's hand comes onto his back, flat and warm between the shoulder blades, and stays there,
which it can, because it is two in the morning and there is nothing in the world down here but
concrete and water.

And after a while Reggie says, to the pen, in an ordinary voice:

"He used to hum. Cholmondeley. Down here at night, when he thought there was nobody about.
Always the same eight bars and never any more of it, and I never found out what it was, and I
could not tell you now whether I never asked him because it did not matter or because it did."

He does not look round.

Beside him Pascual does not say anything at all, and does not take his hand away, and it is
some considerable time before either of them goes up.
