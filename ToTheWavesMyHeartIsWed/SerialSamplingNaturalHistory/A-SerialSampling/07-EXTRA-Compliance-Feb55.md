# Compliance

He has his hand round the man's wrist because he likes it there.

That is the whole of the reason and it is worth putting down plainly, because of what comes
after. He is a doctor and has been one for thirty years and there is a place on a wrist where
a body tells you the truth about itself whether or not the head has agreed to it, and he found
in December that he could keep two fingers there and have the entire account: the going-fast,
the place where it steadies, the long slow coming-down afterwards. It has become his favourite
thing in the world. He has never told anybody. He would not know how to say it without
sounding like a lunatic.

So he is holding a wrist, in the dark, at some point in a night that has been going extremely
well, and under his fingers the rate does something that is not physiological.

It hitches. Twice. In a pattern.

And Pascual, who has taken a hundred thousand pulses and could do it in his sleep, understands
before he permits himself to understand it that the man is counting.

---

The rest of that night he will not set out. He said what he said and he meant all of it.

But the thing he was actually injured by he does not manage to say, and he turns it over
afterwards for weeks.

It is not the bed. He is fifty-three years old, he has been used before, and he is not a girl;
if Reggie Tar wanted to come up here on Thursdays for an hour of not being able to think, he
could have had it, he could have had it with a signed statement and no questions asked, and
Pascual would have thought it a sad way to live and would not have thought it an injury.

It is the fifteenth of January.

Because on the fifteenth of January the man walked across the yard in front of Jean with the
Aberdeen offprint under his arm, and put it on the bench, and said, *I thought you would want
this,* and did not stay to be thanked. And Pascual carried that about for the rest of the day
like a boy. He was *happy*. He caught himself whistling at the centrifuge.

And it was a condition. It was Tuesday's condition, run to see what Tuesday night would do,
and Tuesday night did nothing, and so it was not repeated.

That is the injury. He was given the one entirely good day he has had in a year and it was an
instrument reading. And the worst of it, which he does not say to anybody and will be carrying
for a long while, is this: he cannot get the day back. It is not that it has been spoiled going
forward. It has been spoiled backwards. He cannot now remember standing in the yard with the
offprint without also knowing what it was, and there is no operation that separates them.

---

He holds him for half an hour on the floor of his own room and does not manage to stay angry,
and is furious about that as well.

The shaking is not distress. He knows distress; distress has a shape and comes down inside
twenty minutes. This is the other thing, which he has seen twice in his life, in men who have
had something taken out from under them that they had been standing on without ever knowing
they were standing on it. Both of the others were in a war. It takes as long as it takes.

He says the diagnosis out loud because it is true and because it will be believed — nothing
sentimental is ever believed by this man and everything clinical is — and he says the rest of
it too, that a diagnosis is not a forgiveness, because it is not, and because he is not going
to be lied to in his own room by himself.

And then he sets the condition.

He has about four seconds to design it, and he designs it well, and he knows exactly what he is
doing, and it is the only piece of cleverness he is proud of in the entire business.

*Nine words. I have counted them for you.*

Because there is no possible way for Reggie Tar to execute a sentence like that mechanically.
He cannot run it as a protocol; there is nothing to calibrate; there is no dose. He has to say
the words, aloud, sober, to a man's face, and mean them, and then stand there. And by giving
him a number Pascual has put the whole thing into the one language he trusts, which is the
nearest thing to a kindness available at two o'clock in the morning.

*That is not a procedure.*

*No. You will have to manage.*

---

He does not come.

Six weeks.

---

They go down to the pen every night. That continues, because it was a good idea on its own
merits and because neither of them is going to be the one who stops it, and so from the middle
of February Pascual spends an hour of every night standing three feet from him at a rail in the
dark, taking observations on two animals, and going up.

It is exquisite. He would not have believed it could be done to a man of his age.

Reggie is scrupulous. That is the word for it. He is courteous in the yard, he is exact about
the data, he asks after Delta every single day — every day, without fail, having been told in
February that he had stopped — and he does not touch him and does not come up and does not say
the nine words.

And Pascual works out, about three weeks in, standing at the rail with his hands in his pockets,
what is actually going on: the man is not sulking and is not punishing him. He has been given a
rule and cannot establish the point at which he is permitted to invoke it. He is waiting to be
certain that when he says it, it will not be a manoeuvre. He will wait for ever on those terms,
because certainty of that kind is not available to anybody.

Pascual understands this completely and does not go to him.

He wants to. He is at the foot of the stairs twice in March and turns round in the corridor both
times. Because if he fetches him, then whatever happens next is a thing Pascual arranged, and he
will spend the rest of his life not knowing whether it would have happened otherwise; and because
he has just spent eleven weeks inside somebody else's design and he is damned if he will start
running one of his own; and because — this is the real one, and he does not care for it — he told
the man that a diagnosis is not a forgiveness, and he is not certain, if he goes up those stairs,
which of the two he would be carrying.

So he waits. Six weeks. He does not think much of it as a method. It is simply the only thing
available that is not another experiment.

---

The twelfth of April. Late. He has been asleep and is not now, because there is a knock.

Reggie is in the corridor with his hands at his sides.

He looks bad. He has been at the typewriter — Pascual can hear from the yard that it stopped
about twenty minutes ago — and Spilsbury's letter came on Tuesday saying the paper is first-class
and asking two questions about the eleventh of November, and Pascual has watched him carry that
letter about for four days.

He gets about half the sentence out.

"The typewriter has been going," he says, "and I —"

And then he stops, and stands there, and cannot do the rest of it. Pascual can see it costing him,
physically, in the jaw. The hands have gone flat to the sides in the way they do when he is
standing to attention in front of the Colonel. He is thirty-five years old and he cannot say *I
cannot stop* to a man he has been to bed with eleven times.

Pascual stands back from the door.

"Six of nine," he says. "Come in."

---

What happens after is not what he expected and he is not, at first, certain that it is better.

He gets him sitting down, and gets the letter out of him, and the two questions about the eleventh
of November, which are good questions and are the questions of a man who intends to go on asking.
And then he makes him say the rest: that Delta came off the bottom for an animal she had never met
and put her hand flat on his face, and that there is not one word of it in the paper and there
never will be, and that somewhere in the Gulf, if the arithmetic holds, there is a thing that
explains it and a man who could explain it, and that Reggie will publish this and be thanked for
it and it will be, in the sense that matters, a lie he tells four times a year for the rest of his
working life.

It takes about an hour and it is not pleasant, and at the end of it Reggie says, "That has not
helped at all."

"No," Pascual says. "It does not. Not the first six or seven times."

And then he takes him to bed, and the difference is immediately obvious and Pascual is not
prepared for it.

The man's eyes are open.

That is all it is. He does not shut them. Whatever he was doing before — going wherever it was he
went, that far-off place Pascual could not follow, and was frightened by, and then was not — he
does not go. He stays. He is present for every part of it, in the room, looking up, and it is
plainly a great deal harder for him than the other way was, and Pascual has to stop twice, not
because anything is wrong, but because he finds he cannot be looked at like that continuously and
carry on.

Afterwards Reggie lies with his forearm across his face for a long time, which is new, and says at
last, from underneath it, "That was worse."

"Yes."

"Considerably worse."

"Yes," Pascual says. "Go to sleep."

He does. Not for hours; but he does, at about four, with a hand shut round Pascual's wrist — his
wrist, the other way about — and Pascual lies awake in the dark being held on to by a man who has
never in his life held on to anything.

---

In May, Reggie comes across the yard with something in his hand.

It is the Copenhagen number, which Pascual has been trying to get since January and has twice
complained about within his hearing.

And Pascual takes it, and says thank you, and turns it over, and thinks — before he can stop
himself, in the half-second before anything else — *what is this a condition for.*

There is nothing. Of course there is nothing. He knew it before the thought had finished.

But he had it. And he is going to have it again, probably for years, possibly always. The man put
a hand into that part of him and it does not appear to close up.

So he stands in the yard with the journal and looks at the back of Reggie's head going away towards
the pens, and makes the only decision actually available to him, which is to take the thing at its
face and be pleased with it and say nothing whatever about the half-second.

That is not forgiveness either. He is not sure what it is. It is what he has got, and it is what he
does again on the fourteenth of June, and on the second of August; and he becomes, over about a
year, rather good at it.
