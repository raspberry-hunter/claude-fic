# Contraindication

He has his head inside the refrigerator, which has been losing its charge since August and which
he has now taken apart twice, when Reggie says from the doorway: "Jean is out. I have sent her
the course."

Pascual comes out from behind it a good deal faster than a man of fifty-nine ought to, and puts
the spanner down on the bench, and finds that he is holding on to the edge of the bench, which is
not a thing he decided to do.

Three years and eight months. He has thought about that girl every week of it. He has thought
about her walking into a building in Maryland with a thing in her head she could not put down and
could not tell anybody, at twenty-six, with a written condition in her contract that she had
negotiated in the second hour by asking for it and not moving, and he has known the whole time
that there was nothing whatever he could do about it from a concrete apron four hundred miles
south of anywhere.

"Good," he says.

It is not adequate. He is aware that it is not adequate. It is what is available at the mouth of a
refrigerator.

And then Reggie says the other thing — that he has been wrong about something since 1946, and that
it is going to take rather longer, and that he has not got any of it written down — and Pascual
hears the shape of it before he has any of the content, and says the sentence he has been saying
for four years.

"Then I shall put the kettle on. And you will start at the beginning, and you will not summarise."

He does not know, at the moment he says it, what he is clearing the afternoon for. He would like
that recorded. He has been given a great deal of credit over the years for things he did with his
eyes open and this was not one of them.

---

His own nineteen minutes with Alan Spilsbury were in the March of 1957 and he has never given a
full account of them to anybody, including the man they were about.

He gave the account. Verbatim, inside ten minutes, sitting on the bench with his arms folded, and
he has been glad of that ever since, because it is the single most useful thing he has ever done
with his mouth. What he did not give is what it was like.

It was like this. A pleasant Englishman with excellent manners asked him, kindly and at length,
whether Dr Tar slept, whether he ate, whether the isolation of the station had told on him,
whether there was anything in his service record that a man ought to know before putting him into
a three-ship establishment. Every question proper. Every question one would ask about a candidate
for a directorship, and Pascual would have asked them himself, and did in fact ask two of them of
a locum in Ponce in 1938.

And Pascual sat there for nineteen minutes with thirty-two years of clinical discretion, which he
had understood all his life to be the thing that protects the patient, and used it to keep a
government scientist away from a man he goes to bed with. He was good at it. He was good at it
because he has been good at it since he was twenty-four and because none of it required a single
lie, and he came out of that room and was sick in the yard behind the stores, quietly, and washed
his face at the tank, and went and told Reggie the whole thing in the laboratory ten minutes
later and left that part out.

What he has never resolved is whether he was careful or whether he was watched being careful. The
man did not press. The man was not surprised. There are two constructions of that and he has never
been able to eliminate either, and he has carried both for three years and eight months, and it
turns out today that he has been carrying them the wrong way up as well.

---

Reggie starts in 1946 and goes in order.

Of course he does. Pascual has known since about 1956 that this is not a mannerism and not
vanity: the man cannot get to the end without the working, because the end is only load-bearing
if the working holds it, and if you stop him in the middle you do not get a shorter version, you
get nothing at all. The load is at the bottom. It is always at the bottom. It took Pascual the
better part of two years to work that out and rather longer to stop interrupting.

So he pours the tea and sits down and takes the history.

He does what he does with any long presentation, which is to say almost nothing, and to make the
small noise at the ends of paragraphs that tells a man he is still being listened to, and to
notice where the account goes fast.

It goes fast in three places. 1946, which is a single sentence and a date and no detail whatever
and which Pascual does not touch. The two years outside Salisbury, which get about ninety seconds
and a phrase about a building and a room. And the afternoon in September 1954 with the food logs,
which he covers in a paragraph, because he has told that one before and it has smooth edges from
handling.

Everything else is slow. Fourteen years of a policy set out properly with the reasoning attached:
that a man is safe if there is nothing on him that can be pulled, and that there are two ways to
see to it, and that he has used both, and that both of them proceed from the same assumption,
which he has never examined, and which is wrong.

Pascual drinks his tea and lets it come and does not once look at the clock.

---

The word arrives at about the fifty-minute mark.

"— and I want it stated plainly, because you are entitled to it and because it is the part I have
never said. I did not construct this. It was got out of me on the twenty-ninth of August 1954
when I had three whiskies in me and no design whatever, and by every rule I held at that time I
should have ended it inside the week. I did not. I have never once been able to produce an
account of why I did not. I have carried it since as the single place where the thing failed in
me. As a fault."

He says it looking at the table.

Pascual sits very still.

There is a thing happening in his chest which he would, in an animal, describe and sample and
decline to interpret, and what he does with it is to put the cup down carefully and keep his face
in order for four seconds, which is all that is required, because the man is not looking up.

*A fault.* Six years. Every day of six years, and the whole time, filed under the one heading he
would have died rather than be under.

---

In the February of 1955 this would have killed him.

He is entirely certain of that and he works it through while Reggie is still talking, because he
can do the two things at once and has been able to since he was thirty.

In 1955 he would have stopped the man at minute ten. He would have got up out of the chair at the
word *fault* and it would have gone off in the room like a shell, and they would have had a
tremendous row about a thing that was not the thing, and Reggie would have been unable to finish
the working, and the load at the bottom would never have come out, and both of them would have
gone to bed that night believing they had had it out. He knows the shape of that evening exactly.
He has had it. He had it on the fifteenth of January and the eleven weeks after it, and it cost
them six months.

What has changed is not that it hurts less. It hurts. It is not his favourite hour.

What has changed is that he has five and a half further years of data and he can read the sentence
properly, and read properly it says this:

*I held a rule for eight years. I have never once broken a rule in my life. I broke it for you
inside a week, and I could not stop, and I did not know why, and I have called it a fault every
day since because I had no other heading available and I went on anyway.*

That is not a confession of doubt. It is the largest thing that man has ever said about anybody
and he thinks he is apologising for it.

Pascual lets him go on.

---

He does the other operation later, when Reggie has got to Bimini and is deep in the arithmetic and
does not need him for a while.

He takes out the fourth of July 1956.

It is his best one. The schooner in at midday and the man at the bottom of the steps at the jetty,
who has never met a boat for anybody in his life, and three days of the pens being done at seven
without a word said, and the third evening at the rail: *I turned round to tell you and you were
not there.*

He puts the fault into it and looks at what happens.

It does not come apart. That is the finding and he had expected the other one. He cannot now have
the fourth of July without also having a man who had spent two years privately classing him as
the one place his discipline had failed — the two things arrive together, joined, and there is no
operation that separates them.

He has done this before, from the other end. He knows exactly what it is. He spent a year unable
to stand in a yard with an Aberdeen offprint without also knowing what it had been for.

The difference is that this time he does not want the operation. He would not run it if there were
one. It is a better fourth of July with the fault in it than it was without, because without it the
man merely met a boat, and with it the man met a boat while believing himself to be in error, and
met it anyway, and stood at the bottom of the steps.

*Ay, Dios,* Pascual thinks, and gets up and fetches more hot water, chiefly so as to have his back
to the room for a moment.

---

Bimini takes about half an hour and it is the part Reggie has plainly worked hardest on, because
it is the part with figures in it.

The offers are not a countdown. That is the finding. A man is offered a thing when compelling him
would cost more than compelling him would return; and Bimini has been offered warmly, in a good
room, with no hurry, four springs running, because Reggie Tar has become expensive; and he has
become expensive by doing the best work of his life in print under his own name for six years, and
he has been able to do the best work of his life because there is somebody in the building who
tells him things.

Reggie sets this out the way he would set out a correction to a published figure. Then he says
that the lever exists, that it is unbreakable, that it will be there for the whole of his life,
and that it is not the same thing as a debt, and that he has spent six years mistaking the one for
the other.

And Pascual, who has been listening for two hours and has said perhaps forty words, hears himself
say:

"I have been counting them."

Reggie stops.

"The offers. Every spring since '57." Pascual turns the cup round on the table, which is a thing
he has caught off this man and has never mentioned. "I had you at two more. I have had a figure
since 1958 and I have kept it and I have never asked you one question about Bimini, not one, in
four years, and you will have thought that was tact."

"I thought it was tact."

"It was not tact. It was that I could not have borne the answer and I did not want it in the room
with me." He makes himself say the rest, because the man has just done two hours of it and there
is a standard now. "A three-ship American establishment does not take a Puerto Rican veterinarian
of fifty-nine with it as part of the fittings. I have known that since the day he first put it to
you. Every spring I have watched you decline it and I have thought: that is one more, and there is
a number, and when he reaches it he will go, and he will be right to go, and I shall stay here
with the animals."

Reggie has gone entirely white about the mouth.

"You have had that since 1958."

"Since the second spring, yes."

"And I have been declining it in front of you every year and saying nothing about it because I did
not wish to make a performance of declining it."

"Yes."

"Ah," Reggie says.

They sit with that for a while. Outside, John is shouting about something on the apron and the new
pump is running and it is a completely ordinary Tuesday.

"There is no number," Reggie says at last. "I want that put beyond doubt because I can see that I
have had four years to say it and have not. There is no figure at which I go. There has never been
one. I have declined it four times and I shall decline it in the spring and I shall go on
declining it, and the reason is not the co-housing series, which is what I say to him and which is
true, and it is not the animals."

"No."

"No."

---

It is dark by the time they finish, and neither of them has eaten, and Pascual makes him eat,
which takes about four minutes and no argument at all, which would have taken half an hour in
1955.

Then, at some point, in the dark, he does the thing he does: two fingers laid along the inside of
the wrist and the thumb round the back, in the place, and the whole account comes up under his
hand the way it has come up under his hand for six years.

And he thinks: *he has just spent five hours giving me fourteen years of himself in order and out
loud, and I have given him a number.*

So he says it.

"You have known since 1956 that I do this."

"Yes."

"You have never once asked me why."

"No."

"You have assumed it was clinical," Pascual says, "and I have let you assume it, and it has not
been clinical since about the December of 1954."

Reggie does not move.

"There is a place on a wrist where the body tells the truth about itself whether or not the head
has agreed to it. I have known that since I was twenty-four and I have had it off ten thousand
animals and about as many people and it has never been anything to me but an instrument. And then
in December of 1954 I found that I could keep two fingers there in the dark and have the whole of
you — the going-fast, and the place where it steadies, and the long coming-down after — without
asking you for any of it, and without your having to say a word, which you could not do at that
time and I knew you could not."

He makes himself finish it properly, because the standard has been set today and it was not set by
him.

"That is the part. Not that I take it. That I have it *without being given it*. I have never said
that to a living soul and I would not know how to write it down, and I have called it a lunacy for
six years in order not to have to call it what it is."

There is a silence of some length.

"May I give you mine," Reggie says.

"You may."

"It is not the same as yours. I want that established before I say it, because you will assume it
is, and it is not, and the difference matters to me." A pause. "You have understood, I think, that
I do it for the reading."

"I have."

"I do not do it for the reading. I have all the reading I could want off your face and I have had
since about 1956." He is quiet for a moment. "I have one fact about Cholmondeley. It is that there
is no point at which the arithmetic fails. I have had that for six years and two months and it
will not ever resolve into anything, and I do not know, and I shall not know, and that is the
whole of what I possess on the question of whether a particular person is still there."

"Reggie — "

"You are there," Reggie says. "It is under my fingers and it is going. And I can check it, and
put my hand down, and check it again in a minute, and it will still be going. It is the only
question of that class I have ever been able to answer in my life and I can answer it as often as
I like."

Pascual lies in the dark and finds that he has nothing whatever to say and that this is perfectly
all right.

Six years. The same two fingers in the same place on the same two men, every night, and one of
them was taking what could not be given and the other was confirming that somebody had not gone,
and neither had the smallest idea, and each had privately settled that his own half was the
disgraceful one.

He turns his hand over, palm up, so that the wrist lies open and can be got at properly.

Reggie's fingers arrive inside a second, which means he has been waiting, which means he has been
waiting for some considerable time.

"Well," Pascual says. "Take it, then."

And they lie there in the dark, each of them with two fingers on the other, and it is not a
measurement in any sense that either of their professions would recognise, and both of them go on
taking it for a long while, and this time each of them knows exactly what is being had off him and
has said so out loud, which is new, and which appears to be the whole of what six years was for.
