# Null Result

The launch goes out at twenty past four and he watches it the whole way to the point,
which takes eleven minutes, and he counts them.

He does not decide to count them. It is simply that the machinery has come back on
sometime in the last day and a half and this is the first thing it has been given.

---

Spilsbury has been on the station five days and has been, throughout, extremely pleasant.

That is the part nobody who has not met the type will ever credit. He shook hands. He
admired the pens and asked intelligent questions about the drainage. He was good with the
Colonel and better with Mrs Bevan, and he told John his drawings were the best working
record he had seen at any station in the hemisphere, which was true, and John has been
insufferable about it for four days and will be for four more, and does not know and will
never know what he did.

He put Delta in the small pen on Thursday and she has not eaten since.

And on the second afternoon he sat down opposite Reggie with the food logs and the intake
sheets and Charles's log, and went through them at a rate that would have been a pleasure
to watch in any other circumstance, and after about forty minutes he stopped, and put one
finger on a column, and said: "Your man wrote *no change* for eleven months on an animal
that put on nine pounds."

There it was.

"Yes," Reggie said.

"Every week. In the same hand. *No change.*"

"Yes."

"That is either a very stupid man or a very interesting one, Tar, and I have read his
transfer papers and he is not stupid."

And this is the point at which Reggie found out what he was going to do, since he had not
known in advance and had refused to rehearse it, on the grounds that a rehearsed answer
sounds rehearsed.

"He kept a poor log," Reggie said, "and I did not check it."

Spilsbury looked at him.

"For eleven months," Reggie said. "I had two specimens and four postgraduates and I was
doing the acoustic work myself, and I read the food sheets, because the food sheets had
figures in them, and I did not read the behavioural log, because it had nothing in it. That
is the whole of it. If you want it in writing you may have it in writing."

There was a pause of some seconds during which Reggie's future was decided, and it was
decided in his favour, and the mechanism by which it was decided was that Spilsbury's face
changed from interest to something quite different.

"Well," Spilsbury said. "Well, well." And then, kindly: "You have got slack, old man.
It happens. It happens particularly to the clever ones, if you want my view — you stop
looking at the dull half because you have decided in advance that it is dull." He squared
the sheets and handed them back. "I shall have to say so."

"I imagine you will."

"You want a chief of station and a scientist, and you have been trying to be both, and it
has cost you an animal and a research student and I am afraid it has very nearly cost you a
programme." He stood up. "Get the co-housing trial done and get me a paper out of it and we
shall say no more about the rest."

And he was gone before Reggie's hands began, and Reggie sat in the room for a further
quarter of an hour with them flat on the table until they stopped.

---

It worked. That is the thing he keeps arriving back at.

It worked because a slack man is of no use to anybody. There is nothing in a slack man to
be got at, nothing to hold over him, no reason to put him in a room with a paper. Spilsbury
came here to find out whether there was a phenomenon and whether there was a man who
understood it, and he has gone away having found a tired administrator who does not read
his own logs, and he will write that down, and the writing-down is the safest thing that
has ever happened to Reggie Tar.

He has had five days to look at what it cost and he has looked at it every hour.

There was a result. It was the only one he has ever had that was worth anything — not the
tapes, the tapes are competent and derivative and he has always known it — and it was
*Beta beat the mortality tables and here is why,* and the why walked down the beach on the
twenty-ninth and got into the water, and there is now no experiment in the world that
recovers it. He can co-house Gamma and Delta and he will, and it may even work, and if it
works he will publish it, and the paper will have a hole in the middle of it the size of a
man, and he will spend the rest of his working life writing round that hole and no reviewer
will ever see it and he will see nothing else.

He is not eating. He noted that on Wednesday and has done nothing about it. He has read the
same page of Norris four times and could not now say what is on it. Twice this week he has
gone to the pen — Beta's, the empty one, not Delta's — for no reason he can produce, and
stood at it, and come away.

The machinery is the thing that concerns him. For three days it did not work at all. He
gave it the tide and the annotations, deliberately, on Tuesday night, as one gives a rifle
a round to see whether the pin drops, and nothing happened, and he sat there in a state he
has no name for and has provisionally logged as fatigue.

It came back yesterday. He is aware that he is relieved. He is aware that the relief is
disproportionate and he has declined to look into it.

---

The other thing that happened this week is that Spilsbury interviewed Pascual on his own
for fifty minutes on Wednesday morning, in the dining room, with the door shut.

Reggie was in the laboratory. He knows what was in the laboratory with him and the order in
which he touched it and nothing whatever about what he did with any of it.

Pascual came out and went straight past him and said, without stopping, "He wanted to know
about the muscle damage," and that was all, and it was enough, and Reggie's hands were
perfectly steady for the rest of the day.

Jean was in there for an hour and twenty and came out looking bored. She is a considerable
liar. He had not known that about her and files it with some respect.

---

He goes at nine.

He has been carrying a sentence for five days — *tomorrow you will be sober and I will still
be standing a foot too close, then we will see what your nine observations were worth* — and
he has taken it out and examined it perhaps two hundred times, and the trouble with it is
that it does not contain an instruction. It states a condition. It does not say what the man
in the condition is supposed to do.

So he stands outside Pascual's door for a while, working on it, and gets nowhere, and knocks
because standing there is worse.

Pascual opens it and looks at him and does not say anything at all, which is not fair.

"I have no procedure for this," Reggie says.

"No."

"I have thought about it for five days and I have not produced one. I want that understood.
I am not being — " He stops. He starts again. "I have said *I do not know* twice in a
fortnight and before that I had not said it in eleven years, and I find I would rather do
almost anything than say it a third time, and I am about to."

"Say it, then."

"I do not know how this is done."

Pascual stands back from the door.

---

He tries, at first, to keep the record. It is what he has; it is the only apparatus he has
ever consented to be.

He gets about as far as the buttons.

What he has of the first minutes is that Pascual is neither gentle nor rough and has plainly
decided something in advance, some while ago, and has no intention whatever of being asked
about it now. And that is the last observation Reggie makes *as* an observation, because
after that he is simply somewhere, being handled by a man who has thought about it, and the
part of him that stands off at one side and takes the reading is not standing off at one
side.

He has no name for the state and has never been in it. He has been unconscious under
anaesthetic twice and it is not that; under anaesthetic there is nothing.

But the record is not lost. That is what he discovers afterwards, lying still in the dark
with his heart going — that the record is enormous, that there is a great deal more of it
than he can hold, that it arrived faster than it could be laid down and none of it in any
order, and that he has all of it and can do nothing whatever with it.

He has the buttons. He counted them, the count is four, he is entirely certain of the four,
and there are seven on the shirt. He has the lamp going out and neither of them putting it
out. He has Pascual's hand at the back of his skull, the exact weight of it, and nothing at
all of the ten minutes on either side. He has one sound, which was his own voice, and no
idea what it said. He has the extraordinary circumstance of his own eyes being shut, which
he does not permit, because a man with his eyes shut cannot see the room, and he cannot
establish at what point he stopped permitting it.

Against the clock none of it works. By his own reckoning the thing took either six minutes
or the better part of an hour, and there is no arrangement of what he has that yields a
third answer. He has never in his life had more material than he could sort.

*I have no data on that.*

He hears himself think it in his own voice, and it goes through him like cold water, because
he said that sentence in this condition five days ago about a man walking back up a beach,
and he said it flatly, as though it settled something.

He had data on that. He had eleven months of it. He had the log and the food sheets and the
weight curve and the boy's face across a verandah every evening, and he had every part of it
in his own hands, and he did not put them together.

Assembly is not a thing that happens to him, then. He has said all his life that he cannot
switch it off. It appears that he can, and did, and has been calling a decision a
constitution for some considerable time. That is a serious fault in an instrument and he
does not care for having found it in himself at thirty-four.

He thinks, with great clarity: *Cholmondeley did not walk back up the beach.*

And there it is, and it is not a shock, and that is the worst of it. He has had it for five
days in the way one has a stone in a shoe one has decided not to stop for. The cot is still
made up. The razor is still on it. A man does not leave a razor.

Beside him Pascual says, in a voice that has been asleep for about a minute and is not now,
"Stop it."

"I have not said anything."

"You have gone stiff as a board and your breathing has changed and it is not from what we
have been doing." A hand comes across his chest, heavy, deliberate, laid on him the way a
hand is laid on an animal to keep it flat. "Whatever it is. In the morning."

"It is that he went with him."

Pascual is quiet for a moment.

"Yes," he says. "He went with him. He was always going to go with him. Now go to sleep."

And Reggie lies there with the weight of the arm across him, and does not go to sleep, and
does not get up, and does not go and get the bottle, which he notices, because he notices
everything and it has all come back on at once.

Some time after three the machinery finishes what it has been doing quietly at the back
without being asked: the annotations, the tide on the twenty-ninth, the westerly set through
the passage, the four hundred miles, an animal that can do a fifth of it in a day and a man
who cannot do any of it. It produces a course. It is entirely coherent. It has taken five
days and it would have taken forty minutes on the night if he had let it.

He lies in the dark and holds the whole of it and has nowhere to put it. There is no one to
tell. There is no boat to send and no one to send it after and no question anyone will ever
ask him now, because he made quite sure of that on Tuesday afternoon with a column of
figures and a lie about his own competence, and it is his, and it will be his for as long as
he has a head to keep it in.

He does not write it down.
