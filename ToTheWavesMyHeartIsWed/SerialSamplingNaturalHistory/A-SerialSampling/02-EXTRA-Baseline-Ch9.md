# Baseline

The window of his room faces the yard and the water tanks. He has complained about
this twice in writing. Tonight it is the only piece of luck he has had all year, because
it means he need not decide against looking out of it; he need only decide against going
outside, and that is a decision of a class he can hold.

He can hear the generator. He can always hear the generator. He has raised it at three
separate meetings as an obstacle to acoustic work, and it is now the most useful feature
of the station.

He pours the second at half past nine.

The difficulty is not that he wants to know. The difficulty is that wanting has nothing
to do with it. There were nine annotations on that chart and he supplied six of them
himself; he has the tide; he knows what the boy assembled, because he has watched him
assemble it over four days with the particular thoroughness of a man who has not yet
admitted what he is doing. Sit here sober until midnight and he will not guess the
course. He will simply have it, the way he has the fourth fact by Tuesday, and then he
will be a man who knows a thing, and a man who knows a thing can be asked.

Whisky is the only instrument he has ever found that interferes with it.

One does nothing at all. He notes that. Two does nothing that he can measure. Three
takes the edge off the arithmetic -- the annotations will not stay in order, the tide
table slides -- and he is holding the third up against the lamp, deciding whether the
effect is real or whether he has merely decided it is, when Pascual knocks and comes in
without waiting.

"You are drinking alone," Pascual says.

"I am."

"With the shutter closed and one lamp." Pascual looks at the bottle, and at the level in
it, and does the sum, because he is incapable of not doing the sum. "Well. That is a
new one."

"Sit down."

"I did not come to sit down. I came to ask what that was, on the verandah." Pascual sits
down. "Spilsbury. You made a noise like a man standing on a nail, and then you took the
boy away with you, and now here you are with the shutter closed. So."

Reggie puts the glass on the desk and turns it a quarter-turn, which does nothing to it.

Then he tells him.

He tells it in order, because he has never learned to tell anything in any other way: that
John assembled it out of a conversation he overheard between the two of them and a thing
he saw at Beta's pen and something Jean said in his hearing; that John is not malicious
and is very pleased with himself; that the connection is Fleming, in Jamaica, and Fleming's
connection is the Navy, and that the visit was already coming, because they are bringing
the next animal, so what John has done is not to summon them but to give them a second
piece of business while they are here. That the second piece of business is a man and an
animal, and that they will not be separated from either of them again. That he knows what
Spilsbury's people do, because he has done it, in a building outside Salisbury, for two
years, to things that could not leave the room.

And that he has told Cholmondeley to take the animal tonight, and given him a chart in a
tin, and that they will be gone by now, or they will not.

Pascual does not say anything for some time.

"You have put a compromised animal into open water," he says at last.

"Yes."

"Six weeks after muscle damage that I could not sample properly and could not sedate for."

"Nine weeks."

"Nine, then. Do you know what the last figures were?"

"Urine clear from the eleventh. Intake at twenty-two pounds, which is his best. Surfacing
intervals four to five and a half minutes and no shortening under exertion. He has been
swimming full lengths of the pen since the twenty-third and I have watched him do it every
evening for eleven evenings." He hears himself and stops. "I would not have sent him
otherwise."

"Ah," Pascual says. "You would not have sent him otherwise."

"No."

"Then you did the arithmetic. Of course you did the arithmetic." Pascual rubs his face
with both hands. "Reggie. Eleven evenings. Standing at that pen. Do you know that in
eleven evenings you did not once ask me a question about him, and I am the one holding
the samples?"

"I had the samples."

"You had my samples," Pascual says, "and you did not have me," and Reggie does not know
what to do with that sentence, so he leaves it lying where it fell and reaches for the
glass.

"There is a further matter," he says. "I should be obliged if you did not go down to the
pens before breakfast. Not for my sake. If it is found at first light there is a boat
that can be got out, and if it is found at nine o'clock there is not."

"And what do I say, when it is found at nine o'clock?"

"That you were told nothing and saw nothing, which will be true, and that I was drunk in
my room, which will also be true. That is why I am doing it in front of a witness rather
than behind one." It is a good sentence. He has had it ready since the verandah. He puts
it down and finds that it does not stand up in the room the way it stood up in his head.

Pascual looks at him for a long moment. Reggie is aware that he is blinking, and that he
is doing it quickly, and that Pascual has known what that means for at least a year.

"And the boy," Pascual says. "He drags the animal down the beach, and he gets him past the
breakers, and then he walks back up on his own, does he."

"I have no data on that."

"No," Pascual says. "No, I don't suppose you have."

The generator carries on. Somewhere on the other side of it a man is doing a thing that
will take him rather longer than he expects, on wet sand, in the dark, without his glasses,
and Reggie has the distance in yards and the state of the tide and declines to put them
together, and finds the declining is costing him more than it did an hour ago.

He drinks the third off and pours the fourth, and it is at this point that he discovers
the third has done something after all, and that what it has done is not to the arithmetic.

Pascual stands up. "You should sleep."

"Sit down."

"Reggie --"

"Sit down. I want to tell you something and I would rather not say it to a man who is
already at the door."

Pascual sits down.

"You stand closer to me than the argument requires," Reggie says. "I have checked it
against the Colonel and against Jean and it is not a thing you do generally, it is a thing
you do to me, and the difference is roughly a foot. During the experiment you came to this
room three times when a note would have done, and on two of those occasions you had
nothing new to tell me. After you said what you said about the government labs you did not
look at me for six days, and a man who believes he was right does not do that; he looks at
you rather more, not less. You put your hand on me only when you are angry, and you put
your hand on everyone else without its meaning anything at all, which is the wrong way
round. You say my name differently from the way you say other people's names -- I cannot
characterise it further than that and I have tried. That is five. There are nine."

Pascual has gone very still.

"In April I handed you the acoustic plates across your shoulder and I held the transfer
about two seconds longer than the transfer wanted, and I counted your breathing. Then I did
the same thing to Jean, in the same room, under the same lamp, at the same hour, and
counted hers. Then I repeated both, twice, because one of anything is nothing."

"You ran a control," Pascual says. "On me. You ran a *control* on me."

"Of course I ran a control. Without one it is an impression, and I do not act on
impressions." He turns the glass another quarter-turn. "I have had the result since April
and I have done nothing with it for four months, and I want that understood, because I am
about to stop."

"And why is that?" Pascual's voice has gone somewhere down in his chest. "Why is that,
tonight?"

"Because a result is not a warrant," Reggie says. "That is the whole of it. I can produce
certainty about very nearly anything if you leave me in a room with it long enough, and I
have never once been able to produce permission, and I do not know where other people get
it from. In March they put a peer and a journalist and a landowner away, and everyone I
know drew a lesson from it about discretion. It is not about discretion. There were two
airmen in that case who had done precisely what the defendants had done. They were not
charged. They gave the evidence, their letters were read out in open court, and they went
back to their stations. That is not mercy, Pascual, it is procurement. Somebody looked at
five men and worked out which two were worth more as instruments than as convictions."

He turns the glass. "I am not the peer. I have no name worth ruining and nothing would be
gained by ruining it. I am the other sort. A man with my file who is also that does not go
to prison; he goes on working, and one day somebody comes down from London and puts a paper
in front of him and he signs it. I have known since I was twenty-six what I am, and I
decided at twenty-six that I would not be the handle, and I have not been. There has been
nothing to be had. In eight years there has been nothing whatever to be had."

He looks at the fourth glass.

"And tonight I have had three of these in order to stop being able to work out where he
has gone, and it appears that it does not take the machinery down in any particular order."

"And what am I, in your arithmetic? Now. Tonight."

Reggie opens his mouth. He is aware, distantly, that this is a question of a kind he
answers, that he has answered worse ones in worse rooms, and that the machinery is turning
over and producing nothing at all.

"I don't know," he says.

Pascual says something under his breath in Spanish, and gets up, and takes the fourth glass
off the desk and puts it out of reach, and does not go to the door.

"How much."

"Three. It is not enough. I shall need to revise it."

"You will *revise* it," Pascual says, and laughs, once, terribly, and then he has Reggie by
the front of the shirt and is not laughing.

It is not, in the event, anything he had a figure for. That is the first observation, and
he makes it somewhere near the desk with his glasses pushed askew and Pascual's mouth on
his and Pascual's hand flat and hard between his shoulder blades, holding him in as though
he might otherwise go somewhere: that he had nine observations and a control and a design,
and none of it told him anything whatever about this, and the failure is total and he does
not mind it. The second observation is that he has got both hands in Pascual's hair and does
not remember putting them there. The third is that the sound he can hear is his own, and
that it is the second uncontrolled sound he has made in one evening, and that a rate of two
in an evening is not a rate he has ever run before.

He does not get as far as a fourth. There is a stretch of some minutes for which he
subsequently has nothing at all, which has never happened to him in his life, and then he
is sitting on the edge of the desk with his shirt open and Pascual standing between his
knees with both hands on his face, breathing like a man who has run up a hill.

"No," Pascual says.

Reggie waits. He is not, at this moment, capable of the sentence, but it assembles itself
anyway, because it always does: *that is not consistent with the last four minutes.*

"I don't put a needle in a compromised animal," Pascual says. "I have said it to you about
Beta, I said it to you in front of Jean, and I am not going to make an exception of you at
midnight with three whiskies in you and that boy out there on the sand. No."

"That is not a refusal."

"No," Pascual agrees. "It is not." He takes his hands away, slowly, in the manner of a man
setting down something he has been asked to be careful with, and steps back, and picks up
Reggie's glasses off the floor, and puts them into his hand. "Tomorrow you will be sober
and I will still be standing a foot too close. Then we will see what your nine
observations were worth."

Then he goes out, and Reggie hears the tap, and Pascual comes back with a glass of water
and stands over him while he drinks the whole of it, exactly as he stands over Charles at
mealtimes, and it is at that point that Reggie has to look away.

Pascual takes the chair.

"You are not staying."

"I am staying," Pascual says, "because I am not going down to the pens before breakfast,
and I would rather be somewhere I can see the man who asked me not to."

Reggie lies down on the bed in his clothes. The generator goes on. He thinks, once, with
great clarity, that if the surf is running at all the boy will not manage the last twenty
yards on his own and will have to get the animal to take some of it, and that this is
information, and that it is of no use to anybody, and then it goes, and he sleeps.

He wakes at first light with a blanket over him that he did not put there. Pascual is
asleep in the chair with his arms folded and his chin down, and looks, in that position,
about sixty.

Reggie lies still for a while.

Then he gets up, quietly, and does not go to the window, and does not go out. The fourth
glass is standing where Pascual left it, out of reach, and he drinks it off warm because
it will otherwise be wasted, and it tastes of nothing at all.

He takes a clean sheet from the desk and writes on the corner of it, *3 -- insufficient*,
and underlines it, and puts the sheet in the drawer with the mortality tables.
