# The Field Season

It took fifteen months to arrange and not one hour of it was dishonest, which was the part Jean
was proudest of and the part nobody would ever believe.

She wanted salinity gradients and the fish fauna of the lower caños. She'd wanted them since
1958 — which is to say, since the year after a man in a boat shed told her that her file was the
wrong file and wouldn't give her the right one, and told her, in the course of not giving it to
her, that there were no harbour masters where he'd gone. There was one coast in that sea with no
harbour masters on it. It had a great deal of fresh water in it and a very interesting fish fauna.
She never once pretended the two things were unconnected: she went and made herself want it, and
then she wanted it honestly, and both of those were true and neither cancelled the other.

She wrote to the La Salle people at Margarita in the January of 1961, on Torry letterhead,
with a reading list and a costed proposal and nine reprints, and they wrote back within the month
sounding rather pleased, and by the autumn she had a boat, a season, a permit, and a Venezuelan
co-author who turned out to be excellent company and who laughed at her Spanish for six weeks
without ever once being unkind about it.

She was thirty-five. She had a scarf, a hat that had been through two North Sea cruises, four
hundred jars, and four lines in another person's handwriting in her breast pocket, which had come
to Aberdeen in the November of 1960 and which she had read so often she could have said them in
the dark.

*Springs on the twenty-ninth of August, two days past the full.*

---

The delta wasn't what she'd drawn in her head, and what she'd drawn in her head was, she realised
on the second morning, essentially the Ythan with palms.

It was brown. It was brown all the way up: brown water with a skin of light on it, brown mud
walls at low tide with the mangrove roots coming out of them like the legs of something, and above
all that an appalling green that went on for hours and didn't vary and was full of birds. The
smell was mud and flowers together, which she hadn't expected and didn't care for on the first
day and had begun to like by the fourth. It was hot in a way that had weight. She perspired
straight through her shirts and stopped minding.

She'd thought she would find it oppressive. Instead she was very nearly delirious with pleasure
for the first three weeks, because every net she put in came up with something she had never seen
and about a third of it wasn't in the literature at all. She wrote to her mother that she had at
last understood what people meant by a rich system, and that she would probably be insufferable
for the rest of her life.

Anselmo took the boat. He was Warao, from a settlement two hours below the mission, and he had
worked for a survey party in 1957 and for two priests before that, and he had a manner of
receiving European enthusiasm that Jean recognised inside a day, because it was exactly Dr Pobil's
and she'd spent three years on the receiving end of it.

He was better at finding fish than she was. He was better at it than anybody she had worked with,
including the Danes.

---

On the twenty-second of March she was doing local names.

It was a dull job and it was also the only way anybody ever found anything, and she had a notebook
with the Warao name, the Spanish name if there was one, the specimen number and a sketch, and she
was working through the morning's haul on the thwart with the sun coming off the water.

She held up a small ugly greyish thing with a hard head that she'd taken forty of and couldn't
place at all.

Anselmo gave her the name. She wrote it down. Then he said, in Spanish, that it was the name
people used, but that it wasn't the right one; the right one was the one the Englishman had.

Jean didn't move.

She said, "Which Englishman?"

And Anselmo said the one who lives up at the fork past Guayo, the one with the books, who asks
about the fish also and has asked about them for a long time, and who had two of that kind in a
jar in 1959.

It was very odd, what one did. Jean put the fish down. She wrote the name in her notebook with a
perfectly steady hand and drew the dorsal spine correctly and put a query beside the count. Then
she asked, in a voice she was very pleased with afterwards, how long the Englishman had been
there.

Anselmo thought about it and said: since before the flood year. Seven, perhaps eight.

She got the rest of the morning's haul done. All of it. She has never been able to explain that to
herself.

---

It took two days to get up there, and she spent the first of them composing and the second one
frightened.

Frightened of the obvious things — that it would be a Dutchman, or a missionary, or a man who had
gone up a river in 1948 for reasons of his own and wouldn't care to be found. And frightened of
the other thing, which was that it might be him, and that she'd built a person out of eight years
of not knowing, and that she was about to meet whoever was actually there instead.

There was a landing, and a path up, and a house on posts with a roof of palm and a good deal of
netting, and outside it a long bench under a shelter with jars on it. Rows of jars. Labelled.

And a man came round the corner of the house with a bucket in his hand and stopped dead.

He'd gone very brown and very thin and his hair had gone half grey at thirty-two, and he was
barefoot, and he was wearing a shirt that had been mended by somebody who could mend, and he was
wearing spectacles that weren't the ones she remembered and had plainly been made up by a man in
Tucupita from a prescription somebody had guessed at.

"Jean," Charles said.

And then he put the bucket down very carefully on the ground, as though it were the bucket that
mattered, and stood there with his hands empty and said, "Is she alive?"

Not *how did you find me*. Not *what are you doing here*. That was the first question, straight
off, before anything, after seven and a half years, and Jean didn't have to ask who he meant.

"Yes," Jean said. "Yes. She's alive, she's in the second pen with Delta and she's been in it since
1955, and I've a great deal to tell you, and I'm going to sit down because I think I must."

---

She stayed nine days.

It wasn't what she'd imagined, and what she'd imagined — she's honest enough to put it down
— was a rescue. A boat, a man carried out of the green, a great deal of weeping, a passage home,
and Jean Leslie standing on a quay at the end of it having Done Something.

He wasn't lost and didn't want a boat and hadn't for years.

He had a house he had built, mostly, with help. He had a garden that wasn't very good. He had a
standing arrangement with two families at the settlement below and a debt to a third that he
described at length and with obvious anxiety, which she gathered was the central social fact of
his life. He spoke the language, badly, he said, and Anselmo told her afterwards that he spoke it
well. He had had malaria three times. He had a machete, four cooking pots, an astonishing
quantity of string, and fifteen notebooks.

Fifteen didn't sound like a great deal until he put them into her hands.

He showed her them on the third day, and Jean sat on the bench under the shelter and went through
them for six hours with her mouth open. Daily observations from October 1954. Water temperature
and salinity twice a day with a hydrometer he had made himself out of a bottle and had calibrated
— he apologised for the calibration for about four minutes. Tide. Rainfall. Every fish he had
taken, drawn, with counts. The flood of 1957 hour by hour. A twelve-page appendix on the behaviour
of a species of catfish that she's fairly sure is undescribed and that she means to have named
after him whether he likes it or not.

Paper came up the caño twice a year when it came at all. He had written both sides of every leaf,
and then across the top and bottom margins, and then down the inner ones, in a hand that began to
get smaller in about 1957 and by 1960 wanted a glass. The last two books were in pencil, because
the ink had run out in the March and the March wasn't a month in which anything came up the caño.

Six months of *No change*, and then this.

"You wrote it all down," she said.

"Well," Charles said, "there was no one to tell."

---

On the fifth day he took her down to the settlement, and made a considerable business of getting
ready, and was wretched about it for the whole of the hour it took to get there.

She'd assumed the debt was money. It wasn't.

In the November of 1958 he'd had his third malaria and had gone down with it by himself in the
house on the posts, and a woman called Rufina and her eldest girl had come up and stayed twelve
days and got him through it, and had lost the twelve days out of their own season doing it. There
was no arrangement anywhere in the world by which twelve days in November could be handed back.
He'd tried string. He'd tried fish, and labour, and one attempt involving money, which he
described to her on the way down at considerable length and which had plainly been the worst
afternoon he'd had since 1954.

He told her the whole of it on the way down in the flat particular voice of a man who has been
over a thing several thousand times in his own head and got nowhere with it.

Jean found it faintly ridiculous for about twenty minutes.

Then they got there.

What she'd been expecting, on the strength of an hour of it, was a debt sitting in the middle of a
room. There wasn't one. Rufina came out and said something short to him and he said something
short back, and she took the sack he'd brought and put it down without looking into it, and inside
four minutes he was on the ground being talked at by two men about an engine and a girl of about
eleven was going through Jean's hat.

Nobody there was carrying the twelve days at all. It had been settled in 1958 by everybody except
him.

Then Rufina's girl brought out a bowl of something for the Englishman, because he was thin, and he
took it, and it cost him something Jean could see from six feet away.

She stopped finding it ridiculous at about that point.

He had told her on the second day that he spoke the language badly. Anselmo had told her
afterwards that he spoke it well, and she'd put the two down to modesty and courtesy respectively
and left it there. She couldn't assess Warao. But she'd been a foreigner in three languages and
she knew what to count, and what she counted, sitting on a step for two hours with a girl working
through the contents of her hat, was that nobody waited for him. Nobody slowed down. He was
interrupted four times in one conversation and interrupted somebody else twice, and when he got a
laugh it came at the end of his sentence and not two seconds after it.

She wrote in the notebook that evening: *He speaks it. Nobody has told him.*

The other thing happened at about five, on the path down to the boat.

One of the men put a question to him. Charles answered it, and the man nodded and said something
else, and Charles laughed and said something back, and they parted, and Charles went on down the
path holding a branch out of her way.

"What was that?"

"He asked after him."

Jean stopped on the path.

"There's a word for him," Charles said. "Everybody on this stretch of river has got the word. It
isn't a very interesting word and I've never been able to get a decent translation out of anyone."

"They know."

"They've known since about 1955. Rufina's view is that it's a great deal of trouble to go to and
that I'd have done better with a wife who could carry things." He looked back at her. "You've gone
a very strange colour. Come along, we've an hour of light."

She stood there in the green with her hat in her hand.

Eight years she'd spent composing herself for a thing everybody on this river had absorbed in
1955, filed under a word that wasn't very interesting, and formed an opinion about.

She was starting to see why he didn't want a boat.

---

They talked about Reggie on the seventh evening, and it went badly for a while, which she had
expected.

She'd come with eight years of anger to hand over and she found, once she was actually sitting
in front of the man it had been on behalf of, that she couldn't get it out of the bag cleanly,
because Charles kept correcting her.

He hadn't been sent. That was the first correction and she took it badly, because it was the
member the whole structure rested on. He'd packed the knapsack on the twenty-seventh, two days
before Reggie ever spoke to him on the verandah. Reggie had given him the tin — the chart, the
annotations, the tide — and had told him it was tonight, and had then gone upstairs and got drunk
on purpose so that he'd be unable to work out where they had gone; but the going was Charles's,
and had been Charles's for two days.

"There were nine annotations," Charles said. "Six were his. Three were yours."

Jean put her cup down.

She'd done them in the June of 1954. Reef openings and the fresh water at two of the cays and a
note about the ordnance ground off the north point, because Dr Tar had asked her for a hazard
sheet for the small boat and she had written it out on a Thursday afternoon and thought nothing
whatever about it, and had gone on thinking nothing about it for seven and a half years.

"I helped," she said. "I helped and he never told me."

"Yes."

"That's exactly the thing I've been angry about."

"I know," Charles said. "It's also why I'm here."

She sat with that for a while.

"He couldn't have told you," Charles said. "He made himself unable to know. He said it in one
sentence on the verandah and I didn't understand it until about 1956." Charles turned his cup
round in his hands, and Jean thought, with a small internal lurch, *oh, you got that off him too.*
"That was the whole of the plan. So that when they asked him he'd be telling the truth."

"He worked it out anyway. Five days later. He has had the course since the third of September
1954 and he told me so in a boat shed in 1957 and refused to give it to me, and then he waited
until I was out of Spilsbury's building and then he sent it, and that is how I am sitting here."

Charles put the cup down.

"He never asked me anything," Jean said. "Three years I was on that station after you went, and he
never said your name, and I decided he was cold."

"Yes," Charles said. "You would."

"I beg your pardon?"

"I did the same thing for four months. He's the most frightening man I've ever met, and he was
absolutely horrible to me for the better part of half a year, and then he arranged a way for Monty
and me to get off that island such that if it came apart he'd be the one holding it, and he saved
both our lives." He smiled at her, suddenly, and for a second he was
twenty-four again on a verandah with his glasses pushed up. "And so did you."

Jean didn't say anything for a while, and Charles let her get on with it and went and did
something unnecessary with the jars for a quarter of an hour, which was tactful, and was also
exactly what Dr Pobil would have done, and she has no idea where either of them learnt it.

Later she told him about Pascual. She was careful about it for approximately one sentence before
she understood that there was no need.

"Oh, *thank God*," Charles said. "Since when?"

"The night you went, apparently."

"*That* night?"

"That night."

"Well," said Charles, and looked out at the water for a bit, and said, "good," in a voice that had
a great deal in it, and didn't say anything else.

It was after that, and she was never able to settle whether it was because of it, that she sat on
the bench in the dark and did the arithmetic she'd been declining to do since 1957.

She'd looked for him from the autumn of 1954. Harbour masters, consulates, the Dutch, the
missions, two hundred and some letters, three years of it.

Then in the March of 1957 a man had stood among the drums in a boat shed and asked her to go on
not knowing, and she'd said yes, and had gone to Aberdeen, and hadn't known and hadn't looked for
three years and seven months.

It was the correct decision. She'd checked it perhaps forty times since and it came out correct
every time and it had never once been the smallest use to her.

Charles came out with the lamp.

"Why did you never try to send word?"

It was out before she'd decided on it, and she heard the whole of it come out of her mouth and
couldn't get hold of it anywhere.

Because if he had, she'd have gone in 1955. That was what was underneath it. If he had, the three
years and seven months wouldn't have been her responsibility.

Charles put the lamp down on the bench and sat on the far end of it.

"I could have, by about 1957," he said. "There's a post at Tucupita and I've been down to it twice
a year since the flood."

She hadn't expected that and had nothing whatever ready for it.

"I didn't want to be argued with. That's the whole of it, and it isn't a good reason, and I've had
five years to find a better one and haven't." He was looking at the water. "So be as angry about
that as you like. It's fair, and I've been angry about it myself. It won't do the smallest thing
for the other business. I've tried it."

That was the whole of what he gave her.

She went to bed at about nine and lay in the netting being furious with him for declining to let
her off, which she was perfectly aware was an extraordinary thing to be furious about, and was
furious about that as well.

---

On the eighth day she did the jars.

There were two hundred and forty of them to label and enter and there was nothing in any of it
that asked a thing of her, and she started after breakfast and finished at four and got up off the
bench and found she'd done the whole of it and couldn't remember one jar. She'd been able to do
that since she was nineteen. It was the only ability she'd ever had that she wouldn't have chosen.

Charles left her to it, which she took at the time for tact and has since decided was a good deal
more practical than tact.

She sat with the Aberdeen years for a while and didn't get to the end of them.

---

On the eighth evening, at about six, she was on the bench writing up and Charles was in the water
at the landing doing something with a fish trap, and Jean looked up.

She's not going to set down what she saw. She has thought about it a great deal and she's not
going to, not in a notebook and not in a letter and not to any colleague living, and the reason
isn't that it was frightening, because it wasn't.

It was about fifteen feet away from her. It looked at her for some seconds with an expression she
couldn't read and had no business trying to. Charles said something to it, quietly, in no
language she recognised and which she doesn't believe was a language, and put a hand flat on the
side of its face.

Then it went under, and the water closed, and Charles came up the bank wringing out his shirt and
said, "He'd like it known that you're extremely loud."

"I haven't said a word for twenty minutes!"

"No," Charles agreed. "You haven't."

---

On the ninth day she found Anselmo and asked him what it would cost to bring two more up this caño
in 1964, and wrote the figure in the back of the notebook, under the local names.

---

She left on the tenth morning. It was foul. She cried on the boat and Anselmo very kindly looked
at the bank.

She has a post box in Tucupita and a family who will take letters up. She has an arrangement to
come back in 1964 with proper equipment and a better hydrometer, and she has a promise to get the
temperature and salinity series copied out entire and the catfish written up, with his name on it,
and she has spent a good part of the journey down arguing with herself about whether one can
publish an author whose address is a caño, and has concluded that one can and that anybody who
objects may take it up with her.

And she has this, which she posted from Margarita on the eleventh of April 1962, addressed to the
station, marked personal, and which is almost entirely about the work. She wrote the last line of
it four times. The three she put over the side were the ones that argued.

> — and the local nomenclature was got for me not by our people but by a European resident of the
> lower caños, who has been at the fish since October 1954 and keeps a daily record of temperature,
> salinity, tide and rainfall in fifteen notebooks written on both sides and into the margins, and
> whose observations are a great deal better than mine and put my last three seasons to shame. He
> read your co-housing paper in 1959, in a mission at Guayo, four years late, and he says the
> intake correction is mine and that he knew it the moment he saw it, and he was insufferable about
> it for a whole afternoon.
>
> He is well. He is thin and he has had malaria and he is not coming back and does not wish to, and
> I am satisfied that this is his own judgement and not anybody else's.
>
> He asked me to tell you two things and made me repeat them. The first is that the animal is well.
> The second is that the tin was watertight.
>
> I am going out again in 1964. I have room in the boat.
