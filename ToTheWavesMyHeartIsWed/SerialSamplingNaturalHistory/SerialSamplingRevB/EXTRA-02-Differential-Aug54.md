# Differential

The noise Tar makes on the verandah isn't a noise a man makes about a colleague.

Pascual has spent twenty years listening to animals make sounds they didn't intend to
make, and the useful thing about such a sound is never its content, which is nothing; it
is that the animal didn't decide to produce it. He has heard Tar say *no* to the Colonel.
He has heard him say *no* to Pascual with a knife in it. He has never in a year heard him
make a sound he didn't first approve.

So. *Do you know him?* And: *We have worked together.* And then the man takes the boy away
with him and doesn't come back to the verandah at all.

At half past nine Pascual goes to his room to ask about Spilsbury.

He does not examine why he goes at half past nine rather than at breakfast, when the
question would keep perfectly well until breakfast. He has a rule about examining things
for which he has no instrument.

---

There is one lamp, and the shutter closed, and the level in the bottle is down four
fingers, and Pascual does the sum on the way to the chair because he can't help doing the
sum. Four fingers is a bad number. Four fingers is not a man having a drink, and it is not
a man drinking to be drunk either, which takes longer and is untidier. Four fingers in an
hour and a half, with the shutter closed, is a man taking a dose.

Then Tar tells him, and he tells it in order, because he is constitutionally unable to tell
anything in any other order, and Pascual sits and lets it come.

The Enthusiast overheard them. Naturally he did. Fleming; the Navy; the visit already on
the books and now carrying a second parcel. Salisbury, two years, which Pascual has been
guessing at for a year and had guessed lower. And then: he has told the boy to take the
animal tonight, and given him a chart, and they are gone by now or they aren't.

"You have put a compromised animal into open water," Pascual says, which is the objection,
and which is his to make, and which he is going to make even though he can hear that it is
not the largest thing in the room.

Tar gives him the figures.

He gives them without hesitating and without reaching for anything, urine and intake and
surfacing intervals and full lengths of the pen since the twenty-third, and he says *I have
watched him do it every evening since,* and then he stops, because he has heard himself.

The twenty-third to last night. Pascual does the sum before he has decided to do it,
because he can't help doing the sum, and it comes out at six.

*Ah,* Pascual thinks.

Six evenings at that pen. Six evenings, holding the samples that Pascual took, and in six
evenings not one question to the man who took them. Pascual has a great deal of data and no
permission to do anything with it, and this is a rule he has held for eleven months, and
holding it has become a species of physical effort, like carrying something up a stair.

He says the thing about the samples anyway. It comes out with more in it than he intends.
Tar looks at him as though the sentence has been handed to him in a language he doesn't
read, and reaches for the glass, and Pascual lets it go.

And then the man asks him — carefully, having plainly had the sentence ready since the
verandah — to stay away from the pens until after breakfast, and explains that this is so
that Pascual will be able to say he was told nothing and saw nothing, which will be true,
and that Tar was drunk in his room, which will also be true.

It is very neatly done. It is the sort of thing a man builds when he has been building
such things since he was young and has never once been able to stop.

And it is a lie. Not the content; the content is exact. Tar is sitting in a room at ten
o'clock at night with a witness and a dose because he can't be alone with what he has
done, and he has produced, at short notice, a procedural reason for it, and Pascual would
put money on his believing the reason entirely.

The man is blinking. Quickly. Pascual has known what that means for eight months and has
never told him he knows.

"And the boy," Pascual says. "He drags the animal down the beach, and he gets him past the
breakers, and then he walks back up on his own, does he."

"I have no data on that."

Pascual has data on that. He has months of it. He has a boy who wouldn't leave that pen
to eat, who had to be sent to his hut like a child to sleep, who came back grey and got in
the water again; he has a boy whose hand goes to that animal's face when he thinks nobody
is looking and who has never once been able to explain how he knew to go down there in the
night. He has watched him and been unable to name what he was watching, and he can name
this. He hasn't needed a design for it. He needed one morning, and he has had that since
the tarp.

He does not say it.

He sits in the chair with it and lets the man opposite him keep the sentence *I have no
data on that*, and he does not ask himself why, and gets no answer to the question he does
not ask.

---

Then Tar begins to talk about *him*, and everything else goes out of the room.

He stands closer than the argument requires; a foot, measured. He came to this room three
times when a note would have done and twice had nothing to say. He didn't look at Tar for
six days after the government labs and a man who thinks he was right doesn't do that. He
touches Tar only when angry and everyone else without meaning. He says the name
differently. That is five; there are eight.

*Eight,* Pascual thinks. He had needed one.

And in April, the plates, across the shoulder — Pascual remembers the plates, remembers the
whole afternoon, has in fact taken it out and looked at it more than once and put it back.
What he has out of that afternoon is the hands, which are small and quick and never once
uncertain, and the back of the neck when the man bent over the tray. He has never counted
these. It hasn't occurred to him that they could be counted.

And the two seconds, and the counting.

And then Jean, in the same room, at the same hour, under the same lamp.

"You ran a control," Pascual hears himself say. "On me. You ran a *control* on me."

"Of course I ran a control. Without one it is an impression, and I do not act on
impressions."

There is a thing happening in Pascual's chest that he would, in an animal, describe and
sample and not attempt to interpret. He is aware of being angrier than he has been in
years. He is aware that the anger isn't about the plates. It is that the man built an
apparatus to find out something he could have found out by asking, and then sat on the
result for four months, and has produced it now only because he has taken three whiskies
in order to stop being able to think about where a boy has gone.

Four months. April to August, while they screamed at each other on the verandah in front
of everyone, while Pascual told him he might as well have stayed with the government labs
and then couldn't look at him for six days and hated himself for both halves of it.

*You have known since April,* he thinks, *and you let me say that to you.*

He says none of this. What he says is, "And what am I, in your arithmetic? Now. Tonight."

And Tar opens his mouth, and nothing comes out of it.

"I don't know," he says.

*Coño*, Pascual thinks, and gets up, and takes the fourth glass off the desk and puts it
where the man can't reach it.

There is a quarter of a bottle gone and a hand going back to it every ninety seconds, and
he would take the glass off any man on this station in that state, and he has taken it off
this one. Both are true. Nothing he has would separate them; the two conditions have never
once occurred apart. He is not declining to look at it. He has looked at it. It does not
come out.

He does not go to the door.

He asks how much, and is told: three, and that three is insufficient, and that the figure
will have to be revised.

And then Tar stands up.

He stands up out of the chair like a man whose name has been called, and then he is simply
standing there with his hands at his sides, and doesn't do anything else. There is no next
thing. Pascual watches him not do it for what is probably four seconds and is not a short
four seconds, and understands, with perfect clarity and no charity whatever, that the man
wants something and has no procedure for getting it and will stand there until somebody
else supplies one.

He has seen animals go like that in a crush. He has never seen a man do it standing up in a
room.

---

He has the man by the shirt before he has decided anything, and after that there is a
period he couldn't give an account of.

What he retains, afterwards, is small and clinical and won't leave him: that Tar's
glasses go askew and he does not put them right; that his hands come up into Pascual's hair
without any of the deliberation that governs every other thing he does with his hands; that
he makes a sound, the second of the evening, and that Pascual is almost certain the man is
counting them.

Then Pascual has both hands on his face and has stopped, and is standing between his knees
breathing like a fool, and the whole of the last year is available to him for the taking,
and he says no.

He says it in the only frame he has that will hold. *I don't put a needle in a compromised
animal.* Which is true, which he has said to this man about Beta and said it in front of
Jean, and which is the one principle of his that Tar has never once been able to argue
with.

Taking his hands off the man's face is the hardest piece of work he has done since he came
to this station. He does it slowly, because doing it quickly would be a different message
and he has run out of messages he is prepared to send tonight, and he is aware the whole
time of exactly where the jaw sits in his palms, and that he is going to have it there at
four in the morning and again at breakfast and for as long after that as it cares to stay.

It is also convenient. He notes that it is convenient and declines to work out what it is
convenient *for*, and gets him a glass of water instead, and stands over him while he
drinks it the way he stands over the boy at meals, and Tar looks at the floor.

Then Pascual takes the chair.

"You are not staying."

"I'm staying," Pascual says, "because I'm not going down to the pens before breakfast,
and I'd rather be somewhere I can see the man who asked me not to."

Which is true, and isn't the reason. The reason is that there will be Americans on this
station inside two days, and the last thing on God's earth he intends to be is a man
who can be found on his own in a separate room and asked about a separate thing.

Tar lies down in his clothes and is asleep inside ten minutes, which tells Pascual more
about the state of him than the bottle did.

Pascual doesn't sleep much. He fetches the blanket at about two. Putting a blanket over a
sleeping man isn't touching him; he establishes this on the way across the room, and it
holds for about as long as it takes to do, and then he goes back to the chair and sits in
it three feet off for four hours. He does not go to the window; there is nothing at the
window he needs. He knows what is happening down there and he has known since the man said
*tonight*.

---

Breakfast is the hard part.

He eats it. He eats all of it, at the table, while John talks about the Americans and what
Fleming will make of it all, and the Colonel rumbles, and Jean sits with her hands round
her cup and is a great deal calmer than a woman ought to be who hasn't seen Cholmondeley
since yesterday.

*Ah,* Pascual thinks, and looks at her for perhaps two seconds, and puts it away unexamined,
because it isn't his and because he hasn't the evidence.

At nine o'clock he walks down.

The pen is empty. The water is flat and green at the sides where the algae grows and there
is nothing in it at all. The tarp isn't in the stores; he checks. The morning fish are in
the bucket where Juan left them at six, and the sun has been on them for an hour, and they will
have to be thrown out.

The boy's cot is where it has been for months, four feet from the ledge, and his
things are on it. His razor. Two shirts on the line, dry. A book. His log, squared up on
the box he uses for a table, with yesterday's entry in it, which says *No change.*

Pascual stands there for rather a long time.

Then he does what he does with any presentation he cannot resolve, which is to make the
list; and the list has one thing on it, and he writes down two more, because a list with
one thing on it isn't a list, it's a conclusion.
