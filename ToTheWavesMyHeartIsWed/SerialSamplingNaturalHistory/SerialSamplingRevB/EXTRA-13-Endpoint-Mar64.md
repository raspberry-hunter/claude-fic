# Endpoint

The correspondence runs to two years and forty letters and there is not one sentence
of feeling in any of them.

There is salinity. There is a daily temperature series from a caño below Guayo taken with a
home-made hydrometer, which Reggie has now had calibrated properly against three standards at the
Martin and which is out by four parts in a thousand and consistent, so that the whole eight years
of it can be corrected in an afternoon and used. There are the co-housing returns going the other
way, and the intake correction, and Delta's weights, and fourteen reprints, and a long argument
about a catfish that occupied the whole of 1963 and which Reggie lost.

He can produce, from memory, every letter in order. He can also produce the fact that in September
of 1962 he sat with a pen for forty minutes and did not write *I have thought about you every day
since the twenty-ninth of August 1954* and wrote instead a paragraph about oxygen tension, which
was accurate.

There will be a reckoning with the Colonel about six weeks' leave in the autumn. It is not today's
business.

---

Pascual nearly does not come.

His reasons are good and there are three of them and the first two are about the animals. Reggie
listens to the whole of it and then says that there is an assistant, and a second man since '61,
and John, and that the pens have run for a fortnight without either of them twice already, and
that none of this is why he is asking.

"Then why are you asking?"

"There is no operational reason whatever," Reggie says. "I should like you to be there. That is
the entire case and I am aware it is not much of one."

Pascual looks at him for a moment and then goes and gets his hat.

---

Jean has command of the expedition and is insufferable about it, and is entitled to be.

She has four hundred jars again and a great deal more money than in '62 and a Venezuelan
co-author and a permit and a launch, and four dozen notebooks and a gross of pencils in a tin box
that nobody is to touch, and she runs the thing like a woman who has been running things for four
years, which she has. She puts Reggie in the bow with the sounding line because, she says, he is
the only man aboard who can be trusted to write down what he actually sees.

He does that for three days. It is the best three days he has had in ten years and he notices at
some point on the second afternoon that he has been happy for about an hour without checking.

Anselmo and Pascual establish a complete understanding inside forty minutes and neither of them
has more than eighty words of the other's language. By the second morning they are conducting a
running professional conversation about the condition of the boat's engine, entirely in nouns, and
laughing. Jean is in it within the hour, being taught the Warao for *piston* and getting it wrong
four times running and enjoying herself extremely.

Reggie watches this from the bow with the sounding line in his hands and thinks that there is a
whole category of human competence he has never had five minutes of and that it is rather late to
begin.

The green goes on for hours and does not vary. It is very hot. He has a fever of some sort on the
second night and Pascual has it down inside a minute as nothing and gives him nothing and is
right.

---

There is a landing, and a path, and a bench under a shelter with the jars on it, and there are a
great many more jars than Jean described.

The man who comes round the corner of the house is thirty-four years old and thin and grey and
barefoot and is wearing spectacles that were made up in Tucupita by a man working from a guess.

Reggie has had two years of correspondence and forty letters and eight months' notice of this
exact afternoon and four hundred miles in a launch to compose himself, and none of it is worth
anything at all, and what happens is that he stops on the path and puts a hand out to the post of
the shelter and holds on to it.

"Hello, Reggie," Charles says.

*He is standing up. He is thirty-four. He has a house and a garden and a debt to a family below
and eighteen notebooks and he is standing up in front of me on his own two feet in the sun.*

"Your hydrometer is out by four in a thousand," Reggie says.

"I know. Jean told me in '62 and I haven't corrected it, on the grounds that it's consistent and
that you'd rather have the raw."

"I'd rather have the raw."

"I know," Charles says.

And then he comes up the path, and Reggie puts out a hand and takes hold of his shoulder.

He has not decided to do it. What he appears to have wanted is the reading — bone under cloth,
warm, at a particular height off the ground — which is the one measurement that has been
unavailable to him for nine years and seven months and which he has now taken.

Charles looks at him for about a second. Then he gets both arms round Reggie and pulls him
in, which exactly one other person has done in Reggie's life since about 1931.

There is no instruction anywhere in Reggie for letting go. He stands on the path in the sun and
does not, and Charles says something perfectly cheerful about the state of the path and does not
remark on it either.

It goes on for some considerable time. Nobody comments on it, then or afterwards. Jean is very
busy with the jars and Pascual is very busy with Anselmo and the engine, and Reggie is aware, in
the part of him that is always aware, that both of them are managing him, and he lets them.

---

He delivers the message on the second evening because he promised he would deliver it exactly and
he wants it done before he is too comfortable to do it.

"Hester gave me a sentence for you. She couldn't get away. The Colonel is — she couldn't get
away. She made me say it back to her twice and I have not altered a word of it."

Charles goes still.

"She says: *I was thirty-one, and it was four years, and nobody has ever asked me a single
question about it in twenty-six years, and you are the only person alive I could have told.*"

Charles sits down on the bench.

He sits there for rather a long time and does not say anything, and then he says, in a voice that
is not steady, "Thank you," and then, after a while, "Did she — do you know what that means?"

"No," Reggie says.

He has thought about it, in the launch, for four hundred miles. He has a machine in his head that
has never once declined a set of figures and it has been at that sentence since Antigua, and he
has been taking it off the sentence every time he catches it, deliberately, the way one takes a
dog off something.

"I have not worked at it," he says, "and I am not going to. It is not mine, and she gave it to me
to carry and not to open, and I find I am now able to do that. It has taken me about ten years."

---

On the third morning they are working a haul on the bench, jars and forceps and the good light,
and Charles says, without any preamble whatever:

"Thirty-nine letters."

"Forty."

"Forty letters," Charles says, "running to something over a hundred thousand words, of which
perhaps forty thousand are about oxygen tension, and not one single one of them mentions that
you're living with Pascual."

Reggie continues sorting.

"I didn't know how to put it in a letter."

"*Dear Charles. I am living with Pascual.*"

"Yes. Well."

"Ten years!" Charles is delighted. He has plainly been saving this and has had two years to get it
into order. "Jean told me in '62 and I've had to sit on it for the whole of two field seasons and
about twenty of your letters, and do you know what the worst of it is? I said so. In 1954. In
about my fifth week I said to Jean that what Dr Tar wanted was a fortnight's leave and somebody to
be — "

"Thank you," Reggie says.

" — and she said I was being impertinent, which I was, and she was quite right, and I should like
it noted that *I* was also — "

"*Thank* you, Charles."

Charles laughs and goes back to the fish, and it is a very good sound, and there is a full minute
of nothing but forceps.

Then he says: "Are you happy?"

Reggie puts a *Rivulus* into the jar and screws the cap down.

"I don't know how to answer that in the form in which you have put it. I have never been able to.
I can give you something else instead, if that will do."

"It will do."

"I haven't taken a drink for the purpose of stopping myself thinking since the ninth of March,
1955," Reggie says. "I know the date because I wrote the figure down that morning, which was four,
and it's the last entry there is."

Charles stops with a fish in his hand.

"Nine years."

"Nine years and about a fortnight."

"Reggie — "

"That's the whole of the answer and it's the only form of it I have got," Reggie says, "and I've
never said it aloud to anybody, including him, and I would be obliged if you did not make
anything of it."

Charles puts the fish in the jar and does not make anything of it, which is decent of him and
costs him something visible.

And then he says a thing Reggie is entirely unprepared for.

"You had to build it."

"I beg your pardon?"

"You had to build the whole thing. Wrongly, I imagine, and then again, and then it broke and
somebody mended it." Charles is looking at the water and not at him. "I've never had a first
move to make. Not one, in nine years. I didn't ask him and he didn't ask me and there was never a
point at which either of us could have said no, and I've gone round and round that and I'm not
going to get to the bottom of it in this life. It arrived complete. I've never once had to find
out whether he'd have me."

"Charles."

"Do you know what I'd give," Charles says, "for one bad evening? A real one. Where I said the wrong
thing and he was cross with me and I had to work out how to put it right, and then I put it right
and we both knew that I'd chosen to." He shrugs. "I've got the best thing anybody's ever had and I
can't test it. Not once. Not ever. You've tested yours every week for nine years."

Reggie stands with the forceps in his hand.

He has spent nine and a half years quietly certain that the man in front of him got the whole of
it and he got the difficult version, and he has never examined that once, and it turns out to have
been sitting in the open the entire time like everything else.

"I broke it once," he says at last. "Badly. It was entirely my doing and cost us
three months, of which six weeks were spent with me standing at a rail every night unable to say
nine words to him, and he did not come and fetch me, which was correct, and which I did not
understand for about a year. And I have thought ever since that this was the disgraceful part of
the record."

"It's the part I haven't got."

"Yes," Reggie says. "I'm beginning to see that."

They work for a while. Down at the landing Anselmo says something and Pascual laughs the enormous
laugh he keeps for engines, and both men on the bench look up at exactly the same moment and then
both look down again.

"He's very good for you," Charles says.

"He is the entire reason there is anything here to be good for," says Reggie, and puts the lid on
the jar, and finds that he has said it perfectly easily, out loud, to another person, in the
morning, and that the sky has once again declined to come down.

---

On the fourth day he gives Charles the six.

He has carried them since a quarter past ten on the evening of the twelfth of April 1955 and has
never said them out loud to anybody, not to Pascual and not to Jean, and he sets them out
properly, in order, with the objections and the answers to the objections, and it takes an hour
and twenty minutes.

Six things he could have done instead on the twenty-ninth of August. One of them so simple that
when it arrived he had to stand up and go to the window.

Charles listens to the whole of it without interrupting once, which Reggie is not expecting and
which he later understands to have been learned somewhere, from someone, and probably from a
mermaid.

Then Charles says: "Number four would have worked."

"Yes."

"It would have kept Monty at the Martin, safe, in a pen with Gamma and Delta, for the rest of his
life."

"Yes."

"Reggie," Charles says, "I'd have gone anyway."

Reggie does not say anything.

"I want you to hear it as a fact and not as a kindness, because I know what you do with kindness."
Charles is turning a jar round in his hands and looking at it and not at him. "I'd packed the
knapsack before you ever spoke to me on the verandah. Two days earlier, and I told myself it was
in case he was found by somebody. There was never any chance of my staying. Your number four was
a better plan than mine and it was a plan for a thing I didn't want and would have refused, and
you couldn't have known that, because I didn't know it myself until I was about twelve miles
out."

"You'd have gone with no chart."

"I'd have gone with no chart," Charles agrees, "and I'd be dead, and so would he. So your six
designs and my one bad idea produce, between them, exactly one arrangement in which we're both
alive, and it's the one that happened, and you built the half of it that worked. I've had eight
years to check that. I've checked it."

There is a long silence, and a bird somewhere going off like a cracked bell.

"That is a null result," Reggie says at last, unsteadily.

"No it isn't," says Charles. "It's a *result*. You're simply not used to them."

---

Beta comes to the landing on the fifth afternoon.

Monty. Charles calls him Monty. He has had it in forty letters and has written it himself
perhaps a hundred times, and he still thinks the other one first, every time, and corrects it, as
he has been correcting it for two years, and one day it will take.

Reggie is on the bank with the temperature book and does not see him arrive; he looks up and there
is a head and a pair of shoulders out of the water four feet from the bottom step and he has been
there for some unmeasurable time.

He is enormous. He is in the sort of condition Reggie has spent ten years and a great deal of
public money failing to produce in a pen, and his colour is entirely different, and Reggie's first
thought — instantaneous, involuntary, entirely creditable as science and disgraceful as anything
else — is *I was wrong about the diet and I have been wrong for ten years and it is the shrimp*.

His second thought is nothing at all.

Monty looks at him. It goes on. It goes on long enough that Reggie's hands begin to sweat on the
book and he makes himself keep still and be looked at, because it is the least he can do and it is
also, at this moment, the only thing he is capable of.

Then Monty turns his head and makes a short noise at Charles, and goes under, and the water closes.

"What did he say?"

"It isn't language, Reggie. I've told you fifty times."

"What did he say."

Charles rubs the back of his neck and looks faintly embarrassed. "He says you're the one who
brought the fish."

Reggie sits down on the bank.

Four months. The tank, the light, the eleven days of the crossed design, the thing with Charles
and Gamma, the whole apparatus of the worst piece of work he has ever done and the only piece of
his work anybody will remember in a hundred years — and the entire surviving entry in the animal's
account of him is *that one brought fish*.

It is not forgiveness. It is a great deal worse than forgiveness, because forgiveness would at
least require that he had registered as something. And he finds, sitting on the mud with the
temperature book open on his knees, that it is also the first time in nine years that he has been
able to breathe all the way down.

"Take it," Pascual says from behind him, some minutes later, arriving with two cups of coffee and
no comment whatever. "Take it and don't turn it over. Some readings you write down and go and
have your supper."

---

On the sixth evening Charles asks whether they can stay a fortnight rather than a week.

Reggie says yes.

He says it in about a second and a half, without consulting Jean, who is in charge, and without
consulting Pascual, and without any reason at all — not the hydrometer, not the notebooks, not
the catfish, not one of the four excellent operational grounds that his head produces about
fifteen seconds later and which he does not use, because he has already answered and because they
are not why.

Jean says it is perfectly all right and that she had allowed for it in the fuel.

---

She had allowed for it in the fuel.

Reggie sits with that after she has gone up to the house. She costed the extra week before they
left Margarita, which means she knew he would ask, which means she has been carrying an estimate
of him since at least February and has run the whole expedition on it and has not mentioned it to
anybody.

He goes down at about ten and finds her on the launch with the ledger and a lamp.

"May I put something to you."

"You may put whatever you like to me," Jean says, without looking up. "You've been able to do that
since 1954."

"There were nine annotations in that tin. Three of them were yours."

"I know. Charles told me in '62." She turns a page. "You needn't."

"The third was the fresh water on the two cays inside the north passage. I've had the figures for
that passage since the third of September 1954 and I've run them a great many times, and there's
no version of the first two days in which a man gets past the second night without it."

Jean stops writing.

"He was out of the water eleven hours on the thirtieth and about nine on the thirty-first. He
couldn't have been in it for those hours and there was nowhere else on that heading he could have
been. It doesn't come apart anywhere, and the reason it does not come apart is three lines written
on a Thursday afternoon by somebody who had been asked for a hazard sheet for the small boat."

The lamp does something in the draught and settles.

"You've had that ten years," Jean says.

"I have."

"Why am I hearing it now?"

"Because it didn't occur to me until this week that you hadn't got it. I had the figures and you
hadn't, and I never put the two facts side by side."

"In ten years."

"No."

Jean puts the ledger down on her knee.

"You are an extraordinarily annoying man."

"I'm aware of it."

She picks the ledger up again and turns back a page to find where she was, and he sits on the
thwart and lets her, which is a thing he could not have done in 1957 and can now.

"You'll have two years to work out what else you haven't told me," she says, without looking up.

Reggie does not answer at once, because he is establishing whether she has said what he believes
she has said.

"Two years."

"1966."

"You will be — " He stops. "Why."

Jean looks up.

"Because Charles wants you there. I can't altogether see why, but he does. And Pascual won't come
without you. And Anselmo's boy can do the engine, and Charles can't be trusted with a lead line,
and I'm not spending an entire season taking my own soundings." She goes back to the ledger. "I'm
still annoyed with you, so mind you work out the rest of it by then."

"Yes."

"Good. Good night, Reggie."

---

There is no rail. That is the thing he cannot get used to. He has stood at a rail every night for
nine years, at a numbered bolt, next to a specific man, and here there is a bank and mud and a
million insects and a sky with a great deal more in it than there is any need for.

They sit on the bottom step of the landing with their feet in the water, which Pascual says will
give them both something and which he does anyway.

"I have had a question since the twenty-ninth of August 1954," Reggie says, "and I have had no
means of answering it and I have run the arithmetic on it perhaps a thousand times, and this
afternoon he came up the path with a bucket in his hand. And I find I do not know what the
machinery is supposed to do now. It has been on that for nine years."

"It will find something."

"Yes. That's rather what I'm afraid of."

Pascual takes his wrist, two fingers laid along the inside of it, the way he does, the way they
both do now, and holds it and says nothing for a while.

"It's going very fast," he says.

"I'm aware of that."

"I'm not taking a reading," Pascual says. "I'm telling you what it's doing. That's a different
service and I've decided to offer it."

And Reggie laughs — out loud, on a step, in the dark, four hundred miles up a river, at
fifty-two-year-old Pascual Pobil being pleased with himself — and hears the noise he has made and
does not go back and examine it.

Up at the house, Jean is arguing with Charles about the catfish. She is losing. She has been
losing all week and is enjoying it enormously.

---

Somewhere in the second week, after dark, on the bench with the lamp going and the insects coming
at it, Reggie says:

"I should like to put some things to you and I have been assembling them for four days, and I
would rather you did not stop me in the middle."

"All right."

"I was a bad chief to you and I was a considerably worse supervisor, and I want the particulars
said out loud because you have never had them said and because I do not intend to leave here
without having said them.

"One. In the March of 1954 I gave you the difficult animal deliberately. Not because you were the
right man for him. Because I judged that you would go at him harder than anybody else on that
station and I wanted the result and I did not tell you that was why.

"Two. When you asked for co-housing I refused you, and every reason I gave was true, and not one
of them was my reason.

"Three. I then ran a design on you for eleven days without informing you, having decided in
advance that informing you would spoil the arm.

"Four. I never once said to you that your work was good. Not one word in six months. And it
was good. It was better than mine had been at your age and I knew it inside your first term."

He stops. The insects go on at the lamp.

"There's one more," Charles says.

"The twelfth morning. Yes." Reggie has both hands flat on the bench. "By then it had already come
apart. There was a result on the ground larger than the one I had spent the eleven days trying to
get. And I opened my mouth to start it up again. I did not decide to. There was no design in it
whatever, which is why it is the one I mind, because the other four I can at least call stupidity,
and that one was simply what I was when nothing was holding me."

Charles does not say anything for a while.

Then he says: "Yes. All of that's true and I'm not going to tell you it wasn't."

"No. Don't."

"And since we're doing lists I'll give you mine, and mine isn't in your order."

He turns the lamp down a little, which is something to do with his hands.

"The eleven days is not third on my list. It's first and there's nothing within a mile of it. On
the eleventh night I came down and he was out of the water. On the concrete, in the dark, dry all
the way across the back — he had got himself up that ladder on his arms alone and crawled, and he
had been lying there for hours, because there was nobody who was permitted to come." His voice is
not steady. "I got him onto a tarp and I got him back in the water, and he settled, and I thought
I'd got there in time. I went to bed thinking I'd got there in time. And then in the morning
Pascual told me I might be wrong, and I've never in my life had the floor go out from under me
like that, before or since. I was twenty-four and there wasn't one person in that place I
could say a single word of it to.

"I've never had that in a box. It comes up perhaps four times a year and it comes up entire, and I
expect it'll go on doing that until I die, and I've decided to let it come and to get on with the
day afterwards. That's the whole of my management of it. It isn't a cure and I'm not offering it to
you as one."

Reggie sits with his hands on the bench and does not say anything, because there is nothing that
can be said to it and because saying something would be for his own benefit.

"The other one is smaller and it came up a great deal more often," Charles says. "You were the
only person there whose good opinion was most important to me and in six months I never had
a syllable of it, so I decided the obvious thing, which was that I was a competent pair of hands
and nothing else whatever. I took that decision at twenty-four and I carried it about with me
every day for nine years.

"And then in the February of 1963 your third letter came. You said the surfacing intervals I took
in '54 were the only clean series anybody ever had off that animal and that you had quoted them
three times." Charles laughs, not quite evenly. "I read it about nine times and then I went and
sat outside for an hour."

"I've quoted them four times."

"*Reggie.*"

"I am aware of what that was," Reggie says. "I have not got anything else to give you and the
figure is at least accurate."

---

Later Reggie says: "Then how is it that I am sitting on your bench."

"Three things, and none of them is that I forgave you, because I don't believe I've ever done that
in the sense you mean." Charles counts on his fingers, which is a thing he does. "One. I'm ten
years older and I've been responsible for things, and I've made two decisions on this caño as bad
as any of yours, and one of them cost a man his boat. He has a new one and he's never once
mentioned the old one to me. It's very difficult to keep a grievance in good repair after that.

"Two isn't a virtue and I'll put it as arithmetic, since that's the currency here. You built the
half of it that worked. I've been alive for nine years and married for nine years on the strength of
it, and I'm not able to hold a grudge against the reason I've got my life, and you may check that
sum if you like.

"Three is the real one. I've spent nine years with somebody who can't tell me anything. Not one
proposition, ever, in a decade. I get what he sends and I never get why, and I've had to learn to
sit with a person who has an entire interior and no means whatever of putting it out — and to stop
requiring that it be put out before I'll credit that it's there."

Reggie sits very still.

"So when your letters started coming I didn't read a cold man," Charles says. "I read a man
transmitting on the only channel he's got, at very great length, about oxygen tension. I've had
nine years of practice at precisely that and I'm extremely good at it now, and I should think I'm
the only person alive who was in a position to be good at it *before* you learned to talk."

"In the September of 1962," Reggie says, "I sat down with a pen for forty minutes and did not
write *I have thought about you every day since the twenty-ninth of August 1954*, and wrote a
paragraph on oxygen tension instead, and posted it."

Charles does not say anything at all.

"I am saying it now," Reggie says, "because it is true, and because I have been able to say things
for about four years, and because you've just told me you'd have read it off the oxygen tension in
any case, and I find that I do not care to have you take it off an instrument when I am sitting
here."

They sit for a bit. The lamp is going down.

"There's one thing I haven't got past," Charles says, "and I'll say it, since we're doing records.
There's a bound volume at the mission at Guayo with the '55 co-housing paper in it. I know the
shelf and I've known since 1959."

"There is no paper on those days," Reggie says. "I want that said. I did not write it. There was
never any question of my writing it — not out of decency; I had nothing to write, the arm was
spoiled on the eleventh day and the record from it is worthless. What is in that volume is the
co-housing series with eleven days missing out of the middle of it, and anybody who has the
figures in front of him can see the hole."

"Ah."

"Do not read it," Reggie says. "I am not asking you not to. I am telling you that there is nothing
in it for you and that the gap is the only true thing in the whole of it."

"That isn't the only true thing in it," Charles says. "The method works. That's why I don't open
the volume, and it isn't the reason you think."

He sits forward with his elbows on his knees.

"How many are there now?"

Reggie does not have to look it up.

"Seven at the Martin. Six at Bimini. Four in Florida, and a pair at Naples that I have not seen and
am told are doing well. Nineteen alive in tanks. In the August of 1954 there were two and one of
them was dead."

"And every one of those nineteen is there because the method works."

"Yes."

"I opened it once," Charles says. "1959, at Guayo, four years late. I got as far as the abstract
and shut it again and went and sat on the step, and I did the sum out there, and it is not a
difficult sum. A way of keeping them alive in captivity is a licence to take more of them. You'll
have had that a good deal earlier than I did."

"The September of 1955. On the apron, the day the second pen was filled. It took about as long as
the water took to come up two feet." Reggie has his hands flat on the bench again. "I have never
written it down and I have said it aloud to one person."

"Pascual."

"Pascual."

"Well," Charles says. "It was mine."

Reggie does not say anything.

"I asked you for it three times in that January. You refused me and your reasons were all true and
not one of them was your reason, and you put that on your own list twenty minutes ago as a thing
you did to me." He is looking out at the black water. "It isn't on my list at all.

"And it wasn't an idea and I didn't invent it, and I want that put properly, because it's the part
that won't let me alone. I sat by that pen for four months and I watched him and got in the water
with him, and what I saw was that he was dying of being by himself, and I came and told you so.
That's all it was. It's the only thing I've ever given anybody that was worth having, and there
are nineteen of them in concrete because I gave it to you, and I can't wish it back. That's my
whole difficulty. I've had five years of it and I'm no further on."

"Gamma."

"Gamma. She'd have been dead by the March of '55 and you know that better than I do."

"She would have been dead by the February."

"There you are, then." Charles laughs, once, with nothing in it. "I'm the only person alive who has
both, Reggie. I've got mine out there in his own water, tonight, doing whatever he likes, and I've
got the thing I gave you that's put nineteen of them in tanks, and I can't give either of them
back."

The lamp gutters and neither of them attends to it.

"There is a version of the argument in which you are wrong about Gamma," Reggie says.

"I know the version. I've had it out with myself perhaps a hundred times and I've never once been
able to finish it."

"Nor have I."

They sit.

"What I wonder about," Charles says at last, "is the figure. How many have been taken that would
not have been taken but for that paper. It gets at me perhaps once a month and it has done since
'59, and I've never worked it out and I'm not going to."

"I have it."

Charles puts a hand up straight away.

"No," he says. "No — I wasn't asking. I want it on record that I wasn't asking. Keep it."

And Reggie keeps it, and sits in the dark next to a man he has known for ten years, and finds that
he is a good deal better at this than he was.

---

They go down on the twenty-first.

The temperature and salinity series is copied out entire and packed in oilcloth, eight years of it,
in four hands and four different inks. The catfish is settled and will go in
under his name with a caño for an address, and Jean has undertaken to fight anybody who wishes to
make an issue of it, and will win. There is a box of glassware left behind, and a proper
hydrometer, and two hundred labels, and a standing arrangement about the post at Tucupita, and a
season fixed for 1966 with Pascual's name on the list, written there by Pascual.

Charles comes down to the landing with them and stands about in the way that people do.

Jean gets hold of him with both arms and hangs on to him for a good ten seconds and says something
into his shoulder that nobody else is meant to hear, and then holds him off at arm's length by the
elbows and tells him that if the '66 notebooks are not ready she will come up this caño and take
them off him herself, and kisses him on the cheek, and goes aboard blowing her nose.

Then, when she is settled and Pascual is doing something entirely unnecessary with a rope that
requires him to have his back turned:

"I never finished my sentence. On the third day."

"No," Reggie says.

"What Dr Tar wanted," Charles says, "was a fortnight's leave and somebody to be glad to see him."

Reggie stands on the bottom step with his hat in his hand.

"I was twenty-four and I'd been on that station five weeks and I couldn't have told you then why I
said it." He puts his hands in his pockets. "Well. You've had the fortnight. And as to the other
half of it — there's more than one person glad to see you, Reggie."

And he smiles at him.

There is a very long moment in which Reggie discovers that he has nothing at all, in any register,
in either of the two languages he has ever been fluent in, which are figures and refusal.

"Don't try to answer it now," Charles says comfortably. "You'll write."

And he steps back off the boards to let the launch away, and stands there with the green going up
a hundred feet behind him and the jars in rows on the bench and eighteen notebooks in the house on
the posts — barefoot, thirty-four years old, mended shirt, absurd spectacles, waving with his
whole arm like a boy on a station platform — and he is still doing it when the
caño turns, and Reggie watches him the entire way, right up until the green closes.
