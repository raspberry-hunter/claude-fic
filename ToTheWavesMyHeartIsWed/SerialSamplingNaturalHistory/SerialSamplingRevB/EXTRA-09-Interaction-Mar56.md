# Interaction

He does not start it. He has never started it.

That is what he finds on the fourteenth of March, on the stairs, going up behind a man who has
read every private paper he possesses and has just handed him the fifteenth of January like a
thing off a shelf. It is not a new fact. It is a fact that has been sitting in the open for
eighteen months without ever once being counted, which is the sort of thing he would sack a
student for.

Sixty-one occasions. He has the figure before the landing, because of course he has the figure.
Sixty-one, and on every one of them the first move was made by somebody else, and on forty of
them he had walked up the stairs on purpose in order to be there when it was made.

He does nothing about it. There is nothing available to be done about it at half past one in
the morning with the door already shut behind him.

---

What is different is that there is nothing left to keep.

He had not understood how much of the apparatus was in service of that. The sheet is read. The
whisky figures are read and the absence of a third one is read, which was the item he had been
most careful about; and the fifteenth of January is not only known but has been taken away from
him and answered, and there is no operation left running anywhere in his head.

He would have predicted exposure. What he gets is an hour with nothing whatever arriving in it
about himself.

He files it as a new thing.

It is not a new thing. It is the December of 1954, in every particular, and he does not go and
look, because the whole of that December has the design lying across it and he has not opened
that stretch of the record since it was read out to him at a rail. So he has two states and
three headings to put them under, and he does not notice that he is short by one.

It is not the August way, which was a dose. It is not the April way, which was an hour of being
looked at while he made a decision every four seconds and had no data for any of them.

What he finds is that he can attend to Pascual. Not in bursts and not against anything: at
length, continuously, for as long as the thing goes on, with nothing arriving on the other side
that has to be dealt with first. He has not been able to do that since April.

And there is a great deal to attend to.

He will have all of it afterwards, in order, at a resolution he has no use for. The weight of a
hand and where it went and how long it stayed. Two changes in the breathing that he could put a
time to. A place at the base of the throat that produces a particular sound when his mouth
is on it at a particular pressure; established twice, and then a third time, and the third time
was not to establish anything.

None of it goes to the bench. He does not take it there and he does not notice that he has not
taken it there, and it sits where it is, in no order, doing nothing at all.

He does not count, and the not counting is not an effort, because counting would want his
attention and his attention is spoken for.

At some point he becomes aware that his eyes have been shut for a considerable while. He does
not open them.

At some point he says something. He has no idea what it is, and afterwards he does not go
looking for it, and Pascual — who has stopped, and is holding on to the back of his neck, and
has his mouth against Reggie's temple — makes a sound that Reggie will be keeping.

And he has the half minute before that, of which he has more than he has of anything else that
has ever happened to him.

He has the breathing altering at the top of it — not louder; shorter, the interval halving and
halving again until there is not one. He has the sound that begins somewhere underneath
that, which is not a word and is not addressed to anybody and which Pascual does not appear to
know he is making. He has the rhythm of it accelerating, and going on accelerating past the
point where a man could be doing it on purpose, and then coming apart altogether at the end,
which he will still be in the middle of two days later in the laboratory with a pipette in his
hand. He has the grip on the back of his neck. He has the whole weight of him coming down and
staying down. He has the heat of him and the mouth at his temple and the fact that at no point
in any of it was one single thing required of Reggie except to be where he was.

He has the four seconds afterwards in which nothing at all happens.

Later he lies in the dark with his heart going and thinks, quite calmly, that he has spent
eighteen months waiting on a landing for a man to decide something, and that he has never in his
life waited on a landing for anything else, and that there is a name for what that is and he is
not going to look for it tonight.

---

The other finding is John's and John does not know he made it.

It is the twenty-sixth of March. Reggie comes into the drawing office at eight in the morning
and the whole of the March plate work is done — the pen elevations, the scale bars, the lot,
correct in every particular — and John is extremely pleased and says that he had noticed Reggie
was looking rather done in and that it was no trouble at all, old man, no trouble at all.

Reggie thanks him. He does it properly, and gets out of the room, and stands in the corridor in
a condition he can only describe as fury.

He has no ground for it. There is no ground. The work is good, the work is John's own province,
nobody has been inconvenienced and something has been taken off him that he did not want. He is
still standing there four minutes later with his hands on the sill and his ears going, and he
makes himself do the thing he does, which is to hold still and take the reading rather than get
rid of it.

Then he goes and gets the sheet out of the drawer and reads the entry for the twenty-second of
January, which he wrote in a state of considerable bafflement, and it comes up whole in about
the time it takes to sit down.

He has been treating the act as the treatment.

That is the error and it is a first-year error and he has been sitting on it since January. The
act is not the treatment. The act is only the instrument by which a proposition is delivered
about the man it is done to, and the man cannot decline any part of that proposition, because it
arrives joined.

John's has two clauses in it. *You are tired* — which is true, and which is the astonishing part,
because Reggie has been at the typewriter until two for a fortnight and John notices nothing
whatever and has noticed this. And *therefore you cannot do your own plates*, which is false, and
which is the entire content of the last four minutes.

He cannot say so. That is the second half of the finding and it takes him rather longer to get
at, sitting in his room with the drawer open. There is no form of words in which a man says *I
could perfectly well have done the plates* that is not vanity and ingratitude both, and so the
false clause is delivered, in front of the drawing office, and stands, and the only response
available to him is to thank the man for it.

In January there were two clauses as well and both of them were false. Pascual was in good
order; he had had a fortnight of nothing but routine and had said so; and a man of thirty-nine had
come and done his evening pens for him and asserted the opposite in the plainest terms available
and in his own handwriting. And Pascual had stood on the apron with a bucket in his hand and said
something short about the log format, because the log format was the only part of it he was
permitted to object to.

It is not an effect of the act at all. It is an interaction: the act with the condition, and
neither of them means anything by itself, and one may vary the act for the rest of one's life
and never once get at it.

He writes that down in one line and puts it under the mortality tables. It is the most useful
thing he has had in a year and it is entirely unusable in advance, because to apply it he must
know what is true about a man at a given hour, and there is no instrument for that and never
will be, and there is only looking at him.

---

The letter comes on the fourth of May.

He knows about it inside the hour, because Jean comes and tells him — not what is in it; that
Pascual read something at the stores and put it in his shirt and went and did the morning pens
— and Jean has never once come to him with anything of that kind and does not stay to be asked
why she has now.

He goes down at four. He has spent an hour and a half assembling four sentences and he says
three of them.

The third is the one that costs, and he has had it since about half past two, and it is: *I want
to be clear that there is no operational reason for any of this. I have arranged it anyway.*

He has been trying to say a sentence of that class since he was twenty-nine. Everything he has
ever arranged has had a reason attached to it, and the reason has always been true, and the
reason has always also been the thing that made it safe to do — a man doing something for a
reason cannot be asked what else he might have been doing it for. He hands the whole of that
over on a concrete apron at four in the afternoon and finds that the sky does not come down.

Pascual says there is no point in going.

Reggie says the schooner is there until Friday, and goes away, and does the evening pens for
three nights without mentioning it, and on the ninth Pascual sails.

He has no account of what changed. He has thought about it perhaps twice and stopped both
times, and it is not the same as the fifteenth of January; it is simply not his, and he has
found that he can now leave a thing alone provided he decides to leave it alone at the outset,
before the machinery has had it.

---

The six weeks are not what he expected either.

He expected it to be acute. He gets something with no crisis in it whatever, which turns out
to be worse in one narrow respect and better in every other, and the narrow respect is that a
thing with no crisis in it cannot be got through, because there is nothing to get through.

He works. The work is good. He answers Spilsbury's second round of questions and starts the
new paper, and the new paper is a better piece of work than the first one, and he knows exactly
why: the first one was written by a man routing round a hole and this one is not.

And in the second week of it he takes the intake correction — the one for water temperature,
which is what makes the whole of the 1955 result defensible and without which Spilsbury would
have had the thing apart in a fortnight — and looks up who did it, and it was Jean, in October
1954, on her own, in a notebook, and it went into the first paper under nobody's name at all.

He goes back further and finds two more.

He puts her second author on the new one. It takes three letters — one to the Colonel, one to
the editor, who queries it, and one to Spilsbury, who does not object and merely wishes to know
whether Reggie is quite sure, which is a sentence that means what it means — and he wins it, and
it holds, and it costs him something at the Colonel's end that he will be paying off for a year.

Then he tells Jean, and also tells her about the first paper, which he cannot fix.

She is angry. It lasts about four seconds and she does not trouble to conceal any of it, and
then she says thank you, and then she says that the temperature correction was the easy part
and that the surfacing intervals were the difficult one and nobody has ever asked her about
those either.

Reggie writes none of this down. He has understood, since March, that he is not owed a reading,
and that a man who does a decent thing and then goes and records how it went has taken his
payment in a currency the other party never agreed to.

---

He posts a letter on the twenty-second of June.

He has written four. Three of them are in the stove. What goes into the bag is a single page of
Gamma and Delta figures with the surfacing intervals set out properly, because Pascual has
never once been given those in a form he could use, and two sentences at the end.

The first sentence says that Jean is second author on the new paper and why.

The second sentence he works at for some time and it comes out as: *The pen is being kept up to
your standard and it is a good deal more trouble than you have ever let on.*

He is aware of exactly what he is doing. The whole of the fifteenth of January, as he now has
it, is that the thought occurred in a room the other man was not in, and a letter is nothing but
evidence that a thought occurred in a room the other man was not in, and he has therefore
constructed a vehicle for the one thing he was told could not be arranged.

He stands with the letter over the bag for some while.

Then he posts it, on the grounds that a man who declines to do a good thing because he has
worked out why it is good has arrived at a position no reasonable person could defend, and that
he has spent two years arriving at positions of that kind and has nothing whatever to show for
it.

---

The other thing that happens in the six weeks is that he keeps going down to the rail.

Every night, at half past one, and he stands at the tenth bolt, which is where the man would be
if the man were there. He is aware of it on the first night and does not correct it, and by the
end of the second week he has stopped noticing that he does it.

On the second of June, Gamma brings Delta up to the rail. Both of them, together, at the near
end, which she has done alone perhaps forty times and has never once done with him following.

Reggie turns round to say so, and there is nobody to say it to, and he is left standing on a
concrete apron at two in the morning holding a piece of information with no destination, and he
finds it is the worst quarter of an hour of the six weeks and that nothing whatever has gone
wrong.

---

The schooner is in on the fourth of July and he goes down to the jetty, which he has never done
for anybody.

Pascual comes up the steps looking, at first, entirely himself. It takes about a day and a half
before Reggie has it properly, and what he has is that the man has come back smaller. He is
quiet at meals in a way that is not his quiet. He does the work exactly and does not complain
about any of it, and Pascual complains about everything and always has, and its absence is the
loudest thing on the station.

Reggie does not ask. He has the sheet's whole education behind him now: the wrong occasion
ruins the act.

On the third evening they are at the rail and Pascual says, without being asked, that his
brother's house is to go to Rosa, and that there was some talk of his stopping, and that there
were four people in that room who assumed he would and one who did not say anything at all, and
that he came back.

He does not say why he came back. Reggie can do the arithmetic and declines to do it out loud.

What he says instead is:

"On the second of June, Gamma brought Delta up to the rail. Both of them. She has never done it
with him following and she has done it twice since." He keeps his eyes on the water. "I turned
round to tell you and you were not there. It was two in the morning and I stood here for about
a quarter of an hour, and I did not know what to do with it, and I have not known what to do
with it since."

There is a silence of some length.

"Ah," Pascual says, in a completely different voice from the one he has been using for three
days.

---

Afterwards Reggie will note that this is the first occasion in his life on which he has said a
true thing about himself to another person for no reason other than that the other person
needed it, and that he did it standing up, sober, at a rail, and that it took nine seconds.

He will also note that it worked, and that noting it is a fault, and that he has ceased to be
able to stop himself noting things and has begun instead simply not to act on them, which is a
poorer method and appears to be the only one available.

---

They go up at about one.

And on the landing he stops, because he has been carrying the sixty-one since March and it is
now seventy and it has to be dealt with tonight or it will be seventy-one.

There is no procedure. He has been over it for three months and there is nothing to construct,
because the whole point of the thing is that it must not be constructed; a first move that has
been designed is not a first move, it is a submission. He has no opening. He has never in his
life gone first at anything that was not a piece of work.

He had assumed, in March, that the name he was declining to look for was one of the two or three
obvious ones, and he had put off looking at it in the way a man puts off a bill he intends to
pay. It is not any of those. What waiting on a landing is, is the twenty-ninth of August: it is
arranging matters so that the decision belongs to somebody else, and standing where one can be
found, and never afterwards being obliged to establish what one would have done. He has been
doing it since he was twenty-nine. He did it to a boy with a chart in a tin.

The obvious names are all true as well. They are simply not the one that was being kept from
him.

He takes his glasses off and folds them and puts them on the shelf by the door, which is a thing
he does at some point every night and has never once done at this point, and he sees Pascual see
it.

Then he puts a hand flat on the side of the man's face — which he has had done to him twice and
has never done, and which he has been thinking about since March, and which is the only gesture
he possesses that he is certain means something because he knows what it did when it was done to
him — and he holds it there, and does not move it.

His hand is not steady. There is nothing to be done about that.

He does not manage anything to say. What he manages is a sound, in the back of his throat, and
it is uncontrolled and he does not care, and it appears to be sufficient, because Pascual makes
a noise like a man struck and comes forward into it.

Reggie shuts his eyes, because that is the condition, and finds that he can do nothing whatever
with them shut.

He opens them, and does not revise the filing.

Going first, he discovers, is not one act. It is a great many of them in succession, each of
which has to be undertaken separately and on no more warrant than the last.

He gets the man against the door. That takes his own weight and both hands and he is the
smaller of them by a good deal, and Pascual has never in his life been moved anywhere by
anybody, so the fact that it works is not a fact about strength. He is being *let*. He has
never been let before, because he has never asked for anything, and there is a difference
between a thing handed to a man who did not ask and the same thing handed to a man who did, and
the difference is going through him at a rate he can do nothing about.

His mouth is at the base of the throat, where he established something in March and did not
write it down. The sound arrives.

So he does it again.

That is the whole of the decision and there is no interval in it anywhere. He does it again and
it arrives again and it is not the same sound the second time, and he has both of them, and he
will have both of them in November. Pascual's head goes back against the door and stays there
and his hand shuts in the back of Reggie's shirt and does not let go, and Reggie discovers that
he can produce that, and that he can produce it a second time by doing what he did the first
time, which is the plainest result he has ever obtained and he is not obtaining it.

There is something going on in his chest for which he has no heading. He does not go looking
for one.

What he has instead, arriving at the same moment and out of the same fact, is the other thing.

Nothing in this room is being done to him. It is happening because of him, and it is going on
because he is going on, and if he stops it stops, and there is no version of the next ten
minutes in which he is not the operative variable.

He established on a landing, at some point earlier this evening, that he has spent thirty-nine
years arranging matters so that this is never the case. He finds he cannot say how long ago.
That has not happened to him before and he has nowhere to put it. The landing is four feet away
on the other side of the door.

And a man who is the cause of a thing can be the fault in it.

He gets it wrong twice.

The first time is a question of position and he has it inside a second, because everything
under his hands changes at once and the breathing stops being breathing and becomes a man
waiting civilly for something to be over. He has read exactly that off a hundred animals. He
has never once had to read it off a person he was doing it to, and there is no procedure
anywhere in him for what a man does next.

He does not apologise. A hand comes up and moves his by about two inches and puts it back down,
and nothing whatever is said about it, and Pascual's whole weight comes forward again as though
the thing had not occurred.

The second is worse and is over faster and is dealt with in the same way.

Both times he goes on, which is the part he had not thought he had in him. He had assumed —
without examining the assumption, which is how he holds most things about himself — that an
error of that class ends the matter, because it is what he would do. He has ended a man's
employment for less. Twice, and he could give the dates.

It does not end it. It is not so much as filed.

At some point Pascual says his name in the way he says it, the difference he has never been
able to characterise and has had down as observation four since April of 1954; and then
something in Spanish that Reggie does not have and is not going to ask about; and then, against
his ear, unsteadily: "You've no idea what you look like. You've never once had the smallest
idea."

And Reggie stops for a second with his forehead against the man's shoulder, which is the one
thing he already knew how to do and the only part of the evening he has not had to invent,
because the whole of it has just gone through him at once — sixty-one occasions, and seventy,
and a landing, and a year of standing a foot too close, and every single one of them arriving
in the same place at the same time.

His eyes are shut. He does not notice.

Then he does not stop again for a considerable while.

---

Much later, in the dark, Pascual says: "You've been thinking about that."

"Since March."

"March." Pascual turns his head. "Four months."

"I hadn't a procedure and I wasn't able to construct one, and I've been informed on excellent
authority that certain things cannot be arranged."

"Ay," Pascual says, and then, after a moment, unsteadily, "and so you simply — "

"I simply did it," Reggie says. "It was appalling. I should like to record that it was
appalling throughout and that I intend to do it again on Thursday."

Pascual laughs, once, into the pillow, which he has not done since before the letter came, and
then he does not stop for a while either.

"Not Thursday," he says, when he is able to. "Whenever you want. Don't put it in the
programme."

Then he reaches over in the dark and takes hold of Reggie's wrist with two fingers laid along
the inside of it.

Reggie lies still and lets him have the reading.

It is the only instrument either of them has ever had that tells the truth without asking
anybody's permission, and there is a great deal on it tonight, and he has decided that the man
may have all of it.
