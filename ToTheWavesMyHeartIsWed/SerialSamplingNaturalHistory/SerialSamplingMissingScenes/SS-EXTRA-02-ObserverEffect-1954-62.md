# Observer Effect

## I. Before

She had Dr Pobil down within a week, and what she had him down as was *too much*.

He came in the February of 1954 and by the end of the month there was nobody on the station he
hadn't got something out of. He knew Juan had four children and the ages of all of them. He knew
which of the boat crew had a bad back and had done something about it. He talked at a volume you
could place from the far end of the yard, he touched people on the arm while he was talking to
them and had no idea he was doing it, and if you sat down next to him at supper you did not get up
again for fifty minutes. He was in your business by the Thursday. He was generally right about
your business, which was worse.

Jean, who had been on that station eleven months and had constructed her working life out of not
being noticed until she had something to be noticed for, found him a considerable nuisance.

So did half the Martin, quietly. John said he was a marvellous fellow and then avoided him for a
fortnight. The Colonel got a lecture on the state of the water tanks and did not enjoy it. It is
perfectly possible to be exhausted by a man and to be unable to say one bad thing about him, and
that was the position the whole station arrived at by about April, and it made everybody
slightly ashamed of themselves, which did not help.

Dr Tar came in the March and was a different problem altogether.

He did not fill a room; he audited it. He asked you what you meant and then asked you again when
your answer was worse than your first one. He noticed everything and reported none of it. He said
*quite* and it was not agreement. And within a fortnight of his arrival he had reorganised three
things that had needed reorganising for two years and been thanked for none of them, because
nobody could think of a way to thank a man who had not appeared to want anything.

Jean's assessment, entered privately in the first week of April 1954 and not revised for two
years, was: *a cold one, and very good, and I shall learn a great deal and not enjoy it.*

Two of the three turned out to be right.

---

They loathed each other from about the third day, in the way that only two men can who are both
entirely serious about the same animal.

It was appalling. It was also, Jean will admit now, the most interesting thing that had happened
at the Martin since she got there. Pobil went at him in public, at volume, with the whole
station in earshot; Tar went white about the mouth and said nothing, which was in its own way
worse. Jean sat through eleven evenings of it in the summer of 1954 and formed a professional
opinion, which was that the Colonel was going to have to send one of them home, and that it
would be the wrong one, and that she had better have her own work in a state where it did not
depend on either.

She has been more wrong about things since, but not often, and not by so much.

---

## II. Charles

It was Charles who said it, in the August, four days before he went.

He was on the verandah with his glasses pushed up on his forehead in the way that always made
him look about nineteen, and Pobil was down at the pen doing something and Tar was standing at
the rail with the notebook, forty feet apart, not speaking, and Charles watched them for a while
with an expression Jean did not care for.

"They'll be very happy," he said.

"They detest one another."

"Mm," said Charles.

"He called him a man who ought to be in a government laboratory. On the verandah. In front of
the Colonel."

"He did."

"In front of *me*."

"You told me," Charles said, and went on watching, and did not stop smiling, and it
was not a nice smile at all; it was the smile of somebody at a play he has already read.

Jean asked him what on earth he thought he was looking at.

And Charles said: "Nothing you can't see. I've just been looking at it longer." Which was a lie,
because he'd been on that station five months and she'd been on it a year and a half, and she
knew it was a lie at the time and let it go, because he was twenty-four and had had a
frightful summer and was entitled to a little mystery if it was doing him any good.

She has thought about that afternoon a great many times since. She has never got any further
with it. He would not say how he knew, and she is fairly certain by now that he could not have
said, and she has stopped minding.

What she does mind, slightly, is that he was right, and that he never had the decency to be
smug about it in writing.

---

## III. After

Nothing whatever happened, as far as Jean could see, for two years.

That is not quite fair. A great many things happened; she simply catalogued all of them wrong.

She noticed, in the autumn of 1954, that Tar was being fed. There was a plate put in front of him
and a man standing over him until it was gone, and Pobil doing it in the same voice he used for a
horse and Tar putting up with it, and Jean filed this as medical, because Pobil did it to
everybody and had done it to her twice and it was extremely annoying and she had eaten the
sandwich.

She noticed that they stopped rowing in public, and filed it as the Colonel having had a word.

She noticed, in 1955, that Tar had begun to look like a man who had slept, and filed it as the
work going better, which it was.

And she noticed the thing that ought to have told her, in the spring of 1956, and filed it as
nothing at all, because it was nothing at all: Tar had stopped starting his sentences over.

He used to do it constantly. He would begin, and somebody would come in over the top of him —
usually Pobil, who could not help it and had done it twice an evening for two years — and Tar
would stop dead and go back to the beginning and lay the whole thing out again from the first
step, and by the fourth attempt the room would have moved on and he would put the notebook away
and not say the thing at all. It was maddening to watch. Jean had privately thought it a
weakness; she had once, God forgive her, described it to John as an inability to summarise.

And then at some point in 1956 he simply stopped having to, because there was one person in the
room who had worked out — over four years, by trial, at some cost, like a man learning to handle
an animal that bites — that the load in a Tar sentence is at the bottom, and that if you
interrupt it you do not get a shorter version, you get nothing.

Nobody else on that station ever learnt it. Jean did not learn it. She is not sure she has learnt
it now.

---

She did try Mrs Bevan. Once. In the November of 1956, obliquely, over the duty rosters, in the
manner of a woman who is not asking anything: *they seem to have settled down, those two.*

And Mrs Bevan said, "Yes, they have," and went on with the rosters.

That was the whole of it. Jean has since gone over the exchange perhaps a hundred times and there
is nothing in it. There was no pause. There was no glance. The rosters had been arranged for
about eighteen months in a way that put those two on the same fortnight every fortnight and had
been arranged that way by Hester Bevan, who did not explain her rosters to anybody and never
once got one wrong.

What Jean has, and what she cannot put anywhere, is that when she said *those two*, Mrs Bevan's
pen did not stop, and her face did not change, and she was for about a second and a half
extremely amused.

That woman knew before any of them. Jean would put money on the second week.

---

## IV. The entry she did not make

Here is the part she has never written down anywhere, and is writing down now on a bench in
Venezuela because there is nobody within forty miles who reads English.

The first thing she felt, when she finally worked it out, was relief, and she may as well be
honest about the relief, which was entirely on her own account.

Two of the most exhausting men in the Caribbean had gone and found each other. Pobil was going to
be in somebody's business by the Thursday every week for the rest of his life, and it was not
going to be hers.

And Tar. It was never Jean's place to worry about Dr Tar. He was her superior; he had eleven
years and a doctorate and a war on her; a junior does not sit up at night over a senior, and if
you had put it to her at the time she'd have said something crisp about it and been right.

She sat up over him twice. In the September of 1954, when he stopped eating and was so plainly
and visibly finished that you'd have had to work at not seeing it — and she resented every hour of
that, because she had not asked to be a person who could tell. And again through a stretch of
1955 when the light in his office was on at two and on again at five, and there was nothing
whatever she was entitled to do about it, and she did nothing, and it was one of the longer
winters of her life.

So yes, she was glad. And there was the second half of it, which is less creditable: for two years
that entire apparatus — the questions, and the second question when your first answer was worse,
and the *quite* — had had nowhere on that station to go but at her, because she was the only other
person on it who could take it. She was not sorry to hand it over. Let him ask Pobil what he
meant. Pobil could take it all day and would enjoy it.

When Charles said *oh, thank God* on a bench in Venezuela in the February of 1962, Jean's private
view was that he was about six years behind her and welcome to catch up.

She was not, whatever Mrs Bevan may have supposed and whatever the Colonel certainly did, sitting
in the dark wanting any of it for herself. Her own arrangements were in the town, and were her own
business, and are not going into this or any other notebook.

What she wanted, and did not have, and could not get by working, was the other thing.

She was twenty-eight in 1954 and she was better than her position and she knew it, and she spent
those three years assembling, by hand, in the dark, a case for being allowed to put her own name
on her own work. And there was a period of about eight months in 1956 when she could not walk
past the two of them going through a set of figures at the same bench — disagreeing, taking it in
turns, neither of them senior for the purposes of the argument, each of them perfectly certain
the other would say if it were wrong — without wanting to break something.

That is what she was short of. Not a man. A second name on a paper and somebody to be corrected
by.

It is not a nice piece of arithmetic and she has stopped trying to make it come out otherwise.
She loved them both. She would have gone into the sea for either of them, and did, in a manner of
speaking, in the March of 1957.

---

## V. The mechanism

What she has decided, eventually, and she has had eight years and a great deal of boat travel in
which to decide it, is that it was never luck. It was fit.

Every single thing that made one of those men intolerable to the rest of the human race was the
precise thing the other one required.

Pobil was too much: too loud, too near, in your business by the Thursday, in the room whether
you wanted him or not. And Tar's whole difficulty was being left alone inside his own head with
the machine running. He did not need to be given space. He had far too much space. He needed a
man who could be heard from the far end of the yard.

Tar could not report on himself and would not be asked. And Pobil had spent twenty years getting
everything he knew off animals that could not tell him, and had never in his life had a use for
that faculty after six o'clock.

Tar was an accurate man who could not stop correcting, and Pobil was permanently and
cheerfully wrong about every distance on the station and had never once minded being told.

Tar could not accept a compliment, and Pobil was the only person on that coast who would rag him.

And — this is the one Jean cares about, and she is the only one who ever noticed it, and she
notices it because she was corrected by him a dozen times and it did her more good than any
supervision she has had before or since — Tar would say *I was wrong* inside the minute, in
public, in front of juniors, and Pobil could not do it and never learnt to. He backed down
beautifully. He backed the temperature down and left the position exactly where it was. In
thirty years he has never once handed that man a straight retraction.

And Tar, who ran his entire moral existence off accurate records and prompt correction, married
him anyway, and has never so far as Jean knows raised it.

That is not tolerance. Tolerance is what you do about a fault. That is a man deciding what he is
prepared to pay and then paying it every day for the rest of his life without an entry in the
book.

She would like to have said all this to somebody at the time. There was nobody at the Martin she
could have said it to, including, and perhaps especially, the two of them.

---

## VI. The delta, February 1962

She told Charles on the ninth evening and he was not surprised for a single second, and was
gracious about it for about four hours.

In the morning she came out to the bench and he was already there with the jars, barefoot, in the
mended shirt, and he did not look up.

"Eight years," he said.

"Yes, all right."

"Four days before I went. On the verandah. I said—"

"I know what you said."

"I said they'd be very happy."

"You did," Jean said. "And you wouldn't say how you knew, and you're not going to say now, and
in about ten seconds I am going to ask you anyway."

Charles put a jar down and thought about it for long enough that she began to believe she was
going to be given something.

"The morning after Beta got out," he said.

"I was there."

"You were," said Charles. "You were standing about four feet from me. Pascual told him he had his
results and didn't need any more, and the two of them looked at each other for about a minute, and
Reggie dropped his eyes."

"I remember it perfectly. That was a row and Pascual won it."

"Yes," Charles said.

"And?"

"And I was in the water," he said, "and so was he."

Jean waited.

"There was a third thing," Charles said. "Underneath the other two. I couldn't put a name to it at
the time and I have had eight years and I still can't, and if I gave you a word for it now I'd be
making the word up, and you'd write it down." He picked up the next jar. "That's the part I can
tell you."

And that is every word she has ever had out of him about it.

For eight years she had assumed that whatever Charles saw that summer, he had seen it in Reggie.
She had watched Reggie drop his eyes and had entered it as a defeat. It never once occurred to her
to look at the other one — who was standing in front of her, soaking wet, having just got his own
way, and who was not, on any reading she had at the time, the interesting one in that yard.
