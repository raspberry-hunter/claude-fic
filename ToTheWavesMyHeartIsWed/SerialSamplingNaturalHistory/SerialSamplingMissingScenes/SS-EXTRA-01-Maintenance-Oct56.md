# Maintenance

The launch brought the August journals on Tuesday and there are eleven of them, and by
Thursday supper Reggie has been through nine and has said nothing whatever about any of
them, which means one of the nine is bad.

Pascual gives it until the pudding.

"Something in the post has annoyed you."

"No."

"Nine journals in two days and not a word out of you."

"I have not formed a view," Reggie says, and puts his spoon down at an angle to the bowl
that Pascual could measure, and John starts telling the Colonel about the light on the
north side of the drawing room, and the thing goes by.

But he's up from the table four minutes later, and at half past eight he's in the doorway
of Pascual's laboratory with the offending item under his arm, having taken the long way
round the yard about it, which Pascual knows because he heard him go past the tanks and
come back.

"Sit down," Pascual says. "Which is it."

"*Journal of Mammalogy.* You will not want it."

"I want it very much. Sit down."

---

What it is, is a fourteen-page paper out of a Florida establishment on the maintenance of
captive delphinids, and it cites the husbandry paper four times, warmly, in the manner of
men who have read the abstract; and everything they have then gone on to do is the reverse
of what the paper says, and two of their animals are dead in a table on page eleven and
the deaths are not discussed.

Reggie reads him the citations first. Then he reads him the methods. Then he stops and
looks at the ceiling for a moment.

"Quite," he says.

Pascual, who has nothing to do this evening and no intention of finding anything, settles
back against the bench and folds his arms.

"They have got the intake schedule from Table Three and applied it to an animal of a
different genus at half the body weight. Quite. And they have thanked me for it in the
acknowledgements." He turns two pages back and then forward again. "*Quite.*"

"That's three."

"Three what?"

"Nothing," Pascual says. "Go on. Page eleven."

Page eleven takes twenty minutes and is the best twenty minutes he's had all week. Reggie
does it properly — the way he does the pens, the way he does anything — which is to say
he sets out first what the Florida men were trying to establish, and grants them that it
was worth establishing, and grants them two of their four instruments; and then takes the
whole thing apart from underneath at a speed that would frighten anybody who hadn't seen
it before. The dead animals go last. He does not raise his voice at any point. The
worst thing he says all evening is that the table has been laid out so as to be read
across rather than down, and that he does not believe this to be accidental, and Pascual
finds that he has taken his glasses off and is pressing his eyes.

"That's the filthiest thing I've ever heard you say about anybody."

"It is a description of the layout."

"It's a description of the layout," Pascual agrees, and laughs at him.

Reggie looks up. He doesn't know what it's for; he never does; and there's the small
stillness that comes before he decides to let it go by, and then — Pascual watches it —
the shoulders come down about half an inch and stay down.

He worked this out some while ago and has never said it to anybody. You cannot compliment
this man. A compliment has to be interpreted, and interpreting it costs him something, and
he will be at it all night and produce a wrong answer in the morning. But he can be
laughed at, because there's nothing in it to interpret; a man does not go to the trouble
of laughing at somebody he cannot bear. It's the cheapest thing on the station and it is
the only thing Pascual has that goes in whole.

So he does it as often as he can get away with.

"Write to them," he says.

"There is no purpose in writing to them."

"Write to them anyway. I'd like to read it."

"I shall write to the editor," Reggie says, "which is a different letter and a shorter
one," and takes out the notebook, and writes it down, and squares the notebook up on the
bench beside the journal so that the two of them are parallel; and Pascual says nothing
whatever about that, because there are things one saves.

---

At about ten they are still there and neither of them has proposed going anywhere.

Reggie has got on to the Gamma figures, which are the same figures they were on Sunday and
which he will look at again on Sunday next. Pascual gives him the surfacing intervals off
the afternoon: four twelve, four nine, four fourteen, four eleven, and the last one taken
in a chop that would have shortened it in any animal he has ever handled.

"Four fourteen in a chop."

"Four fourteen in a chop."

"That is the best figure we have had off her since June," Reggie says, and sits up
slightly, and is so plainly and entirely pleased that Pascual has to look at the
centrifuge for a second.

It costs nothing. That is the part he can't get over, even now. Two years he watched half
the station work out what to bring this man and get it wrong — John with his drawings,
the Colonel with his port — and all of it went into the ground, and what actually reaches
him is a number he could have had off the sheet in the morning, handed over by mouth at
ten o'clock at night. There is no man on earth easier to give a thing to and nobody has
noticed but Pascual, and Pascual has no intention of telling them.

---

The generator does what it does and the moths come in at the shutter and Reggie doesn't
say anything for eleven or twelve minutes, and it isn't a mood.

That took learning as well. In the first year Pascual would have gone into a silence like
that with a question, because in his experience a silence at ten o'clock at night is a
symptom and a man is entitled to be asked. Here it is nothing of the kind. He is reading,
and then he is not reading, and then he is looking at the middle of the bench with the
whole apparatus running on something Pascual will hear about in three days or never; and
if you leave it alone it comes back on its own and is perfectly cheerful.

Nobody else in Pascual's life has ever permitted him to be quiet. Every other room he goes
into is a room he has to keep going.

He gets up at some point and makes the coffee, from the tin, and doesn't offer any, because
the man doesn't drink it after six and would say so.

---

"You will have been in practice by then," Reggie says.

Pascual gets as far as opening his mouth.

"No," Reggie says. "I have done it again."

"You have."

"I have given you the last sentence." He puts the pencil down square to the edge of the
bench. "You said the road above Ponce, at supper. I have had it going since and have not
said so, and I have begun in the middle, and the middle is not the beginning. You will
want the beginning."

Pascual folds his arms.

"You're getting quicker at that."

"I am not getting quicker," Reggie says. "I am getting it afterwards rather than not at
all, which is a different thing and is worth less."

It is not worth less. Twelve months ago it did not happen; in the spring it happened once,
in front of Jean, and he went the colour of the wall; and it is October and this is the
fourth time since the launch came in August, and Pascual, who counts things, has been
counting it and has not said so and does not propose to.

"Do you want the chain of it?" Reggie says.

"Very much."

And he gets it — seven steps, in reverse order, from the Florida paper back through the
Gamma figures back through something John said at supper about the launch and out the far
end at a road in Puerto Rico; every step of it accurate and none of it the slightest use
to a man who wanted the beginning; delivered flat, in the voice of somebody reading out a
bill.

Pascual has to put the cup down.

"Thirty-eight," Reggie says, when he has finished. "You will have been in practice by
thirty-eight."

"Three years in. Why?"

"No reason. Go on."

So he goes on. He is not certain how he got here and by the time he is certain he is
eight minutes in and describing a mare in the hills above Ponce in the spring of 1938,
and the man who owned her, who was called Nieves and who would not have a vet on the
place; and the four separate occasions on which Pascual went up there and was turned back
at the gate; and the fifth, when he did not go to the gate at all but went to the man's
brother-in-law with a bottle and sat there for two hours saying nothing about horses.

It is a completely useless story. There's no finding in it. It doesn't bear on anything
and never has, and he has told it perhaps twice in eighteen years and both times to men
who were waiting for him to be finished.

Reggie hears the whole of it without moving.

"How long from the town."

"Four hours up. Rather less coming down."

"Then it was the eastern road," Reggie says.

Pascual puts the cup down.

"It was."

"You said the western one was out from the November. In — " and he stops for a moment,
which is the only sign there is that he is doing anything at all — "the March of last
year, at the rail, when we were talking about something else."

"I said that once."

"Yes."

"Eighteen months ago."

"Yes."

Pascual sits with this for a bit.

He has spent twenty-one years being listened to by people who were being polite about it.
He has a whole practice in his head — the roads, the four towns, the two hospitals, the
professor with the moustache, Nieves and his brother-in-law, an entire country nobody on
this station has ever asked him one question about — and it turns out that all of it has
been going somewhere the entire time; that there is a man three feet away with a map of
Puerto Rico in his head assembled entirely out of things Pascual mentioned while thinking
about something else, and that the map is more accurate than his own.

"You've been keeping it."

"I have not been keeping it," Reggie says. "There is nowhere to put it. It is simply
there." He turns a page. "It does not sort itself, that material. That is the pleasant
part of it."

"Say that again."

"There is nothing to be done with any of it," Reggie says, without looking up. "It has
no outcome attached and it never will have, and I am therefore not obliged to do anything
whatever except have it. It is very restful. I should like more of it and I have no way
of asking for it that would not spoil it."

And he goes back to the Gamma figures, and Pascual stands at his own bench in his own
laboratory with a cold cup in his hand and finds that he has been standing there for some
little time.

---

At half past eleven Reggie stands up and says that he will take the journal, and squares
the notebook up on top of it, and stops in the doorway.

"You are quite certain about four fourteen."

"I had the watch on him."

"Yes," Reggie says. "That is why I asked it in that form."

Pascual looks at him.

"Had you estimated it," Reggie says, "I should not have asked at all. I should have
entered it as three feet and gone to bed."

He is halfway across the yard before Pascual gets his mouth shut.
