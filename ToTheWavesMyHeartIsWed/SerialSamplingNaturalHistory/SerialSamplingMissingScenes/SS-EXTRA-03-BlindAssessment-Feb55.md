# Blind Assessment

They stopped rowing in the February and it was the worst six weeks anybody had on that station,
and Jean would like it recorded that she said so at the time.

The rowing had been perfectly all right. You could work through a row. Pobil went at Tar over the
sampling intervals in the November, at volume, in front of the whole dining room, and Tar said
*quite* and went away and did not mention it again, and three days later the intervals were
changed — Pobil's way, in Tar's handwriting, with a line ruled under. That is a functioning arrangement.
Jean has worked in three institutions since and has never seen a better one.

And then in the February it stopped, and what came in its place was courtesy.

---

She has tried since to describe it to people who were not there and it does not carry. *They were
polite to each other* is not an anecdote. But you would have had to stand in that dining room.

Tar asked after Gamma every single day. Every day, without fail, in the same words, at the same
point in the morning, whether or not there was anything to ask about — and Pobil answered him
every day, fully, at length, in the voice he used for the boat crew, and neither of them ever once
said anything else. Pobil stopped putting his hand on people. Not on Tar; on *people*. He was
across the table from Jean for a fortnight and did not touch her arm once, and she had spent
eleven months wishing he would stop and discovered that she would rather have had it.

He took to standing at the rail in the evenings with his hands in his pockets. That was the part
she found hardest to look at, and she could not have said why, and she still cannot. A very loud
man, on a rail, at nine o'clock, not going anywhere, with his hands in his pockets.

And Tar was scrupulous. That is the only word. He was courteous in the yard and exact about the
data and he carried a crate up from the jetty one afternoon and then would not sit down for the
rest of the day, as though he'd been caught at something.

---

The Colonel thought it was splendid. The Colonel said, in Jean's hearing, that the two of them had
finally learnt to rub along, and that it only wanted a firm hand and a bit of time. John thought
they'd made friends and was delighted for about five weeks, which is longer than John has ever
been delighted about anything.

Mrs Bevan did not say a word, and moved the March duty roster, and moved it back.

Jean's own theory — and she held it with total confidence, and wrote a version of it to her sister,
and would like the letter destroyed — was that there had been a row so bad that neither of them
could afford another one, and that they were both waiting to be the second to speak, and that one
of them would crack by Easter and it would be Pobil, because Pobil could not hold a silence
through a whole meal.

She was right about the waiting and wrong about everything else, including which one of them was
being waited for, and including Easter.

---

And then, somewhere in the second week of March, Dr Tar began to be nice to her.

She would like to convey what this was like. He asked her what she thought about the mid-water
tows *before* he said what he thought about the mid-water tows. He had never done that in her
life. He did it again on the Thursday. He did it a third time and she gave him a bad answer,
because she was so busy watching him do it that she wasn't listening to the question, and he
took the bad answer seriously and worked at it for a minute and a half before setting it aside,
which was worse.

He put her initials against the tide work.

He said thank you, twice, in a voice he had plainly had to construct out of parts, and both times
Jean said *that's quite all right* in the manner of a woman backing out of a room containing an
animal.

She did not think: *how kind*. She thought: *he is going to tell me I'm being sent home, and he
is working up to it, and he has never had to do it before and does not know how.* She thought
about that for four days and then she went and found Pobil in the laboratory and asked him
straight out whether Dr Tar was ill.

She would like to be clear about what she was hoping for. She was hoping the answer was yes. She
had two hypotheses and one of them ended with Jean Leslie on a boat to Southampton with three
years of nothing behind her, and the other one ended with a man she respected being unwell, and
she walked across that yard wanting the second one, and she knew perfectly well while she was
walking that she wanted it.

---

What she got was fifty minutes.

She has thought about that fifty minutes a great deal since and she has come round to the view
that she was not answered at any point in it. What happened was that she was debriefed.

He put down what he was doing, which he always did, and he asked her what specifically, and she
said the tows; and he asked her what else, and she said the initials; and he asked her what else,
and she found she had six instances and the dates of four of them, and he had them all out of her
inside ten minutes, in order, with the times of day. He asked whether it was in front of people or
not in front of people. He asked whether Tar had been able to look at her while he said it. He
asked — and she remembers thinking at the time that this was a very odd thing to want — whether
the thank-you had come at the start of the sentence or at the end.

Then he said that Dr Tar was perfectly well, and told her a long story about a man in Ponce with a
gallbladder, and gave her a cup of coffee, and asked after her mother.

She went out into the yard feeling thoroughly looked after and did not notice until she was
halfway across it that she had been given nothing whatever.

---

She asked him again three weeks later, and this time she was not so polite about it.

She said she was not a fool and that something was going on and that if she was being managed she
would rather be told.

And Pobil — who had gone very slightly still, for about a second, which she did not register
until she had eight years to think about it — said: "Nobody is managing you."

"Then what is he doing?"

"He is being decent to you," Pobil said, "and he is extremely bad at it, and you are the first
person in the Caribbean to be frightened by it," and he laughed, and the laugh was real and it
was not at her.

"Then there's nothing under it."

"I didn't say that." He picked the tray up. "I said he's being decent to you. That man could not
put a second thing inside a thank-you if he sat down and worked at it for a week — it is the one
piece of equipment he hasn't got, and you have been on this station two years and you know it
better than I do. If there's anything underneath it at all," he said, going out, "it isn't aimed
at you."

Which was true in every particular, and warm, and answered a question she had not asked; and she
took it as a remark about Dr Tar's honesty, which it partly was, and went away satisfied, and was
satisfied for about six years.

Because he also said, at some point in that conversation — and she has gone back over it and she
is certain of the order — he said: *has he put your initials on the tide work yet.*

*Yet.*

She had not told him about the tide work that day. She had told him about it three weeks earlier
and he had had it out of her in a list of six things at ten o'clock in the morning, and he had
kept it, and he had been waiting to see whether it held.

He never said one word to her about where any of it came from. Not that spring, not the following
year, not in the letters. He knew — she is entirely certain now that he knew, and that he had
known before she ever came to ask him — and he sat on it for the rest of her working life, out of
delicacy, as far as she can reconstruct it, on the grounds that telling her would have made her a
project and turned the one straightforward thing in that fortnight into a manoeuvre.

Nobody asked her whether she'd have preferred to know. It did not occur to either of them that
this was a question. It has occurred to Jean.

---

It ended on about the twelfth of April, and she has the date only because it was Tar's birthday
and John had made a fuss about a cake and been talked out of it.

Nothing announced itself. There was no row and no reconciliation and nobody said anything at any
meal. It was simply that on the Monday Pobil came into the dining room and took hold of Juan's
shoulder on the way past, the way he used to, without thinking about it; and Tar did not ask after
Gamma, having presumably already asked; and the whole station let out a breath it had been
holding for six weeks without ever having agreed to hold it.

The Colonel said it had wanted a firm hand and a bit of time.

---

She kept the tide work, and her initials stayed on it, and it went into the 1955 paper, and her
name did not.

That came out in the autumn. She read it standing up in the corridor with the post still in her
other hand, and then went out and did the afternoon's tows, and did them properly, and has never
told anybody what that half-hour was like and is not going to start here.

He put her on the 1956 one. He came and told her beforehand, which was not necessary, and stood
in the doorway of her own laboratory to do it.

"You should have been on the one before," he said. "You were not. That was mine. I have gone
through the reasons and they are not reasons." And then, because he had evidently decided that the
sentence was not finished and could not leave it: "Would second position or third be of more use
to you? I do not know which, and I would rather be told than guess."

Nobody had ever asked her that. Nobody has asked her since.

She was twenty-nine. She took the offprint out onto the jetty on her own and read her own name on
it four times, and it was the best afternoon she had at the Martin; and underneath it the entire
time was the flat fact that it was eighteen months late, and that it had been handed to her by a
man who was being kind to her for a reason nobody would tell her. Both of those were true on that
jetty at the same moment and neither of them cancelled the other and neither of them ever went
away.

She has been told a great many things since 1962 about that spring, by people who were in it. She
has not been told this one and does not expect to be. She has worked out, being a scientist, that
there was a cause and roughly when it must have operated; she does not know what it was, and both
of the men who do know have had thirty years in which to mention it.

She would have taken it anyway. That is what neither of them understood, and what she has never
had the occasion to explain: she would have taken the thank-you *and* the reason, and been pleased
with both, and it would not have spoiled one thing.
