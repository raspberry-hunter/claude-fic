# Presenting Complaint

At half past six he says, from the stove, without turning round: "There's the rice and there's
the rest of the snapper. I'll do the rice."

"The snapper wants eating," Reggie says. "It's been in since Thursday."

"Has it."

"You know perfectly well it has."

So they have the snapper, which is what Pascual had in the pan before he opened his mouth. It's a
small dishonesty and he's been running it for years — name the wrong one and the man will correct
you, because correcting is free — and it has never once failed him, and he has never once had to
ask Reggie Tar what he would like for his supper and receive the answer *whatever is easiest*,
which is the only answer that would ever be given.

He is fifty as of last month. He has a house with somebody in it. There is a great deal that
works.

The wrist works, since November. It works both ways now and out loud, which he had not expected to
survive daylight and which has survived seven months of it. The food works. The boats work — the
schooner's day is the schooner's day and neither of them has to say anything about it. And there
is the thing from March of 1956, which is the largest working thing they have and which is now so
ordinary that Pascual has to make an effort to see it: that if you put the kettle on and sit down,
the man will start at the beginning and go in order, and you will get the whole of it, and the
load is always at the bottom.

He has, at fifty, a functioning arrangement with a difficult man, and he knows exactly how much of
it he built, which is about a third.

---

The new receiver comes off the schooner on the Friday in a crate with straw in it, and it is
beautiful.

He has had it ordered since March. It is a Hallicrafters and it has a band spread and it will
pull Caracas at eleven at night without the whistle the old one has been putting on everything
since the wet season, and it cost him rather more than he has told anybody, and John helps him
carry it up and stands in the doorway with his arms folded and says it is a very fine thing, which
it is.

Reggie comes up at seven and stops in the door and looks at it on the table for what Pascual would
put at four seconds.

"That is the Hallicrafters," he says.

"It is."

"It has the band spread."

"It has."

"It is a very good set," Reggie says. "It will do everything you have wanted the other one to do
and it will do it without the whistle. I am glad you have it."

Every word of that is true and warm and correct and there is not one contraction anywhere in it,
and Pascual stands there with his hand still on the crate and thinks: *ah*.

Reggie has his supper and is pleasant throughout and asks after the boy's hand, and at twenty past
eight he says that he has the salinity series to get down while it is still in his head, and goes
down to the office.

---

Which is what he does. He has done it since 1958 or thereabouts. Pascual has had a long time to
get used to it and has got used to it.

The theory was never complicated. The man is a scientist and scientists work at desks; he has been
alone in rooms for most of his life and a person doesn't come out of that at forty; there is
something in him that requires an hour a day with nobody in the room, and the hour has to be got
from somewhere, and it is being got from after dinner, and that is entirely reasonable and Pascual
would not want a man who had nothing he did alone.

Somewhere in 1959 he stopped waiting up. That was a decision. He remembers taking it, on the
verandah, with the light on down at the lab and the moths at it, and what he decided was that this
is what seven years does to a thing, and that a man of forty-eight had no business minding it, and
that minding it would be a way of asking the other man to be different, which he had promised
himself in the February of 1955 he would not do.

It took him about a year to stop minding. He was quite proud of it.

---

He gets it wrong for eleven days.

He does what he does, which is to take a history, because there is a man in his house who has gone
formal about a wireless and formality in that man means load. So he watches for four days and then
sits him down on the Wednesday and asks him whether he is sleeping, and whether the head has been
at him, and whether there is anything wrong with the animals that he has not put in the log, and
whether he has heard from Jean.

And Reggie answers all of it. Accurately, in order, at length, because accuracy is the one thing he
is never short of. He is sleeping. The head has not been at him since April. Number seven has the
skin thing again on the same flank and it is in the log, dated. Jean wrote on the second and is
well and is being obstructed about the tank time and has taken it up with the director and will
win.

Not one of the answers is it. Pascual can hear that not one of the answers is it, the way he can
hear a heart that is going perfectly well in a dog that is dying of something else, and he sits
there with a complete and truthful history in his hands and no diagnosis in it anywhere.

So he does the next thing, which is to reach for a hypothesis with age on it, and the hypothesis
available in the June of any year since 1954 is Cholmondeley. He is careful about it. He is
oblique. He says something about the water being warm early this year and lets it sit there.

Reggie says: "It is not that."

"No."

"It is not that," Reggie says again, and looks at the table, and that is the end of the Wednesday.

And the thing Pascual does not find out until the following week is that this was true, and that
Reggie let him have it anyway for four more days rather than correct him further, because
correcting him further would have required saying the thing.

---

What breaks it is not cleverness. It is that he is doing the books on the Sunday and he goes to
put a date on something and cannot, and to fix the date he goes back through the year in his head,
and having started he does not stop, and by about eleven o'clock he is in 1954 and going forwards
and he has found what he was not looking for.

He is trying to produce one occasion on which Reggie Tar has asked him for something.

He produces dozens. That is the trouble. *Would you look at the boy's hand.* *I should like the
launch on the Thursday and not the Wednesday.* *You will have to come out with me on Saturday
because I cannot hold the animal and take the reading.* *I want your opinion on the flank and I
want it before I write.* Seven years of a man asking for things, plainly, promptly, without a
trace of difficulty, in a voice that could get a stone to say yes.

Every single one of them has a reason attached to it. Every one. There is not one instance
anywhere in seven years of that man saying *I should like* and stopping there.

Pascual sits with the books open in front of him and the new set on the table and works through it
the way he would work through a case, and arrives at two findings and does not care for either.

The first is that when the reason is missing the man does not ask. He *arranges*. He does not say
he cannot work with a voice in the room, he goes down to the office; he does not say he would
rather have the Thursday, he finds a reason why the Thursday is operationally better, and the
reason is always sound, because he is good, and Pascual has been accepting sound reasons off him
for seven years and calling it a marriage.

The second finding is his own and it is worse.

Every time in seven years that the man has produced a small thing — the head, the light, the noise
of the pump, the fact that he does not care for being touched on the back of the neck when he has
not seen it coming — Pascual has taken a history. He has done it out of love and he has done it
without exception and it has never once been under half an hour. So a complaint costs the man an
hour of being read and a set of questions he cannot answer inaccurately and a treatment he did not
ask for, and after enough of that any organism stops presenting.

He has made complaining expensive. He has done it with his own hands, for seven years, at some
cost to himself, believing he was doing the opposite.

And it is not even kindness, or it is not only kindness. He sits there and makes himself have the
rest of it. He *likes* it. The reading is the thing he loves. It is what he has instead of a
religion and it is the largest part of what he feels for that man, and he has been taking it off
him whenever it was going, and he has been calling it care.

*Ay, Dios,* Pascual thinks. *It's the wrist again.*

It is exactly the wrist again. It is having it without being given it, in another form, running
alongside the first one for seven years; and last November they sat up until well after dark and
got the whole of the one out and neither of them so much as glanced at the other.

---

He makes one more mistake before he gets it right, and he makes it on the Monday evening, and it
is: "What is it you want?"

He watches the man begin to construct. He can see it happen — the head goes down half an inch and
something behind the face starts assembling an answer that will be true and will be about the
station.

So he says, "No. Wait."

And then he gets up and puts the kettle on, which takes eleven seconds and is the whole of the
apparatus, and comes back and sits down and says the sentence he has been saying for five years.

"Start at the beginning. And do not summarise."

---

It takes forty minutes, and it is about the wireless.

He starts in November of 1958, which is when the old set came, and he goes in order.

He is not able to work with speech in the room. Music is nothing — music he has worked through
since Cambridge and would not notice a band in the same building. Speech he cannot do. Speech
arrives as speech and he reads by saying it to himself, and the two cannot run at once, and he has
known this about himself since he was nineteen and has never in his life mentioned it to anybody
because there has never in his life been anybody in the room at nine o'clock at night.

He gives the November. He gives the fortnight after it, in which he established that it was the
speech and not the set, by testing it, twice, with a stopwatch, on the same passage. He gives the
first occasion on which he intended to say something, which was the eleventh of December 1958, and
the reason it did not occur, which was that Pascual had had the pump out that afternoon in the sun
and it would have been a poor evening to choose.

Then he gives the second occasion.

Somewhere around the half hour, in the middle of 1959, Pascual understands what is being read to
him and takes hold of the arm of the chair.

"How many," he says.

"Nine."

"Nine what?"

"Occasions," Reggie says, "on which I formed the intention and did not carry it out. I have them
because I recorded them. I recorded them because after the fourth one it became a thing I was
doing repeatedly and I do not leave a repeated thing unrecorded." A pause. "The ninth is the fourth
of November last year."

"Last November."

"Yes."

"And since?"

"Nothing," Reggie says. "Since the fifteenth of November there has been no occasion, and there was
not going to be one, and I should like to give you the reason for that because it is the only part
of this I have worked out and I do not think you will arrive at it on your own."

He sets it down flat, the way he sets down a correction to a published figure.

"On the fifteenth of November I gave you fourteen years in five hours and I was heard through
without being stopped. That established a standard. What I have found since is that it established
a floor as well. There is now a class of thing which is too small to bring to you, and the class
was created by the size of what I brought, and it did not exist before, and it contains everything
I actually have." He turns the cup round on the table half a turn. "I could not follow the account
of 1946 with a complaint about a wireless. There is no order in which those two things can be
said."

---

Pascual does not say anything for a while.

"Go on," he says. "You said it was the only part you'd worked out. There's more of it than that."

"There is more of it. I do not have it."

"Have a try."

"Very well." Reggie sits back. "I am able to ask for anything that has a reason. I have thought
about that a good deal in the last fortnight and I believe it is exactly correct and not
approximately correct. Where there is a reason, the reason performs the asking and I am merely the
person reporting it, and there is nothing in the transaction that is about me, and it costs
nothing at all — I can do it in front of Spilsbury, I can do it in front of a room. Where there is
no reason there is only the fact that I should prefer it. And I cannot get a preference to
constitute a ground."

"Why not?"

"Because it does not," Reggie says, and stops, and Pascual watches him find the bottom of what he
has and it is not very far down. "That is not an answer. I am aware that it is not an answer. It
is what I have. I have gone at it from four directions in a fortnight and the best I can produce
is that it would be an imposition, which I do not believe, because you impose on me constantly and
I like it."

"That's not it."

"No. That is not it."

"You want to know the part I've got," Pascual says.

"I should like it very much."

So he gives it. Badly, because he has not had a fortnight and has not written anything down.

"Every time you've said a small thing to me in seven years I've taken a history off you. Every
time. An hour of it, minimum, questions you can't answer wrong because you're incapable, and then
some treatment you never asked for." He makes himself go on to the second half, because the
standard was set last November and it wasn't set by him. "And it isn't kindness. Or it isn't only.
I like doing it. It's the best thing I've got and I've been taking it off you whenever it was
going, and calling it looking after you, and what it's actually done is make it cost you an hour
to tell me the pump's loud."

Reggie is very still.

"You made it expensive."

"I made it expensive."

"And I stopped presenting," Reggie says, and there it is, in his mouth, in about four words,
because he is quick, "which you then read as there being nothing to present."

"Which I then built a house round," Pascual says. "I decided in 1959 that this is what seven years
does. I decided that a man your age needs his hour and I've no business minding, and I stopped
minding, and it took me the best part of a year, and I was proud of it." He looks at the table.
"Two and a half years of evenings. It was a wireless."

---

The protocol is Reggie's and it takes them until about one in the morning and it is, Pascual
thinks, one of the least romantic hours of his life and he would not give it up for anything.

Reggie will not have an agreement to try harder. He says so, flatly: he has been trying harder
since 1958 and has nine dated failures to show for it and there is no reason to believe the tenth
attempt differs from the ninth. What he wants is a procedure, because a procedure does not require
him to be a different person, and a person is not obliged to justify operating a procedure that
has already been agreed.

So:

He may state a fact. Not a request. *The wireless is on.* *That light is in my eyes.* The fact
requires no ground because a fact is not a claim on anybody, it is only true or not true, and
accuracy he has unlimited licence for.

Pascual will act on the fact and will not take a history.

Pascual may ask one question. One. It must be about the fact and not about the man.

And if Pascual wants the history — and Pascual, who has been drafting his own clause in his head
for ten minutes, is not giving up the history — he must ask for it separately, on another day,
and it must be a request that can be refused.

"That last one is yours," Reggie says. "I should not have put it in."

"You'd have given me everything and kept nothing."

"Yes."

"And then in a year you'd have been paying me a fee," Pascual says. "In a house I'm living in for
nothing. No. It goes in."

Reggie writes the whole thing out. Of course he does. Four clauses, in his hand, on a half sheet
of foolscap, and dates it, and Pascual watches him do it and does not say one word about it, and
the effort of not saying the word is the first payment he makes under the agreement.

---

It fails on the Tuesday.

Reggie says, at the table, "There is a whistle on the second band," and Pascual moves the set six
inches and asks his one question, which is whether it is on the band or on the mains, and gets his
answer, and then hears himself ask whether it has been going all week, and stops with his mouth
still open.

"That is two," Reggie says.

"That's two."

"I am recording it."

"You are not recording it."

"I am recording it," Reggie says, "in the interests of establishing a rate," and gets up and goes
and does, and Pascual sits at the table and laughs until he has to put his head in his hands.

---

It works on the Thursday of the week after.

They are up at the house at about nine and the set is on and it is a Caracas station and it is
music, which is nothing, and Reggie is at the table with the salinity sheets, up at the house, at
nine o'clock at night, for the fifth evening running, and Pascual has not remarked on that either
and is not going to.

Reggie says, without looking up: "I would rather sit on that side."

There is no reason in it anywhere. It is a chair.

Pascual has a thing ready — he has had it ready since the man started the sentence, a small wrong
guess about the light off the water, which would get the whole of it out of him in about nine
seconds and which he has been running on this man since 1954 and has never once had to defend.

He puts it down. It costs him something to put it down and he is surprised by how much.

He gets up and moves his glass and his tobacco to the other end of the table and sits down again,
and it takes four seconds, and neither of them says anything at all.

After a while Reggie moves round, and settles, and squares the sheets, and goes on working.

The whistle is entirely gone off the second band. Pascual had said it would be and it is, and he
would like it recorded that he was right about the Hallicrafters, and there is nobody in the room
he can say that to without starting something, so he keeps it, and turns the music down two turns,
and does not ask whether that is better.
