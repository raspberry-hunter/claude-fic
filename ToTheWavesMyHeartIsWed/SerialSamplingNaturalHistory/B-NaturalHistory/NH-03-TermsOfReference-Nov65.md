# Terms of Reference

He had kept the Tar file since the autumn of 1946, and it had begun, like everything else about the man, as a perfectly ordinary piece of establishment work: a name, a degree, two referees, a note in his own hand reading *possibly the best field observer of his year; will require handling; do not put him on anything he can see the end of.*

It ran now to eleven years of a second thing. One entry a year, ruled off, in the same hand: the date of the offer, the terms as put, the form of the refusal, and the length of the interview in minutes. There was no operational use for the last of these. He had begun taking it in 1957 because Tar was taking it — he had watched him at the water tanks with his thumb on the crown of his watch, a man of thirty-nine counting a door — and he had thought at the time that it was the most naked thing he had ever seen a scientist do in the open air, and he had gone away and started a column of his own.

In April of 1965 the entry read: *fifty-one minutes. Terms as constituted. Not refused.*

It was the first time in nine years he had written those last two words, and he had sat with the card in front of him for some while afterwards in a room in Miami with the fan going, being pleased, and being aware that he was pleased, and declining to look at that any further.

---

He had run Special Studies at the establishment outside Salisbury from the October of 1946 until it was wound up.

He was not going to be delicate about it, at least not in a room by himself. The programme did what such programmes do. It required a great deal of careful work by first-class people on subjects that could not leave the room, and the subjects, in the ordinary course, did not, and the people who did the careful work had been selected for the quality of their attention, which is precisely the quality that makes a man unfit to give it. That was the whole of the design fault and everybody in the building could see it and nobody could think of any way round it that did not involve using second-class people, which would have meant worse results and more of them.

Tar had been there for two years and had been better at it than anybody, and had gone out of the building in the spring of 1948 without a scene of any kind, and had spent the following eight years making himself, deliberately and with great skill, into a man nobody would ever want in that sort of room again. Spilsbury had watched the whole manoeuvre from a distance and had admired it as a piece of engineering and had been perfectly clear about what it cost, because he had the man's publication list, and a publication list is a life, and there were four years in the middle of Tar's with nothing in them at all.

He regretted it. He would like that recorded and would not like anything constructed out of it. He regretted what the two years had done to a particular man; he did not regret the two years, which had been necessary in the view of people whose judgement he had accepted at the time and had no reason to revise, and he would run them again, with the same design fault, and would appoint Tar again, because Tar was the best instrument available and there was a war on and then there was the other thing which was also, in every sense that mattered to the estimates, a war.

He was aware that this was not a defence. He had never offered it as one. Men who require their own conduct to come out clean should not take establishment posts.

---

In September of 1954 he had gone to the Martin for the animal and had stayed for the man.

There was no use pretending otherwise now. The Navy were putting a specimen ashore and the trip was justified on that alone; but John Godfrey's letter had gone to Fleming and Fleming's people had passed it on and it had arrived on the Washington side in a form that could not be ignored, and what it said was that a junior research man at a small British station in the Leewards appeared to be in some kind of direct sensory communication with the captive animal, and that the animal responded to him and to nobody else.

If Charles Cholmondeley had been standing on that jetty on the second of September 1954, Spilsbury would have had him in a room in Maryland by the Friday. He would have done it courteously and with a proper contract and a salary that would have astonished the young man, and he would have got what was in him, and inside four years he would have had the mechanism, and there would have been a paper with somebody's name on it that changed the whole of what is known about that order of animal. And the young man would not have come out of the building, in any sense that his friends would have recognised, and the animal would not have come out of it at all.

He had known all this at the time. He had come off the launch knowing it. He had been pleasant for five days, and he had been pleasant on purpose, and it was not a false pleasantness — that was the part people never got right about him — because the work he was offering was the best work in the world and he had never in his life had to pretend otherwise.

What he had got instead was Tar, across a table, with four months of food logs, being slack.

He had believed it for about a week. He was not ashamed of the week; the performance was extremely good, and it was built out of true figures, and it was delivered by a man who was visibly and genuinely in poor condition. By the second week he had the intake series in front of him and could see that no man capable of producing that series was capable of failing to read it, and by the end of the month he knew that he had been worked, in a dining room, by a scientist with a column of figures, and he had never been able to construct what for.

That file was still open. Eleven years. It was the only one he had.

He had asked Jean Leslie twice. The first time gently, in her second month, when she was thirty and new and might have said anything; the second time in 1959, properly, with something offered. Both times she had given him nothing whatever, and the second time she had let a silence run for long enough that he understood he had made an error of judgement about a person, which is the one error in his line of work that cannot be repaired, and he had dropped it inside a fortnight and had not raised it again.

He had written her the best reference of his life eighteen months later. Both things were true. People assumed that one of them must be the performance, and he had given up minding.

---

The survey element was funded in the January of 1965 after eleven years of asking.

He wished to be exact about the sequence, because the sequence was the whole of it and he had gone over it more than once.

He had wanted a wild-population survey since 1954 and had put it up in one form or another six times and had been refused six times, on the grounds that a survey produces distributions and distributions do not produce animals, and animals were what the money was for. In 1958 a schedule came round on the advisory circulation from the Martin — a small line, costed at nothing, retainers to observers and boatmen along the southward stations, non-specimen — and it was, in nine lines, a better-designed instrument than the thing he had been putting up for four years, because it did not attempt to survey the sea; it surveyed the people who lived on it. He had voted for it in 1958 and in 1959 and in 1961 and in 1963. It had been struck every time, for the same reason his own had been struck, and he had watched a man in the Leewards go on submitting it into a seventh year, which is not a thing scientists do, and had drawn his conclusions.

In the autumn of 1964 the money changed. It changed because the sponsors had come, by their own route and for their own reasons, to an interest in the communication question; and a wild-population survey, which had been useless to them for a decade, became overnight the necessary first stage of something else.

He had known that when he drafted it. He had drafted it anyway, and he had drafted it well: three years, a vessel of ninety feet, no tanks aboard, no licence to fit them, no animal to be taken or held, and he had had the no-tanks provision minuted twice because a thing minuted twice is a thing that has to be revoked in the open.

And then the establishment list came back from the Washington side with one post on it that he had not written.

*7. Officer (Special Studies), seconded. Vacant.*

He had looked at it for a long time. He had thought about telephoning. He had, in the end, done the thing he had done all his life, which was to conclude that a post is a post and a vacancy is a vacancy, and that a vacancy in the hands of a man who controls the sailing orders is not the same object as a vacancy in the hands of a man who does not; and that the correct response was not to refuse the money but to be certain that the sailing orders were his.

And that the second-best guarantee available in the world was Reginald Tar aboard as scientific director, because Tar was the only man alive who could produce a survey of that coast so complete and so obviously correct that nobody would ever have occasion to send anybody down it again.

He had put that to himself in exactly those terms and had found it good. He had noticed, somewhere behind it, an older and less presentable proposition, which was that the Salisbury programme had been given precisely one verdict in twenty years and the verdict was a man walking out of the building, and that the verdict stood for as long as the man stayed away. He noticed it the way one notices a noise in an engine at night, and he did not get up and look at it, and in the morning it was not making the noise.

---

He had put the schedule on the table on the sixth of April, in the dining room at the Martin, at about two o'clock, and Tar's face had moved.

It was not much. A man who did not have eleven years of the same face would not have had it. But he had been reading that face annually since 1954 and he had never once got anything off it except an interview length, and at two o'clock on the sixth of April Reginald Tar looked at his own 1958 schedule with a vessel attached to it and wanted something, visibly, in a room, in front of a witness.

Spilsbury had gone on talking without the smallest alteration of manner, because a thing looked at closes, and had come out at the end of fifty-one minutes with the first purchase he had ever had.

He had spent Tuesday evening and Wednesday morning establishing what he had. What he had was not a lever in the ordinary sense; there was nothing on the man that could be pulled, and there never had been, and eleven years had settled that. What he had was a want. A want is better than a lever, because a lever must be applied and a want merely has to be supplied, and no one who is given what he wants ever afterwards describes the transaction accurately, including to himself.

By Wednesday afternoon he had drafted a post for the veterinary officer.

He was clear about what he was doing. He was buying a man of forty-eight through the only person that man was attached to, and the price was a genuine post that a genuine establishment genuinely needed, and it happened also to be true that Pobil was very good and would have been an ornament to the element. Both true. Always both.

Pobil had refused it in under a minute, which had told him the number he wanted; and had done it with a face that gave nothing away, which had told him a second number he had not asked for.

And he had said the name in the doorway. Cholmondeley. He had said it kindly, on his way out, to see what happened, and what happened was thirty years of clinical discretion arriving all at once like a shutter; and he had gone down to the launch entirely satisfied, having spent nothing and learned that the file that had been open for eleven years was open at the Martin as well.

---

John Godfrey applied for the illustrator's post in the June.

The application was perfectly regular and came through the Martin with a good covering letter from a man who was plainly glad to see the back of him. Godfrey was forty now and had a decade of first-rate scientific drawing behind him and would come cheap, and there was nobody in the field who could do a plate of a living animal from a boat and get the proportions right.

Spilsbury read the file and put it on the appointing side and did not, at any point in the eleven minutes it took him, write down the other thing he knew about the man, which was that in 1954 John Godfrey had assembled a piece of information out of overheard conversation and enthusiasm and had passed it to a novelist at a dinner party, and had thereby produced the entire subsequent history of everybody concerned.

He told himself that the man drew well.

It was true. It was also, he was aware, the shape of sentence that he had been putting into files all his life at the exact point where a different sentence was called for; and he thought about it for a little while, and appointed him.

---

The pre-sailing conference was in Miami in the September, and there were fourteen at dinner on the second night, and Godfrey was at the far end being enjoyed.

That was the thing about him and it was why nobody had ever stopped him: he was excellent company. He had the whole table. He did the mermaid that got away — the tarp, the drag across the sand, the empty pen at nine in the morning, the Navy's face — and he did it beautifully, with the timing of a man who has been telling it since 1954 and has cut the dull parts, and he was not being malicious for one instant of it.

Then he said: "And the extraordinary thing is that the boy who did it had some sort of connection with the animal. Not a figure of speech. It would come to him and to nobody. I worked it out myself, actually, in about my fourth month — nobody believed me at the time."

He was pleased about that. Eleven years, and it was still the best thing that had ever happened to him, and it was still his.

And then, over the pudding, entirely happy, with his glass in his hand: "And of course he's turned up. Cholmondeley. He's in the February number — the new siluroid out of the delta, quite a good description, and there's an address on it. He's been sitting up a caño on that coast the whole time. Eleven years. We shall be going straight past him."

Spilsbury sat with his hands quiet on either side of his plate.

Down the table, the sponsors' man — a courteous fellow named Hollis, with excellent Spanish and no scientific training whatever — took out a pocket book and wrote in it, and put it back in his coat, and said something pleasant to the woman beside him about the weather in November.

That was all. It took him perhaps six seconds.

Spilsbury had spent nineteen years in and around establishments of this kind and had watched a great many things be written down, and had done a good deal of the writing, and he had never once in his life been on this side of it. The sensation was not guilt; he was not going to dignify it with that. It was the sensation of putting a hand out to an instrument one has used every day for twenty years and finding that it has become warm, and is running, and is not going to stop because one has taken one's hand off it.

He got the conversation on to the vessel's water capacity inside a minute and a half, and did it well, and nobody noticed.

Afterwards he went up and sat on the end of the bed with his coat still on, and worked out, in order, what he could do.

He could have Godfrey off the element. He could not do it without a reason and any reason would go on paper and the paper would say what Godfrey had said. He could speak to Hollis, who would be perfectly agreeable and would not tear the page out. He could write to Tar, who would then know, and who would then be a man who knew, and would act on it, and would be visible acting on it.

He could do nothing, and be the only person on the element with the whole picture, and take the vessel down that coast himself with the stations chosen in his own hand.

He did the last, which was also the thing he wanted to do, and he noted the coincidence.

---

Tar's letter came at the end of October, on the Martin's paper, in the hand he had been reading for nineteen years.

> *Spilsbury.*
>
> *I have the establishment list. Item seven is Officer (Special Studies), seconded, vacant, funded on a vote I recognise; and Special Studies is the form of words that stood at the head of our returns from the October of 1946 until the thing was wound up, and you chose it, and I typed it four hundred times.*
>
> *I do not charge you with concealment. You told me in April that there was a matter you had kept out of the minute and could not keep out for ever, and you told me warmly, in a room, and I have concluded since that you told me in order to have it on the record that you had told me. It is a technique. I know it because I have used it, on somebody who deserved better, on the twenty-ninth of August 1954.*
>
> *The difficulty is not a moral one and I will not put it as one. The survey is the instrument that finds the animals. Item seven is the instrument that does the other thing. There is no version of the first which does not deliver to the second; my presence aboard does not alter the delivery, it dresses it. I should be the reason the returns were believed.*
>
> *You will reply that if I am not aboard, item seven has the survey in any case and has it done worse. That is correct. I have run it a great many times and it does not come apart, and I am declining nevertheless, and you are owed the reason, because you have read my work accurately for eleven years and nobody else has troubled to.*
>
> *I left the building in 1948 because I had found out what I would do if the work were good enough. It is a fact about me and not about you. You have now found the work that is good enough.*
>
> *My answer is no.*
>
> *R. Tar*

He read it three times.

The third time he read it for the thing he had been reading that hand for since 1946, which was the sentence that was not about the work; and there it was, in the fifth paragraph, unemphasised, in the middle of an argument, where the man had plainly put it on purpose: *on somebody who deserved better.*

Eleven years of correspondence, and a great deal of it excellent, and it was the first sentence Reginald Tar had ever written him that was about Reginald Tar.

He entered the refusal on the card. *Ninth. Terms as constituted. Declined in writing, October, five paragraphs.* And then, in the second column, which had one entry in it and now had two: *P. Pobil, veterinary officer, declined April, under one minute.*

He had begun the card in 1946 as a record of a man's usefulness. It had become, at some point he could not now identify, a record of a man's life, and he sat in the office in London with it in front of him and could not establish which of them he had been keeping it for.

---

There was one more item, and it came through the ordinary Colonial and Colonial-adjacent circulation in the November, which was where such items always came.

*The Martin. Colonel R. F. Bevan has relinquished the administration of the William Martin Institute for Marine Studies on grounds of health, with effect from the 30th instant. Mrs H. Bevan acting.*

Spilsbury put the flimsy down and looked at the wall for a moment.

Because he had the other half of it. In the August, the element's establishment list — which was a sponsors' document, and not a document that goes to a British station in the Leewards under any circulation whatever — had been requisitioned from the Colonial side by a station administrator, in writing, over his own signature, on grounds he had stated as operational and which were not.

It had been an old man spending the last of what he had, in the one currency he had ever dealt in, to put a piece of paper on a desk four thousand miles away; and it had been noticed, and somebody had minuted it, and the minute had gone up, and here was the answer, in eleven words, on grounds of health.

Nobody would ever put the two facts side by side. He had them side by side because he had been in this business for nineteen years and had a filing system and no family.

He thought: *that is a man who read the list, and read item seven, and knew exactly what it was, and passed it to Tar, and paid for it out of himself, and will now be sitting in a chair by a window in the sun with a stick beside him.*

And then he thought, without any warning at all and in his own voice: *and everyone in this affair has now spent something except me.*

He got up and went to the window and stood there until the thing had gone off, which took rather less than a minute, because he was fifty-eight years old and had been managing himself since before Tar was qualified.

---

The element sails on the fourth of March.

The stations for the first season are chosen and they are chosen in his own hand, and they run from the Grenadines down to the Gulf, and the delta stations are the third season's business and not the first's, and no one has queried it, because a man who has spent eleven years getting a survey funded is permitted to sequence it as he thinks best.

Godfrey will be aboard. He draws well.

Hollis will be aboard, with the pocket book, and Hollis is a decent man and will do what his people ask him, and what his people ask him will not be asked in a manner that permits refusing.

Item seven remains vacant, and Spilsbury has written to the Washington side to say that in his judgement the post cannot usefully be filled until the second season, when there will be material for it to work on; and that is true, and it has bought a year, and he has been buying years since 1954 and knows to a month what a year is worth.

And in the drawer of the desk there is a letter, dated forward to the April, warmly worded, addressed to Dr R. Tar at the Martin, offering him the scientific direction of the survey element for the second and third seasons on terms as constituted, with no hurry attached and no answer required before the autumn.

The twelfth.

He will send it. He has sent one every spring for nine years and he has been refused nine times and he will very probably be refused a tenth; and he has never once been able to give a colleague an account of why he does it that survived being said out loud, and has stopped attempting to, and goes on.
