# Paid Off

The first season's returns came in at the end of April 1967 and they were the best piece of work he had ever had his name anywhere near.

He wished to be exact about that too. Eleven years of asking and two of getting, and what came out of it was a distribution for the whole southern range, a population estimate with defensible error bounds, a seasonal movement between the Gulf and the delta that nobody had so much as suspected, and four hundred and ten sighting records bought from men who had been carrying them about unasked for their entire lives, at an average of eleven dollars a head.

It was Tar's design. Every line of the method was Tar's, off a nine-line schedule costed at nothing in 1958 and struck eight times by people who could not read. Spilsbury had run it at approximately ninety times Tar's budget with a ninety-foot vessel, and had got, so far as he could establish, about four times Tar's result per station, which is the sort of figure one keeps to oneself.

He wrote and told him so. It was not a manoeuvre; it was simply true, and there was nobody else on earth to say it to. Tar's reply was one paragraph, and it queried the pooling of the two Boca stations, and it was correct, and Spilsbury re-ran it and lost about nine per cent of his effect and published it that way.

---

Item seven was filled in the February for the second season, which was the earliest it could be filled, because there had been nothing for it to work on and he had said so and had bought a year with it.

The officer's name was Ferris. He was thirty-four, a comparative physiologist, seconded from the establishment, extremely good; and Spilsbury had gone through five files and had appointed him for one reason, which he did not write down.

Ferris was honest. Not scrupulous — scrupulous men are common in that line and are no use to anybody, because a scrupulous man will do the thing and mind about it. Ferris was honest in the specific and rather rare sense that his reports said what he had found, including when what he had found made his own post look unnecessary, and there was an instance of that in his file from 1964 which had cost him a promotion and which was the only interesting page in it.

Spilsbury appointed him and told himself that the man was the best physiologist available at the salary.

It was true. It was also the shape of sentence he had been putting into files all his life at the exact point where a different sentence was called for, and he was fifty-nine now and had stopped pretending not to notice, and had not altered his practice, because noticing and altering are different departments.

---

Hollis's pocket book had become a file in the September of 1965 and the file went to Ferris in the March of 1967 with the second season's terms of reference.

They went up the Guayo channel on the nineteenth of June, Ferris and Hollis and a Trinidadian pilot, in a launch with an outboard, with a letter of introduction from the Fundación that Hollis had obtained without difficulty because there was no reason on earth to refuse it.

Ferris's report ran to nine pages and Spilsbury read it three times and then read it again in the January when the whole business was over, and he could still put most of it down from memory.

They had been expected. Not in any sinister sense — the man had had a letter announcing them a fortnight before, from the Fundación, and had written back to say that the nineteenth was convenient and that they should come at the top of the tide or they would be walking the last mile.

They found a house on posts, a garden, a bench under a shelter with about two hundred jars on it in rows, a boat, a man of thirty-seven in a mended shirt, and a Venezuelan residence permit in a tin with the seed packets.

He gave them six hours.

He answered everything. He took them out in his own boat and showed them a feeding station and two haul-outs and did not once ask what they wanted it for. He gave Ferris — a stranger, unasked, on the first afternoon — a summary of the seasonal movement that agreed with the element's own returns in every particular except one, and the exception was his and he was right about it and Ferris said so in the report. He talked about sleep for two hours. He talked about the sense: what it is, what its working range is in brackish water at a stated conductivity, what it is manifestly for, and what it categorically cannot do, which is convey one single proposition from one animal to another under any conditions whatever, ever, at any distance, and he was quite blunt about the number of people who had assumed otherwise and about the quality of their evidence, which was none.

And then he had offered to send them the manuscript.

Ferris put it in the report in one flat sentence, and it was, Spilsbury thought, the finest thing he had ever read in an official document: *The subject volunteered that a paper covering the whole of the above is in preparation with two co-authors at Torry and at the Martin, that it is expected to be submitted in about eighteen months, and asked whether the element wished to receive the manuscript at the draft stage, on the grounds that it would be useful to us and that he would value criticism of the third section, which he considers too long.*

And Ferris's conclusion, which was in his own hand at the end and had not been written for anybody's comfort:

*The material sought by this post will be in the open literature within two years, in fuller form than any programme of ours could produce in five, because the observer has twelve years of continuous residence and we cannot manufacture that. As to the subject himself: he is a foreign national resident under permit, affiliated to a national institution, published under his own name, with the type specimen of a new species bearing that name and correspondents in four countries. Any approach to him other than the scientific one would require sponsorship at a level at which the question is no longer scientific, and would, in my judgement, be objected to in a manner and by persons this establishment would find inconvenient. There is no scientific case for the post and I recommend it be discontinued. I should add that I have never in eleven years been given so much by anybody, and that I do not think for one moment that I was given anything he did not intend me to have.*

---

Spilsbury is going to set down the next part plainly, because it is the part that people would prefer not to be told and because he has never in his life required his own conduct to come out clean.

If Ferris's report had come back the other way — if the man up that river had been a British subject with no papers, at no address, unpublished, holding the whole of it in his head — Spilsbury would have proceeded. He would have proceeded courteously and with a proper contract, and the sponsors would have found the money, and he would have regretted it in a specific and limited way for the remainder of his life, and he would have got the mechanism, and there would have been a paper that altered what is known about that order of animal.

He did not proceed, and the reason he did not proceed was not that he had grown better. It was that a research student in Aberdeen in 1962 had gone up a river and had spent four years quietly making a man too expensive, and had done it with reprints and an address on a title page and a species name, using no instrument that was not available to Spilsbury himself, and had beaten him with it.

He worked out afterwards, roughly, when it had become impossible. It was the February of 1965, the number with the siluroid in it, the description he had praised at a dinner table because it was the best thing in the number. It had been over then. He had spent two years and a considerable sum arriving at a position that had already been foreclosed by twelve pages and two plates that he had read with pleasure on a Tuesday morning.

That is the whole of it. He was outplayed by better people, one of whom was thirty and one of whom was living in a hut, and it was done in the open, in print, where he could see it, and he did not see it.

---

The post was discontinued in the November of 1967.

The sponsors' interest went with it, and it went the way such interest always goes, which is not by decision. Nobody wrote to say that the communication question was closed. There was simply a reappraisal in the spring estimates, and a query about the element's cost per station, and a proposal to consider the southern programme in the context of overall priorities, and by the March of 1968 there was no third season and the vessel had been paid off at Miami and the ship's company dispersed.

Two seasons. He had asked for that survey for eleven years and had got two seasons of it, and it had been funded not because anybody wanted a survey but because for about thirty months a survey was the first stage of something else, and when the something else evaporated the survey evaporated with it, exactly as he had known in the January of 1965 that it would, and had drafted anyway, and had told himself he could manage.

He had been managing for nineteen years. This was the account.

Godfrey went to a fisheries station in Trinidad and sends a Christmas card. He drew four hundred plates in two seasons and about ninety of them are the best work of their kind that will ever be done on that fauna, and he has no idea whatever that he is the proximate cause of anything, twice, thirteen years apart, and nobody is ever going to tell him, and Spilsbury has thought about it and can find no version in which telling him serves any purpose except the pleasure of the telling.

He wrote to Pobil in the December to say that the veterinary post had ceased to exist and that he was sorry for it. It was four lines and it was the last of the offers that had been standing since 1965. Pobil's reply was three lines, entirely courteous, and it ended with a joke about the salary range, which had been described in two ways in the original letter because the original letter had been drafted in one afternoon in a hurry, and which the man had evidently noticed at the time and had said nothing about for two and a half years.

Spilsbury sat in the office in London with it for a while and found, somewhat to his annoyance, that he minded.

---

In the June of 1968 a flimsy came round on the Colonial circulation: *Colonel R. F. Bevan, formerly administrator of the William Martin Institute for Marine Studies, died at Antigua on the 2nd instant.*

He put it down and looked at the wall.

He had never met the man. He had been in the same building as him for five days in 1954 and had thought him a competent administrator of the ordinary kind, and had been wrong, and had found out he was wrong in the November of 1965 by putting a requisition slip and a relinquishment minute side by side and being the only person alive who had both.

There was nobody to say any of that to. That is the thing about a filing system and no family: one accumulates the halves of things.

Mrs Bevan was confirmed as administrator in the September. He wrote to congratulate her and had a note back, three sentences, perfectly civil, containing not one word that could be built on. He had asked Jean Leslie twice, in 1954 and 1959, and had got nothing. He had asked Pobil in 1957 and had got nothing. He had asked Tar every spring for twelve years and had got nine refusals, four of them in writing, all of them courteous.

He had never once been given anything by anybody at that station. It is, on reflection, the most striking single fact in the file, and it is not a fact about their discretion, since none of them is especially discreet and one of them is Godfrey. It is a fact about what he is, and about what people who have watched him for a while conclude, and he has had it in front of him since about 1957 and has been declining to read it for eleven years.

---

He sent the survey returns to the Martin in the January of 1969.

The whole of both seasons: station data, the sighting records with the informants' names, the movement analysis, the plates, the lot, in two crates, unconditionally, with no request for acknowledgement and no proposal attached and none intended.

He told himself it was efficiency. Two seasons of first-class data going into a cabinet in Miami to be looked at by nobody is a waste of public money, and there is exactly one institution on earth with the husbandry record and the coast and the personnel to use it, and it is running on a trust income and a schedule of nine lines and retainers to boatmen at fifteen dollars a year.

That was true. It was also, he was aware, the last available means of remaining a participant in something, and he had been putting sentences of that shape into files all his life, and this time he wrote the other one down as well, on a separate sheet, and then burnt it, because a man may be honest with himself in a room and still have some idea of what belongs in an office.

Tar's acknowledgement came back in the April. It was one line.

*This will be used, and cited, and it will still be being used in thirty years, and you knew that when you sent it. — R.T.*

He kept it. It is in the file, and it is the only thing in the file that is not a card or a carbon.

---

The manuscript came to him in the November of 1969, from the editor, with the usual note.

*Leslie, J. M., Tar, R. A. J., and Cholmondeley, C. E. — 'The natural history of Nereopithecus aestuarius in the Orinoco delta and Gulf of Paria: distribution, seasonal movement, feeding, rest, and electroreception in a wild population.'* Eighty-one pages, forty-two figures, fourteen years of observation, three authors in that order.

He knew what it was by the end of the second page and it took him a fortnight to read properly.

There was no reason on earth for him to declare an interest. He was a referee of standing in the field; he had never met two of the three authors; the third he had asked to join him nine times and had been refused nine times. The convention did not cover it. Nothing covered it.

He wrote three pages. It is the best referee's report he has ever produced and it will be read by an editor and two people in Aberdeen and then filed, and it recommends acceptance without reservation and without revision, and it says of the third section — which the junior author has been calling too long since 1966, and has cut twice, and which is still too long — that it should on no account be shortened, because it is the only account there will ever be of how these animals sleep, and because the thing that has been killing them in tanks for twenty years is set out in it in four paragraphs by a man who has never once been in a room with any of us and does not appear to have noticed that he has done it.

Then he took out the card, which had begun in the autumn of 1946 as a note about a young man's usefulness, and he ruled a line under the second column, which had two entries and would not now have a third; and in the first column, under twelve years of offers and refusals, he wrote the date and: *Referee, Proc., November 1969. Recommended without reservation. Not to be disclosed.*

And then he sat in the office in London with the fan off and the winter coming on, fifty-nine — sixty-two, now; he had stopped keeping that one — and considered that he had spent fifteen years attempting to acquire, by every proper means and one or two he would not care to itemise, a man, an animal, a mechanism and a station, and had acquired none of them; and that what he had instead was two seasons in somebody else's cabinet, a paper with three names on it and not one of them his, and a single line in another man's hand saying that a thing he had done would still be being used in thirty years.

It was not, on any reckoning he had ever used, an adequate return.

He drafted the spring letter anyway, on the last day of the month, dated forward to the April, warmly worded, addressed to Dr R. Tar at the Martin: an invitation to give a paper, no salary, no post, no proposal, and no answer required.

The thirteenth.

He will send it. He has never once been able to give a colleague an account of why he does it that survived being said out loud, and he has stopped attempting to, and he goes on.
