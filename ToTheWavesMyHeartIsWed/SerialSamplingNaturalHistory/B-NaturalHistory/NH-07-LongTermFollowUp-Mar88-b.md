# Long-Term Follow-Up

## April 1970

The thirteenth letter came in the April as usual and Reggie read it at breakfast and put it down and said, "He is asking me to give a paper."

"He asks you something every April."

"He is asking me to give a paper," Reggie said again, in a different way, and Pascual put down his cup and paid attention, because in sixteen years he had never once heard the man read one of those letters twice.

There was no post attached and no salary and no proposal. There was a symposium in Miami in the September and a session on wild populations, and would Dr Tar consider giving forty minutes on survey method in the southern Caribbean, and no answer was required.

Reggie thought about it for four days and then wrote and accepted, and did not explain himself, and Pascual did not ask him to, and told him instead that he was coming too, and Reggie said, "I had assumed so," which was as close as he ever got.

---

Spilsbury at sixty-three was greyer and slower on his feet and had exactly the same manner, and he came across the hotel lobby with his hand out and said what a very great pleasure it was, and it was true, and it was also, as always, both things.

He had aged the way men of that kind age, which is not much and then all at once. There was a stick he was pretending was an affectation. Pascual looked at his colour for about two seconds out of thirty-five years of habit and then made himself stop, because the man was not his patient and never would be and it is not a decent thing to do to somebody in a lobby.

The paper was on the Thursday morning and it was very good and it was forty-one minutes, and Pascual sat at the back and watched a man of fifty-three stand up in front of two hundred people and do the thing he is actually for.

In the discussion somebody from Woods Hole asked where the seasonal movement figures had come from, since they were better than anything in the literature and covered a coast nobody had worked.

"They come from two sources," Reggie said. "The first is fourteen years of sighting records collected by the Martin from local observers under retainer, which is a method I costed in 1958 and which was refused by my own funding authority eight times and is now in its twelfth year. The second is the two survey seasons of 1966 and 1967, the whole of which — station data, sighting records, informants, plates — was given to my Institute in January 1969 by Dr Spilsbury, unconditionally, without any request attached, and without which the analysis you have in front of you could not have been done. I should like that in the record and I should like it in the printed version."

He then went on to the next slide.

Pascual, who had been sitting eleven rows behind Alan Spilsbury for three-quarters of an hour, had a very good view; and he wishes to record that for something under a second and a half the man's face was not under management.

Thirty-five years of watching faces across tables. Three encounters in sixteen years and one finding, entered in 1957 and never revised: *the man is never surprised.* Pascual went back to the hotel that evening and revised it.

---

They spoke once, alone, on the Friday, on the terrace, while Reggie was being argued with about pooling by two Danes.

"May I ask you a question, Dr Pobil? It is the last one I shall ever ask anybody about this and it is of no operational significance whatever, since I retire in eighteen months."

"You may ask."

"Was there ever a point at which I could have had him?"

Pascual thought about it properly, because the man had asked properly.

"Not 1954," he said. "I know that's the one you've been looking at, and you can put it down. You sat in a dining room with him for five days and you thought you were being worked by a scientist with a column of figures, and you were, and he was also in pieces — he'd stopped sleeping, he was drinking in the afternoons, and he'd done a thing eight days before that he believed was going to kill him. A man in that condition doesn't go anywhere with anybody. He was holding a whole station up with both hands and he'd have gone on holding it if you'd offered him the moon. You never had him in 1954. You had something that looked like him from across a table."

"Then never."

"April 1965," said Pascual.

Spilsbury turned his head.

"He wanted it," Pascual said. "Not the way a man wants a post. He designed that survey in 1958 and costed it at nothing and put it up eight times and had it struck eight times, and you put it on a table in front of him with a vessel attached to it. He wanted it so badly that a man who has spent his entire life making certain nobody can read him was read across a room inside a quarter of an hour, by you, and he knew you'd done it, and he told me so that evening standing at a rail. And he did not say no to you for six months. He has never in his life failed to say no to anybody."

"And?"

"And then the establishment list came, and —"

"I have his letter," Spilsbury said.

Pascual stopped.

"I have had it in a drawer for five years and I can put down most of it from memory. Five paragraphs. He told me exactly why, in order, with the objections answered and my own counter-argument conceded before I could make it, and he did me the courtesy of not putting it as a moral question. I am not asking you why he refused. I know why he refused. I have known since the twenty-ninth of October 1965."

"Then what are you asking?"

"Whether there was anything else in it."

The waiter came and went. Pascual let him.

He was fifty-nine years old and had spent thirty-five of them being asked things across tables by people entitled to ask them, and he knew a properly constructed question when it was put in front of him. That one was not about 1965 at all. It was the last cast of a file that had been open since the September of 1954, dressed as a courtesy, put by a man with eighteen months left who would never have another opportunity, and it was beautifully done and it deserved an answer, and it was not going to get the true one.

"There's one thing," he said. "It isn't in the letter, because he doesn't know it. It's mine, not his, and I've had it for sixteen years and never once said it to him."

"Go on."

"He wrote you that he left in 1948 because he'd found out what he would do if the work was good enough."

"He did," said Spilsbury. "It is the only sentence about himself he has written to me in twenty-four years, and I have thought about it perhaps a thousand times."

"It's wrong."

Spilsbury turned his head.

"He didn't walk out because he'd discovered he was dangerous. He walked out because he was coming apart, and some part of him that wasn't doing the deciding got him through the door, and he has spent twenty-two years filing it as a decision — because a decision is a thing a man does, and the other is a thing that is done to him, and he cannot hold the second one and never has been able to." Pascual turned the glass round on the table. "I met him in the February of 1954. Six years after. I'd been a doctor nineteen years by then and I'd worked in three countries, and I want you to understand that I had never before, and have not since, seen a human being that carefully constructed. Everything was load-bearing. He'd built the whole man out of procedure, on his own, with no help from anybody, and it took him eight years, and it held.

"That isn't conscience. It's scar tissue. It's very fine work and it was laid down over an injury, and you are the man who caused the injury, and he does not know that is what happened to him and I am not going to be the one who tells him at fifty-three."

Spilsbury sat quite still and looked at the water for a good while.

"I have carried that departure for twenty-two years as the one verdict ever passed on the programme," he said. "It was the only judgement anybody delivered on it. Nobody resigned. Nobody wrote anything. One man walked out of the building in 1948, and I have found that useful in a way I have never been proud of, because a verdict implies a man in a condition to pass one." He put his hands flat on the head of the stick. "You are telling me it was not a verdict. It was a casualty."

"Yes."

"I preferred the verdict."

"I know," said Pascual. "That's why you're getting the other one."

Spilsbury took that without any alteration of manner at all, which Pascual had expected and found he minded anyway.

"Then I will give you the corresponding item," he said, "since you have been exact with me. I did not write item seven. It came back on the list from Washington and it is the single post on that establishment I had no hand in whatever. What I did was look at it in the January and decide that I could carry it, on the ground that I should be the man holding the sailing orders.

"I was right. I should like that put plainly, because it is the whole of my position and it is not a comfortable one to hold. I had the sailing orders for two seasons. Nothing was taken, nothing was held, the delta was not worked, and the post was discontinued in the November of 1967 without ever once being used. And there was no version of the thing without the rider: the money came when it came because of what the rider was for, and had I refused it there would have been no survey at all, and no two seasons, and your man would not have had that movement analysis on the screen this morning.

"So I did the correct thing in the January, and I was correct in every particular of it afterwards, and a quantity of correctness is precisely what I have to show for nineteen years. It is not, on any reckoning I have ever used, an adequate return."

"You'd rather it had been a mistake," said Pascual.

Spilsbury turned his head.

"You've been at it all afternoon looking for the place where you got it wrong," said Pascual, "and there isn't one, and that's what's the matter with you. A mistake is a thing a man can be better than."

Spilsbury considered this for some while.

"Yes," he said. "That would appear to be it."

Then he looked at Pascual for a moment longer than he needed to, pleasantly, with his hands on the stick, and said: "Thank you. That was generous, and it was not a kindness, and it was not what I asked for, and I am aware of all three."

"I know you are."

"Quite," said Spilsbury.

And that was the whole of it. What Pascual did not say — and had settled with himself in the lift on the way down that he was not going to say, whatever was asked and however well — was that item seven had not been the whole of the arithmetic in the October; that there had been a man on a river four hundred miles south whose entire safety turned on the same page; and that nobody at that station had given Alan Spilsbury one single thing in sixteen years, and he was not going to be the first.

"May I have one back," he said, "since it's the last of these."

"You may."

"That week in 1954. You were extremely careful with him and I watched you do it and I've thought about it for sixteen years. Were you kind because you were buying, or were you kind?"

"Both," Spilsbury said, with no pause whatever. "It has always been both. I have never been able to get the two apart and I gave up attempting it somewhere about 1950. It is the only interesting thing about me and it is not an achievement."

He put out his hand and Pascual took it, and that was the last conversation they ever had.

---

## 1970 – 1976

The paper came out in the September of 1970: Leslie, Tar & Cholmondeley, eighty-one pages, and the third section was not cut.

It did what such things do, which is nothing at all for eighteen months and then everything. By 1973 there was not a person working on that order of animal anywhere in the world who had not read it, and the four paragraphs in the third section about sleep had closed a twenty-year argument about tank mortality so completely that people began citing it for propositions it did not contain, which Reggie complained about weekly and enjoyed enormously.

Charles became, without ever leaving the caño for more than a month at a time, the world authority on his species. He was extremely bad at it. He answered every letter, at length, giving everything away, and had to be prevented three times by Jean from putting unpublished data into replies to men who would have used it.

The Martin ran on the trust and the grant and never had any money. The line ran every year: sighting records; retainers to observers and boatmen; non-specimen. By 1974 there were forty-one observers on it from the Grenadines to the delta, at fifteen dollars a year, and a field post on the Guayo channel with a research student in it, and the students came back changed and would not shut up about it, which is the only reliable indicator in science that anything is happening.

There is a thing Pascual did every quarter from 1964 until 1987 which appears in no estimate, no minute and no letter.

It began as a package Reggie carried up the caño in the March of 1964, made up at eleven at night in a dispensary with the door shut, out of what a veterinary surgeon can get hold of and a certain amount of arithmetic done twice. After 1966, when there was a man on paper with an affiliation and an address, it became regular and properly obtained and came through the Fundación with the name of a Venezuelan physician on it whom Pascual never met in twenty-one years and wrote to twice a year.

It helped. He would like that written down, because he has never once been able to feel it. From 1964 the man had what he ought to have had, without a break, for twenty-three years.

It was ten years late, and nothing was ever going to give the ten years back, and Pascual knew that perfectly well in 1964 with the string in his teeth, and made the package up and posted it and opened a file.

Pascual turned sixty-five in the November of 1976, and sixty-eight in the November of 1979, and did not say one word to anybody about either.

---

## 1979

The last of the seven died in the October.

She was the female taken as a juvenile off Tobago in 1955 and she had been in the second pen for twenty-four years and had outlived every animal held anywhere in the world by eleven years, and there was no cause of death except that she was old and the season turned.

Pascual did the post-mortem himself. He had done all of them since 1954 and there had been nineteen and this was the last one there would ever be, and he took eleven hours over it and wrote the report at two in the morning and it was the best thing he ever wrote.

In the morning John's successor's successor pulled the sluice and the second pen ran out, and Reggie stood on the apron and watched an empty concrete box fill up with sunlight.

He was sixty-two. He had come to that station in 1948 and had built the pens and had written the husbandry paper that put nineteen animals into tanks in five countries, and had spent the twenty-five years since taking it apart, and there is no version of the ledger in which the second half cancels the first and he never once claimed there was.

"Is there anything you want done with it?" Pascual asked him.

"It is a hole in the ground with a wall round it," said Reggie. "I should like it filled in. And I should like the plaque left, because I put nineteen animals in tanks and there ought to be something at this station that says so, and if I am not here to say it then a piece of brass can."

They filled it in in the February. The plaque is still on the wall. It gives the years and the number and nothing else, and people who visit assume it is a memorial to the animals, which it is, and Pascual has never corrected anybody, and neither did he.

---

## 1980 – 1982

The veterinary and pathological section ceased to exist in the estimates of 1980. Jean kept a line in at forty pounds under *veterinary services, station*, and Pascual has never asked her why and has never needed to.

So at sixty-eight he had no work, and no end to it that anybody could name, and it is the second of those that did the damage, and he did not understand that at the time.

He went down to the second pen at seven every morning for about five weeks after they filled it in, because the legs go on their own. Then he stopped. Stopping was worse than going, for about a fortnight, and after that it was not anything at all.

And he was foul.

He would like that recorded plainly, since he has spent a great deal of this account being clear-eyed about a man who cannot see himself, and it would be cheap to leave out his own spring of 1980. He was not polite and correct, whatever somebody else in this narrative managed. He was short. He was short with the boat crew and short with Jean, who had been administering the place for a fortnight and did not need it. He answered questions with the shortest true answer available and then sat there. He made a remark about the new man from Plymouth at the long table which was funny and accurate and should not have been made in front of him, and had to go and find him afterwards. Four months of it; and Reggie absorbed the entire thing without one word of complaint, which was somehow the most maddening part.

And in the June, in the dispensary, at about nine at night, with nothing whatever happening, he said it.

---

"You have never once asked me to stay. Twenty-six years and you've never had to. That's better than asking, isn't it. You've had a man who won't leave, for nothing, and you've never had to spend a thing on it, and you'll not have to now either."

Reggie was at the far bench with both hands on it and did not turn round.

Pascual had it back before he had finished saying it. He was sixty-eight and had been in medicine forty-five years and had never in his life said a thing to a patient he had not decided to say; and he had just picked the cruellest sentence available to him and used it on the person it would work best on, on purpose, because he had been wretched since February and the man had been being kind about it.

"Pascual —"

"No. I'm sorry. That isn't —"

"Do not withdraw it." Reggie still did not turn round. "I would rather you did not, because I am going to have to check it, and it is a great deal easier to check a thing that has been said than a thing that has been unsaid."

"There's nothing to check. It isn't true."

"Then the check will establish that, and it will have cost nothing." He picked up whatever he had come in for. "I should be obliged if we did not discuss it in the interval."

They did not discuss it. The interval was thirteen months.

---

What Pascual did with those thirteen months was two things.

The first was the monograph.

Nineteen animals, every post-mortem performed by the same man over twenty-five years, with the intake series and the tank records and the water chemistry and the sleep observations set against each of them: the complete pathological record of that species in captivity, four hundred and eleven pages, and the only one there will ever be, because there is not going to be a second series anywhere on earth and that is the entire purpose of the document. It took him two years and seven months and it is the best work of his life and he does not much like looking at it.

The last sentence of the preface is his and Reggie tried three times to improve it and could not. *This series is complete. It is complete because it was closed on purpose, and the object of the present work is that no one should ever again have occasion to open another.*

It has been cited in every husbandry decision taken anywhere in the world since 1983, and there have been eleven of them, and nine went the way he wanted.

The second was the goats.

A man from up the road came down in the March of 1981 about a donkey, because somebody had told him there was a doctor for animals at the station who was not doing anything; and Pascual saw the donkey, and would not take money for it, and by the June was doing two mornings a week in the old dispensary and by the following year four, and was being paid in fish and mangoes and a quantity of rum he had to give away, and put on nine pounds.

It was the first general practice he had had since 1947 and he loved it, and he wants it understood that he knows the difference between work and something to do, because he is a doctor and has spent forty-five years watching men fill their days. A dog with a wire in its foot is a patient. It is not a research programme. He had one of those from 1954 to 1979 and he is not going to pretend on paper that four mornings of goats was the same size.

---

In the July of 1981 Reggie came out onto the apron in the evening with a sheet of paper and gave it to him without a word.

It was a costing. The Institute's directorship, a proposed handover in 1982, a proposed consultancy thereafter at no salary, the sighting-record line's oversight retained by correspondence, a schedule of two visits a year, and an air fare from San Juan to Antigua costed to the dollar for eleven years.

Pascual read it twice standing up.

"This is the check."

"This is the check," said Reggie. "It took thirteen months. The first four versions were dishonest and I destroyed them, and I should tell you that the second version had you staying and me making it up to you, and it was the worst thing I have ever written, and I burnt it in the yard."

"Reggie —"

"I am going to say the whole of it now and I would be grateful if you would let me get to the end, because I have had it in order since about the March and I have not been able to start it." He put his hands behind him. "First. You were right in the June of last year and the check establishes it. I have never asked you to stay. I did not ask you in 1954 and I did not ask you in 1965 and I have not asked you since, and I have told myself for twenty-seven years that this was scrupulousness — that a man who does not ask has not applied pressure. It is not scrupulousness. A man who does not ask gets the thing for nothing and keeps his hands clean, and I have been doing that to you since before you had the wrist, and you have known it for years and did not say it until you were miserable enough to. So: you were right, it cost me a great deal to establish it, and I am not going to apologise, because an apology would be another thing you had to receive.

"Second. I have had everything I wanted." He looked out at the water. "I came here in 1948. I have had thirty-three years of the exact work I would have chosen, on the exact coast, with the person I would have chosen. Every man I trained with settled for something. I have not settled for anything, and I do not know of another case.

"Third. In the October of 1966 I said that the seven were the last, and I put my hand up for it, and you put yours up after mine, and it was right and I would do it again this afternoon. It has one consequence neither of us stated. That decision ends your work and does not end mine. This Institute still has a directorship, a coast, a line of nine words, a field station and sixty observers — and it has no post for you at all, and has not had since the October of 1979, and I have watched you fill nineteen months of it with a monograph and a donkey.

"So it is not an exchange and it is not a debt and it is not making anything up to anybody. It is a distribution, and there are two available. In the first, I have all of what I want and you have almost none, for as long as we both last. In the second, you have all of it and I have about two-thirds — I have costed the two-thirds, it is in the fourth column, and it does not balance. I go and live in a city where I know nobody, in a language I have not got, two aeroplanes from the only work I have ever done. I shall be a man who used to do something.

"The second distribution is the larger total. I have run it eleven times and it does not come out any other way, and that is the whole of my reasoning, and I would be obliged if nobody ever calls it a sacrifice, because it is not one."

Pascual stood on the apron with the paper in his hand for a while.

"I'm not going to pretend I don't want it," he said.

"I should be extremely annoyed if you did."

"Right." He folded it along its own crease, because it was the only thing his hands could think of to do. "Then here's the one thing I'll give you back, and you'll not believe it. You'll get more of it than you think. You'll hate the first two years and you'll be unbearable and I'll want to put you in the sea. And then somebody will post you a bad paper to referee and you'll be all right."

"That is not an argument."

"No," said Pascual. "It's a prognosis. I'm better at those than you are."

"I had intended to be carried out of this building," Reggie said. "I want that on the record and then I shall not mention it again. It is not a figure of speech. I have had it as an actual plan since about 1959 and I costed my pension round it. I have watched men retire and go to pieces inside two years and I have written to their widows."

"I know. I've read the letters."

"Have you."

"You leave them out to be posted," said Pascual. "Twenty-seven years."

They did not mention any of it again.

---

And then he had a date.

That is the thing he would want said, if anybody ever asked him what the difference was between the first eighteen months and the last sixteen, and nobody ever has. From the July of 1981 he knew the month. A man who knows the month in which a thing ends can do very nearly anything until then; a man who does not know is doing something else entirely, with the same hands, and it will take him apart at about the fourth month. That is a clinical observation and he stands by it, and it is the whole of what he had failed to understand in the spring of 1980, and it is why he said what he said in the dispensary in the June.

---

One evening in the September of 1981 he was in the dispensary at half past eight with a mongrel bitch on the table and a wire in her foot and nobody to hold the lamp, and the door opened, and Reggie came in and took the lamp and held it.

He held it badly. He held it in the wrong place for the first ten minutes and had to be told twice and was, for the first time in thirty years, entirely biddable about being told. He did not say anything whatever, then or afterwards.

He came the next week, and the week after that, and every Wednesday for fourteen months until they left, and he never once got the lamp right and he never once missed.

---

Hester handed over the Martin in the September of 1980, at seventy-three, having administered it for fifteen years and founded it for thirty-four.

She gave it to Jean Leslie, who had been at the station since 1954 as a research student and had spent the first six years of it having her findings published under other people's names, and who was now fifty-three and the most cited person in the field and had been offered a chair twice and had said no twice.

At the handover, in the room with the long table, Hester said eleven words and sat down, and the eleven words were: "I have given this to the person who did the work."

Charles came up for it. He arrived on the Thursday boat with a crate of specimens for Jean and a hat that was worse than Hester's, and he was fifty, and he looked, to a doctor of sixty-eight who was not on duty and could not help it, like a man of fifty who had had malaria three times and lived twenty-six years on fish.

Pascual examined him in the dispensary on the Saturday because he always did and Charles always let him, in the way of a man humouring an old friend's superstition.

It took longer than usual, and he made him lie down for the second half of it, and then made him walk up the path to the tree and back and listened again at the door.

The heart was larger than it had been in 1978. Not by much. Enough to find, if you were the man who had been finding it every eighteen months for fourteen years and had the measurements in a file in a cabinet at that station. The apex was further out than it had any business being at fifty. He had been breathing on the path before he ought to have been, and had known it, and had said nothing about it, which was the piece of information that actually mattered.

"Anything?" said Charles, buttoning up.

"You are not eating enough and you are hauling that boat about like a boy of twenty."

"You always say eat."

"I am always right," said Pascual, and wrote in the file, and did not say the rest.

The rest was that he had known the shape of this since 1966 and had been managing it at four hundred miles for fourteen years. That ten years with nothing whatever, from the August of 1954 until Reggie carried the first package up that river, done to a body that had been on it since he was nineteen and was still being built, does something to a heart that nothing afterwards undoes. That the crossing itself — whatever it actually was; he never described it to anybody in thirty-three years and neither of them ever had the nerve to ask — had very probably done as much again inside a fortnight as the ten years did in ten. And that a heart made to work in the wrong condition for that long gets bigger and then gets weaker, and takes decades over it, and does not stop.

He did not say it because it was not yet actionable, and because there was nothing to be done about it on a caño four hundred miles south, and because he had thirty-two years of experience of what happens to a man's remaining life when you hand him a thing he cannot act on, and because Charles was fifty and had another book in him.

He has gone over that decision perhaps a thousand times. He would make it again. He is not certain that is a defence.

---

They went to San Juan in the November of 1982.

Reggie was sixty-five and gave the directorship to a good man from Plymouth and did not weep at the jetty, and Jean did, and Hester, who was seventy-five and had not come down to the jetty for anybody since 1966, came down to the jetty.

The house is in Ocean Park, two streets back, with a flat roof and a fig tree that was in the deeds as an asset and is in fact a menace. Pascual's sister lived four minutes away until she died in 1985.

---

## 1983

He was perfect for eight months, and Pascual would like it recorded that in fifty-seven years he had never watched anybody try as hard at anything as that man tried at Puerto Rico.

He learned Spanish. Badly, at sixty-six, with a discipline that was frankly alarming: two hours out of a schoolbook every morning, a list, and a weekly test which he set himself and marked himself and entered in a book. He came to the sister's on Sundays and sat in the noise and was courteous to eleven people whose names he had written down with their relations and their children's ages, and got every one of them right, which nobody else in that house has ever managed.

And it was awful.

That is the part Pascual could not have predicted and cannot now describe to anybody without it sounding like a complaint about manners. He was not rude. He did nothing wrong. In five years he has not done one thing that a man could be asked to stop doing, and that is exactly what was the matter, because there was nothing to take hold of.

He was polite. He was polite at half past seven in the morning and he was polite at eleven at night. He said thank you for the coffee every day for eight months in a language he was learning at the rate of a list a week. He asked after people's work and remembered the answers and asked again a fortnight later, correctly, by name. He never once put his feet up, or came to the table without his collar, or interrupted, or told a story, or said he was tired, or asked for anything at all.

And nobody knew what to do with him.

Pascual knew exactly what he was looking at, which did not help either of them. He had been that man himself in the spring of 1980, in a building with no animals in it and no date on anything, and he had done it the other way about — short instead of polite, which at least gives people something to push against. He had had thirteen months of it and he had come out of it because somebody handed him a sheet of paper with a month written on it.

There was no sheet of paper available here. San Juan was not going to end.

Pascual watched it happen at his sister's over about five months and could not stop it, because you cannot say *don't do that* to a man who is being kind to your family with everything he has. The talk would drop half a step when he came out onto the terrace and then start again a shade more carefully. They spoke to him in Spanish slowly and in English apologetically. His sister began saying *dile a Reggie* — tell Reggie — with him three feet away, not out of unkindness, out of not knowing which register he took; and Reggie, who had spent thirty-four years being able to read a room in under a minute, heard it, and understood it, and put a little more effort in, which made it worse.

He reorganised their own kitchen twice, once in the February and once in the June, and after the second time Pascual could not find the coffee for three days and did not say so, and has thought since that this was cowardice.

And he stopped sleeping.

Pascual did not raise it. He noticed it in the February — the bed going cold at about three — and did not raise it in the March or the April, and by the June it was four nights in seven, and he had begun out of thirty years of habit to keep a record, and had then thrown the record away, because he could not sit in a room with what it was.

The man had not been up at three in the morning since 1961.

---

In the July, on a Sunday, in the sister's kitchen with the door open and eleven people through it, Reggie said, quite quietly, in the voice he uses for figures:

"I have perhaps eleven working years. I have spent one of them here."

Pascual put the dish down.

He had been waiting eight months for the man to say one true thing in this country and he was not going to receive it badly now it had come. He took Reggie's face in both hands, which he has done perhaps five times in thirty years and never before with the door open, and held on to it.

"I know," he said. "I have known since you handed me that paper on the apron. I knew what it was going to cost you and I let you do it, and I'd let you do it again tomorrow, and that is the worst thing there is to know about me and you may have it and keep it.

"And listen to me. In the June of 1980 I said a worse thing to you than that, in a dispensary, on purpose, because I was wretched and you were being kind about it — and you went away and checked it for thirteen months and came back with a costing. I am not going to check anything. There is nothing to check. You have not lost a year. You have spent a year, and you spent it on me, and I know exactly what it is worth because I have spent twenty-six of them the same way.

"And you have not lost a year. You have spent a year, and you spent it on me, and I am not going to insult you by pretending it was anything else or that it was free. Now come and eat, because there are eleven of them through there and if we are not at that table in two minutes my brother-in-law will start on the status question and nobody will be allowed to leave."

Reggie did not say anything. There was not, in fairness, anything available.

It is still the worst thing that has ever been said in that house, and it was said by the man in it who was trying hardest, and Pascual has never once held it against him. That is not the same as it being over, and it was not over, because the person it was not over for was Reggie.

---

He could not put it down. That is the whole of what the next four months were, and it had nothing to do with Pascual, who had settled it inside ten seconds with a dish in his hands and considered the matter closed. Reggie had been forgiven in a kitchen, immediately and without conditions, by somebody who meant it, and had no procedure whatever for that, and went round and round it from the July to the November like a man checking a sum that keeps coming out right.

Then, in the November, he came out onto the roof in the evening and stood about for a while and said:

"In the July, in your sister's kitchen, I made a statement which was accurate and which I wish to withdraw."

"Reggie, that's been —"

"I have been four months on the mechanism. Accuracy is not a defence; I have established that, and it took some doing. And I have not been able to discover the procedure by which a true statement is withdrawn, because there is not one, and I have therefore concluded that the procedure is to say that I withdraw it." He had his hands behind him. "I withdraw it. I am not going to tell you it was untrue, because you would know, and because I do not lie to you about findings."

Pascual sat down on the parapet.

"That'll do," he said.

"It is not adequate."

"No. It'll do."

## 1984

What broke it was the fig tree.

It is a large fig, it was in the deeds as an asset, and from about the March it drops on the tiles at a rate of some pounds a day, and it had been dropping on them for a year and a half while a man of sixty-seven swept them at seven every morning and said nothing about it whatever.

In the April the sister's eldest, who is a decent man of forty with a lorry, came round to say that a cousin would take the tree out for nothing if it were a nuisance, and asked, in the way people ask a guest, whether it was any trouble at all.

And Reggie said: "It is a menace and it has been a menace since the day we arrived and I have wanted it down for eighteen months and I have not liked to say so."

The whole terrace stopped.

Then Pascual's sister put her glass down and said that she had hated that tree since 1961; and her son said his father had planted it out of spite; and there was then an argument in two languages lasting some forty minutes about a tree, in the course of which Reggie was contradicted twice, contradicted somebody else once, said the word *ridículo* with the wrong stress and was corrected by a child, and lost — the tree is still there — and came home visibly better than he had been in a year and a half.

Pascual thought about that for a long time afterwards and it is not complicated. They had been given eighteen months of a man with nothing he wanted, and there is no way in the world to be close to such a person. The first time he wanted something out loud, in front of them, badly enough to be rude about it, they had something to take hold of.

He got worse after that, and it was an enormous relief. He was annoyed by the radio. He was annoyed by the eldest's lorry, on grounds of where it was left, and said so, and the lorry was moved, and the two of them are now thick as thieves and argue about the sea wall. He came home from the market having been overcharged and was furious for a whole evening. He told the sister's youngest, who is a bad driver, that she was a bad driver, and she told him he was an old woman, and he laughed — Pascual was in the room — and said that this was correct.

By the autumn they had stopped talking past him. They had begun instead to talk to him, and then, in the way of that family, at him, and by 1985 they were interrupting him, which is the highest honour that house confers.

---

The conversation with the sister was in the September and Pascual was not present and has it in two versions.

She was seventy-four and she had not seen her brother work since 1948, and she asked Reggie — over the washing-up, in the kitchen, in her slow careful Spanish, because she had decided he could take it — what Pascual was like at it.

Nobody had ever asked him that in his life.

He gave her, by her account, the best part of an hour: the post-mortems, and the hands, and the eleven-hour report at two in the morning on an animal that had outlived every one of its kind in captivity by eleven years; and the fact that in twenty-three years he had never once seen the man be short with a frightened animal or with a frightened student; and that there were three occasions on which Pascual Pobil had been offered a great deal of money and a great deal more scope by better-funded people and had declined inside a minute, and that he, Reggie, had never been able to establish exactly why, and had come to the conclusion that some men are simply not for sale and that it is not a virtue in them, it is a fact about them, like a blood group.

His version is that he was asked a question and answered it accurately at some length.

Hers, which she gave Pascual on the terrace afterwards with her hand on his arm, was that she had asked a polite Englishman about her little brother and had got a man talking about the person he loves for fifty minutes without once using a word she would have to translate, and that she had had to go and sit down.

She died in the March of 1985. Reggie carried at the funeral and had been asked to.

---

The students were Jean's doing and they came later, and they were a grace note rather than the cure.

She is not sentimental, and she had been watching from Antigua and had said nothing to anybody, and in the March of 1985 she wrote to ask whether Dr Tar would take a research student for a fortnight, at the student's own expense, to have her second chapter taken apart. She came; he took it apart; he gave her eleven days and forty pages of notes and made her cry twice; and she went back to Aberdeen and finished in nine months and is now running her own group in Bergen and cites him in everything.

By 1986 there were four a year, and two men from the marine station at Mayagüez on Thursdays, one of whom is thirty-one and unwise about salinity and comes anyway. They sleep in the back room and eat too much of Pascual's cooking and get up at six because he does, and they go away better than they came and write to him for the rest of their lives. Pascual, who spent twenty-three years watching a man take young people apart on an apron in Antigua for their own good, finds he prefers it done in his own house, where he can feed them afterwards.

He kept the coast as well. Two visits a year, the sighting-record line by correspondence, forty-one observers and then fifty-two and then sixty. He refereed everything anybody sent him. He is, from a flat roof in Ocean Park, the reason a line of nine words costed in 1958 was still funded in 1988.

He slept, from about the end of 1984, most nights.

---

It never came back all the way, and Pascual is not going to pretend on paper that it did.

He got about two-thirds of it. That is the figure Reggie costed in the fourth column of a sheet of paper on the apron in the July of 1981, sixteen months before he had to live in it, and Pascual has never settled whether that is the most impressive thing about the man or the most frightening.

He is a man who used to do something. He said so on the apron and he was right. There are afternoons on that roof — fewer than there were, but there are still afternoons — when he sits with a reprint on his knee and does not read it, and Pascual, who is not sensitive and has never once needed to be, knows exactly which building he is in.

And Pascual is not sorry, and it was the larger total, and both of those are true at once. He has never yet met anybody who could hold them together and he has given up explaining it.

The spring letter came every April.

It stopped in 1981. Pascual came out onto the apron in the May of that year and found Reggie sitting with the post on his knee, and he said, "It has not come," and it was the only occasion in thirty-four years that Pascual saw him unable to place his own reaction to something and know that he could not place it and say so out loud.

The notice was in the July. Alan Spilsbury, seventy-four, at Winchester. There was a paragraph about the wartime establishment that said nothing, and a list of the offices he had held, and it did not mention the survey, because the survey ran for two seasons and was paid off in 1968 and there is no line in any obituary anywhere for the best thing a man ever did.

Reggie wrote to the widow. He never told Pascual what he put in it and Pascual never asked.

## 1985

Charles wrote in the September, four pages, and three and a half of them were about a feeding association he had been watching for two seasons, and the last half page said:

*Something is up with M. and I can't get at it. He has stopped going north in the season — third year now — and stays in the lower channel where I am. That is not what he does; he has gone north every June since 1955. And he is on me. Not distressed, not sick — he eats, he's in good condition, Ferris's man weighed him in June and he's four pounds up. But he is at the landing every night now and he will not settle unless he is against the steps, and when I go in he comes and puts his head on my chest and stays there, sometimes for an hour.*

*I've been reading it as one thing and I now think it is something else and I have no way whatever of finding out. You'll say it's age. I'd like it to be age.*

Pascual read that letter three times on the roof in the evening and then went and stood at the rail of it for a while, and then said nothing to Reggie for two days, which is the longest he had gone in thirty years.

Because he knew what puts an animal's head on a man's chest and keeps it there for an hour.

It is not affection and it is not grief and it does not carry one single word, and it never has and it never will. It is the only channel that animal has ever had and it carries what a body is doing, involuntarily, at close range, in conductive water, and it had been carrying that man's heart to that animal for thirty years, every night, at four feet, and something in it had changed.

Monty had known since 1983.

## 1986

He went down in the February with Jean's season and made Charles sit on the bench in the sun with his shirt off, and listened for eleven minutes, and then took his wrist, and then made him walk up the path to the tree and back and listened again.

The rhythm had gone, and had been gone for some time, and he had not mentioned it, because it had come on over years and he had adjusted his life to it a little at a time, which is what people do. There was fluid at both bases. He had been sleeping propped up since the rains and had told himself it was the damp.

Pascual sat down on the bench next to him and told him all of it, in order, in plain words, and did not stop and did not soften it, because he had promised himself at four that morning in a hammock that he would not.

It was not a valve and it had never been a valve. It was the muscle. It had been given the wrong instructions from the inside for ten years, at an age when a man is still being made, and had been asked in the middle of those ten years for whatever that crossing was; and it had compensated for thirty years, magnificently, the way they do, and it had now stopped compensating and would not start again.

"There is no operation. I am going to say that twice, because you will go looking. There is nothing in Caracas and nothing in Houston and nothing anywhere else that cuts this out or puts a new one in or gives you back one hour of it. Nobody is going to fix this. That's the first thing.

"The second thing is San Juan. There's a man in Santurce I'd trust with my own, and he's forty-one, so he'll still be there in ten years. There's a room, and a hospital eleven minutes away, and drugs I can't get here and can't post. You'd be in a chair instead of hauling that boat and you'd be seen every month instead of every eighteen. And I'll give you the figure, because you'll ask for it and you're owed it. I think that's five years, and it might be seven."

"And here?"

"Fifteen months. Perhaps eighteen. It won't be sudden at the end and the last few months will not be pleasant, and I'll be here for that part and so will Reggie, and you may argue about anything you like except that, because it isn't your decision, it's ours."

Charles sat and looked at the river for a long time.

"He can't come," he said.

"No."

"Then that's the whole of it and we can stop." He said it with no heat at all, which was the worst of it. "And it isn't courage, and I'd be obliged if nobody ever says it was. Five years in a chair in Santurce with him in this channel not knowing where I've gone, against two here. That is not a hard sum. I've had a good deal of practice at it."

"I know."

"You'd have done the same."

"I would," said Pascual, "and I'd have been wrong too, and I'm not going to argue with you, and I want it minuted that I said San Juan twice."

"Minuted," said Charles. And then, after a while: "He knew. Didn't he. That's what all this has been."

"He's known about three years. He can't have any idea what it is — he's got no way of knowing what a heart is for, or that yours is going to stop, and that is not the kind of thing the sense does and you know better than anybody alive that it never will be. All he's got is that something in you isn't what it was in 1982 and is getting further from it, and he's answered it the only way an animal can, which is to stop going north and put his head on it."

"Oh," said Charles. "Oh, hell."

He had his hands over his face for a while. Then he wiped them on his shorts and asked what the drugs were called, and got a pencil out.

## 1986 – 1987

He got fifteen months, which was the bottom of the figure Pascual had given him and no worse than he had feared.

They were good for the first nine of them. He finished the monograph. He got the twelfth season of the delta counts done from a chair in the bow with a student doing the work and swearing at him. Hester came down in the March of 1986 at seventy-nine, by aeroplane and boat and launch, alone, having told nobody until she was in Trinidad, and stayed five weeks, and they sat under the shelter every afternoon and were audible from the water laughing.

Reggie and Pascual came down in the November of 1986 and stayed, and did not go back.

Pascual doctored him. There was digitalis and there were diuretics and there was a hammock arranged at an angle that took four attempts, and there was a great deal of sitting up at night, which Pascual was seventy-five for and managed, mostly, and Reggie took the second half of every night without ever once being asked and without ever once mentioning it.

Charles died on the ninth of May 1987, at fifty-seven, in the late afternoon, in about two feet of water at the bottom of the steps, where he had asked to be and where they had carried him, with Monty holding him up on the river side and Pascual on the other with his hand under his back.

It took a while. He was not frightened, and he was not, at the very end, particularly there, and the last thing he said that was language was in Warao, to the water, and Pascual did not understand it and has never asked anybody to translate it.

Monty stayed against him the whole time.

He did not thrash and he did not cry out and there was no flare of any kind; Pascual had wondered about that, and Reggie had said beforehand, flatly, that a slow decline does not produce one and never has, and it did not. He simply held the man up, and went on holding him after there was no reason to, and would not let him go for a long time, and then did.

Then he went out into the channel and stayed there, in the middle, on the surface, for two days.

---

Reggie said nothing for a week and then said all of it at once, at two in the morning, at the table under the shelter, with the lamp between them.

"I have done the arithmetic and I am going to give it to you and I should like it checked, because I have checked it eleven times and cannot find the error, and I have arrived at the point where I am not certain that I want to."

"Go on."

"On the twenty-eighth of August 1954 I arranged, deliberately, to be in a condition in which I could not know what was going to be done, and I arranged it because I had already worked out what would be done and wished to be able to state truthfully afterwards that I had not known it. He went the following night. He was in the sea for however long that was. He was then without what he required for ten years, and he was without it because he was up a river, and he was up a river because of a room I had arranged to be out of." He had his hands flat on the table. "That is the chain, and there is no break in it. Every link is mine, I made each of them on purpose, and I would make three of the four again."

"Which wouldn't you?"

"I do not know," said Reggie. "That is the eleventh check, and it is where I stop, every time."

Pascual let the river run for a bit.

"Right. You'll get it as his doctor, and you're not going to care for it, and you're getting it anyway because you asked for the check.

"You did not cause it. You made it possible, and those are different things, and I'm the one entitled to say which, because I'm the one who has been listening to that heart since 1966. He made three choices. The first at twenty-four, on the twenty-ninth of August, with the animal dying in front of him and you not in the room — and if you had been in the room he'd have gone anyway and taken the boat you refused him. The second in 1966, when he was on paper and could have come north to a hospital and a life, and did not. The third in February 1985, on that bench, when I offered him five years in my own house and he gave me three words. You get the first. You don't get the other two."

Reggie was shaking his head before he had finished.

"No. That is a partition, and it is a kind one, and I do not accept it." He said it quite levelly, in the way he says things he has already been over. "A man chooses among what is available to him, and I determined what was available to him. In 1966 he could have come north to a hospital and a life — and he could not, because there was an animal in that channel; and the animal was in that channel because it went into the sea on the twenty-ninth of August 1954 with him on it; and it went into the sea because of a room I arranged to be out of. In 1985 the same, only harder. You have divided one act into three and given me a third of it. The division is the whole of your argument, and the division is not real."

"It was real to him." Pascual did not raise his voice. "He's the one it happened to, and he'd have said so, and you know he would, and he'd have been rude about it."

"Probably. I am not able to use it."

"No."

Pascual sat with that a while. He had known it was coming — he had known it in Antigua in 1962, watching the man read a letter at a bench — and he had written the answer out in his head perhaps forty times in twenty-five years and had never once got it to hold.

"Then I'm not going to argue it," he said. "I've been at this fifty-two years and I have never once seen a correct answer do anything at two in the morning, mine or anybody else's. I gave you it because you asked to have it checked and I don't lie to you about findings. And I'll give it you again tomorrow, and the day after, and you'll go on not accepting it, and I'll go on saying it. That's the arrangement and it isn't up for discussion."

Reggie turned the cup round once on the table.

"I cannot put the chain down. I have tried eleven times. I am unwilling to be in this house in the mornings. I have not eaten since Saturday. I do not know what it is that I am describing, and I should be extremely glad if somebody would tell me what to do next."

Pascual reached over and took the wrist, two fingers along the inside and the thumb round the back, in the place, and said nothing whatever about what he found there.

"You don't have to know what it is," he said. "There's nothing to solve and you'll not think your way out of it, and I'm not going to try and talk you out of it either, because I've stood at about four hundred of these and talking has never once done it.

"Here's what you do next. You go to bed. In the morning you help me with his papers, because there are thirty-one years of them and Jean wants the lot and you're the only man alive who can put them in an order that means anything. Then we do it again the day after. That's the whole of the treatment. It isn't much of one and it's what there is."

Reggie considered this.

"That is a real task," he said, "and you have selected it for that reason."

"I have."

"Thank you," said Reggie, and did not take his arm back.

They sat like that for a while with the lamp going and the river going, and then went in.

---

Hester came in the June.

She was eighty. She travelled alone, again, and Jean's students met her at Tucupita and she was, by her own account, sick for most of the launch and did not mind.

Pascual watched her go down the steps into the water at half past five on her first evening in a bathing costume of some antiquity, an old woman with a bad hip going into a brown river, and Monty came in from the channel inside ten minutes.

She was there an hour and a half.

Pascual is not sensitive and Reggie is not sensitive and neither of them has the faintest conception of what passed between those two, and Reggie's rule, which he stated once and never again, is that the channel carries no propositions and therefore nothing was said, in the only sense in which the word means anything, and that whatever occurred is not knowable by them and never will be.

Pascual thinks that is correct and thinks it is also the least interesting true thing anybody has ever said about it.

What he saw was a woman who lost the same way in 1938 and got twenty-eight years of nothing, sitting in four feet of water with the only other creature in the world in her position; and he thinks it is worth setting down that they were not doing anything, either of them, that a person on the bank could have described.

She stayed six weeks that year. She has been three times since. She has an arrangement with the field post and with Jean and it is written into the Institute's estimates as *travel, administrative (emeritus)*, at forty-one pounds a year, which is a lie of Jean Leslie's, and Pascual hopes it is never audited.

Monty went north in the season of 1988, for the first time since 1982, and came back in the September, and the student on the Guayo channel logged it and sent the sighting record in, and it was bought at retainer for fifteen dollars, on a line of nine words costed in 1958 and struck eight times, and it is in the cabinet at the Martin with four thousand others.

## March 1988

The reprint came on a Tuesday from a woman in Caracas neither of them has met.

It is on feeding association in the lower delta and it is not very good and the statistics are optimistic, and it cites the 1970 paper eleven times and it cites the sighting-record series, and in the acknowledgements it thanks the observers by name, all sixty of them, over four lines of small type, which is a convention that started at the Martin in 1971 because Charles refused to publish without it and would not be argued out of it.

Reggie read it on the roof with a pencil and got through it in about forty minutes and then sat with it on his knee.

"It will be somebody I have never heard of, in about 2010," he said, "who does the thing this ought to have done, and she will do it off this series, and she will cite it as *Tar et al.*, which is inaccurate, because it is Leslie's method and Cholmondeley's material and the only part of it that is mine is the costing."

"You've been saying a version of that since 1958."

"I have been correct since 1958."

The light was going. Down the street somebody's radio was doing what radios do at that hour in that country, and the fig tree was dropping its filth on the tiles, and Pascual, who was seventy-six and had had a good day, reached over out of forty years of habit and took the wrist, two fingers along the inside and the thumb round the back, in the place.

It was going slow and steady, the way it does in a man who has finished thinking for the day.

"You are not taking a reading," Reggie said.

"I am, actually."

"Ah." He let it be taken. Then he said, "It is the ninth of the month on Friday and I have written to Hester and I have written to the field post and I have not written to Jean, and I find I do not want the anniversary observed and I also find that I have arranged for three people to observe it, and I have been unable to reconcile those."

"You don't have to reconcile it, Reggie. It's just that you miss him."

Reggie sat with that.

"Yes," he said, after a while. "That would appear to cover the observations."

Pascual kept hold of the wrist a little longer than the reading required, which he had been doing since 1954 and had never once been challenged on, and then let it go and went in to see about the supper.
