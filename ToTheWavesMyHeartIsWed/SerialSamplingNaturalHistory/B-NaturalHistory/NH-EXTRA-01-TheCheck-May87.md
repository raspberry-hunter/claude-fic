# The Check

## September 1970

The paper went well, at forty-one minutes, and then two Danes disputed the pooling of the Boca stations for the better part of an hour, and were wrong, and had to be shown why they were wrong in a manner that would not prevent them from citing it.

He came out at about six and Alan Spilsbury was in the corridor with his stick and said that if Dr Tar had a half-hour to spare he should be glad of it, and that there was nothing in it and nothing would be asked.

They sat at the end of the bar where it was quiet.

It was the first conversation the two of them had ever had with nothing on the table. Twenty-four years. There had been an establishment between them from 1946 to 1948 and an offer between them every spring from 1957 to 1965 and a refusal in five paragraphs in the October of that year, and there had never once been an occasion on which one of them was not either buying or declining. Reggie found that he did not know how to begin, and observed that his hands had put themselves flat on his knees, and moved them.

Spilsbury asked one question about Charles and asked it plainly.

"Is he well?"

"I am not going to discuss him."

"No," said Spilsbury. "Quite right, and I knew you would not, and I asked because I should have thought the worse of myself if I had spent the whole evening not asking. There it is. It will not come up again."

And it did not, and Reggie has thought since that this was the single most decent thing that man ever did in his presence, and that it took him under six seconds, and that it cost him something.

They talked about the survey for a while, which was easy. Then about a Danish error, which was easier. Then Spilsbury said that the movement analysis had been a great pleasure to be cited in that morning, and that he had not expected it, and that he had been obliged to look at the carpet.

"It was not gratitude," Reggie said.

"I did not think it was."

"You sent me two seasons of first-class data in the January of 1969 without conditions attached, and I had spent fifteen years telling myself and other people that you do not do anything without conditions attached. That is a false statement. I have made it in this building and in Antigua and once in writing, and I do not leave false statements standing when I have been shown they are false. That is the whole of the reason and I would rather you had it correctly than warmly."

"I would always rather have it correctly," said Spilsbury, "and you are the only man of my acquaintance who has ever understood that, and it is why I have gone on writing to you for twelve years after it became apparent that you would not come."

---

The half-hour became rather more than two, and somewhere in the second of them Reggie asked him the question he had been carrying since the spring of 1948.

"Did it work?"

Spilsbury did not pretend not to know what was meant.

"That is two questions and I shall answer both. Did it produce anything: yes. Four lines of work. Two produced nothing whatever, one produced a thing which is at present in the hands of every forward dressing station in the alliance and has kept a number of people alive whom I could not name for you, and the fourth produced what you and I are not going to sit in a hotel and name.

"Was it worth it: I am not going to answer that, because I do not think the question has an answer and I have stopped respecting people who say that it does. What I will give you is the other figure, which nobody has ever asked me for. Thirty-one. That is the number of people who went into that programme as subjects between 1946 and the winding-up. Seven came out of it in a condition their families would have recognised."

He turned the glass round once.

"I have both of those figures. I have had them for twenty-two years and I have never once put them into the same sentence, and I find that I can do it in a bar in Miami and could not do it anywhere else, and I am not going to enquire why."

Reggie sat with it.

It was not the numbers. The numbers were not his and had nothing to do with his. It was that the man kept two of them: a figure for what the work had produced and a figure for what it had cost, both true, both his, held in separate places for twenty-two years and never once set down together.

Because Reggie keeps two. There is a husbandry paper of 1955 which is cited in every marine station in the world, and there is a mortality table in a cabinet in Antigua with nineteen animals on it, and he has never in sixteen years put those two objects in the same paragraph, and until that evening he could not have told anybody that he was declining to.

So he understood, at fifty-three, in a bar, opposite a man of sixty-three who was ordering another drink, that he was not sitting across from his opposite number. He was sitting across from the same arrangement with a different species in it and forty years' more nerve; and that the whole of the distinction he had been living on since 1948 was that he had walked out of the building, which is a difference in conduct and not a difference in kind.

He did not say so. There was no version of saying it that was not either an accusation or a confession, and he did not know which he had meant it as, and he had learned by then not to speak until he did.

What he said was: "Thank you for the figure."

"You are the only person I could have given it to," said Spilsbury, "and I should like to be clear that this is not a compliment to either of us."

---

Pascual came back from the terrace at about nine and gave him the whole of it in the usual way, verbatim, sitting on the end of the bed with his shoes off, beginning at the first minute.

Except that it was not verbatim.

Reggie has never mentioned this and does not intend to. He had sixteen years of that arrangement by then and he knew its shape the way one knows a handwriting, and somewhere in the middle of the account of the terrace there was a place where the joins did not meet: a question answered a shade too completely, and then a change of subject that was Pascual's and not Spilsbury's.

He decided inside a minute not to ask, and has not asked in eighteen years, and the reason is not delicacy. The reason is that there was exactly one subject on which Pascual Pobil would ever have edited anything for him, and if the edit was there then the subject had come up, and if the subject had come up and Pascual had dealt with it, then it had been dealt with by the best man available and there was nothing whatever for Reggie to add except the pleasure of knowing.

He has, on reflection, wanted the pleasure of knowing for eighteen years. He has not had it. It is a small standing debt in the other direction and he finds he does not mind carrying it.

---

## June 1980 – July 1981

He was standing at the far bench in the dispensary with both hands on it when Pascual said the thing about not being asked, and the first observation available to him was that his hands had tightened on the edge of the bench sufficiently to be uncomfortable, and the second was that he did not appear to be able to turn round.

He heard the apology start and stopped it.

He would like to set down why, since it looked from the outside like magnanimity and was not. If the sentence were withdrawn it would become a thing said by a miserable man in a bad year, and a thing said by a miserable man in a bad year can be forgiven and filed. If it were left standing it was a proposition, and a proposition can be checked, and Reggie had spent thirty-four years knowing precisely what to do with a proposition and had no procedure whatever for the other.

So he asked to be allowed to check it, and went away, and did.

---

The check took thirteen months and it is the only piece of work of his life he has never shown to anybody.

He began where one begins, which is with the record. Twenty-six years of estimates, minutes, the correspondence file, his own diaries, which are not diaries but appointment books with figures in them. He was looking for occasions on which he had asked Pascual Pobil for something, and occasions on which he had arranged not to have to.

The first tranche came out well and he was pleased with it for about a fortnight. He had never applied pressure. He had refused to apply pressure in the March of 1957, at some cost. He had refused to apply it in the April of 1965, at a great deal more, and had said so at the time, on a rail, and had been correct.

Then, in about the September, he did the thing he has been doing to other people's data since 1946, which is to ask what the record would look like if the opposite were true.

And it looked identical.

That is the finding. A man who never asks and a man who arranges never to have to ask leave exactly the same documentary trace, and there is no instrument anywhere that distinguishes them, and the only difference between the two is in the man, and Reggie was not able to establish which of them he was by any method he could construct. He tried for four months. He is very good at this and he could not do it.

What he could establish was the consequence, which was not in doubt at all. Whichever kind of man he was, he had had twenty-six years of somebody who would not leave, and had never spent one thing on it, and had told himself a story about scrupulousness while it accrued.

---

There were five versions of the costing and four went into the incinerator in the yard.

The first was dishonest by being generous: it gave Pascual everything and Reggie a martyrdom, and he saw what it was inside a week and burnt it and was ashamed of it for a month.

The second was the bad one. In the second, Pascual stayed at the Martin and Reggie made it up to him — leave, a salary he could not have justified, a laboratory refitted out of a trust that had not the money, the two visits a year to Puerto Rico instead of one. He worked on it for nine days. It was the most detailed of the five and it balanced beautifully, and what it was, when he finally read it as a document rather than wrote it, was a schedule of payments to a man in exchange for continuing to accept an arrangement he had never agreed to.

He burnt that one at four in the morning in the yard and stood there until it had gone, and then went and sat on the apron until it got light, and he has never told anybody that it existed and is telling it now because the alternative is an account with a hole in it.

The third and fourth were merely wrong.

The fifth is on a sheet of paper which Pascual folded along its own crease in the July of 1981 and which is in a box in Ocean Park, and it has four columns, and the fourth column is the one that matters and is the only honest thing he has ever produced about himself.

---

He did not apologise on the apron and he would like it understood that this was the last decision he made about the whole business and the one he is least sure of.

An apology is a transfer. It is a thing one person hands to another, and the other has to take it, and having taken it has then to do something with it — dispose of it, absolve, say it was nothing. Reggie had spent thirteen months establishing that he had been handing that man things to receive since 1954 and calling it restraint. He was not going to close the account with one more.

He gave him the arithmetic instead, because arithmetic can be put on a table and left there, and does not require anybody to say it was nothing.

He is aware that this may simply be a man who cannot apologise, constructing a reason. He ran that too. It came out at about even and he went ahead anyway, which is not a thing he does, and he has noticed that he does not regret it.

---

## September 1981 – November 1982

He began going down on Wednesdays because of a light.

He had gone to the dispensary at half past eight on some errand he no longer remembers and had found a man of sixty-nine bent over a table with a dog on it and a wire in its foot, holding a lamp in his teeth.

There is no version of Reggie Tar that walks out of a room in which somebody is holding a lamp in his teeth.

He took the lamp and held it. He held it badly, which surprised him, and went on holding it badly for fourteen months, which surprised him more; he has an excellent hand for dissection and could not for the life of him work out where a light was wanted until he was told, and had to be told twice on the first evening, and found that he did not mind being told, and has thought about that since.

He never said anything about it in fourteen months. There was nothing available to say that was not either an offer of sympathy or an inspection, and the man on the other side of the table had had four months of a station being sorry for him and had gone half mad on it.

What he had instead was one evening a week in which the arrangement was: Pobil worked and Tar assisted, badly, and was corrected, and did as he was told. He would like it recorded that this had not happened in twenty-seven years, in either direction, and that it was the best hour of his week from the September of 1981 until they left the island, and that he has never been able to give an account of why that survived being said out loud, and has stopped attempting to, and simply went every Wednesday.

---

## 1983

He was, he thought at the time, doing rather well.

He had a schedule. Two hours of the language every morning out of a schoolbook and a list, with a test on Fridays which he set and marked and entered; the names of eleven members of the Pobil family with their relations, occupations and children's ages, in a notebook, revised weekly; and a standing determination to be, in a house where he was a foreigner and a novelty and had no function whatever, the least trouble it was possible for a man to be.

He was six months into it before he registered that the noise in a room dropped when he came into it.

He entered it as data and drew the wrong inference. The inference he drew was that he was not yet trying hard enough, and the correction he applied was more of the same, and it took him another five months and one very bad afternoon to establish that he had been running an experiment in which the treatment and the disease were the same substance.

He is aware — he was aware at the time, at about three in the morning, which was when he was awake — that a man who has spent his working life diagnosing that exact error in other people ought to have got it faster. He would point out in his own defence that he had never before been the material.

---

He said the thing in the sister's kitchen on a Sunday in the July and knew what it was before the end of the sentence and could not stop it arriving.

Then Pascual put a dish down and took his face in both hands with the door open and eleven people through it, and forgave him, entirely, inside about ten seconds, and told him he had not lost a year but spent one, and then told him to come and eat because the brother-in-law was going to start on the status question.

Reggie has been over the following four months a number of times.

The difficulty was not guilt, which he would recognise, having read about it. The difficulty was procedural. He had been handed an absolution he had not asked for, by somebody who plainly meant it, at a speed which made it impossible to examine, for an offence which he had committed deliberately and which remained true. There was no step available to him. He could not accept it, because accepting a thing is an act and he did not know what the act consisted of. He could not decline it, because declining it would have been an insult to the only person he had. He could not amend the statement, because it was accurate. He could not leave it, because it was in the room.

So he went round it from July to November, for some hours most nights, at the rate of a man checking a sum that keeps coming out right, and he lost about nine pounds, and Pascual — who had closed the matter with a dish in his hands and thought no more about it — watched him do it and said nothing, and Reggie is now reasonably certain that not saying anything was the second hardest thing that man did that year.

The hardest was that at no point between the July and the November did he offer to take it back.

It was available to him every hour of every day and it would have wanted one sentence. That he had changed his mind. That Antigua was still there and Jean would have them back inside a fortnight. That the whole thing had been an error and they would go home in the spring.

Reggie would have taken it. He would have taken it inside a second, and been grateful, and gone; and it would have been a lie; and the arrangement it restored would have been the one in which one man had the whole of what he wanted and the other had a monograph and a donkey and no post at all.

So a man of seventy-two sat in his own country, in his own house, and watched somebody he had waited twenty-nine years to bring home lose nine pounds and go round and round a sum at three in the morning, and did not offer him the one thing that would have stopped it, for four months, on the grounds that the arithmetic had not altered.

Reggie did not work that out until about 1986, and has not raised it, and does not intend to. There is nothing available to say about it which is not either an inspection or a condolence, and he has had both done to him and neither is any use.

In the end the procedure he arrived at was to withdraw a true statement by saying that he withdrew it, which is not a procedure, and to say it standing on a roof in the dark where he could not see the other man's face.

It worked. He does not know why it worked. He has entered that as an open item and it has been open for five years.

---

## 1984 – 1985

There was an argument about a fig tree in the April and everybody shouted for forty minutes and he lost, and afterwards the family were entirely different with him and he has never been able to establish what the mechanism was.

Pascual can. Pascual gave him a full account of it that evening on the roof — that a man with nothing he wants cannot be got at, and that the first time he wanted something out loud they had something to take hold of — and Reggie accepted it as a hypothesis, and it fits the observations, and he has no better one.

He would say only that it made him bearable, and that being bearable is not the same as being well, and that the second thing did not arrive until the following March.

---

What saved him was Jean, and he knows exactly how and he has no uncertainty about it whatever.

She wrote in the March of 1985 and asked whether Dr Tar would take a research student for a fortnight, at the student's own expense, to have her second chapter taken apart. It was a perfectly ordinary letter and it was not an ordinary letter, and he read it four times, and Jean Leslie has never once in thirty-one years done anything by accident.

The student came on the eleventh. Her name is in the acknowledgements of four papers and she runs a group in Bergen now. She arrived with ninety pages, of which about thirty were any good, and a design fault in the sampling which she had been carrying since her first season and which nobody at her institution had had the courage or the interest to tell her about.

He told her about it on the second morning.

He gave her eleven days. Forty pages of notes. He made her cry on the fourth day and on the ninth, and on the ninth she cried and then argued with him for two hours and was right about one thing in four, which is an extremely good ratio, and he told her so, and she has repeated that sentence in print twice since.

Here is what he would want set down about it, and it is not about her.

For fourteen months in that country he had been a man with a schedule and no function. He had been a guest, and a study of the Spanish language, and a person who was politely handled. And on the fourth morning of the March of 1985 a young woman sat down at a table on a roof with a chapter that was wrong, and for six hours he was not a guest and not a foreigner and not sixty-eight; he was the only person within two thousand miles who could see what was the matter with page thirty-one, and she needed him to say it, and he said it, and it was the same room he had been in since 1948.

He went to bed that night and slept seven hours.

He has looked at that and there is no other reading available. It was not the tree and it was not the language and it was not time. It was work, which is the thing he has always said it was about everybody else and has never once been believed about.

By 1986 there were four a year, and two men from the marine station at Mayagüez on Thursdays, one of whom is thirty-one and unwise about salinity and has been coming for two years and will be very good in about ten.

---

## November 1986 – May 1987

He took the second half of every night from the middle of the November until the ninth of May.

The arrangement was never discussed. Pascual took until about two and then Reggie took until it got light, and the reason it was never discussed is that Pascual was seventy-five and would have argued, and Reggie had established by the fourth night that he could do without the sleep and that the other man could not, and there was therefore nothing to discuss.

He would like to say what those nights actually consisted of, because he has heard people talk about such things afterwards and the accounts do not resemble it.

They were not solemn. Charles could not lie flat by the January and so he was propped up, and being propped up meant he was awake, and being awake at three in the morning with a man who does not sleep meant that they talked for perhaps a hundred and sixty nights about the following: the delta counts; a dispute with a man in Leiden about dentition which Charles was losing and enjoying; whether the Guayo field post should be moved half a mile; the correct ordering of thirty-one years of jars; what Monty had done that day; Hester; the students; the third section of the 1970 paper, which Charles still maintained was too long and which Reggie told him, in the March, had been protected in the referee's report by a man neither of them would have expected, and Charles said "Oh, that's marvellous," and laughed, and had to stop laughing, which by then took a while.

He was not brave about it and Reggie would object to the word. He was frightened on some nights and said so plainly and then talked about something else, which is a technique, and Reggie recognised it because it is his.

Twice he asked Reggie to check whether Monty was still on the steps, and Monty was.

---

On the nineteenth of March, at about four in the morning, Charles said that there was a thing he wanted to put on the record while he could still do it in one go.

Reggie said that he would rather he did not.

"I know you would," said Charles. "Get the lamp off my face, I can't see you.

"On the twenty-eighth of August 1954 I asked you to be out of that room. It was mine. I worked out what I was going to do and I worked out that you would stop me if you knew, and I arranged for you not to know, and I have thought about it every year since and I did it well and I would do it again. You have been carrying it as something you allowed. It was something I organised, and you were the instrument, and I am not going to lie here and let the only person who could have stopped me go on thinking he was the author of it.

"And I have had thirty-three years. I have had a river and an animal and a species and Hester and about four hundred jars, and I would not swap one week of it for the whole of the life I would have had, and I have had a great deal of time up here to check that and it does not come out any other way. So if you are keeping a ledger on this — and you are, you have been keeping it since the September of 1954 and I have watched you do it for thirty-three years — then I am telling you that you have got it in the wrong column, and I am the only person on earth entitled to tell you that, and I am telling you now while I still can."

Reggie sat with the lamp in his hand.

"I am not able to enter it," he said.

"I know you're not. I'm not asking you to enter it. I'm asking you to have been told." Charles was quiet for a moment. "Right. Then I'll say it to Pascual in the morning, because he'll enter it, and he'll say it to you for the next twenty years."

"I would ask you not to."

"Why on earth not?"

"Because he would then know that it had been offered," Reggie said, "and that I had declined it. He is going to spend some years attempting to get me to put that ledger down. If he learns that you offered to close it and that I would not let you, he will understand that the thing cannot be closed from the outside by anybody, and he will stop. And I would rather he did not stop."

There was a long silence. Somewhere below them the water did what it does.

"That," said Charles, "is the single worst thing I have ever heard anybody say about himself, and it's about the kindest, and I don't think you can tell the difference, and I'm too tired to explain it to you."

"That is possible."

"All right." He put his hand out and got Reggie's wrist, which he had never done in thirty-three years and did not do again. "All right. I won't tell him. But I said it, and you were told, and you can't unhear it, and I'm going to be very annoying about that for as long as I've got."

He was.

---

## The ninth of May 1987

They carried him down at about four and put him in two feet of water at the bottom of the steps, which is what he had asked for in the February and had written down in case anybody argued.

Pascual was on the landward side with his hand under his back. The animal was on the river side and had been for two days and was holding him up.

Reggie sat on the third step with his watch in his hand and timed the respirations, because it was the only thing available to do with his hands and because somebody ought to have the figures.

He got them to nine minutes past five and then they stopped, and he went on for some while after that, which he was not aware of at the time and was told about afterwards, gently, by the person best qualified to tell him.

---

He is not going to attempt to describe the eight days that followed, on the grounds that he has no vocabulary that would not be a construction after the fact.

What he can put down is the arithmetic, which he ran eleven times between the tenth and the eighteenth and which comes out the same every time: that he arranged to be unable to know; that the man went the following night; that the ten years without what he needed and the crossing itself are what killed him at fifty-seven; and that every link in that chain was made deliberately and by him.

He put it to Pascual at two in the morning on the eighteenth, at the table under the shelter, and asked to have it checked, and it was checked, and the answer was a partition of the act into three choices of which he is entitled to one; and he did not accept it and does not accept it and said so.

He would like one thing recorded about that conversation which the other man could not have known.

He had already been given the answer. He had been given a better one, in the March, at four in the morning, by the only person in the world with standing to give it, and had declined it, and had asked that it not be passed on. So when Pascual sat across a table from him and constructed, out of thirty years and no help from anybody, an argument that was structurally the same argument and about two-thirds as strong, Reggie was sitting there having heard it before from the source, and could not say so.

That is what he was doing when he did not answer for so long. He was not weighing it. He was noticing that two people had now independently arrived at the conclusion that he was not the author of the twenty-ninth of August, and that he could not make it hold in his own hand for longer than about four seconds at a time, and that the failure was therefore in him and not in the argument, and that this was, in a way he was not going to be able to explain to anybody at that hour, an enormous relief.

Then Pascual took his wrist and told him there was nothing to solve and that he would say it again tomorrow and the day after, and gave him a real task for the morning, and did not let go.

Reggie did not take his arm back.

---

He did the papers.

Thirty-one years of them, in a house on posts, in the heat, at a rate of about a foot a day: field notebooks from 1956 forward, the jar register, the correspondence in four languages, the two hundred and eleven pages in the tin box which he did not open and put aside for Hester, and eleven cardboard boxes of the sort of thing a man accumulates who has never once thrown anything away because he might want to check it.

It took nineteen days and it is a good job of work. Everything in Aberdeen now is in an order that means something and there is a finding aid, and a woman of twenty-six in Bergen used it last year and wrote to say so.

On the fourteenth day he came to a notebook from 1962 with nine annotations in it in a hand he recognised, and had to go out and stand on the landing for a while, and Pascual came and stood next to him and did not say anything, and after some minutes said, "Right?" and Reggie said, "Yes," and they went back in and finished the box.

That is the whole of what he has to say about it and it is not adequate, and he is aware that it is not adequate, and it is what there is.
