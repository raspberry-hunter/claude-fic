# The Estimates

The estimates went up in October. They had gone up in October since before there was concrete in the pens, and they came back down in the new year approved, amended or struck, and by the end of his second year on the station Reggie had understood that they were the only instrument in the place that read in both directions. A man put a line in and learned, from what came back, what the people at the far end wanted; and the people at the far end read the lines and learned what the station was, whatever the covering letter said. He had never put a sentence of this to anybody. It was not the sort of thing one said about money.

The Colonel had understood it for forty years. That was the part Reggie was slowest to see, because the Colonel wrote nothing that was not a figure and asked his questions a year apart, and an instrument with a period that long does not look like an instrument. It looks like furniture.

---

In the April of 1957, the week after Jean Leslie's letter, the Colonel sent for him.

He had been building up to it since Christmas, and Reggie had watched him build: the 1956 pathology returns went up to the house in January and came back with the docket creased in the way that meant they had been read twice; in February he came down to the small laboratory and stood in the doorway for a while, watching the assistant do the staining, and asked nothing, and went away. In April, with the season's credit to the station still warm in his hands, he asked.

"The veterinary and pathological expansion," the Colonel said. "I can defend every line of it, and I have, twice, in writing. The assistant on the establishment. The second refrigerator. The glassware, which costs what a boat costs. What I cannot do is state its purpose in the long term, and I am too old to go on defending a thing I cannot state. So." He folded his hands on the desk. "What is it for?"

Reggie gave him the answer. It took eleven minutes and every sentence of it was true: that the method was in print now and out of the station's hands; that animals were being taken on the strength of it and would go on being taken, in numbers, by establishments with the money for pens and not for medicine; that they would sicken of things nobody at those establishments could name, because there was exactly one place in the hemisphere that had sampled the species alive, weekly, for three years running, and it was this one; and that if the Martin did not hold the pathological record for these animals then nobody would hold it, and every death anywhere would teach nothing, for ever.

The Colonel heard the whole of it with his hands folded. Then he said that it was well argued, and thanked him, and went on to the drainage, and wrote nothing down.

Reggie noted that. A man who has accepted an answer writes it down. Neither of them said anything further, and the matter stood open between them for three years the way a file stands open: in a drawer, quietly, known to both parties.

---

In the October of 1958 he put a new line into the estimates. It was small — it cost rather less than the year's ink — and it read: *Collection of sighting records; retainers to observers and boatmen, Leeward and Windward stations southward; non-specimen.* He costed it at what a research student had once spent on the same class of work out of her own salary, which he had to the page, because she had given him the figures in a boat shed and he had kept them.

It came back struck.

He put it in again in 1959, re-costed, with a paragraph of justification about distribution. Struck. In 1960 he moved it up the schedule and hung the whole southern question on it. Struck. In the third year he looked at the pattern of what was approved, and the pattern was that every approved line had an animal in a pen at the end of it, and the struck line was the only line he had ever submitted that did not; and he entered the regularity in a file he kept in no drawer, and went on submitting.

The rest of the station's year kept its fixed points. The careening in June. The estimates in October. In the spring, an offer, warmly made, and a declination, dully worded; he was aware that each declination went into a ledger he would never see, one entry a year, in another man's hand, and he had given up costing what the entries were worth to their keeper.

And on Thursdays, Mrs Bevan came down to the pens.

She had come down on Thursdays since the November of 1954. She stood at the rail of the first co-housing pen for sixteen minutes, and said nothing to anybody, and went up again. He had timed her through the first year before he noticed that he was doing it; the figure held to inside a minute, week upon week, which no natural behaviour does; and by 1957 he had a series more than a hundred long and had stopped attempting to construct what the sixteen minutes were. He noticed the stopping. It was not a thing he did with any other series on the station, and he never examined the exception.

He had one other fact about her, and it was old. In the September of 1954, when the launch had gone and the station had settled into the official account of itself, everybody had asked him something. The Colonel had asked about the log, once, formally, and closed the file. John had asked a great many things, none of them the right one. The boat crew had asked what was to be done with the cot and the razor. Mrs Bevan had asked him nothing — not that month, not that year, not in the years since — and a person who does not ask is not a person missing an answer. He had filed that in 1954 and never opened the heading, and could have produced it in his sleep.

---

The estimates of 1959 came back down carrying an item he had not put in.

The veterinary and pathological section was moved onto the permanent establishment. Not the money — the money had been annual since 1955 and had been got each year by argument — the posts. What it meant, in the language the document was written in, was that the section now existed the way the jetty existed: to abolish it would take London, deliberately, in the open, over two signatures, with reasons given. He had not requested it. He had not known it could be requested. He read the minute twice, established that it was not a clerical error, and entered it under administrative weather: a thing that happens above one's head and comes down as rain.

He carried it under that heading for a year.

Then, in the November of 1960, on a Tuesday afternoon, he was given cause to re-read a good many things he had filed, and spent the evening saying some of them aloud in a room with a failing refrigerator; and in the week after, he took the 1959 minute out and read it under the new heading, and had it whole in about a second and a half.

A grant lapses. A contract ends. A man, however good, can be replaced by a contract man in Miami, and establishments replace men the way tides turn — without malice, on schedule. A permanent establishment is none of these things. Somebody had sat down a year before Reggie had so much as framed the question and made Pascual Pobil the single most difficult object on the station to remove; and had done it in the one currency that leaves no sentence lying about for anybody to find; and had never mentioned it; and had let him price it as weather.

He was never able to establish which of them it had been. The Colonel's hand was on the covering minute; the drafting had the administration's cadence, which was Hester's; the idea could have originated with either, or with both, or between them, in that house, in the evening, in the place where the two of them evidently kept the things they said to nobody. He went at the question on and off for a month, and then entered the finding as a fact not about the Colonel and not about Mrs Bevan but about the marriage, and closed it.

And he took out his own eleven minutes of April 1957 — true in every sentence — and found the middle they had been built round, and it was a man with his head in a refrigerator; and he noted, in the manner of a man annotating a superseded method, that he had routed round that middle for years without once being able to say that this was what he was doing, and that other people had evidently been able to say it, silently, in budget lines, since 1959.

---

Jean Leslie's letter came in the May of 1962, posted from Margarita, addressed to the station and marked personal, and was almost entirely about the work.

He read it four times in the office with the door shut. Then he went and found Pascual, who read it once, standing at the bench, and put it down, and stood with a hand flat on either side of it for some time, and then said: "Thursday."

It was not a non sequitur. They had been keeping the same file.

On the Thursday he went down at her hour with the letter in his pocket. She was at the rail. He took the place at the next stanchion but one and waited, and at about the fourth minute she turned her head, without surprise, as though he were a scheduled thing that had at last arrived.

"Mrs Bevan. I have had a letter from Jean Leslie, from the Margarita season. The most of it is about fish. There is a paragraph at the end of it that I believe belongs to you, and I should like to read it to you, and you are not obliged to hear it."

She looked at him for a moment. Then she looked back at the water. "Read it."

He read it: the European resident of the lower caños, at the fish since October 1954; the fifteen notebooks written on both sides and into the margins; the daily record that put three seasons of professional work to shame. *He is well. He is thin and he has had malaria and he is not coming back and does not wish to, and I am satisfied that this is his own judgement and not anybody else's. He asked me to tell you two things and made me repeat them. The first is that the animal is well.*

He stopped there. The second thing was about a tin, and was his.

For the space of several breaths Mrs Bevan did not move at all. Out in the pen one of the animals surfaced, blew, and went under.

"Again," she said. "From *he is well*."

He read it again from *he is well*.

"Thank you, Dr Tar." Her voice was in good order. Her hands were not, and he saw them once and did not look at them again. Then, after a while, in another register entirely: "You had better call me Hester."

The sentence arrived complete, at speaking pace, in the cadence of a thing said before — in another room, he thought, to somebody else — and he could take the observation no further, and kept it anyway.

He offered her the letter. She declined it. "I have it," she said, and he understood this to be accurate.

She stood her sixteen minutes. Then she stood eleven more, and he stood them with her, and nothing further was said by either of them; and at the end she settled her hat and said, "When there is news of the work, I should like to have it," and went up the path.

He entered the eleven minutes in the series and did not construct them.

---

There was news of the work about every ten weeks, as the post and the water allowed, and he brought it to the rail on Thursdays: the salinity, the flood season, the catfish and the long argument about the catfish, the temperature series and what eight years of it would be worth once the hydrometer was corrected. She listened the way she watched the water — entirely, without notes. She asked questions about the fish, good ones; she had been reading. In two years she did not ask one question about anything else, and he had by then a certain respect for the cost of that, having spent eight years learning the price of it from the inside.

In the December of 1963 the Colonel's heart went. Quietly, at his desk, between one page of the year-end returns and the next. Pascual had him flat inside three minutes and did the correct things in the correct order, and the doctor came out from town and confirmed them and could add nothing to them; and the Colonel spent six weeks on his back and the better part of two months more in a chair by the window, with the returns brought up to him against everybody's advice, annotated in a hand that had got smaller and did not wander.

The station ran. The administration had been Hester's for years; what changed was that the papers now said so.

---

The leave application went up in the January: six weeks; collection, Orinoco delta, in cooperation with the La Salle people; the chief of station and the veterinary officer, concurrently. It came back down in three days, signed in a small firm hand from the chair by the window, without comment of any kind.

In the February, Hester sent for him.

He had been in the sitting room perhaps a dozen times in ten years, always on business. There was tea, which was not business. Overhead, at intervals, a stick crossed the floor, and each time it crossed she stopped — mid-sentence, where necessary — and listened to it until it reached the chair and the chair took the weight; and the attention she gave it was of the class she gave the water on Thursdays, and he entered that where he had been keeping such things since 1960.

"I had thought, at the beginning of the winter, that I might — " she said, and did not finish it, and poured instead. "I can't get away. You understand that I can't."

"Yes."

"I have two sentences for Charles. You will say them to him exactly as I give them to you. Not the sense of them. The words."

"Yes."

"You will want to know what they mean." She set the pot down. "I'm sorry. They aren't yours."

"I know the class of thing they are," Reggie said. "It is the only thing about them I need."

She looked at him then for rather a long time, with an expression he did not attempt. And then she gave him the two sentences.

*This is what I should have shared with you that day but did not. I was thirty-one, and it was four years, and nobody has ever asked me a single question about it in twenty-six years, and you are the only person alive I could have told.*

She made him say them back to her twice. He said them back twice, exactly, and watched what it did to her to hear her own sentences in another voice, and stood the watching, because it was the fee.

At the door she said, "Bring me his answer."

It was not a request, and he did not enter it as one.

---

What the caño was is set down elsewhere: in forty letters, a temperature series, and a catfish.

On the twelfth morning, at the bench, in the good light, Charles gave him two sentences to carry back, and made him say them back twice; and when Reggie had finished the second saying, Charles laughed — at the procedure, Reggie decided afterwards; at the two of them keeping, four hundred miles apart, a form that nobody had taught either of them — and went and did something unnecessary with the jars.

Reggie carried the sentences four hundred miles down a river and across a sea without writing anything down, which was not difficult. He had carried heavier freight with less handle on it.

---

He delivered them on the second evening after the launch came in, in the sitting room, with the stick going overhead at intervals that had shortened since February; he had the figures without having wanted them.

"He made me say them back twice," Reggie said, "which I mention because you will wish to know the words are exact. He says: *Nobody asked me a single question for seven years either, and then somebody came up this river and asked, and I have been easier ever since. I am asking now: send me the four years — the whole of them, at whatever length. The post is slow, and I keep everything I am given.*"

Hester put her cup down on its saucer very carefully, as though it were the cup that mattered.

"Again," she said.

He said it again.

She got up and went to the window and stood with her back to the room, and Reggie regarded the tea things and gave her the room, which was a service he had learned by having it done to him. When she turned round she was the administrator again.

"How does the post go?"

He told her: the box at Tucupita; the family who took the letters up when the water served; twice a year, oilcloth, and patience. She took it in the way she took in a procedure — once, completely.

"Thank you, Reggie."

He had been Dr Tar in that room for ten years. He noted the change and did not examine it, and rose; and at the door she said, "Tell Dr Pobil the Colonel wants his Tuesdays back. He says the new man listens to his heart as though it were hiding something, and that Pobil listens to it as though it were a heart."

Which was, he understood, the other message he was carrying that evening, and it was not sealed at all.

---

The estimates went up in October, and before they went, the Colonel sent for him.

He was at his desk. He was lighter by some pounds that had not come back, and his colour was serviceable rather than good, and the returns in front of him were annotated to the margins in the smaller hand.

"Sit down, Tar. This is not the estimates."

He took a paper from the drawer and laid it between them: the leave application, the small firm signature, the date in January.

"Six weeks. Collection. The chief of station and the veterinary officer, concurrently." He let it lie. "I signed that in January because I was in no state to weigh anything and knew it, and a man who knows he cannot weigh signs on policy or not at all. My policy where you are concerned has been settled for ten years. I am now in a state to ask questions."

"Yes."

"I am not going to ask them."

The clock on the shelf got on with it.

"I want it understood that this is not convalescence," the Colonel said. "It is the decision I took in the September of 1954, when the Navy's people asked me to state whether I was satisfied with the station's account of its losses, and I stated that I was satisfied. I have been making official statements for forty years, and that is the only one I have ever made without first establishing that it was true. I made it on one datum. I shall not be giving you the datum. It was not mine to collect, and it is not mine to hand about."

Reggie sat with his hands still on his knees.

"You asked me a question in the April of 1957," he said, "and I gave you an answer, and every sentence of it was true, and it was not the answer."

"No," the Colonel agreed. "I had the rest of it off the estimates by the following autumn. A man builds a section round another man for one reason." He squared the returns, which in him was punctuation. "I have signed this station's papers for the same reason since there has been a station to sign for. I take it we need not go on."

"No."

"Good. Then one question, and it is operational, and you may decline it." He took his glasses off. "Is anything coming?"

Reggie gave him the true answer, which was that he did not know. That there was a man who asked him for a thing every spring and had been declined every spring for eight years, and that eight declinations were eight entries in a ledger he had never seen and never would; that he had spent those years making himself expensive, on the only theory of safety he had ever watched hold; and that expensive is a price, and a price holds until the day somebody wants the goods more than the apparatus, and no man is told in advance which day that is.

The Colonel heard it through.

"That is the answer I should have given at your age," he said. "It is the right one, and it is no use whatever. Very well. We shall both go on filing." He drew the returns back towards him, which was the adjournment; and then, as Reggie reached the door: "Tar."

"Colonel."

"Hester writes letters now. Long ones. They go down with the specimen invoices, and she is better in the mornings than I have seen her in years, and I have not asked her one question about any of it." He put his glasses back on and took up his pen. "The not-asking is work. I have done it for twenty-four years, and it is the best work I have done, and I find I wanted that known by somebody living. You may go."

---

The estimates went up on the fourteenth.

There was a new line in them: *Live crustacean supplementation; feeding trial, co-housed animals* — costed, scheduled, and already three months ahead of its own funding, because Pascual had declined to wait for the money and had been buying shrimp through the mess account since June, and the intake curve was doing what the field said it would do. Under *authority* Reggie had written: *field observation, 1964*, and had declined, in the schedule, to source it more exactly than that.

And there was the other line, the seventh year of it, re-costed, re-argued, the southern question hung on it again: sighting records, retainers to observers and boatmen, non-specimen.

The answers came back in the January. The shrimp were approved in full. The other line was struck.

He read the two answers side by side for a while, at the desk, in the evening, with the pens running quiet below the window and the kettle going in the other room. Then he entered the pattern where he had been entering it since 1958, and took a clean sheet, and began the eighth submission.
