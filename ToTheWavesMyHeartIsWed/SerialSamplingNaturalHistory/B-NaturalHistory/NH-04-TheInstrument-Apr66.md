# The Instrument

The proposal came down in the January, in a buff envelope, with a covering letter of two paragraphs that was among the most courteous documents she had ever been sent.

It offered to bring the William Martin Institute for Marine Studies under the establishment of a survey element operating in southern waters, as its shore station: the pens maintained, the pathology section maintained and enlarged, every post on the current establishment guaranteed for five years at not less than existing salary, a new intake building, a boat. The Martin had run for eighteen years on a trust income and an annual grant fought for line by line. The sum in the third paragraph was rather more than the Martin had spent in a decade.

Hester read it twice through and then went and stood at the window of the office that had been her husband's until the thirtieth of November.

She had founded this place. Not in the way the plaque said, which was money; in the way that actually founds a thing, which is that in 1946 she had sat in three rooms in London with a folder and had not gone away. She had chosen the site and she had written the trust deed with a solicitor in Antigua and had put a clause into it that she had never once had occasion to use.

She was fifty-nine years old, and she had been acting administrator for six weeks, and she understood exactly what she was holding. It was not a threat. Nobody who wanted the Martin would ever need to threaten it. It was an offer to make everybody on this station safe and well paid and equipped, for ever, on one condition that was nowhere in the document, which was that the animals in the pens and the record in the cabinets and the man who had made both would belong to somebody else.

The reply was due by the end of September.

---

Reggie came to her on the fourteenth with a sheet of paper and a face she had seen on him twice before, and both of those had been in 1954.

"I should like you to look at something," he said, "and I should like to say first that I have had it for three days and have not been able to make it come out any other way, and that I am hoping very much that you can."

He put the establishment list on the desk. She knew it; she had seen it in August, briefly, and had asked her husband nothing about it, and had watched him carry it down to the laboratory himself rather than send it.

"This is the covering slip," Reggie said. "It went with the list. I have had both since the eleventh of August and I have read the list perhaps two hundred times and I had never once read the slip, because the slip is not information."

It was a requisition, in her husband's hand. Sponsors' establishment schedule, element (survey), southern waters. Requested by the administrator of the Martin. And in the box for the ground on which it was sought: *operational; station lies within the element's declared area.*

"That is not true," Reggie said. "It was not true when he wrote it and he knew it was not true, and there is no version in which it becomes true. It is a false statement made on an official form."

"Yes."

"In October he told me a thing about himself which I have thought about a great deal since, which is that he had made exactly one official statement in forty years without first establishing that it was true, and that it was made in the September of 1954, and that he would not tell me what it rested on." Reggie's hands were flat on the edge of her desk. "This is the second. It is dated the eleventh of August 1965. He relinquished the administration on the thirtieth of November."

The clock did what clocks do.

"I am aware that I cannot prove it," he said. "There is no minute. There will never be a minute; that is not how such things are done, and a man who has had a coronary at his desk supplies the whole of the explanation by himself, and everybody who reads the file will read it correctly and nobody will ever write it down. I have three facts and a date and I have no document. I would be very glad to be told that I am constructing."

Hester looked at the requisition for a while.

She had a choice in front of her, and she had perhaps ten seconds to make it, and she made it in rather less than that, because she had spent twenty-eight years on the other side of this exact transaction and knew to the ounce what it weighed.

"You are not constructing," she said. "He knew what it would cost when he signed it. He told me so, in that chair, on the tenth of August, the evening before."

Reggie did not move.

"He said: *There is a list, and the list will have something on it, and Tar will not be able to get it and I can, once.*" She kept her voice in order; it was not particularly difficult, she had been doing it since 1938. "And I asked him whether it would be noticed. And he said that it would certainly be noticed, and that a man who has been signing papers for forty years knows precisely which of them are noticed, and that this was the only occasion in his life on which he had ever wanted a paper to be irregular enough to work."

"Mrs Bevan — "

"Hester."

"Hester." He stopped, and started again, and she watched a man of forty-nine who had spent his entire life at a distance from everybody attempt to construct a sentence out of materials he did not have. "He never said one word about it to me. He gave me the list on the eleventh and he asked me two questions about the shrimp trial and he sent me away."

"Yes. That is what he is." She put the slip back in his hand. "And I am going to say the rest of it, because you have come to me instead of going round to the house with it, which I have noticed, and which was correct. He is not going to discuss it with you. If you go to him he will tell you that his health has been indifferent since 1963, which is true, and that he was of an age to go, which is true, and he will ask you about the estimates. You will not get past it. I have been married to him for twenty-six years and I have never once got past it, and the one time he came at all near it was in the October, and I understand he came near it with you."

"He said the not-asking was the best work he had done."

"Yes," said Hester. "He would."

---

She went to the house at six and did not raise it, and he asked her about the proposal, and she gave him the whole of it, and he listened with his eyes shut and the stick against the arm of the chair.

"Generous terms," he said.

"Extremely."

"They always are. That is what generous terms are for." He opened his eyes. "What have you got?"

"A trust deed, and a clause in it, and eighteen years of running this place on nothing, which they cannot see from where they are sitting because it does not appear in any figure."

"Then use the clause and make them do it in the open. They will very probably win. Make them win in daylight." He shifted, which took him longer than it used to. "How long have you?"

"September."

"And Leslie's season?"

"February. She has the launch from the twelfth."

He was quiet for a moment. Outside, the light was going off the water and the generator started, the way it had started every evening for eighteen years.

"You will go," he said.

"I shall not. There is a document on that desk that decides whether this station exists in five years, and I have six weeks in the post, and I am not going to be four hundred miles up a river in Venezuela while — "

"Hester," said her husband. "You will go."

She stopped.

"I have had a letter go out of this house with the specimen invoices every three months since the May of 1964," he said, "and I have not read one word of one of them, and I have not asked you a single question about any of it, and I have arranged my mind for twenty-six years so as not to be in a position where a question could occur to me. I should like it understood that this was not stupidity and it was not indifference. It was the only thing I had that was any use to you."

"I know that."

"You do not, quite, or you would not be standing there." He put his hand on the stick. "I am seventy-one and I have relinquished the administration and there is nothing further that anybody can take off me. And there is a man on that river who has the other half of whatever it is, and he is thirty-six years old, and Leslie has a launch on the twelfth of February. And you are going to sit in an office and answer a letter that is not due until September."

She did not say anything at all.

"I have never asked," he said. "I am not asking now. I am telling you to go and see him, and I should be obliged if you would not make me say any more of it than that, because I have had it ready since the November and I have not been able to get it out until this evening."

She went and sat on the arm of his chair, which she had not done in about a year, and after a while he put his hand over hers and left it there.

"You may take the estimates with you," he said. "Tar cannot be trusted with them alone. He gets them right and then he puts a line in that nobody will pass."

---

The launch took eleven days from Antigua and she was ill for two of them and did not mind.

In the Gulf, off the Boca de Navios, they passed a white vessel of about ninety feet standing north with her booms rigged, working slowly, and Jean Leslie put the glasses on her for rather a long time and then put them down and said nothing, and Reggie took the glasses and put them down and said nothing, and Pascual said something short in Spanish that Hester did not catch and was glad not to.

That was the second of March. The element had been on the coast since the middle of February. It had sailed a fortnight early.

---

The caño was brown, and the green went up on both sides for hours and did not vary, and the smell was mud and flowers, and she had a headache from the middle of the second afternoon that was not a headache.

She had been in salt water perhaps six times since 1938 and never for longer than she could help. She had lived, deliberately, on a dry hill above a set of grounded concrete pens where the field died at the wall, and she had done it because eleven years of being able to feel exactly nothing was preferable to the alternative arrangement, which was standing at a rail for sixteen minutes a week feeling something at the extreme limit of nothing and going up the path afterwards.

The caño was not nothing.

She sat in the bow with her hands in her lap and the water going past a foot below them, and the whole of it came up through the boards: the traffic of a rich brackish system, ten thousand small transactions a second, the ordinary noise a living body of water makes and has always made and will make when everybody now alive is dead. Twenty-eight years. She had told herself it was silence she had been living in. It had not been silence. It had been deafness, and there is a difference, and the difference arrives all at once.

She got through it without producing anything for anybody to look at. Jean was forward with the sounding; Reggie was writing; Pascual, who missed nothing, came and sat on the thwart opposite and offered her a cup of tea and talked to her for twenty minutes about the engine, and never once looked at her face, and she understood that she had been seen and managed and was not going to be discussed.

There was a landing, and a path, and a bench under a shelter with the jars on it in rows.

Charles came round the corner of the house and stopped, and put down what he was holding, and said, "Oh," and then nothing else at all; and Hester came up the path and got hold of him with both arms, which she had not done to a living soul since her husband, and stood there in the sun holding a very thin man of thirty-six who had been the only person alive she could have told and had been four hundred miles away for twelve years.

Nobody remarked on it. Jean was extremely busy with the jars.

---

They gave her the fourth day, all of them, without discussing it, and she and Charles went up to the shelter and did not come down until it was dark.

She had brought the four years with her in a tin box: two hundred and eleven pages, in her own hand, written over twenty-two months, at the desk, after the house was quiet. She had sent none of them. She had written every one of them to be sent and had put each one in the box instead, and had told herself the post was slow.

She gave him the box.

What she told him that afternoon is not going to be set down here. She had had it for twenty-eight years and had never said one word of it aloud to anybody living, including to the man who had spent twenty-six of those years arranging not to be in a position to hear it, and she said all of it, in order, out loud, to a barefoot man on a bench in Venezuela; and he listened the way he listens, which she has since learnt he was taught by an animal, and did not once say that he was sorry, and asked her four questions, and every one of the four was the right one.

At about six he said: "Would you come in the water?"

"I have not been in water since 1938."

"I know," said Charles. "That's why I asked."

---

The rule she had lived under all her life was that two of them in a dry room are nothing to each other. She had had it demonstrated in the sitting room at the Martin in the August of 1954, sitting three feet from a young man with the same defect as herself and getting from him exactly as much as she got from the furniture.

In the water off the landing, at half past six, in four feet of brackish water at a conductivity she could not have put a figure to and Reggie certainly could, it was not like that at all.

She is not going to set that down either.

Monty came at about seven, out of the dark water, and stopped some way off and stayed there.

He looked at her for a long time. She stood in the water and let herself be looked at, with her hair down and her skirt ruined and her hands open at her sides, and what came off him was not language and not a proposition and never would be; it was weather, and it was the weather of an animal in condition in his own river who has been told about somebody for two years by the only person he talks to.

Then he came the rest of the way, and put the side of his face against her shoulder, briefly, the way one does, and went under, and the water closed.

Hester stood in the caño in the dark with her hand where his face had been.

"He knew you," Charles said, from the bank, in a voice that was not steady. "I don't know how much of it. I've never known how much of it. But he knew you."

"I know," said Hester. "Charles, I am going to sit down on that step now and I should be glad if you would let me do it by myself for about ten minutes."

"Yes," he said. "I'll be up at the house."

---

Anselmo brought it on the sixth day, and he brought it to Jean, because he had been working for Jean since 1962 and knew what she would want.

Men had come up the Guayo channel in the January in a launch with an outboard and a Trinidadian pilot. They were polite, and they paid well, and they were buying names of fish, and the names of the men who knew the names of fish, at a rate that Anselmo repeated and Jean wrote down and then sat looking at.

Retainers to observers and boatmen. Non-specimen. It had been struck by London eight times and funded by Washington once.

"And they asked one more thing," Anselmo said, "at the end, when the money was already given. They asked whether there was anybody on this water who knows about the *other* animal. And I said there is not."

There was a silence under the shelter.

"Anselmo," said Jean. "Did anybody else answer that question?"

"I do not know," Anselmo said. "There were four boats. I am one boat." And then, because he was a courteous man and this was a hard thing to say to people he liked: "It is a great deal of money, and there is a word for him on this river, and everybody has had the word since 1955, and nobody has ever been paid for it before."

---

They had it out that night on the bench with the lamp going, all six of them, which had never happened before and did not happen well.

Reggie set out the arithmetic. It took him twenty minutes and it did not come apart anywhere: the element's method was his own method and it worked; four boats had been paid in January and there would be forty by 1967; the delta stations were sequenced to the third season, which was a gift from somebody and would not be repeated; and there was a post on the establishment list, vacant, described in a form of words he could recognise across nineteen years, which could be filled the moment there was anything for it to work on.

Jean set out the answer, which was hers, and which she had been running on herself since 1957 and on Charles since 1962: leave the lever standing in the open and make pulling it cost more than pulling it could return. Regularise him. Get him papers. Get him a residence, an affiliation with the Fundación, a permit in his own name, a nationality that is not merely a passport nobody has seen in twelve years. Make him a foreign scientist working on Venezuelan soil under a Venezuelan institution, publishing under his own name, with correspondence in four countries and a species named by him — and then let anybody from a Washington-funded vessel try to take him off a caño in a launch and see what it costs their sponsors.

"It would work," Reggie said. "I have run it and I cannot find where it fails, and I want that stated before I say the rest."

"Say the rest," said Charles.

"To be that man you must exist. On paper, at a stated address, with a date of arrival. And the moment there is a date of arrival, there is a British subject who did not die in the sea in the August of 1954, and everything written down about the August of 1954 becomes a false document. Jean lied for eighty minutes to a government man in a dining room. I signed a log. The Colonel stated to the Navy that he was satisfied with the station's account of its losses, and that statement is the reason nobody ever came, and it is one of two false official statements in his life and I have recently been shown the other one."

"No," Charles said.

"You have not heard the whole — "

"I have heard enough of it to know the shape and the answer is no." He had his elbows on his knees and he was looking at the lamp. "I have been alive for twelve years on the strength of four people telling one lie, and you are asking me to be safe by making all four of them liars in writing. Jean would lose her position. You would lose yours. He has already lost his — Hester wrote it to me in the December and did not say why and I have not asked and I am not a fool. So, no. I am extremely comfortable up this river and I have a house and a garden that isn't very good and I have got through eleven years by being nobody, and I shall go on being nobody, and if a launch comes I shall go a hundred miles up into the swamp where no launch has ever been and where I have friends, and they will not find me, and that is my answer and I should like it minuted."

"It is a bad answer," said Reggie.

"It is my answer."

"It is a bad answer and it is bad in a way I am uniquely placed to describe, because it is mine. I ran it for eight years. Own nothing, want nothing, be attached to nobody, and there is then nothing on you that can be pulled. It held perfectly and it cost me everything I did not have the sense to know I was spending, and the only reason I am sitting on this bench is that in 1954 it failed."

"And it kept you alive until it failed."

"Yes," said Reggie. "That is precisely what I am objecting to."

It went on for some while after that and got worse before it got better, and Pascual — who had not said one word in an hour, and who had spent the afternoon making Charles sit still while he went over him and had come away saying only that the man would do, if he ate — got up at some point and put the kettle on the fire and made everybody stop for ten minutes, which is a technique, and which worked.

And Hester, who had not spoken either, and who was sitting at the end of the bench with the tin box on her knees where she had put it four days ago and where it had stayed, said:

"May I say one thing, and then I shall go to bed, because I am fifty-nine and this has been a long week."

Nobody stopped her.

"I did what you are proposing to do," she said. "In 1938. Not up a river; in Hampshire, which is worse. I made myself into a person that nothing had ever happened to. It is not difficult and I was very good at it, and I have done it for twenty-eight years, and I am the only person here who can tell you what the price actually is, because everyone else in this conversation is estimating it and I have the invoice.

"It is not that they find you. It is that nobody ever asks you anything. And you tell yourself that is the price of being safe, and it is, and you pay it every day for twenty-eight years, and you are safe, and you are still paying it. And in 1964 I gave a man two sentences to carry four hundred miles because it was the only way I had of saying one true thing to one person, and I made him repeat them back to me twice, in my own sitting room, because I could not trust the words to leave the room in the right order. That is what it comes to. That is the whole of what being nobody buys you at the end: two sentences and a courier.

"So do not do it because I did it. Do it if you have another reason. But do not do it because it worked for me, because it did not work for me, and I have four days of proof of that in a box on my knees, and I would have given anything I possess to have been asked one question in 1939."

Charles sat with his hands over his face for a while.

"That is not fair," he said at last, thickly.

"No," said Hester. "It is accurate. I have found the two are very rarely the same thing and I have stopped apologising for it."

---

He came down to the landing in the morning and said he had it, and that it was his, and that nobody was to improve it.

He would go to Tucupita in the May with Anselmo and he would go to the Fundación people at Margarita in the June, and he would regularise, and take the affiliation, and take the permit in his own name, and be visible; and the account he would give of the twelve years was that he had gone overboard from a small boat off the north coast in the August of 1954 and had been picked up by fishermen and had been ill for a long time and had not communicated with anybody afterwards, which was his own doing and nobody else's, and that he was not prepared to discuss the reasons and never would be.

Reggie said that it was a false statement.

"Yes," said Charles. "It's mine. I'm the only person in it. Nobody signed anything, nobody swore anything, nobody's position is touched, and when they come at it sideways — and they will, and I've thought about that — the whole of it is one man being an ass in 1954, and one man is what they get." He was standing in the mud with his hands in his pockets, and he was perfectly cheerful about it, which Hester found she could hardly bear to look at. "In 1954 you made yourself unable to know a thing so that you could tell the truth about it. I've had twelve years to think about that manoeuvre and I've never got over how good it was, and this is the same one the other way round. I'm making myself the only person who has to lie."

"Charles — "

"Reggie. It is not on your list. It isn't going on your list. I'd be obliged if you'd write down that I said that, since you write down everything else."

---

They went down on the eleventh of April.

There is a photograph, which Jean took and had printed in three copies, of five people on a landing on a caño below Guayo: a woman of fifty-nine in a ruined hat, a very thin man in a mended shirt with a species named after him, a veterinary surgeon of fifty-five with his arm through the woman's, a research director who is looking slightly to the left of the camera because he has never in his life managed to look at one, and a marine biologist who set the shutter and ran and is therefore blurred. It is the only photograph of the four of them together that exists.

In Port of Spain, on the way up, the chandler's boy told Jean that the white survey vessel had been in for water on the twenty-ninth and had gone south again, and that they had a young Englishman aboard doing beautiful drawings of fish, and that he was very good company.

---

She came back to the Martin on the twenty-second and was in the office by nine the following morning.

The proposal was where she had left it, under the glass paperweight, with the reply due in five months. She read it again with the sun coming up the wall and found that it had not altered and that she had.

Then she took the trust deed out of the safe, where it had been since 1948, and turned to the fourth clause, which was hers, and which she had put in at the age of forty-one against the advice of a solicitor in Antigua who had told her that it was unnecessary, that nobody would ever try such a thing, and that it would make the instrument look eccentric.

She sat for a while with her hand flat on it.

Then she rang for the Colonel's — for her — clerk, and asked for a copy of the deed to be made for London and for the whole of the correspondence since November to be brought up; and she wrote at the head of a clean sheet of paper, in a hand that had not altered since 1938, *The Institute is not in a position to accept*, and stopped there, because she had five months and one clause and no illusions whatever about how it would very probably go, and because a thing done in daylight is not the same thing as a thing won.

Downstairs, somebody put a bucket down on the concrete, and the pens went on doing what they do.
