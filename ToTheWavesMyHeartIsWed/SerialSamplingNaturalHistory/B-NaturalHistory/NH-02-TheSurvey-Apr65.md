# The Survey

The reprint came up with the February mail in a stiff envelope from Aberdeen with nothing written in it, because nothing needed to be.

Twelve pages, two plates, a new siluroid out of the lower caños described properly, with the counts and the drawings and eight years of habitat data behind it that no expedition on earth could have got. The junior author's name was printed under the senior author's name in the place where names go, and under the junior author's name there was an address, and the address was a caño.

Pascual read it at the bench with his tea going cold, and then took it along to the office, where Reggie had his own copy flat on the blotter and squared to it.

"It's a good description."

"It is the best thing in the number," Reggie said, which in him was not warmth but measurement, and was also warmth.

They had decided this, all of them, with their eyes open, in a house on posts two years ago: that the name would go into print under first-class work, in the open, with an Aberdeen author in front of it who had undertaken to fight anybody who made an issue of it and would win. It was Jean Leslie's method applied to somebody who had not asked for it. Pascual had agreed with the design and thought it right and had noticed, in the fortnight since the envelope came, how often he picked the reprint up.

---

Spilsbury came in on the sixth of April, off the same launch, in a hat Pascual had not seen before.

Eleven years since the first visit and eight since the second. In between there had been the letters, and the offer every spring, and the reference for Jean Leslie in 1960 that was the reference of a man who had been comprehensively got the better of and was too good a scientist to lie about the work.

Pascual had one finding on Alan Spilsbury and had entered it in 1957 and never revised it: *the man is never surprised.*

The interviews ran again. In 1954 it had been forty minutes with the food logs, and fifty for Pascual, and an hour and twenty for Jean. In 1957, twenty-two and nineteen and two hours and forty. Reggie still took the figures off his watch, standing at the water tanks with his thumb on the crown like a man of thirty-nine instead of forty-eight; and Pascual, who had caught the habit off him somewhere around 1958 and had never mentioned it, took this one himself, because Reggie was inside.

Fifty-one minutes.

That was the first departure and it was a large one, and Pascual was still standing at the tanks with the figure in his head when the dining-room door opened.

Reggie came out into the sun. He looked at Pascual for a moment across forty feet of concrete, and did not come over, and said, "This evening. Not now," and went into the office and shut the door.

In eight years he had never once done that. He had come out of every room in this station and given Pascual the whole of it inside ten minutes, verbatim, not summarised, sitting on the bench with his arms folded, and it was the single most useful arrangement either of them had.

Pascual went and did the afternoon's pens. He did them thoroughly. He was aware, taking a perfectly ordinary set of weights off a perfectly ordinary animal, that his hands were doing the work and the rest of him was standing at the tanks with a number in it.

---

At the rail that night it took two hours and forty minutes, and it took that long because Reggie did what he does, which is to start at the beginning and go in order.

The beginning was the ninth offer, warmly made, which he declined at the fourth minute in the dull words he had been using since 1957 and which cost him nothing, because it was the same offer.

Then Spilsbury had taken a folder out of his case and put a schedule on the table, and it was Reggie's.

"He had my line," Reggie said. "The 1958 line. *Collection of sighting records; retainers to observers and boatmen, Leeward and Windward stations southward; non-specimen.* He had it in the wording of the fourth submission, which is the wording I settled on and have not altered since 1961."

"How?"

"Because a station's schedules go to London and London circulates what it circulates, and he has sat on the advisory side of something since 1956." Reggie's hands were on the rail. "He has had my line in front of him, in one form or another, every year since 1958. He told me that he has voted for it four times."

Pascual said nothing at all, which was correct, and which was also all he had.

"He said: *You cannot get it out of London. You have proved that seven times, which is four times longer than most men would go on. You can get it out of me.*"

Then Reggie gave him the constitution of the thing, and gave it in the order Spilsbury had given it, and there was not one item in it that had been put there carelessly.

A survey element — Spilsbury's word, and not an English word for it, which told a man where the money came from. Three years, renewable. A vessel of ninety feet with a proper laboratory. Southern waters: the Grenadines down, the Paria peninsula, the Gulf, the delta coast. Sighting records bought from the people who had them, at retainer, which was the whole of Reggie's struck line, funded at nine times what he had ever dared cost it at. Condition assessment of wild animals in the water. Scientific direction: named.

And two constitutional facts, which Spilsbury had put last, in the manner of a man laying down a hand he has been holding since the first minute.

*Nothing in the element is taken.* No animal collected, no animal transported, no animal held. The vessel had no tanks and no licence to fit them, and he had written that into the establishing minute himself, in those words, and had had it minuted twice.

And then, pleasantly, without any change of manner at all: *There is nothing in it that resembles the work outside Salisbury. I know precisely what that work was, because I was in the building for four months of it, and I know what it cost you, because I have watched what you have done with yourself since. This is the other thing. This is the only other thing I have ever been able to get funded, and I have been eleven years getting it, and I should like the man who costed it in 1958 to run it.*

The water went on doing what it does under the rail.

"He is offering me the work," Reggie said. "Not a directorship. Not an establishment. The work. Nineteen animals are alive in tanks because of a paper I wrote, and the arithmetic of how many are in tanks who would not have been but for that paper is a figure I have had since 1959 and have declined to give to the one person who has a right to it; and Alan Spilsbury is offering me three years and a ninety-foot vessel to go and count animals in the sea and take nothing out of it."

Pascual stood at the numbered bolt and had the whole of it, all at once, the way you have a diagnosis.

"You want it," he said.

"Yes," said Reggie.

He said it in an ordinary voice, out loud, at a rail, to another person, which he had not been able to do about anything whatever until about 1960.

---

There was a second thing and Reggie made himself say it, and it took twenty minutes, and Pascual understood by the end of it that this was the part that had cost the afternoon.

"He watched me want it," Reggie said. "I did not know I was doing it. I have declined that man eight times in eleven years and I have been correct in every particular on every occasion, and this afternoon he put a schedule on a table and something happened in my face, and he saw it, and he did not remark on it, and he changed nothing in his manner, which is how I know he saw it." He stopped. "I have spent my whole life making certain that nobody could tell what I wanted, on the grounds that a man who wants nothing cannot be reached. He now has an observation. He has had it since about two o'clock."

"Reggie —"

"I did not say no," Reggie said.

The generator carried on. Somewhere behind them John's second man was doing something noisy with a drum.

"I asked for until the autumn. I heard myself do it and I did not stop myself, and I want it recorded that in 1957 I stood in this place and objected to that man on the grounds that he would not permit me to say no; and this afternoon I was permitted to say no, twice, in a warm room, and I did not say it."

---

Pascual's interview was on the Thursday and lasted thirty-one minutes, and the first twenty-five of them were 1957 again: kindly, proper, at length, and about Reggie. Whether he slept. Whether the isolation told on him. Whether ten years in one place did a man of that sort good or harm.

He gave the man thirty years of clinical discretion and not one lie, and was not sick in the yard afterwards, and noticed the difference, and did not go into it.

At the twenty-fifth minute Spilsbury changed his manner very slightly and came to his business, and his business was a post.

Veterinary officer to the survey element. Wild stock at the water, condition assessment, the pathology of animals nobody had ever had a hand on. Three years. Senior. Named.

"It is not Bimini," Spilsbury said. "You looked into Bimini in the first year, I imagine, and found what there was to find, and it was a contract man in Miami, and I could not have altered it if I had wished to. This is not that. This is a post that does not exist unless you are in it."

Pascual sat with his arms folded and his face in order and did the one piece of work available to him, which was to listen to the shape of it rather than the content.

The content was excellent. The shape was three days old.

Everything else that man had ever put in front of either of them had been machined: the establishing minute drafted twice, the tanks written out in his own hand, eleven years of getting it funded. This had a joint in it. The salary was a range and not a figure. The reporting line was described twice in two ways. There was no paper.

*He made this on Wednesday afternoon*, Pascual thought, *and he made it because of a thing he saw on Tuesday at about two o'clock, and the thing he saw was that this year the price has a second name on it.*

"No," he said. "Thank you. I have a post."

"You have," Spilsbury agreed, without the smallest surprise, which was the finding, confirmed now for the third time in eleven years. "You'll allow me to leave it open. There is no hurry. The element sails with a contract man or without one; it will still want you in a year."

And at the door, taking up the new hat, in the tone of a man remarking on a paper to a colleague he respects:

"I have been reading the Margarita work. The siluroid is a first-class description — the best thing in the number, I thought." A pause of no length at all. "Cholmondeley. Now there is a name I had not looked to see in print again."

Pascual gave him nothing whatever, and had thirty years to give it with, and used all thirty.

Spilsbury looked at him doing it for about a second, pleasantly, and said it had been a great pleasure as always, and went down to the launch.

---

Pascual took two days to understand what he had done, and he understood it at four in the afternoon on the Saturday with a syringe in his hand, which is a thing that happens to doctors and is not a coincidence, because the hands work and the rest of a man goes where it likes.

He had refused in under a minute.

He had been pleased with himself about it. He had been carrying it about since Thursday like a good result: *under a minute, no hesitation, and I did not once look at the floor.* And it had taken him two days to arrive at the sentence a man of his training ought to have had inside ten seconds, which was that Reggie had asked for the autumn and Pascual had answered in under a minute, and that his answer went into the room before Reggie had decided anything at all, and that he had never consulted him.

There was a name for that in this building. It had been established in the March of 1957 at half past one in the morning, by Pascual, at some volume, and it was: arranging an outcome for somebody without telling him you are doing it.

He went and found Reggie at the rail and told him the whole of it, because there was a standard, and it had been set by the other man.

Reggie heard him out.

"I am aware of it," he said. "I had it at the time. I did not raise it because raising it would have been an application of pressure, and I am not going to apply pressure to you about this, because the last man who applied pressure to you about a post was your father, in 1929, and you have told me what that was."

"Then we are both very careful," Pascual said, "and neither of us has said anything true since Tuesday, and the man has until the autumn."

"Yes."

"Right." Pascual put both hands on the rail. "Then I'll go first, and I'll say the true thing, and you may have it however you like it. I don't want to go. Not the work — the work is the best offer either of us will ever get and I know it. I'm fifty-four. It took me fifty-four years and four countries to get to one building where the work and the life are the same thing, and I've had ten years of it, and if I go aboard that vessel I'm a veterinary officer on somebody else's establishment with a cabin and a chief who is my — " He stopped. "In front of thirty men. For three years."

"I had got that far," Reggie said.

"You had got that far on Tuesday."

"On Tuesday, at about ten past two."

"And you asked him for the autumn anyway."

"Yes," Reggie said.

---

They had it out. It went on for a good while and it was not a rail conversation, in the end; it finished in the small laboratory at about eleven with the door shut, because there were things in it that were not to be said within eighty yards of the house.

The worst of it was not the wanting. Pascual had had four days to sit with the wanting and had arrived somewhere with it, which was that a man of forty-eight who has been offered the only thing he ever asked for and does not want it is a man who has died and not mentioned it. The worst of it was the arithmetic Reggie did next, in order, with the objections and the answers to the objections, and it was about Charles.

"One. The element goes to that coast whether I am aboard it or not. It is funded, it is minuted, and it sails in the spring of 1966. Nothing I do here alters that."

"No."

"Two. Its stated method is to buy sighting records from the people who hold them, at retainer, along the whole southern range. That is my method. I wrote it. It works — I know it works, because it is precisely what Jean Leslie did on a research student's salary with no retainers at all, and it produced, in one season, a European resident at the fork above Guayo who has a word for the animal that everybody on that river has had since 1955."

Pascual sat down slowly on the bench.

"Three. The description is in print, in February, under his name, with a caño for an address, and every man who joins that element will read the number, because it is the only decent thing published on that fauna in twenty years and I saw to it that it would be."

"Reggie."

"I am not finished. Four. If I am aboard, I design the survey. I choose the stations and the seasons and the informants and the wording of every question put to any man on that coast, and I am the only person alive with the husbandry data and the tide and the course, which means I am the only person alive who could conduct a complete and creditable survey of fourteen hundred miles of that coast with one caño quietly not in it."

There was a silence.

"And five," Reggie said.

"Yes," Pascual said. "Say five."

"Five. I would be the only man aboard capable of concealing it, and I am also the only man aboard whose omissions would be legible. I have published under my own name since 1955 and I am correct in every particular and everybody who has ever read me knows it. A gap in a Tar survey is not an oversight. It is an exhibit. And the man who would be reading it has voted four times for the schedule it is written on and has been keeping some class of record on me since 1954, and I do not know what the record is for, and neither does anybody else in this room."

He stopped, and put his hands flat on the bench.

"So there is no arrangement in which Charles is safe," he said. "There is an arrangement in which he is found by strangers in 1967, and an arrangement in which he is found by me and the finding is not written down until I decide it is, and the second of those is better, and the second of those is also the one in which I am inside that man's establishment with a lever on me the size of a house and a lie to maintain for three years. I have run it perhaps two hundred times since Tuesday. It does not come apart anywhere and it does not come out."

"And the third arrangement," Pascual said.

"There is no third."

"There is. You've had it since Tuesday and you've not said it, and I'll say it, because you'll not say it in front of me." Pascual's voice was not raised. It had not been raised all evening, which was worse and had been since 1954. "The third is that you write to him in the autumn and take it, and you go, and I stay here with the animals, and we do what everybody in this position has always done, which is letters and a fortnight in the winter."

"No," Reggie said.

"Say why. Not *no*. Why."

"Because I have had that arrangement," Reggie said. "I had it from 1946 to 1954 with nobody at the other end of it, and it is the arrangement in which a man is safe because there is nothing on him that can be pulled, and I have given a full account of what that cost and I gave it to you in this room in November of 1960 and I am not going back into it at forty-eight in order to count animals."

"Then the answer's no and you've had it since Tuesday."

"The answer is that I have not said no," Reggie said, "and I would be obliged if you would let me be a man who has not said no until the autumn, because I have wanted exactly two things in my life and this is the second of them, and I should like to be permitted to want it out loud in a room for one summer before I put it down."

Pascual found that he had nothing whatever to say to that, which happened to him about once every five years and had never once been about medicine.

"Ay," he said at last. "Well. All right."

"That is not an answer."

"No," Pascual said. "There isn't one. There's not going to be one, and we'll be at this until September, and the alternative is the pair of us being careful at each other until the launch comes back." He got up and put the kettle on, because his hands wanted something. "And you'll not decide it alone in your own head at three in the morning. That's the whole of what I'm asking. You may want it as loudly as you like."

---

Reggie went up to the house on the Monday and was back inside a quarter of an hour.

"I gave him three sentences. That something has come and is funded. That it is a vessel and it will be on the southern coast from the spring of 1966. That offers have been made to both of us and neither has been declined in writing."

"And?"

"He asked one question, which was whether the man had written anything down. I said that he had put a schedule on a table and taken it away again with him. And the Colonel said: *Then it is real. He does not carry paper about for exercise.*" Reggie was quiet a moment. "And then he said: *Get me the sailing date and the establishment list. If there is to be a British scientific vessel on that coast I should like it to be on my desk before it is on anybody else's, and I am not going to ask you why you want to know it, and you are not going to tell me.*"

"He's not a fool."

"He has never been a fool," Reggie said. "He has been a man who declines to know things, which is a different discipline entirely, and I have been assuming for ten years that the two look alike from outside. They do not. They look alike from *inside*, if you are the one being not-known about."

---

The letter to Aberdeen went down at the end of the month with the specimen invoices.

It was four pages and it was about the work: the intake curves, the first quarter of the shrimp trial, a query on the Torry salinity method, a long and enjoyable insult about the catfish argument which he had lost in 1963 and had not stopped relitigating. In the middle of the third page, in the middle of a paragraph about the hydrometer standards, without any emphasis of any kind, there was a sentence that was not about the work.

*The description has been read in the quarter you would expect; the name was said aloud, once, kindly, in a doorway, and nothing was written down.*

Pascual watched him place it. He put it fourth in a paragraph of five sentences, and read the paragraph over twice with the pen down, and moved it to third.

"She'll want it at the end," Pascual said. "That's where she put hers."

"She put hers in the middle of a paragraph about the Aberdeen tanks," Reggie said, "and I have had eight years to think about why, and the reason is that a thing at the end of a page is a thing somebody's eye goes to. She was in that building at the time and I am not, and I have been in a building of that kind, and I am not going to hand a woman a page that reads like a page with a message on it."

---

That night Pascual took the wrist in the dark, two fingers along the inside of it and the thumb round the back, in the place.

It was going fast. It had been going fast since Tuesday afternoon and had not come down all week, and he had the whole of it under his hand and had had it under his hand for eleven years and was not going to say a word about it.

"I'm not taking a reading," he said.

"I am aware of what you are doing."

"Good."

Reggie lay still for a while. Then he said, in the voice he uses for figures, "Fifty-one minutes. Nineteen fifty-four, forty. Nineteen fifty-seven, twenty-two. This year, fifty-one, and I did not once look at my watch in the room, and I have not been able to establish whether that is the largest departure in the series or whether it is simply the one I can see."

"Go to sleep, Reggie."

"Yes," Reggie said, and turned over, and inside about ten minutes he was gone: all the way down, breathing slow, the way he had been able to do since about 1961 and could not do at all for the seven years before it.

Pascual lay in the dark next to him with his eyes open and did not sleep at all, and thought about a ninety-foot vessel with no tanks in it, and about a man in a mended shirt on a landing four hundred miles south of here waving with his whole arm, and about the autumn, and about the fact that the thing under his fingers had come down at last, and was steady, and was going to have to be given up by one of them or the other before the year was out.

He got about two hours towards morning. It was the first bad night he had had in this building since 1956, and he noted the date, because that is what you do with a series.
