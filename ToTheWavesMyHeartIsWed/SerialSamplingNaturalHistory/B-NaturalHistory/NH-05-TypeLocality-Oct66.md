# Type Locality

The clerk in Tucupita was about twenty-two and had a fan and a rubber stamp and no interest in him whatever, and that was the whole of it.

He had been dreading it since April. He had lain awake up the caño rehearsing the account, which was that he had gone over the side of a small boat off the north coast in the August of 1954 and had been picked up by fishermen and had been very ill for a long while and had not written to anybody afterwards, and that he was not prepared to discuss why. He had it word-perfect. He had worked out four ways of saying the last part without sounding as though he were hiding something, and had settled on the flattest, and had practised it on Anselmo, who had listened with enormous patience and then said that nobody would ask.

Nobody asked.

The clerk wanted a birthplace and a father's name and the date of entry, and wrote them down, and asked how to spell Cholmondeley twice, and then made him say it, and then laughed, which was fair. The twelve years went into the file as one line. There was nothing in the form that had a place for them. That was the thing he had not understood in all those nights: an account only becomes dangerous when somebody is looking for the shape of it, and a man behind a fan in Tucupita in May is not looking for anything except four o'clock.

He came out onto the street with a paper in his hand that said who he was.

He stood in the shade for a while. He had expected to feel caught. What he felt was the very particular lightness of having done a thing badly for twelve years and finally done it, which is not relief and is not triumph and mostly consists of wanting to sit down.

At Margarita in the June the Fundación people gave him an affiliation, a letter, a card, and an argument about siluroid dentition that ran to two hours and was the happiest he had been indoors since 1954. They had his description on the shelf. Somebody had annotated it in pencil, disagreeing with him, in three places, and one of the three was right.

---

The post began in the July and it did not stop.

He had not thought about the post. He had thought about launches and men in hats and had never once thought about the fact that a scientist with an address gets letters, and that within six weeks of being on paper he would have eleven correspondents in four countries and a request for a reprint from a man in Leiden he had been quietly stealing methods from since 1959.

Anselmo brought the sack up on Thursdays. Charles read it on the bench with his feet up and the river going past, and Monty came and lay along the landing with his chin on the boards while it was read out to him, because he liked the sound of it and did not like to be left out of things, and had never in his life been able to be told what any of it meant.

The letter from Spilsbury came in the middle of August.

It was one page and it was very good. He congratulated him, without qualification, on the description; he said that the habitat data behind it could not have been got in any other way and would not be got again in this century; he mentioned, in passing and with evident pleasure, that the illustrator on his survey vessel spoke of him with great affection. And then he said that he was in a position to fund original field work of this kind, that he had been trying to fund original field work of this kind since 1954, and that he would be glad to correspond, with no proposal attached and none intended, and that if it were ever of interest the invitation stood without limit of time.

There was no threat in it anywhere. There was nothing in it that a man could show to a friend and say *look*.

Charles sat with it for a long time and understood, with a kind of horrified admiration, that it was the same letter Reggie had been getting every spring for twelve years, and that Reggie had been describing it accurately for twelve years, and that he had not really believed him.

He did not answer it for six weeks.

---

What he did instead was work it out on the water, which is where he works everything out, lying on his back in the shallow off the landing at three in the afternoon with his ears under and the whole caño going on around him.

It went like this.

The thing he had that nobody else had was not a boat or a river or twelve years. It was a description — the only one anybody would ever have — of what these animals do when there is no wall anywhere near them. What they eat and when. How far they go in a season and why. How they sleep, which nobody at the Martin had ever seen properly done because it cannot be done properly in a grounded concrete pen and that is the whole of the mortality table right there. And the sense: what the sense is for, what it does, what its range is in brackish water at a given conductivity, what it manifestly cannot do, which is carry one single sentence from one animal to another, ever, at any distance, under any conditions, world without end.

And the reason a man in Miami wants that description is that at present it is in one head, up a river, unpublished.

A thing in one head can be taken into a building and worked out at leisure and the working-out can be marked *Special Studies* and put in a cabinet, and the man in the building will be perfectly courteous throughout and will genuinely, sincerely regret it.

A thing in the Proceedings cannot be taken anywhere. It is already everywhere. You cannot classify the September number.

He came up out of the water and sat on the step dripping and thought: *and I never have to write one word about myself, because the sense doesn't carry sentences, so there are no sentences to keep out.* He had spent twelve years assuming his secret and the animal's secret were the same object. They were not. They had never been. The animal's was publishable. His was not even sayable.

He wrote to Jean that evening, four pages, and the fourth page was a list of what would have to be done and how long it would take and it came to about three years, and he wrote at the bottom: *I should like you first, then Tar, then me, in that order, and I want to be argued out of it if it is stupid, and I would rather be argued out of it by you than find out on my own.*

Then he wrote to Spilsbury. It ran to five lines. He thanked him for his good opinion of the description, said that he was in the middle of a long piece of field work which he expected to publish in due course, said that he was not seeking funds, and asked, because he was curious and saw no reason not to be, whether the survey's people had by any chance recorded the local name for the animal on the Guayo channel, since he had four variants and suspected a fifth.

He sealed it before he could improve it. Reggie would have taken a week over it and made it worse.

---

Hester's letter came on the twenty-ninth of September and it was the shortest she had ever sent him.

*They have withdrawn. It went to the fourth clause and the fourth clause held. Come north if you can, in October, for three weeks. There is a great deal to be decided and none of us knows how to decide it and you are the only person alive who does. Ask Anselmo about the boat. I am asking.*

He read the last three words about six times.

---

The Martin, from the sea, in the second week of October 1966, was exactly the same and had a new building on the north side and looked very small.

That was the thing nobody warns you about. He had been carrying it for twelve years as a place with weather in it, and it turned out to be nine buildings and a jetty and about four acres, and the whole of his life had happened in the space between two of the buildings, and you could walk it in under a minute.

They were all on the jetty, which he had told them not to do.

Hester had a hat on. Pascual said something in Spanish that was not repeatable and got hold of him by both shoulders and looked him over from top to bottom like a man taking delivery, and said, "You have put on nine pounds since April, and I want it known that this is because of me." Jean was crying and furious about it. And Reggie stood at the back with his hands behind him and said, "You are extremely welcome," in the voice he uses for the Navy, and then, after a moment, in an entirely different voice, "Hullo, Charles," and could not manage anything further, and did not have to.

---

The fourth clause, when he finally read it, was thirty-one words long.

*The Institute holds no property in any living animal in its care; and no interest in any such animal shall pass, by transfer of the Institute or otherwise, to any person or body whatsoever.*

Hester had written it in 1948, at forty-one, in an office in St John's, against the advice of a solicitor who had told her it was unnecessary, that nobody would ever attempt such a thing, and that it made the instrument look eccentric. It had sat in the safe for eighteen years being eccentric. And in the September a body four thousand miles away had discovered that it could buy the Martin's buildings, its boat, its cabinets, its staff and its name, and could not buy the seven animals in the pens, and could not be given them, and could not acquire them afterwards by any route whatever, because they were not the Institute's to hand over and never had been.

They had withdrawn in eleven days. Charles read the withdrawal letter twice and it was courteous throughout.

"Why did you write it?" he asked her.

"Because I knew somebody would come," said Hester. "I did not know who and I did not know when. I knew what they would want and I knew what they would offer and I knew that in twenty years' time it would be offered to somebody tired." She was looking out of the window. "I have been asked that question once before, by the solicitor, and I told him it was a precaution. That was untrue. I wrote it in 1948 because of a thing that happened to me in 1938, and I have never said that to anybody, and I have now said it twice this year, which is twice more than in the preceding twenty-eight."

---

The trouble with the clause, of course — and he had it inside about a day, and so had Reggie, and neither of them had wanted to be the one to say it — was that it cut both ways and had always cut both ways.

If the Institute held no property in the seven animals in the pens, then the Institute had no answer to the question of what it was doing with seven animals in pens.

They had it out over four days in the room with the long table, and it was not a row; it was worse than a row, it was six people being extremely reasonable at each other about something none of them could bear. Reggie put up the mortality table, which he had kept since 1954 and had never shown to more than two people at once. Nineteen animals alive in tanks in the world, of which seven were here. Median survival in a grounded pen, four years and some months; and the cause was not grief, whatever the popular press had made of it, it was that a concrete box in still water is silent, and an animal that has slept its whole life inside the ordinary electrical noise of a living sea cannot sleep in silence, and eventually dies of it, and takes four years about it.

"They can't go back," Pascual said. "Not these seven. Three were taken as juveniles and have never fed themselves. Delta has no teeth on one side. I've had them all twelve years and I'm telling you as their doctor: put them in the Gulf and you have killed them politely."

"I know," said Reggie.

"So what are we doing?"

"We are doing the only thing available, which is that these seven are the last." Reggie had his hands flat on the table. "No animal is taken again by this Institute for any purpose. Not for exchange, not for replacement, not for the best paper any of us will ever write. We keep the seven alive and comfortable for as long as we can and we bury them, and when the last of them is gone this station has no animals in it and we go on working. Which we can only do if there is something to work on, and there is: I costed it in 1958 and London has struck it eight times, and it is nine lines long, and it is not going to be funded by London and it is not going to be funded by Miami and it is therefore going to be funded by us, out of the trust, at the expense of the second refrigerator and the new intake building we are no longer having."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Sighting records," said Jean. "Retainers to observers and boatmen. Non-specimen."

"Non-specimen," said Reggie.

And Hester, who had been at the end of the table for three hours saying almost nothing, which he had learnt in April was how she works, said: "Put it up in November. I shall sign it. It is the last thing I intend to sign as acting administrator and I should like it to be that."

Charles sat and looked at the table for a while and thought about a young man of twenty-four with a bucket, who had been told in his fourth week that the animal would very probably not last four years, and had believed it, and had been right.

"It's twelve years late," he said.

"Yes," said Reggie.

"I'm not saying it to be unkind."

"You are not being unkind. You are being accurate, and I have been sitting in this room for four days waiting for somebody to say it, and I would very much rather it was you than me."

---

He went to see the Colonel on the fifth day, up at the house, in the afternoon.

He was in a chair by the window with a rug over his knees, which he plainly resented, and he was smaller than Charles remembered and his hands had got old before the rest of him. He looked at Charles for a long moment.

"You are thin," he said.

"I've been told."

"Sit where I can see you. My hearing is indifferent on the left." He waited until Charles had sat. "I signed a statement to the Navy on the eleventh of September 1954 to the effect that I was satisfied with this station's account of its losses. It was the only official statement I made in forty years without first establishing that it was true. I have been asked twice what it rested on and I have declined twice, and I am going to tell you, and then we shall talk about something else and I shall not refer to it again."

"Sir — "

"It rested on my wife's face."

Outside somebody was doing something with a wheelbarrow.

"On the thirtieth of August 1954 my wife was told that a young man on the establishment had gone into the sea and drowned. I have been married to her since 1940 and I have watched her be told a great many things." The Colonel's hands were quite still on the rug. "She was not grieving. She was frightened, which is a different thing and does not look at all like it, and underneath the fright she was — I have never found the word. She had the face of a woman who has had extremely good news at a very bad moment.

"And I concluded that you were alive, and that she knew it, and that she knew it by some route she was never going to be able to explain to me; and I signed the paper, and I did not ask her one question about it then and I have not asked her one since."

Charles found he could not say anything at all.

"I did not tell her," said the Colonel. "That is the part I should like understood, since you are the only person I can tell it to without it costing her anything. If I had told her, she would have had to account for it. She has spent her whole life expecting to be made to account for it. I was not going to be one more person who required that of her." He shifted in the chair. "So it has sat with me for twelve years, and I am seventy-one, and I find I did not want it to go with me. That is all. Now. I am told you have four variants of the local name and suspect a fifth."

"I do," said Charles, "and I'm fairly sure the fifth is a corruption of the second, and I can't prove it."

"Then you had better tell me about it," said the Colonel, "at length, and slowly, on the left."

---

The last night he sat with Hester in the sitting room, which was where they had sat in the August of 1954, and neither of them mentioned that, and both of them knew it.

They had got to the end of the business. The line was drafted. The seven were the last. He was going down on the Thursday boat and there was a plan for 1967 and a plan for 1968, and Jean had the paper's outline in her bag and had already told him that the third section was too long, which it was.

He said, "I've been trying to work out how to ask you something all week, and I've decided the only way is to ask it."

"Then ask it."

"In 1954, in this room, you put your hand on my face."

"I did," said Hester. "I have thought about it perhaps a thousand times since and I have never been sure it was a decent thing to do. You were twenty-four and you did not know what I was and I did not tell you until afterwards."

"You got me."

"Yes."

"All of me. Whatever there is to get. And I've had twelve years to think about that too, and about the fact that I never did it back." He was looking at the floor. "I didn't know that I could. I didn't know that was a thing anybody could do until — " He stopped. "And by the time I did know, I was four hundred miles away and you were on a hill above a lot of grounded concrete, and it wasn't going to happen by letter."

Hester did not move.

"You have had me for twelve years," he said, "and I've never had you at all, and you have gone about like that the whole time and never once mentioned it, and it has been one way, and I've worked out what one way is."

"What is it?"

"It's not nothing," said Charles. "But it isn't the other thing."

He got up and crossed the room and knelt down in front of her chair, which was easier than doing it standing, and he said, "May I?" and Hester said, "Yes," and it came out with about half the air she had meant to put behind it.

He put his hand on her face.

He is not going to try to write down what he got, then or afterwards; it does not go into words and he has stopped believing that this is a failure of his vocabulary. It is a category error, and he had been making it since he was twenty-four, and he was thirty-six now and had had it explained to him at some length by an animal.

But this much can be put down. He got a girl of thirty-one in Hampshire. He got four years. He got the end of the four years, which she had told him in April in the ordinary way, out loud, in words, and which he discovered he had not understood one thing about until this moment. He got twenty-eight years of a very careful woman on a dry hill. And he got, quite clearly, the thing she had never said and would never say, which was that she had not written those two hundred and eleven pages to him. She had begun writing them in 1964 to a man who had been dead for twenty-six years, and had discovered somewhere around page ninety that she was writing to a living person, and had gone on anyway.

He took his hand away.

Hester was sitting perfectly upright with her eyes shut and her hands in her lap and the tears going straight down her face without any performance at all, and after a while she said, "Well," and then, "Well," again, and then nothing else for some time.

"That's two ways now," said Charles.

"Yes."

"I know there's a word for what that is. I know what it means when they do it. I'm not — " He sat back on his heels. "I don't think it's the same thing. I think we shall have to have our own word for it."

"Then we shall," said Hester, "and we shall not tell anybody what it is."

---

He went down on the Thursday.

Reggie came to the jetty with a folder, because of course he did, and it was the November submission, and he wanted a paragraph on sleep in the wild that only Charles could write, and Charles wrote it standing up on a bollard with the folder against his knee while they held the boat, and got salt water on it, and Reggie took it back and looked at the smeared corner and said nothing whatever about the smeared corner, which from him was practically an embrace.

Pascual gave him a box of things and a lecture about protein.

Hester did not come to the jetty. She had said so on the Tuesday, and had given her reason, and the reason was that she had done the other kind of goodbye once and had found that she preferred to be at a window.

He looked up as the launch came round, and she was.

Eleven days later he was on his own landing in the dark with the water going past, and there was the ordinary noise of a living river all round him, ten thousand small transactions a second, and out in the channel there was one particular thing coming up fast from the north-east that was not a fish.

"Yes, all right," said Charles. "I'm here. Yes. Yes, I know. I've been on a boat."
