# Discontinuation

## I

He found it out the way he finds everything out, which is by catching himself at something and
then sitting very still.

It was the fourteenth of July and Reggie had come in at about nine and stood in the doorway for a
moment before going through to the desk, and Pascual, reading, did not look up.

That was all. He did not look up.

Twelve years he'd looked up. Twelve years of taking the reading off the man in the doorway before
a word was said — the set of the shoulders, whether the hands were empty, whether he came straight
through or stopped; the whole of it, in about a second and a half, without effort, the way you
take a pulse. He had never once decided to do it. It was not a service he had elected to perform.
It was simply what happened when Reggie Tar came into a room Pascual was in.

And on the fourteenth of July he was reading something about tetanus in equids and he did not
look up, and forty minutes later he realised that he could not have said how the man was.

He sat with that for four days.

Because here is the arrangement, and he had never had to state it before and found when he tried
that it stated very badly: that man does not know how he is. He is not being brave and he is not
concealing anything. The information is not available to him. It exists — it is all over him, in
the hands and the shoulders and the eleven o'clock light and whether he can finish a plate — but
it exists in a form only somebody else can read, and for twelve years there has been exactly one
reader in the house, and the reader is fifty-five and has begun to look at his book instead.

It is not that he was tired of him. He wants that on the record, wherever the record is. He was
not tired of him.

He was tired in a way that had no remedy inside the arrangement, which is a different thing and a
worse one. The whole virtue of the thing — the reason it worked when nothing in either of their
lives had worked — was that Pascual read and Reggie did not tell. There was no version of it in
which he got to put the instrument down for a fortnight and be told instead. He had asked once, in
1958, without meaning to, and Reggie had been so plainly willing and so plainly unable that
Pascual had never done it again.

So the choice was not between staying and going. It was between a man who read him and a man who
had begun, at fifty-five, occasionally, to be careless.

He would not be that. Not with this one. He had watched what carelessness cost in that house and
he had a boy in a delta and a March in 1964 to price it by.

---

He wrote to San Juan on the nineteenth and had the reply by the eleventh of August, and it was a
good post and they wanted him and the money was absurd, and none of that had anything to do with
it.

## II

He told him on a Tuesday, in the evening, sitting down, having thought for three weeks about
whether to do it standing and got it wrong either way.

He did not construct it. He had constructed things at that man perhaps four times in twelve years
and had been ashamed of all four and this was not going to be the fifth.

He said that he had taken the San Juan post. He said that he was going alone. He said that it was
not about the work and not about anybody else and that he was not going to give him a reason that
was easier to hear than the true one, and then he gave him the true one, which took about two
minutes and included the fourteenth of July.

Reggie listened to the whole of it without moving.

Then he said, "Thank you for telling me the actual reason."

"Reggie—"

"No. I mean it. You could have given me the post." He had his hands flat on his knees. "The post
would have been sufficient. I would have accepted the post and I would not have looked underneath
it, and I should have been wrong for the rest of my life. So. Thank you."

And then he asked when the appointment began.

---

Pascual has gone over the next forty minutes a great many times.

There was no argument. There was not one sentence in it that could be called an argument. Reggie
asked when the appointment began, and what the notice was, and whether the crossing was to be by
air; and then he said that the Michaelmas intake would want reorganising and that he would do it
in the week and that Pascual was not to concern himself with it. He asked whether Pascual wanted
the Ponce books packed or sent on. He said that he would write to the Colonel himself, since it
would come better from him, and that he proposed to write it as an institutional loss and nothing
else, and did Pascual agree that this was the correct form.

He got the whole thing right. Every arrangement. He got it right that week.

And at some point Pascual understood what he was looking at, and it went through him like cold
water, and he has never got warm about it since.

The man was not accepting it. He was not resigned and he was not being brave and he was not
letting Pascual off. He had been given a report, by the person best placed to make it, about that
person's own condition — and he had no grounds on which to dispute another man's account of
himself, and it had not occurred to him for one second that he was entitled to try. It was the
same faculty. It was the identical faculty, the one Pascual had loved him for since 1955 and had
watched him turn on his own published work in front of juniors: *I was wrong, and here is the
correction, and we go on.* No floor under it. Nothing in it that protects him.

Pascual could have said *ask me to stay* and he would have asked. He could have said *tell me not
to go* and been told not to go. He could have had anything he wanted in that room by naming it,
because the man had no defence against him and did not know he needed one, and never has.

That is a thing to be holding at fifty-five with your bag half packed.

And he did not name it, and he is still not certain whether that was decency or the last piece of
cowardice available to him, and he has had thirty years to decide and has not.

## III

*Reggie, September.*

On the ninth of September he wakes at four and does not get back to sleep, and notes it. On the
tenth he wakes at four. On the eleventh, at ten past. He keeps the times for eight days and then
looks at the column and finds that it is a column and stops keeping it.

The Michaelmas intake is reorganised by the seventeenth and is better arranged than it was.

He finds that he does not want food and that this is not a preference but a fact about the body,
in the way that a temperature is a fact, and he considers it. There is an instruction on the
subject. The instruction is eleven years old and was issued by a man who is going to Puerto Rico
in November and who cannot therefore be said to have any standing in the matter, and he examines
the question of standing carefully, because it seems to him the sort of thing one could get wrong
by wanting a particular answer.

He concludes that the instruction was not conditional when it was given.

He therefore eats at one o'clock.

On the twenty-second he is at the rail at some hour and finds that he would like to sit down, and
sits down, and the deck is wet, and he stays there.

He does not at any point form the view that a mistake has been made. Pascual has reported
accurately on Pascual, which is the one report in the world Reggie has always been able to rely
upon, and it would be a poor return on twelve years to begin doubting the instrument now that the
reading is unwelcome. He has been given a true thing. He has been given it kindly and at cost and
in person, which he understands is not what most men get.

What he cannot make come out is the arithmetic underneath. He has been over it. If he had been a
different man, one who could say how he was when he was asked, then Pascual would not have had to
read him for twelve years and would not have worn out reading him; and he was not able to do that;
and he was not able to become able to do it; and he does not know whether a thing you could not
do is the same as a thing you did.

He rules a line under it. It is not a soluble problem and he has a station to run.

## IV

The Colonel took it as an institutional loss, in the form Reggie had drafted, and said several
generous things at supper.

Jean wrote in the October from Aberdeen. She wrote to Pascual, four pages, and there was one
sentence in it he has never forgiven and has never answered and has kept: *I don't say you're
wrong. I say you're the only one of the two of you who was ever able to be wrong out loud, and
you've spent twelve years not doing it, and now you've gone and made the one decision that can't
be retracted.*

He has read that perhaps two hundred times. She is right and it is not usable. There was nothing
to retract. He was not wrong.

## V

He went on the ninth of November and Reggie came down to the jetty and shook his hand and said
that he hoped the crossing would be a good one, and that the practice in San Juan was fortunate.

That was all of it. He said it in the voice he used for the Colonel.

Pascual got about six feet along the jetty and turned round, because there was one more thing and
he had promised himself he would say it, and he said: "You'll tell somebody. If you're ill. You'll
tell Jean, or you'll tell the Bevans, or you'll write to me. Reggie. You'll tell somebody."

And Reggie said, "I shall not know."

Not defiantly. Not to wound him. As a technical objection, offered in good faith, because Pascual
had asked for an undertaking the man could not honestly give and he was not going to give an
undertaking he could not honour.

Pascual said, "Then tell somebody anyway. Tell them the facts. Tell them what you ate."

"Very well," Reggie said. "I can do that."

And he did, for a while.

## VI

What he did was tell Hester Bevan, because Hester Bevan asked him.

She began it on the fourteenth of November, five days after the jetty, by walking down to the
eastern pens at eight in the morning and falling into step with him and asking how many hours he
had slept. Not how he was. How many hours. And when he told her, she said thank you and asked what
he had eaten the day before, and he told her that too, and she said thank you again and talked
about the Michaelmas intake for a quarter of an hour and went back up.

She did it every Friday for four years.

She never once said a consoling thing to him. Pascual has thought about that a good deal and has
concluded that she could not — that for a woman who had been through the identical thing in 1938
there is no sentence in the language that comforts without also naming the wound, and that she had
looked for one for thirty years and it does not exist, and that she had the sense not to
substitute a worse sentence for the missing one. What she had instead was a question with a number
for an answer. She asked it every Friday and she wrote the answers down.

And twice a year, in the June and the December, without telling him, she wrote to San Juan.

They were not letters. They were four lines: weight, hours, whether he had been off the station,
whether he was carrying anything he shouldn't. Facts, in a hand like a ledger, to the only reader
on earth who could do anything with them. She never asked Pascual a question and never once
invited a reply and never gave him the smallest opening to say anything about himself, which he
understood at the time to be a kindness and has since decided was a judgement.

He kept all eight of them.

---

The Colonel's chest went in the winter of 1969 and they were in England by the March of 1970, and
that was the end of it, and nobody did anything wrong.

Reggie did not stop reporting. That is the part Pascual could not make anybody understand
afterwards, on the two occasions he tried. Reggie did not decide that the undertaking had lapsed
and he did not decide that he was well enough to stop and he did not, in any sense available to
him, stop at all. He had given an undertaking to tell somebody, and telling — for that man, for
the whole of his life, in every context there has ever been — is a thing that happens when he is
asked. Ask him and you will get the truth to four decimal places and no rounding. Do not ask him
and there is no mechanism. It is not reticence. There is no engine in there that starts by itself.

Mrs Bevan went to England and nobody on that station ever asked him again, and it did not occur to
him that the undertaking obliged him to go and find a new person to be asked by, because that would
have required him to originate the thing, and he cannot.

He was in perfect good faith about it for the rest of his life.

---

Pascual noticed in the June of 1970 that no letter came, and knew precisely what it meant, and
worked out inside a minute what would have to be done about it: a note to Reggie, or a note to
Jean, or a name given to the Colonel's successor with a quiet word attached.

He did not write it. He has never satisfactorily accounted to himself for that, and he has had
the four lines from the December of 1969 in a drawer in San Juan for twenty-five years, and the
nearest he has ever come to an account is that writing it would have meant admitting that he had
been reading those letters, and that he had wanted them, and that he had gone to Puerto Rico and
arranged for somebody else to keep the watch.

## VII

The letters were quarterly and entirely about work and impeccably courteous and he answered every
one of them within the week for nineteen years.

In the March of 1977 there was one about the eastern pens and the drainage, four paragraphs, with
a figure Pascual had asked for; and in the third paragraph, between a note on the sluice and a
question about a supplier, without emphasis, in the middle of a sentence about the working day,
Reggie mentioned that he had kept the practice of eating at one o'clock.

He did not remark on it. He did not know that he had said anything.

Pascual read it in the kitchen in San Juan with the light on and the coffee going cold, and then
he read it again, and then he put the letter down on the table and put both hands flat on either
side of it, and there was nobody in the house to see him do it, which was the arrangement, and
which he had chosen, and which was correct.

He answered within the week. He sent the supplier's address.

## VIII

Ramón Vidal kept a pharmacy in Santurce and had buried a wife and raised three daughters and
played dominoes very badly and very loudly, and in the spring of 1979 he came round the counter to
argue with Pascual about a veterinary order and did not stop arguing for sixteen years.

He was a good man and there is nothing clever to say about him.

Pascual was sixty-eight. He had been on his own for thirteen years, not out of fidelity — he wants
that plain, because fidelity would have implied a person to be faithful to and there was no such
arrangement and never had been — but because he had not been looking, having not left for that
reason.

What Ramón did, in about the third month, was say: "If something's the matter you'll tell me. I'm
not going to guess. I'm no good at guessing and I don't like it."

And Pascual said yes.

And then he did it, for sixteen years, without effort, at no cost, whenever asked and frequently
when not.

He would like somebody to appreciate what that cost him to discover. Not the doing of it. The
finding out that it was easy. He had spent twelve years carrying a thing he had privately
understood to be the price of loving a particular man, and had been proud of carrying it, in the
way one is proud of a load nobody else could take — and at sixty-eight he learnt that with a
different man in a different kitchen there was no load, there had never been a load, and that
what he had been carrying was not a virtue but a shape that the two of them made and neither of
them chose.

---

The instrument had nothing to do.

That took him about two years to notice and he noticed it, as usual, by catching himself: they
were at the table with the girls and somebody's husband was plainly in trouble about money, and
Pascual read it off him in four seconds flat the way he'd read a horse, and found that he was
tired afterwards, and found that this was because he had not done it in months.

Ramón did not require reading. Ramón told you. Ramón came in and said *I am in a filthy mood and
it is not about you, it is about Herrera and the invoices,* and then was in a filthy mood about
Herrera and the invoices, and it passed, and there was no work in it anywhere.

It is a very pleasant way to live. He has never once said otherwise and never once wished it
different and would not have swapped it.

But the faculty was the whole of him. Forty years of getting everything he knew by looking at it
— and it turns out that the only object it was ever built for was a man who could not report
himself, and that Pascual had gone to Puerto Rico and found somebody who could, and that the
instrument now sat in the corner of the house like a good horse in a field with nothing to carry.

He was happy. That is the part that has to be got right. He was happy for sixteen years and it was
not a consolation and it was not second-best and Ramón was not standing in for anybody.

He was just not being used.

---

Ramón asked him once, in about 1983, on the porch, why he had left the man in Trinidad.

And Pascual — who could be asked anything, and answered everything, and had for four years —
gave him the careless version. The fourteenth of July. Not looking up. A man who had begun to be
careless with somebody who could not survive it, and had rather gone than become the man who
missed something.

Ramón listened to all of it and thought about it for a while and said: "That's a very handsome
reason."

"It's the reason."

"I'm sure," said Ramón, who had been married for twenty-nine years to somebody, and who did not
say the other thing, because he was a kind man and it was not his to say. And he got up and went
in and put the kettle on, and that was the whole of it, and it never came up again in twelve
years.

So there it is. The man who could always be asked and always answered had exactly one thing he
would not answer, and he held it for the whole of his life, in a house where he was asked
directly, on a porch, by somebody who would have listened.

There was no engine in there that started by itself either. It only ever looked as though there
was, because for forty years there had been so much else for it to do.
