# §VI (expanded) — The two years

## The letters after Kingston

The correspondence did not change and that is the thing to be exact about.

It went on being about the work. It was weekly by the January of 1976 and twice weekly by the
summer, and the contents are what they had been: the fourth week, the stagger, a query about a
supplier of glass, four pages on a paper of Finney's that Nairn thought overrated and Reggie
thought correct and misapplied. Anybody going through the file would find one hundred and
eighty-one letters between the September of 1975 and the September of 1977 and would find
nothing in them.

What changed was the question at the end.

It had been put there since the February of 1973 for a stated reason, which is that a letter
without a question in it does not require an answer, and the reason had been sufficient and had
not needed examining. From the November of 1975 it was doing a second thing, and the second
thing was load-bearing, and Reggie did not know about it for nine years.

He can be acquitted on that. The questions were real. He wanted the answers; he used them; two
of them went into the 1977 paper. There is nothing in the file that would show it to a stranger.
It is only that the interval at which he needed a technical answer from Hertfordshire had, over
a period of about four months, come to be the interval at which the post ran.

## The twenty-six

*(Existing paragraphs at 348–366 stand. What follows sits under them.)*

The four variants he used most are the four already set down. There were others, and they are
worth recording, because the record is the only thing that survives an operation like that one
and because nobody but him has ever had the whole of it in front of them.

The twenty-six run:

In the December of 1975, whether the December letter, which was two pages shorter than the
November letter, was shorter for a reason, and if so whether the reason was one he ought to be
told.

In the February of 1976, whether Nairn's not having mentioned the Kingston paper in three
consecutive letters constituted a view of the paper or a view of something else.

In the March of 1976, whether the position stated in the corridor at Kingston on the third night
had been correctly transcribed, and whether Nairn would set it out again in writing so that it
might be checked against the transcription.

In the May of 1976, after the fortnight, whether the fortnight had been what Nairn had expected
when he proposed it in the January, and whether he would propose it again on the same terms.

In the June of 1976, the same question in different words, in a letter which also asked why a
plot had been redrawn.

In the August of 1976, whether a delay of nineteen days was attributable to the post.

In the October of 1976, whether it was still the case that Nairn wished to be asked.

That is the one Reggie has never been able to look at squarely, and it is not because of what it
says. It is because it is the only one of the twenty-six that is not a question about the
external world. He noticed that in about 1980 and filed it and takes it out.

## What it cost the other party

Nairn answered every one of them in full and never once indicated that it had been asked before.
What is not on the record anywhere is that he kept a count as well.

He started it in the January of 1976, in the back of a field notebook, and the purpose of it was
not sentimental and was not administrative. It was so that he could never be surprised. A man who
is surprised by the twenty-second instance of a thing will say something about it being the
twenty-second instance; a man who has been expecting it since the twentieth will not. He was
running an instrument at a known interval and he had priced the interval in the March of 1975 in
writing, and he had written *Noted. Carry on,* and he was not going to be the reason it stopped.

He got it wrong once. In the August of 1976 he began a reply *You asked me this in the* and had
four more words down before he saw what he was doing, and he burnt the page in the grate rather
than put it in the basket, which is not a thing he does with paper and which he could not have
justified to anybody.

He also worked out, in the January of 1976, that they clustered.

They do not come after a bad week. They come after a good one — after a letter of his that
Reggie had liked, after the fortnight, after anything that went well. He had the dates in front
of him and there was no ambiguity in it and it took him about ninety seconds to understand the
mechanism, which is that a good result is precisely the class of result that most requires
checking, and that a man who has had one thing go right in his life and lose it has no reason
whatever to trust the second.

He did not report the finding.

He had already made the November error by then and had understood the rule it broke — the work
can be praised without limit, the man cannot be praised at all — and this was on the wrong side
of that line and he could see that it was. It is one of two findings he has held for nine years
without stating, and he keeps them in the same place, and he is aware that a man who keeps
findings from his only correspondent has stopped being purely an experimenter and does not know
what to put in place of that.

## The November error

*(Relocated from §VI. Text stands as written, with the following after it.)*

What he had said was eleven words about Reggie and not about the work, and it was true and mild
and would have gone unremarked in any room in England.

Reggie's account of it, entered that night, is: *Nairn made a statement about me which I was
unable to evaluate. I do not think he was in error. I have no procedure for the class.* He did
not record having been distressed by it because he had not been distressed by it. What he had
been was stopped, in the way an instrument is stopped, and it had lasted about four seconds and
he had come back on and the evening had continued.

Douglas has never known that the entry exists. He assumes, and would say if asked, that he
frightened the man. He did not frighten him. He handed a fifty-eight-year-old a reading in a
unit he has no gauge for and then watched him try, in good faith, for four seconds, to convert
it.

## The fortnight — April 1976

Nairn proposed it in the January, in the last line of a letter otherwise entirely about
acclimation, in the formula he had used in the corridor at Kingston and has used four times
since and will not vary:

*I'd like to come out for a fortnight in the spring. You can say no and it won't make any
difference to the fourth week.*

Reggie replied inside the week. He wrote that he should prefer it exceedingly; that the first
fortnight in April would be better than the second on account of the intake; that there was a
room; and that the Institute would meet nothing whatever and Nairn should be clear about that
before he committed himself to the passage. Then he asked a question about the Rothamsted
classification of a soil which had nothing to do with anything and which Nairn answered in two
and a half pages.

*I should prefer it exceedingly* is not a courtesy and Nairn did not read it as one, and this is
the first of the two occasions in the two years on which he had the thing in his hand and did not
understand what he was holding.

It went into the index as *Visitor: Nairn, 3rd–17th April,* and that is why it worked.

Nobody understood that at the time, including both of them, and the failure to understand it cost
the whole of the winter of 1977 and eight months of 1978. A fortnight has a start date and a
finish date and a heading over the top of it, and a heading over the top of it makes it a state,
and Reggie can hold a state indefinitely. He was got through a war on a state. For fourteen days
there was a man in the house at all hours and it did not once present itself as a series of
arrivals, because it was not one; it was a fortnight, and it had been entered, and the entry ran
until the seventeenth.

He was easier in that fortnight than he had been since 1966, and the ease is on the record in a
form neither of them recognised: Mrs Ramnarine told Nairn on the eleventh day, unprompted, in the
corridor, that the doctor was finishing his lunch.

Nairn wrote it down that evening, under the count, with the date. He did not know what to do with
it for two years. It is what he went and got in the March of 1978 when he asked what Reggie
weighed.

## The other channel

There is a thing to be set down here and Douglas is the one to set it down, since Reggie has
already given his own account of it and it is not the same account and neither of them is wrong.

Douglas does not make comparisons. He spent thirty years telling agronomists that two trials on
different sites do not pool, and he applies the rule to his own life and has never once let
himself run Alec against anybody, and he would say, if asked, that a man who does that is not
being loyal, he is being lazy with a method he knows perfectly well.

So the finding stands on its own and it is this.

The man cannot originate a sentence. He has stated the mechanism in writing and the statement is
accurate and Douglas has tested it thirty ways and has never found it to fail. There is no
engine; nothing starts by itself; ask and you get four decimal places, do not ask and you get
nothing, for fifty-six years, with no exceptions.

Except one, and it is not a partial exception, it is a whole other apparatus running in the same
building. In that room he originates continuously. He says what he wants, in order, without being
asked, in the plainest English Douglas has ever heard out of him, and he is not embarrassed and
has never once apologised for any of it, and the wanting is specific and detailed and entirely
unhedged, and it arrives *first* — before Douglas has asked, before Douglas has done anything at
all.

Douglas had it on the third night at Kingston and did not believe it, and had it again on the
fourth and did, and has spent nine years failing to account for the discontinuity.

He has a rule about findings he cannot support and he keeps to it, and what he does with this one
is hold it as an anomaly and not build on it. He has one observation and no explanation: whatever
was done to that man, and something was, it was not done to that. He has never asked, because the
question would have to be put to somebody who does not know the answer, and would be answered
truthfully and completely and would produce nothing.

What he will say, and has said, twice, to Reggie, in eleven words, is that it is the best of him.

---

The touching was worked out in the April fortnight and it took eleven days, and it is a separate
matter and neither of them expected it to be.

It began by accident, which is not the same as carelessly.

Douglas does not touch people. He shakes hands, and there is not a soul at Rothamsted in thirty
years who could tell you what his hands are like, and it is not a policy and he has never once
thought of it as restraint. The impulse has never presented itself outside one context in
fifty-six years and there is no reason it should have. Inside that context he is another article
entirely and always has been. Alec had twelve years of it. There had been nobody since the
February of 1958 and the fact had not required thinking about.

On the second evening he came round the back of Reggie's chair to get at a plot on the table, and
put his hand on the back of his neck, and left it there for about four seconds while he read. He
had not decided to do it. He had not known it was coming. It was the first time in eighteen years.

He felt the whole man stop.

He took the hand off and apologised properly and went on about the plot, and filed it as an error
of the November class, and did not do it again for nine days; and what he did instead was watch,
because that is the trade, and by about the sixth day he had a difficulty with the data, which is
that a man who has been made uncomfortable does not sit where he sat.

On the eleventh day he asked. In the kitchen, in the voice for reading out figures.

"That business with your neck on the second night. I've had it down as a mistake for nine days
and I want to know whether it was, because I'm getting a reading I can't reconcile."

"It was not a mistake."

"Right. Then what was it? And I'd like it in your own words rather than mine, because mine are no
use to me here."

Reggie put the pencil down, which he had learnt off him.

"I liked it," he said. "I like it very much. I stopped because I did not know it was going to
happen and I had nothing prepared." He looked at the middle of the table. "You had better be
told that I am unlikely to ask you for it. That is not reticence and it is not a preference
against. It is the same mechanism as the letters and it does not improve. If you want a signal
you will have to take *I liked it,* because that is available and I will give it every time and
it is accurate."

"That'll do," said Douglas.

"It is not much."

"It's a damn sight more than most men get, and I've had the other kind." He got up and put the
kettle on with his back to the room, which is what he does instead of pausing. "So I'll go on
doing it and you'll go on saying that, and if it ever stops being true you'll tell me the same
week."

"I shall tell you the same day."

"Aye. You will, and all."

---

He brought the tea across and did not go back round the table.

He put his hand on the back of Reggie's neck and left it there and read the plot upside down over
his shoulder for as long as that took, which was a good deal longer than it needed to be, and
said something disparaging about the point at week six.

At about the fourth second the shoulders came down. Half an inch, perhaps less. They stayed down,
and the man went on reading, and did not remark on it, and in Douglas's estimation did not know.

Douglas kept his voice on the week six point and did not look at him and did not take the hand
away for some minutes, and then went and washed the cups.

It is the only observation he made in the whole of that fortnight that he did not write down.

---

That is the arrangement and it has not been altered since and it is the only mechanism in the
whole of it that runs in both directions without an intermediary.

It also produced, in the last three days of the fortnight, a small item which Douglas did not
record at the time and now cannot date, which is that Reggie began to sit where he could be
reached. Not to reach; that has never happened and Douglas has stopped expecting it and does not
regard it as owing. But there is a chair at the end of the table and there is a chair by the
window, and from about the fourteenth of April 1976 the man has sat in the one at the end of the
table, and nobody has ever remarked on it, and there is no heading over it.

## The tell

Two other things belong to the fortnight.

The first is that he heard the tell in a room for the first time. He had had it in letters —
forty-one of them, and the register is visible in writing if you know the man, the contractions
going and the verbs going to the end and a sentence beginning *I am not able to* — but he had
never watched it happen at four feet in a kitchen, and on the ninth evening he asked a question
about 1966 and got the whole answer and heard the machinery change gear in the middle of the
second sentence.

He thought about that for the rest of the fortnight and on the boat and for some weeks after, and
in the November he settled it: *he is not going to be told.* The reasoning is short. It is a
signal that arrives without the sender's knowledge and it is the only reliable instrument Douglas
has; telling him would not stop him doing it, it would only make him aware that it is being read,
and a man who has spent his life being read to exhaustion by one person and has drawn the
conclusion he drew about it is owed at least the one channel he does not know is open. It goes on
needing doing about every six months. It has never got easier and he has never reopened it.

The second is that they settled nothing about the fourth week.

## The winter

Both of them knew by the autumn of 1976 that the arrangement was not going to hold at its present
frequency, and neither of them could say it, and their reasons are different and each of them is
wrong about the other's.

Reggie's file on the question of what another man wants has one result in it. He put it there on a
Tuesday in the September of 1966 and it is the only one and he has never once inflated it. He does
not conclude from it that he can read people. He concludes what the arithmetic permits: that he is
not categorically disqualified, which is a different finding and a much smaller one, and that
anything he produces in the class is provisional until checked.

That is his account of the twenty-six and it is not wrong. A man with an empty file says nothing
at all. A man with one result in it forms a view and then checks it, at intervals, for as long as
it takes, and Reggie has been running the correct procedure for a provisional finding since the
November of 1975 and would defend it in any seminar in the world.

It is also not the whole of the account, and the missing half is in a field notebook in
Hertfordshire in the form of a list of dates. The procedure is sound. What the procedure is for is
that he was, by his own reckoning, in the best year of his life in the June of 1966, and had been
told nothing, and was told in the September. Reggie has the method. Douglas has the dates. Neither
of them has both and only one of them knows there are two halves.

So in the October of 1976 he formed a view: that Nairn would prefer the arrangement to stand at a
greater frequency than the post allows. He entered it. He marked it unverified, on the grounds
that the only available verification was to put the question, and putting a question of that class
is not answering one.

It sat in the file, correctly entered and correctly qualified, and he was in no distress about it,
and he was not going to act on an unverified finding, and he did not act on one.

He wrote to Hertfordshire about the Institute's forward estimates instead.

Nairn's reason is that he had convinced himself he could not find out.

The reasoning ran: he can ask the man anything and will get the truth to four decimal places, and
that is precisely the difficulty. A fortnight can be declined — it is bounded, it is refusable,
it costs the refuser almost nothing. A two-year appointment ten miles off cannot be declined once
it has been undertaken, and it is not the same object, and the man would say *I should prefer it
exceedingly* to both in the same words with the same face, and there is no instrument on earth
that tells you whether the words scale. Every route to the answer runs through a question that
spoils the answer.

He held that for four months and it was wrong, and he found out how wrong in the February, and he
had already acted on it by then.

## October 1976

The letter came on a Thursday and it was a page and a half and it was on the Institute's paper.

It set out, in numbered paragraphs, a methodological question outstanding in the Institute's own
published work since 1956; the fact that it had been the occasion of a substantial correspondence
without resolution; the reason it could not be resolved by post, which is that it requires
simultaneous access to two sets of primary records held four thousand miles apart; the estimated
cost of a passage; and a proposal that fourteen days be spent at Harpenden in the February, when
the pens are quiet.

Paragraph five said that the writer proposed to put the matter to the estimates committee in the
November, and that he did not wish to do so without first establishing whether the dates were
convenient, since the whole value of the exercise depends upon the second party being in
residence and available for consultation throughout.

The question at the end was: *May I know whether you expect to be at Harpenden for the whole of
that period?*

Every sentence in it is true. He has read it since, twice, most recently in 1984, looking for the
place where it goes wrong, and there is not one. The fourth week had been outstanding for
twenty-one years; it was outstanding in the Institute's own paper; it could not be done by post;
and it was not done by post — it was not settled until the October of 1979 and not properly until
1984, and the fourteen days in Hertfordshire in the February of 1977 produced no advance on it
whatever, which he recorded at the time as a null result and entered as such.

He wrote it in the correct order and to the correct party and gave a month's notice and asked
whether the dates suited. It is, on paper, the most punctilious letter of his life. It has never
once occurred to him that a man who has not asked anybody for anything since 1966 sat down in the
October of 1976 and built, out of a genuine outstanding question, an airtight institutional
justification for going to Hertfordshire and being in the same house as Douglas Nairn for
fourteen days; nor that the machinery which produced it is the machinery which put the practice
of eating at one o'clock into a paragraph about a sluice in the March of 1972.

---

Nairn had it on the Thursday and read it at the desk and answered it in the afternoon —
*Yes. Whole period. Bring the daybooks* — and went home, and did not get to the bottom of it for
three months.

What defeated him was that it was correct. There was nothing to see. A senior man had identified
an outstanding question, costed a solution, applied to his committee in the proper season and
confirmed the availability of the other party before spending the Institute's money, which is what
a senior man does, and Douglas had received perhaps two hundred letters of that class in his
career and had written four hundred.

He read it eleven times.

In the January he filled in an application for a two-year appointment at St Augustine, which is
ten miles from the Martin, and told nobody, and could not at the time have said what had put it
into his head; and he has since worked out what put it into his head, and it was on the
Institute's paper in a numbered paragraph and he had it in a drawer four feet away while he wrote
the application.

The two of them, in the same three months, from opposite ends, each originated the thing under a
professional heading and neither knew he had done it. Douglas has had nine years to enjoy that
and has not managed it yet.

## Harpenden

The Institute would not let the 1956 daybooks out of the building. Reggie applied in writing, was
refused in writing on the grounds that they are unique and uncopied, accepted the refusal without
comment because it was correct, and made forty pages of notes by hand in the January which turned
out on the fourth day at Harpenden to be the wrong forty pages. The notebooks stayed in the west
store and were not opened again until the October of 1979.

He came on the eighth in the coldest February since 1963 and he had not brought a coat, because he
had costed the passage and the accommodation and the abstraction of the daybooks and had not
thought about the weather, which is not a lapse he would have made about anything in a pen.

Nairn's is a house in Harpenden with a long garden and a room upstairs that has been shut since
1958, and Reggie was in the room at the front, and there is a photograph on the landing which
neither of them mentioned on the first day or the second and which Reggie asked about on the third
and got the whole of: Alec, the school, the twelve years, the February, the fact that Douglas had
gone back to work on the Monday and that this had been correct and that he would do it again.

Reggie said, "Thank you."

"You can ask me anything about him. There's nothing in it that hurts now, and the bits that do,
I'd rather have out."

"I do not think I have another question at present. I may have one later."

He had one in the June, by letter, and it was a good one, and Nairn answered it in eleven pages.

---

They worked in the basement for eleven days and got nowhere and it was the best fourteen days
either of them had had since the war, and neither of them said so at any point.

The kitchen is where the second error happened, on the fourth day, over the closure of the western
pens.

*(Existing paragraph stands: the four-minute answer, the disposal of the animals, the Michaelmas
intake, the two members of staff, and not one word about how anybody felt.)*

What is worth adding is that Reggie enjoyed the question. He gave the fullest answer he was
capable of, at some length, was pleased to have been asked, and went to bed satisfied that he had
been useful; and Douglas washed up alone afterwards knowing that he had put a question to a man
who had answered it completely and truthfully and that he had learnt nothing at all and that this
was his own fault and not the man's. He fixed it in the March, and the fix was numbers, and it has
held for seven years.

## Aberdeen

It was Reggie who raised Aberdeen, on the second evening, and he raised it as an obligation.

Dr Leslie was three hundred miles off; he was in the country for a fortnight and would not be in
it again; she had done the analysis in Appendix C and he had put it in an appendix; and a man who
is within a day's travel of a colleague of twenty-three years' standing and does not call on her
has been discourteous, and there is no version of the arithmetic in which he has not.

"Right," said Douglas. "I'll come."

"There is no need whatever."

"There's every need. She's my correspondent as well and has been since seventy-three, and I've
cited her twice and never laid eyes on her, and if you think I'm letting the pair of you have the
third-week problem in a room in Aberdeen without me you've formed a very poor estimate of my
character." He was already looking for the timetable. "That's the reason. You needn't look for
another one."

Reggie did not look for another one.

---

They went up overnight on the fifteenth and came back on the seventeenth and it cost the fourth
week two days it could not spare.

Jean was forty-nine and had had the chair for two years and met them at the station, and she had
the whole of it inside four minutes, on the platform, before anybody had said anything about
anything.

It was not the way they stood. It was that Douglas Nairn said *ask him about the Michaelmas
figures, he'll tell you to the animal* — and did not look at Reggie while he said it, and did not
say it to be kind, and said it the way a man says *the file's in the second drawer.* Somebody had
believed her. She had put a postscript under her signature in the March of 1973, four lines, the
only unguarded thing she has ever committed to paper about that man, and she had put it in the
post to a stranger and had not expected anything to come of it, because nothing ever had.

She said nothing to Reggie. There is no form of words for it and she had had twenty years to look
for one.

She said one thing to Nairn, at the station on the seventeenth, while Reggie was buying a paper.

"You had my postscript, then."

"I did."

"Nobody ever has."

"I'd have got there," said Douglas. "Eventually. I'd have taken years about it." He watched
Reggie counting change at the kiosk. "You saved me two."

Jean looked at that for a moment and then said, "Well. Good," and shook his hand, and that is the
whole of it and neither of them ever referred to it again.

---

What she gave him, she gave him without knowing.

They had an hour on the second day while Reggie was with the departmental statistician, and she
was easy and glad and told two stories about 1955, and then, apropos of nothing, in the middle of
a sentence about the Appendix, she said that he was very much smaller than he had been.

Douglas said, "Since when."

"Since I sailed. April of fifty-seven." She stopped, and thought about it properly, because she is
also a scientist. "Not ill. Just — less of him. About a stone, I should think. Perhaps more."

He asked her three times.

Not in the same words. Whether she was certain of it. Then whether it might be the light and the
twenty years and the fact that a man in a Scottish February is wearing a good deal more than a man
at a rail in Trinidad. Then whether a stone was her estimate or her belief, and whether she would
put a figure on it if she were pressed.

Jean answered all three, in full, patiently, without once indicating that it had been asked
before, because she is courteous and because there was no reason on earth for her to find it
remarkable.

He wrote it in the notebook that night, under the count, with the date, on the page after Mrs
Ramnarine, and he wrote the date twice.

He has never put that hour alongside the twenty-six. The notebook has both in it, nine pages
apart, in the same hand. If anybody were to set them down on the same page he would have it in
about four seconds, and he would not enjoy it.

Two women, eleven months apart, neither of them consulted, gave him the two halves of it; and he
sat on both for a year, because a man of sixty who is told a thing twice by people with no reason
to invent it should establish the figure himself before he acts, and there was no scale at the
Martin he trusted and no reason yet to be in the room.

There was one in the March of 1978.

## What was on the table

He had the St Augustine application in the house the whole fortnight.

The papers were in the top drawer of the desk in the front room with the reference books, four
feet from where Reggie sat for eleven evenings, and he did not mention it, and his reason is his
reason and he would give it to anybody who asked. He does not report unconfirmed results. An
application in the January is not an appointment; if he had said it and it had come to nothing he
would have handed a man who does not discard anything a fact to keep for thirty years about a
thing that did not happen.

It is the same reasoning he applied to the chair at Reading in the September of 1982, and it was
wrong on that occasion for reasons he has since accepted and it was not wrong here, and the
difference between the two cases is one he can state and does not enjoy stating: in 1977 he was
withholding a thing that might not happen, and in 1982 he was withholding a thing that might.

---

What he did do, on the nineteenth, on the train coming south from Aberdeen, was ask.

He had four months of reasoning behind him about scale and about refusability and about questions
that spoil their own answers, and he abandoned the whole of it somewhere north of York for no
better cause, he says, than that he had been watching a man read a timetable for an hour; and he
put it badly, which he never does.

The cause was forty-eight hours old and he has never named it. He had been told, by a woman with
no motive whatever, that there was about a stone less of the man than there had been, and he had
made her say it three times, and there was no scale at the Martin he trusted and no reason yet to
be in the room, and he had thirteen hours on a train with the figure and nothing to do with it.

He was not being generous in that carriage. He was a man who had just been handed his own worst

"If I were out there. Not a fortnight. Living there, two years, ten miles off, in and out of the
house. Would you want that."

"Yes," Reggie said.

Douglas waited for the rest of it.

There was not any rest of it. There was a man looking at him with mild surprise at the length of
the question, having answered it in the first word, completely, accurately, without hedging,
because he had been asked.

"You might have said."

"I could not have said." He put the timetable down. "I can tell you now that I have wanted it
since the seventeenth of April last year and that I have known the date because I entered it. I
could not have raised it. If you require the mechanism again I will set it out, but I think you
have it."

"I've got it."

"Then you should also have this, since you have asked, and it is the same answer at a different
scale." The verbs went to the end of the sentence and stayed there. "Anything offered above the
present arrangement, I will take. I have looked for the point at which that would stop being true
and I have not found one. I did not say so because it was not on offer, and a man who reports a
preference for a thing that is not on offer has put a bill in front of
somebody who cannot pay it. That was the whole of the reason. It was not modesty and it was not
doubt of you."

Douglas sat with that as far as Doncaster.

Then he said, "I've had an application in since January," and told him the whole of it, and broke
his own rule about unconfirmed results, and would do it again, and has never once described it as
a mistake.
