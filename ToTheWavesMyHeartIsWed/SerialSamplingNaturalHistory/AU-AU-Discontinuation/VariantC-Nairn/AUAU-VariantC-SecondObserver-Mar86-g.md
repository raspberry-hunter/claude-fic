# Second Observer

## I

He came to the hotel at half past four and had himself sent up for, which meant the desk rang
and Douglas came down and there was no version of it where he could pretend to be out.

Reggie was at the laboratory with the committee until six, having a building shown to him room
by room, which he'd enjoy and would remember the whole of.

Pascual was standing by the window with his hat in his hand.

"I wouldn't have come if there were another way to do this," he said. "There isn't, and I've
been up since three, and I'd like about an hour."

"Aye," said Douglas, and didn't say anything else, because there was nothing in him at that
moment that would have come out well.

---

They stood there for a while and it felt considerably longer than it was.

"You'd like to hit me," said Pascual.

"I would."

"Yes. I had that last night at about half past nine and I didn't know what to do with it, and
I've decided the correct thing is to say so and then go on." He turned the hat round. "I'm not
going to argue with you about it. I've had sixteen years to find the account that gets me out of
it and I haven't found one. I wouldn't stand in front of a committee and defend what I did in
1966. I'd do it again, which isn't the same thing as defending it, and I want you to know that I
know the difference."

Douglas looked at him.

"That's a hard way to start."

"It's the only way to start. If I'm polite about it for twenty minutes you'll spend the twenty
minutes waiting for it, and I haven't the time and neither have you." Pascual put the hat on.
"Not here. I'd rather not do this in a hotel."

## II

He took him to the laboratory. Not the front of it, where Reggie was being walked round by the
committee, but the far end, down a covered way and in at a side door with a key of his own that
he'd evidently not yet given back.

It was a low white building with a corridor down the middle and the fans off. Wednesday
afternoon and there was nobody in it at all.

He put Douglas in a small office with a window on the yard, and Douglas sat down and looked
about him while the man found two glasses and a jug, and what he saw was this: a desk with
nothing on it, a shelf of offprints going back to about 1958 with the dust undisturbed on the
top row, a chair for a visitor, and a bench through the doorway with a cover on it and the cover
laid flat and square, the way a cover lies when nothing's been under it for some time.

He looked at the whole of it and he didn't understand one item.

## III

"I read you last night," said Pascual, sitting down. "I want to say that plainly rather than let
you find it out. I didn't decide to and I can't help it and I've never been able to help it.
Four seconds, at about half past nine, while I was asking you about the weighing.

"Most of what I got was obvious and I'll not insult you with it. You don't like me, you've
decided you've no right not to, and you were being extremely correct with me all evening at some
cost. That's not a feat of observation. Ramón had it too and Ramón isn't an observer."

"He's a better one than he lets on."

"He's a very good one and he's no idea. That's the best kind." Pascual poured. "What I couldn't
place was the other thing. There was something in you at that table that had nothing to do with
me and nothing to do with the evening, and it was old, and it was sitting somewhere it had been
sitting for years. I had it wrong twice in the night. I got it at about three."

"Get on with it, then."

"You're carrying an observation you've never been able to support." Pascual set the jug down.
"About him. One instance, no explanation, nothing on earth to measure it against and no way of
getting anything — and you're a man who doesn't build on a finding he can't support, which I
know because you gave me eleven words last night when you could have given me forty and thought
you were being kind."

Douglas sat very still.

"That's a fair distance to get in four seconds."

"It took four seconds to see there was something and six hours to work out what. Don't credit me
with more than I've got." Pascual looked at him. "I'm the only person alive who can give you the
other end of it. I've known that since about three o'clock. I've spent the time since then
deciding whether to come, and I haven't enjoyed it, and here I am."

---

Douglas took a drink and put the glass down and asked the question he'd promised himself since
Monday that he wouldn't ask, and asked it in the ugliest form available, because he was angry
and because he wanted to see whether the man would flinch.

"Are you over him?"

Pascual didn't flinch. He didn't even take a moment.

"No. I haven't been for twenty years and I don't expect to be and I've stopped expecting to be,
and it interferes with nothing. I've a man at home I wouldn't exchange for any other arrangement
on this earth, including the one you're thinking of, and I haven't once in sixteen years wished
I'd stayed. Every part of that's true at the same time. It isn't tidy and it isn't arranged for
anybody's convenience, least of all mine."

"That's a great deal of answer."

"You asked a question. I answer questions." He almost smiled and didn't quite. "You'll have
noticed that runs in the house."

Douglas found that he'd nowhere to put his hands.

"Right," he said. "Then I'll take my turn. I'm not sorry I asked and I'd ask it again."

"I'd think less of you if you were sorry."

## IV

"Was he always like that?" said Douglas. "The not starting. Was it always?"

"Yes. Since he was a boy, and he'll tell you so himself if you ask him, and he's told me, and he
describes it the way you'd describe a squint." Pascual turned his glass round. "That's the
answer to your question and it isn't the answer I came to give, and the one I came to give
begins by contradicting it."

"Then contradict it."

"Nairn. Before I say the next thing I want you to understand that I know exactly what it'll do
to you, and that I've spent since three o'clock looking for a way of handing it over that
doesn't go through that door, and there isn't one. There's no third kind of sentence. So I'll
say it badly and quickly, which is the only mercy available."

"Say it."

"In one department of his life it isn't a wall. It's a difficulty. That's the whole of the
difference and it's the largest difference there is, because a wall doesn't move and a
difficulty can be worked at."

The fan was off. Somewhere down the corridor a tap was going.

"You'll have him beginning things," said Pascual. "Saying what he wants, in order, unasked,
without hedging, arriving first. I know you will, because I had it too."

Douglas didn't say anything at all.

"He didn't have it in 1954. He didn't have it in 1955. For two years he began nothing — not
once, and I mean that in the strict sense, not once — and I didn't know how many times it was
until he told me afterwards, because he had the figure, of course he had the figure. Seventy
occasions. On every one of the seventy the first move was somebody else's, and on forty of them
he'd come up the stairs on purpose in order to be where it would be made.

"In the July of 1956 he did it.

"And then he could do it, and it didn't go away again."

Douglas started to say something and Pascual put a hand up.

"No. You're about to ask me what it cost him and what it was like and I'm not going to tell you,
tonight or ever, and I want the line drawn where I'm drawing it rather than have you find out
later where it was. That part's his. You may have it from him if you ask him, and he'll give you
the whole of it, in order, with the dates, because he gives everybody everything. You won't ask
him." Pascual put the hand back down. "Neither did I. I've the figures because he brought them
to me, and I've the date because I was in the house, and that's the whole of what's mine to hand
over."

"Aye," said Douglas, after a moment. "That's fair."

It was fair, and he found he wasn't as glad of it as he ought to have been, and he hasn't gone
back over that.

---

Douglas got up and stood at the window with his back to the room, which he hasn't done in front
of another man since 1958, and looked at a yard with two oil drums in it.

He'd like it recorded that his first thought wasn't a decent one. His first thought was *that is
mine,* and it went off in him like a dropped tray, and it was disgraceful and he's not going to
pretend it didn't happen.

The second one took a good deal longer and it was worse.

Eleven years he's had one observation and no way of using it. The third night at Kingston in
1975 and he didn't believe it, and again on the fourth and he did, and he's carried it since as
an anomaly he couldn't build on — one instance, one observer, no control, and no possibility
whatever of getting another, because the only man who could supply it doesn't know the answer
and would give him four decimal places of not knowing.

What he had was a reading with no zero on the instrument.

He has the zero now, and it's 1954, and the instrument was in another man's house for the two
years it took to come off the stop.

---

He turned round.

"You did nothing," he said. "Seventy times. That's what you're telling me."

"I did nothing seventy times. I never went at him about it. I never asked him why, I never
remarked on it, I never once let it become a subject in two years, and there were a good many
evenings when it would've been the easiest thing in the world to say something light about it
and I didn't." Pascual was looking at the desk. "And when he did it in the July I never said one
word about what it was. I'll not pretend I was silent — I said a good deal and I wasn't in any
condition not to. But I never told him he'd gone first, or that I'd been counting, or that
anything whatever had changed. Not that night and not since. I wanted to very much indeed and I
didn't, and that's the only part of the whole two years I've ever been certain I got right.

"I hadn't a theory. I wasn't being clever. I was forty-five and I was in love with him and I
didn't want to frighten him. That's the whole of my method and it isn't a method."

"It's a method." Douglas heard his own voice come out wrong and didn't correct it. "It's the
only kind that works and there's no writing it down."

He sat down again because his legs had opinions.

Seventy trials. One protocol, applied identically, no adverse event recorded, and a capability at
the end of it that he'd been told in writing in 1975 was not obtainable in that man by any means
whatever.

And Douglas has one of his own.

He's had it since about 1980, four years of it, and he's never been able to do a thing with it
because he'd nothing to set it beside — no earlier case, no protocol, and the standing account of
the man, given him by the man, saying the class was closed. He's carried it as a second anomaly
next to the first and hasn't been able to publish either of them to a living soul, including the
subject, who isn't allowed to hear it.

He now has two trials of the same thing, and the earlier one has a method behind it and a man in
the room who ran it.

He said the sentence out loud before he'd decided to, and he hasn't been able to unsay it since.

"You kept it open."

"I don't know that," said Pascual.

"I do. I've the arithmetic and you've not, and I'd give a good deal to be wrong and I'm not."

---

Neither of them said anything for a bit.

"I've told him twice in nine years that it's the best of him," said Douglas. "Eleven words, both
times. I meant it and I'd say it again tomorrow." He put his hands on his knees. "And now I know
where it came from, and I can't hand it back, and I can't stop knowing it."

"No. You can't. I'm sorry."

"Are you?"

Pascual took his time about it, which Douglas noticed, because the man's answered everything
else this week inside a second.

"Some of me is," he said.

---

It was Pascual who gave him the other half, about a minute later, and Douglas has thought since
that he didn't have to.

"It didn't transfer," he said. "I want you to have that as well, and I want it plainly, because
you're sitting there constructing something and it's wrong.

"It wasn't a thing he learnt once and carried about. It went with the house. Whatever he's got
with you he built with you, from nothing, at sixty and after, and I wasn't there and had no hand
in it and he'll have found it exactly as hard the second time." He shrugged, slightly. "What was
got at in my house wasn't the capability. It was that the capability could be got at at all.
That's what I gave him and it's smaller than you're making it and larger than I'm comfortable
with."

"That's foundations," said Douglas. "You've just told me you're the foundations and asked me to
find it a small thing."

"Yes," said Pascual. "I have. I'm aware of how that sounds and I haven't got a better way to put
it and I've had since three o'clock."

## V

The hour he'd asked for went at about a quarter past five. Neither of them remarked on it and
neither of them has since.

Douglas sat and looked at the cover on the bench for a while and then did a thing he hadn't
planned and can't fully account for, and which he's decided, on balance, that he's glad of.

"I'll give you my one," he said. "Not because I owe you it. Because you've just made it worth
something and you're the only man alive I can say it to."

"Go on."

"He told me in the March of 1975, in writing, before I'd ever laid eyes on him, not to expect the
position to improve with familiarity. He'd got fifty-eight years of it not improving and no
reason in the mechanism why it should, and he set it out the way he sets everything out and I
believed him, because he's never once been wrong in writing about anything else in eleven years.

"He was wrong. It's improved. It took from about 1980 to about 1984 and there's a dozen things he
does now that weren't available to him in seventy-five, and he found it out himself in the
January of eighty-four by going through nine years of his own file and counting, alone, on a
Tuesday, nine years late." He turned the glass round. "And here's the part I'd want in the paper.
The range has widened and the cost hasn't fallen. Not by anything I can measure. It's exactly as
dear at the eleventh time as it was at the first, and he's entered that himself and made no
attempt at the mechanism, there being none."

Pascual sat with that.

"Then they're the same thing," he said.

"They're the same thing. Two trials, different sites, thirty years apart, and I've spent thirty
years telling agronomists that two trials on different sites don't pool and I'm not going to
pretend otherwise in an office." Douglas put the glass down. "But do the division and see where
it comes out. You had two years. I've had four. And yours was on a man nobody could put a
question to about himself, and mine was on a man who'll answer anything you like inside a second
and hand you the file after. Mine's the easy site. I've had twice the ground and half the work
and I got the same result slower.

"So it isn't that you did well. It's that as far as I can tell you did the whole of it, and I've
been finishing something."

Pascual didn't say anything for a while.

"Why are you telling me that?"

"Because there's nobody I can say it to." Douglas heard how that came out and let it stand.
"It's a statement about him and not about the work, and that's a rule he made in November of
seventy-five and I've kept it for eleven years and I'll keep it till I'm dead. So the one thing
that's got better in that man's life is the single thing in it nobody's allowed to mention to
him, and I've been carrying it since 1980, and you're the first person I've ever been able to
hand it to.

"That's why. You can have whatever else you like out of it."

"I'll take the division," said Pascual, "and I'll not thank you for it, because you've just told
me it wasn't a kindness and I don't think you knew whether that was true when you said it."

"No," said Douglas. "I didn't."

That's not an anomaly. That's a finding, and it's been made in an empty laboratory in San Juan on
a Wednesday afternoon by a man he came four thousand miles wanting to hit.

## VI

Pascual sat with the division for a minute and a half and didn't drink anything.

"You've given me an improvement," he said, "and no baseline. I don't think you did it on purpose.
It doesn't signify whether you did."

Douglas didn't say anything.

"I'm going to ask you the thing you've just made unavoidable, and it's a good deal worse than the
last one, and you may refuse and I'd think none the less of you."

"Ask it."

"What was he like in 1975?"

Douglas sat with that for a moment. He'd like it on the record that he did think about refusing,
and that the thinking took about two seconds, and that what decided it wasn't charity.

"In his head he was in good order," he said. "That's first and it's the worst of it. There was no
misery in it anywhere. He was fifty-eight and running perfectly well and he'd have gone on
running perfectly well for another twenty years, and every man at that conference would have told
you he was the best of them and they'd all have been right.

"He was not in good order otherwise. And nobody was looking, and he'd not have noticed, and he'd
not have reported it if he had."

"Go on."

"He ate at one because the day said one, and half of it went back, and it went back all week and
nobody at the table remarked on it including me, because I'd not learnt to look yet. He'd nothing
at all after eight in the evening — that's not loneliness, it's that an evening has no heading on
it and never had, so it didn't exist. Nine years of that.

"I got a figure off Jean Leslie in the February of seventy-seven, which she gave me without
knowing she was giving me anything, and I'd two of my own by then, and I put them against what I
could remember of the Kingston week and what Ramnarine would tell me." He stopped. "I'll not give
you the arithmetic. You'll do it faster than I did and you'll get further."

Pascual did it.

He did it sitting down, with his hands on his knees, in an office with the fan off, and it took
him about four seconds, and Douglas watched the whole of it go across him and land.

He didn't say the number.

Douglas didn't ask him for it.

The tap was still going somewhere down the corridor.

"That's what I found," said Douglas. "And I'm not putting it to you as a bill. You'd have been
dead of it by 1972 and then he'd have had nothing at all and no letters either, and I've been
over it a good many times and there's no version where anybody comes out of that house whole. I
know that. It doesn't help me and I don't expect it to help you."

Pascual had both hands flat on his knees.

"That's worse than being hit," he said, "and you know it is, and I don't think you did it on
purpose, and I'm not certain."

"Neither am I," said Douglas.

## VII

Pascual sat for a while with his hands where they were. Then he took them off his knees and put
them on the arms of the chair, which is what a man does when he's getting up, and he didn't get
up.

"I'm not finished," he said. "I'd like to be.

"There's a date on the other side of what you've just given me, and I can give you that too, and
then I'm going to stop, because past that point I've nothing but opinions and I'm a veterinary
surgeon and an anatomist and I'll give you what I've seen and not one inch further.

"In February of 1955 he came apart. I'll not go into it; it's his and you may ask him and he'll
tell you the whole of it in about four minutes with the dates. What matters is that I sat on the
edge of a bed and I had his wrist and he said four words nobody had asked him for. He said: *it
has done it before.* And I asked when, because I ask things, and he said one word.

"He said Salisbury."

Douglas didn't move.

"He was there from 1946 to 1948. You'll know that; you ask him things. What I don't think you'll
have, because it isn't the sort of thing he'd supply and nobody would think to ask, is that he
was entirely alone for the whole of it. Not lonely — alone. No one in the house, no one outside
it, nothing of any kind, for two years and some months, and he's said so plainly when asked and
thought nothing of the saying.

"And in thirty-two years I've known him volunteer information about his own condition perhaps
three times, and every one of the three was when he was in pieces."

He put his glass down.

"That's the whole of what I've got. A date, an absence, and a thing that doesn't close. I haven't
joined them up and I'm not going to join them up in front of you, because if I do you'll take it
as a finding of mine and it isn't one. It's two observations and a name I've never opened and
don't intend to."

"You went in the room that far and stopped."

"I said *ah* and gave him a piece of bread." Pascual was looking at the desk. "I've had
thirty-one years on that four seconds and I don't know to this day whether I did the right thing.
I've decided a man who's coming apart is owed the thing he's asking for and he wasn't asking for
that. It may be I was frightened. Both are available and I can't separate them and I've stopped
trying."

---

Douglas joined them up. He couldn't help it, and he did it out loud, because he does everything
out loud, and he flagged it as he went, because that's the only discipline he's ever had.

"Then it's this, and it's not supported and I'll say so before I say it. Something shut in him
between forty-six and forty-eight and shut hard. Not made — he had it from a boy, you've said
so — but set. Turned from a squint into a wall." He rubbed his face. "And whatever the mechanism
is, it can only close over what's in the building at the time. Salisbury: nothing in the
building, two years, and it shut. Your house: something in the building, two years, and it came
off the stop. Sixty-six to seventy-five: nothing in the building, nine years, and you've just
heard what that came to." He put the glass down. "That's the whole of my great theory and it's
got no more standing than yours."

"It's got exactly the standing of three observations and no mechanism," said Pascual. "Which is
what mine's got. I've had thirty-one years on mine. You've had an afternoon, and you've got
further with it than I have, and I want you to notice that doesn't make it true."

"It's more than I had at ten o'clock this morning." Douglas let out a breath. "Eleven years I've
had one instance sat in a drawer. I couldn't build on it and I couldn't put it down and I
couldn't ask."

"No," said Pascual. "He doesn't know the answer and he'd give you four decimal places of not
knowing, and he'd be entirely willing, and you'd come away with less than you went in with. I had
eleven years of that too. I had twelve."

---

Neither of them said anything for a bit.

"Does he know any of this?" said Douglas.

"No. Not one part of it. He's no idea there's a discontinuity, and if you told him he'd enter it
as a fact about himself supplied by two people who'd compared notes about him in a room he wasn't
in, and he'd thank you for it, and he'd be perfectly all right, and it would sit in him for the
rest of his life doing something neither of us can predict."

"So it stops here."

"It stops here. I've never said it and I'm not going to say it again, and I've no intention of
telling Ramón, who's entitled to everything and isn't entitled to this."

"Aye." Douglas looked at his hands. "It stops here."

He's kept to that. He's never mentioned it, in the aeroplane or since, in nine years of Fridays,
and he doesn't expect to.

## VIII

Then Douglas did the thing he came to Puerto Rico with no intention of doing, and he did it
because there was no longer any reason on earth not to, and because it was the only thing he had
that the man wanted.

He gave him the whole of the apparatus. Standing up, at the window, for about twenty minutes.

The card against the lamp, and what a card's for, and what a start time does that a date doesn't.
The headings. The Fridays. The fortnight in the summer and the fortnight at Christmas and the
eleven years of letters and what's in them and what isn't. The lunch, and Mrs Ramnarine in the
corridor on the eleventh day in 1977, and what he did with that two years later. The platform
scale in the medical room and the column under it going back to 1978, and the blood pressure
twice a year since the October of 1983 and the second figure sent unasked. That he asks questions
with numbers for answers because a number's a thing the man can produce and an adjective isn't.
That none of it requires any gift whatever, that anybody could run it who was prepared to keep
the paper, and that this is the entire reason it's lasted and will go on lasting.

Pascual didn't interrupt once. He asked two questions in twenty minutes and both of them were
about the platform scale.

At the end of it he said, "Thank you," and then, after a moment, "That's better than what I had."

"It's not better. It's cheaper. That's a different thing and it's the only reason it works."

"No," said Pascual. "It's better, and I've been a professional man for fifty-one years and I know
the difference between a thing that's better and a thing I'd like to be worse. Yours has no
genius in it. Mine had nothing else, and it was gone in twelve years and there was no relief in it
anywhere because none could be built. Yours will still be running when you're eighty and I'm
dead."

Douglas looked out of the window at the two oil drums.

"I'd not have got there without Alec," he said, which wasn't an answer and was the nearest he
could come to one.

---

"I want to ask you one thing more," said Pascual, "and you may refuse and I'll not raise it
again."

"Go on."

"Is he happy?"

"Aye," said Douglas. "He is."

It came out flat and it came out at once, and he watched it go into the man like a nail.

He'd asked his own at half past four to make the man flinch, and had been answered in full, in
order, with the inconvenient parts left in. The man asked his at a quarter to six with his hat on
the desk and got the same thing back. Douglas didn't notice at the time that the pair of them had
done exactly the same thing, and he hasn't been able to stop noticing it since.

"I'll give you the grounds, because you'll want them and because I don't put a thing that size
down without them. Sleeps six and says so. Finishes his lunch, which he didn't do when I met him
and has done since about 1978. Nine stone one and steady three years. Goes out on the water with
Ramnarine's boy on a Saturday for no reason at all, which is a thing he'd not have done for any
money in 1975 because there was nothing in the day about it." He turned the glass. "And one
that's not a number. He talks. Not the way you mean — he can't start a thing off and he never
will. But he'll come in from a building he's been walked round and give you eleven minutes on a
seawater intake nobody asked him for, and he does it three or four times a week, and he's no idea
he does it, and I've never told him."

Pascual sat back and didn't say anything for what must have been fifteen seconds.

"Thank you," he said. "I've wanted that for twenty years and there's been no way on earth of
getting it for sixteen."

"It cost me nothing," said Douglas. "I'll have that plain, because you're building me into
something and I'll not have it. I like being asked. I like there being an answer and I like being
the reason there is one, and I'd have told you so on the pavement on Monday if you'd asked me on
the pavement on Monday."

"I know," said Pascual. "That's the cruel part, and I don't think you can help it, and I'm not
sure you would if you could."

He turned his glass round once and put it down.

"I want to be exact about what's just happened to me, because you've been exact with me all
afternoon and it would be poor manners not to. In 1966 I went without knowing whether he'd be all
right. That's the whole of what I took with me. I had something for three years afterwards and
it's not mine to explain and you'll not ask me about it, and it stopped in the March of 1970.

"And since 1970 it's been four paragraphs on a sluice, four times a year. He asks and I answer.
That's the arrangement, and I built it, and I've no complaint to make about it to you or anybody
else. But there's no question in the world I could've put into a letter that would have got me
what you've just given me in one word, and there never was, and I'd worked that out by about
1972.

"So don't tell me it was free. It wasn't free, and the bill didn't come to you."

## IX

They walked back out through the corridor and Pascual locked the side door with the key he hasn't
given back.

On the step, Douglas said: "I still want to hit you."

"I know."

"It's worse than it was this morning, and that's not fair, and I'm telling you because you'd get
it off my face anyway and I'd rather say it than be read."

"That's the most courteous thing anybody's said to me this week," said Pascual, and he meant it,
and Douglas could hear that he meant it, and that was the worst part of the whole afternoon.

"Then how do we get through tomorrow?"

"You'll be at Ramón's pharmacy losing money at dominoes, and I'll be at home, and the two of us
won't be in a room together, and I understand that's been arranged for us by two men in a
corridor and that neither of us was consulted." Pascual put his hat on. "I've decided to be
grateful for it and I recommend it."

---

Douglas walked back to the hotel the long way and got there at ten past six, four minutes before
Reggie, who came in from the laboratory with the whole floor plan in his head and gave him eleven
minutes on the seawater intake without being asked.

He had a zero in his coat pocket, and a second trial to set beside his own, and nowhere in the
world to put either of them.

---

That was the Wednesday.

On the Thursday evening at eight o'clock, with the shutter halfway down and his hand on it, Ramón
Vidal said that in sixteen years the man had never once had anything hard to do.

Douglas had a bottle in his hand. He put it down on the crate.

A desk with nothing on it. A shelf of offprints stopping in 1958 with the dust undisturbed along
the top row. A cover on a bench, laid flat and square, the way a cover lies when nothing's been
under it for some time. A side door with a key in it that a man hasn't given back and has no
further use for, on a Wednesday afternoon, in an empty building, which was the only place in San
Juan he could think of to take somebody he didn't want to talk to in a hotel.

Douglas had walked the length of it and sat in it for an hour and a half and looked at every item
in the room, and had understood none of it, and had spent the whole of that hour and a half being
told about somebody else.

He didn't say so to Ramón. He hasn't said so to anybody.

He's never told Reggie about the Wednesday. Reggie has told him a great deal about the building.
