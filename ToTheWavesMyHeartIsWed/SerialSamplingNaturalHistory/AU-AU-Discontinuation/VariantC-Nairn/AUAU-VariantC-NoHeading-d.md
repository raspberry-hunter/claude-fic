# No Heading

## I

He tried to file it in the week after and could not, and the attempt is worth setting down
because it is the only occasion in fifty years on which the system has been offered something and
declined it.

He constructed three headings and rejected all three.

*Evening: Nairn. From half past seven* was wrong because that heading covers a state, and a state
runs from a start time to a finish, and what had happened at ten past eleven on a Tuesday was an
event inside it and not the thing itself. He does not file events under states. It produces a
class of error he has seen in laboratory records all his life and he was not going to introduce
it into his own.

The medical file was wrong because it is not about anybody's condition. He got as far as
considering it, which tells him something he has not pursued.

The 1966 file was the nearest and he thought about it for two days, and it is wrong because that
file contains findings and this is not a finding. It is an act. There is no provision anywhere in
the system for an act with no result attached to it and no procedure behind it, and he went
through the whole cabinet twice, properly, in the way he would go through it for a junior who had
brought him something that would not classify.

So it went nowhere and has stayed nowhere.

It is the second object in his life without a heading over it. The first is a chair.

## II

The larger difficulty was the four seconds.

He went back over the evening a number of times in the January and the February with the
intention of establishing the method, since a result you cannot reproduce is of no use to
anybody, and what he established is that there is not one. There is a gap of about four seconds
between getting up and being at the other end of the table in which nothing was decided, and he
has looked in it repeatedly and there is nothing in it to find.

He was therefore in the position of a man with a single observation, no protocol, and no account
of the mechanism.

He extracted the conditions as far as they could be extracted. Douglas had had his face in his
hands. The field had been described. It was ten past eleven and there had been an interval before
it in which neither of them had said anything.

Two of those require the man to be in distress.

He wrote that down and then sat for some time with the sheet in front of him, and what he
concluded, and entered, is that a protocol which requires the other party to be in distress
before it can be run is not a protocol, it is a fault, and that he was not going to construct one
and would not have submitted it to a committee, and that this therefore closes the direct route
altogether.

He then wrote four pages on the general problem of an experiment in which the observation and the
intervention are the same operation, which is a real difficulty and a serious one and has a
literature, and he was extremely interested in it for about a fortnight, and there is not one
word in any of the four pages about Douglas Nairn.

## III

Fourteen months, in which nothing happened and he was not in any distress about it.

The position had been dictated to him in December and he had said it back and entered it in those
terms: not asked for, not owed, its not recurring is not a loss. He holds it. He would produce it
verbatim to anybody who asked and it has never once occurred to him to reopen it.

What he did do, in about the March of 1981, was form a view.

He entered it: *N. would prefer it to occur again.* He marked it unverified, and the marking is
correct and he is right to have made it, and the fact that the finding is of the same class as
the one he entered in the September of 1966 has never presented itself to him, because he does
not read his own files for pattern any more than a man reads his own hand for pattern.

The only verification available was to do the thing.

He noticed that. He found it a great deal more interesting than he found anything else that
spring: that here, for the first time in a working life spent keeping the two apart, the
experiment and the act were the same object, so that a man cannot obtain the reading without
having already committed the intervention, and there is no design that gets round it and he tried
four.

## IV

The attempt was in the October of 1981 and it failed, and there was nothing whatever wrong with
the evening.

That is the part he did not expect. He had assumed, without ever putting it in words, that there
would be an occasion. There was no occasion. Douglas was at the far end of the table with a
requisition and a pencil doing the thing he does out loud, and it was about twenty past nine, and
Reggie got up.

He was a foot away when the hand went onto the back of the chair.

He stood there with his hand on the chair-back for a period he did not time, and then went on to
the shelf and took down a volume he did not want, and came back the other way, and sat down.

He has no account of it. He has been over it since and there is nothing in it and it is the
companion piece to the four seconds: one gap in which the thing happened and one in which it did
not, and neither of them contains a reason.

Douglas did not look up.

He went on with the requisition, out loud, in the same voice, through the whole of it, and did
not turn his head at any point, and stayed exactly where he was for a good deal longer than the
requisition needed; and when he finally did move it was to ask a question about the eastern
drainage, which he did not need the answer to and had asked in the August.

That is one of the more useful things anybody has ever done for Reggie Tar, and Reggie Tar does
not know it happened.

## V

The second attempt was on the ninth of June 1982 and it did not fail.

It was an ordinary Wednesday. Douglas was at the table with a set of figures, out loud, arguing
with a man in Reading who was not present. Reggie crossed the room for something and came back
and put his hand on the back of his neck and left it there.

Douglas stopped in the middle of the sentence.

Then he went on with the same sentence, from the same place, without turning round, and finished
the paragraph, and read out three figures, and got one of them wrong.

His ears went scarlet. He was grinning at the requisition and knew that he was grinning and let
it stand, and did not say one word about any of it, then or afterwards, and has not said one word
about any of it since.

Reggie had his verification.

It is the only finding he has ever confirmed without asking a question, and the reason he could
confirm it is that the man's face does the whole of it and always has, and never at any point in
nine years has it been difficult.

He had it on the first evening at Kingston, in a room with sixty people in it. What the April
fortnight gave him was not the faculty but the verification: fourteen days in which he could read
the face at breakfast and find out by the evening whether he had been right. He ran it for the
whole fortnight, said nothing to anybody, and it held every time, and only then did he enter it.
He does not count a reading as a finding until it has been checked against something confirmable.
A face is not confirmable. A fortnight is.

## VI

It has never become easy.

There is an interval, before each one, of three or four seconds, in which he is aware that he is
going to and nothing whatever is happening. It is not reluctance. There is no argument going on;
he has established that and looked for it twice. It is simply a period in which the operation has
been initiated and has not yet begun to run, and it is exactly as long now as it was in 1982.

He told Nairn in the March of 1975 not to expect the position to improve with familiarity, on the
grounds that it had not improved in fifty-eight years and there was no reason in the mechanism
why it should.

He was wrong. It is one of the very few things he has been wrong about in writing, and he
established it himself, in the January of 1984, by the only method available to him, which was to
go through nine years of the file and count what was in it. The class has widened a great deal.
There are a dozen operations he performs now that were not available to him in 1975, and he has
the list, and it is accurate.

What has not moved at all is the four seconds. The range has grown and the cost per instance has
not fallen by any amount he can measure, and he entered both findings on the same sheet and did
not attempt the mechanism, there being none available.

Nobody has ever told him that he is easier than he was. Douglas has had it since about 1980 and
cannot say it, because it is a statement about the man and not about the work, and that rule was
established in the November of 1975 and has not been broken since. So the one improvement in
Reggie Tar's life is the single thing in it that cannot be remarked on, and he had to go and find
it in a cabinet himself, nine years late, on a Tuesday, alone.

It runs at perhaps five or six a year. He does not know the figure.

That is the item. He has the count of the twenty-six and the date of the Kingston paper and the
figure Mrs Bevan read off a Salter in 1969 and the number of animals in every trial he has ever
run, and he cannot tell you within three how many times he has crossed a room, and there is no
sheet anywhere with it on.

## VII

There is a thing his hands do and it does not go in either column.

It has been going on since the first fortnight and probably since Kingston. Reggie is beside him
at a table or handing him something or standing at his shoulder over a plot, and there is contact
of the ordinary kind that occurs between two people who are working, and Douglas's hand closes.

Not every time. Not at any interval Reggie has been able to establish and he has not tried very
hard to establish one. It lasts perhaps a second and a half and there is nothing said before it
or after it and it does not interrupt whatever he is saying, which is usually about drainage.

It is not involuntary. A man does not take hold of somebody's hand without any part of him being
involved. But it is not considered either, and that is the whole of the difficulty: it is a thing
Douglas Nairn does with his hands while the rest of him is occupied elsewhere, and he has never
once put it under examination, because it has never presented itself as a thing requiring
examination.

He has therefore never tested it against the November rule.

If it were put to him he would think for a moment and say that it was not a statement about
anybody, and he would be correct, and he would be a little surprised to have been asked; and he
would not in fifty years have got there on his own, because the question does not arise for a man
who is not attending.

---

What Reggie has is that a reply was made.

He tried twice in 1983 to render it into a proposition, in the way he renders everything into a
proposition, and both attempts failed. The first produced four candidate readings of which none
could be preferred on the evidence. The second produced a sheet on which he wrote out the
problem, which is that the object has no propositional content whatever, and that a man supplying
one would be supplying it entirely himself and would then have to treat his own manufacture as
data.

He abandoned the effort, which was correct, and he entered the abandonment, which is the nearest
he can come to leaving a thing alone.

So it is held as: *a reply was made.* Not to what. Not saying what. He does not know and has
accepted that he is not going to, and it is the only object in fifty years that has gone into him
without first being converted into a finding, and that is a larger concession than crossing a
room and neither of them has any idea that it is.

---

On the ninth of June 1982 it happened in answer to something he had started himself, for the
first time, about forty seconds afterwards, over a set of figures.

Douglas did not notice.

Reggie did.

## VIII

Douglas stopped writing them down after the second one.

The first is in the notebook — *ix.vi.82* and nothing else, no comment against it — and there is
nothing after that, in nine years of a man who has written down the day Mrs Ramnarine remarked on
a plate of lunch and the hour Jean Leslie said *about a stone* and twenty-six dates in the back
of a field book from the January of 1976.

His reasoning is available and he has never had to give it. A man with a list is a man who has
noticed the gap between entries, and sooner or later a man who has noticed the gap says
something, and it need not be a reproach, it need only be a remark, and the remark would be
reasonable and mild and completely fatal.

So there is no list. There is nothing to look at and no arithmetic to do and no bad month to
identify afterwards, and he has arranged it that way deliberately and would tell anybody who
asked, and nobody has.

He has one thing he keeps that is not written anywhere.

He can see the four seconds. He has been able to see them since the second one and he can see
them from across a room and with his back turned and he can see them coming a moment before they
start, and he has never once said so, and he will not, and it is the third finding he holds
without stating and the only one of the three that is not about damage.

## IX

There is a chair at the end of the table which has had a man in it since the April of 1976, and
there is an act which has no place in a cabinet that holds thirty years of everything.

Neither has a heading over it. They are the only two.

Both are about the same person, and Reggie has never set them side by side, and if he did he
would have the whole of it in about four seconds, which is the length of time everything about
this appears to take.
