# The Twelve-Acre

It was a Tuesday in the December of 1979 and there was nothing in the evening. The fourth week
had been in a better condition since the October and the pens were quiet and Douglas had come at
half past seven as he does, and they had eaten, and there was a plot on the table that neither of
them had any real business with.

Reggie said: "On the thirteenth of April 1976, in the kitchen, you said that four words were a
damn sight more than most men get, and that you had had the other kind. I have not asked what you
meant by it. I should like to now, if it is available."

Douglas looked up.

"Three years."

"Three years and eight months."

"Aye. You'd have that." He put the pencil down. "It's available. It'll take a while and you'll not
like the end of it."

"I did not suppose I should."

"Right," said Douglas. "Well. You'll not get it in the right order if I go at it sideways, so I'll
describe the trial first and then tell you what came out of it, which is the only way I know how
to do anything."

## The field

He was nineteen in the autumn of 1939 and in his third year at the college in Edinburgh, and
they took the lot of them out of it in the October and put them on the county committees, because
the country had four months of wheat and a policy about grassland and nobody to walk the farms
but boys.

His job was the ploughing-up. He had a bicycle and a schedule and a book of forms, and he went
round telling men of forty and fifty which of their fields were going under the plough that
season, and they had to do it, and if they did not do it the committee could take the tenancy off
them. He was nineteen. He had never grown anything in his life that was not in a pot.

The farm was above Ceres, six hundred acres, a tenancy. The tenant was Robert Strachan, called
Rab, who was twenty-seven and had had it since his father died and had his mother and two sisters
in the house.

The field was the twelve-acre on the north side and it had been in permanent grass since before
anybody could say.

"I had it down for wheat," said Douglas. "It was on my schedule and my schedule had come off a
map and a soil class, and I'd never set foot on it. He stood in the gateway and told me it was
stones under nine inches and it would break a plough and it would grow nothing, and I told him
that was what every man in the county had said to me that fortnight and that half of them were
lying and the other half were wrong."

"Was he lying?"

"He was not. He was right and I was wrong and it took me two years to know it. It went under in
the November and it gave nothing worth carting in forty, forty-one nor forty-two, and it cost him
a share and a fortnight of two horses he could not spare, and he never once said a word to me
about it. Not one. Three years."

He turned the pencil over.

"That's where I got the sentence. The one I say to agronomists and said to you the second night
in Kingston. *You cannot analyse a trial you've not described.* I've been saying it for forty
years and I've let men think I had it out of Fisher. I had it off a twelve-acre park above Ceres
and a man in a gateway in the October of 1939 who was correct."

## The three years

"It started after the argument. I'll not do the how of it; there's nothing in that anybody hasn't
had. There was a barn and there was rain and I'd have been out on the bicycle another two hours.
That's the whole of the mechanism."

He said it went on from the December of 1939 until he went in the spring of 1942. He was up there
perhaps forty times a year on committee business, which was genuine business, and there was never
any difficulty about a reason to be there. He was nineteen and then twenty and then twenty-one.
Rab was twenty-seven and then thirty.

"Now here's the part you asked for.

"He never said anything. Not once. Not a word about it in three years — not that he wanted it,
not that he didn't, not what it was, nothing. He wasn't cold. He'd have taken the coat off his
back for me and did, twice. He'd talk the hind leg off you about drainage. But there was not one
sentence in the whole three years that touched the thing itself, and there was none coming, and I
knew that by about the February of forty.

"So I'm twenty years old and I've got a set of readings and no description. Every single thing I
had, I'd inferred. Six weeks would go by and there'd be nothing and I'd go up and there'd be
nothing again and I'd come away and work out on the bicycle what it meant, and then the next time
there would be, and I'd work out what *that* meant. And I could not check any of it. Not one item.
There was nobody to ask and there was no form of words if there had been, and if I'd found the
words he'd not have used them, and if I'd pressed him I'd have finished it, and I knew that, so I
didn't press."

"That was correct," Reggie said.

"It was correct and it was three years and it was the whole of my twenties," said Douglas, "and I
came out of it not knowing one certain thing about it, and I've not got any of it since, and I'm
not going to."

He was engaged the whole time. A girl in Cupar, Nettie Bruce, whom Douglas knew and liked and
still could not tell you one bad thing about. It was not a secret and it was not a deceit in any
way that Douglas could have named at the time or has been able to name since. It was simply the
other thing that was also true.

## Forty-two

"He went in the March of forty-two."

Reggie said, "He was not called."

"He was not. Agriculture, tenancy, sole man on six hundred acres — he'd have sat the whole war
out in that steading and no man in Fife would have said a word. He went to the recruiting office
in Cupar on a Tuesday and put his name down for the Black Watch and came back and told his mother
at the tea."

He stopped.

"That's the question," he said. "That's the one. Thirty-seven years I've had it and there's no
answer in it. Did he go because of me? Because of what it was, or because he couldn't have it, or
because he wanted rid of it, or to make it stop, or because he'd have gone anyway and it had
nothing whatever to do with me and I've flattered myself for thirty-seven years? Any of those
fits. They all fit. There's no data. He never said and there was nobody to ask and the man's been
dead since forty-four."

He married Nettie on the embarkation leave, in the July of 1942, and Douglas was at the wedding
in the kirk at Ceres, because you would be. He shook the man's hand in the porch. He has been
over that handshake more often than he will say and there is nothing in it, and there was never
going to be anything in it, and he knows that and goes over it anyway.

Killed in Normandy in the August of 1944, near Saint-Aignan, with the Highland Division.

Douglas heard in the October, in a corn merchant's office in Cupar, from a man who mentioned it
between two other pieces of news because it was not news by then and because there was no reason
on earth for it to be told to Douglas Nairn particularly.

"I wasn't entitled to be told," he said. "That's not a complaint. I want to be exact. There was
no arrangement under which anybody would have written to me, and I'd not have wanted there to be,
because of what it would have meant about Nettie. I'd have designed it the same way. It's only
that I found out beside a weighbridge from a man selling barley and I said *oh aye, that's a
shame,* and I went out and got on the bicycle."

He came south in the spring of 1945 and has been in Hertfordshire since, and met Alec in the
following year, and that is a different account entirely and has already been given.

## What Reggie asked

There was a period after that in which neither of them said anything, and it was not
uncomfortable, and Reggie did not attempt to fill it, having no procedure for filling it and
having also formed the view some years earlier that the attempt is what does the damage.

Then he said:

"Did you tell him he was right about the field?"

Douglas put both hands over his face, which he does.

He was a long time.

"No," he said. "I knew by the harvest of forty-one. I had the yields off the whole parish and I
had his and I'd have had to go up there with the sheet and say *you told me in a gateway and I
was nineteen and I put it under and I was wrong,* and I did not, and I had the whole of the
winter of forty-one to do it in and I did not do it, and then he was away in the March, and there
was no address I could send a thing like that to that a wife wouldn't open."

"You had until the March."

"I had until the March."

"That is the item," Reggie said. "Not the enlistment. That has no answer in it and you have
established that, and a man who goes on interrogating a question that has been shown to be empty
is not being conscientious. The field is not empty. The field has an answer and you know it and
the answer is that he was right, and the only defect is that it was never transmitted."

"Aye," said Douglas, into his hands. "That's it. That's what it is."

"It is a fault in transmission and not in the finding."

"Aye."

"You are, I think, the only man of my acquaintance who would consider that a small distinction,"
Reggie said, "and it is not a small distinction, and you would not permit anybody else to treat
it as one."

## The one instance

Reggie got up.

He would not afterwards be able to give an account of the interval between getting up and being
at the other end of the table, and he has looked for one; there is a gap of about four seconds in
which nothing was decided, and he has been over it a number of times, and there is nothing in it
to find.

He put his hand on the back of Douglas's neck and left it there.

He did not say anything, because there was nothing available and he was not going to manufacture
something; and he did not take it away for a considerable time, and he could not have said how
long, and afterwards it was ten past eleven.

Douglas did not move at all for the first minute.

Then he said, from behind his hands, in a voice with nothing much left in it: "You'll not do that
again."

"I do not know whether I shall be able to."

"No. That's what I meant." He came out from behind his hands and his face was wrecked and he did
not do anything about it, being a man who does everything openly. "I'm not asking for it. I want
that said out loud and in the room, because you'll go and put it in a file and I'd rather you
filed the right thing. I'm not asking for it, I'll not ask for it, and if it never happens again
as long as I live that's not a loss, that's the arrangement, and the arrangement's the best thing
that ever happened to me."

"Very well."

"Say it back."

"You are not asking for it. It is not owed. Its not recurring is not a loss." He considered.
"That is a proper statement of the position and I shall enter it in those terms."

"Good."

He has one instance.

He does not generalise from it, which would be indefensible at n=1 and he knows it. He does not
conclude that the man will reach; he concludes what the arithmetic permits, which is that the
class is not empty, and that is a different finding and a much smaller one, and he keeps it as
exactly what it is and has not inflated it once in five years.

He has never mentioned it. Not to Reggie, not to anybody, and there is nobody to mention it to.

It is in the field notebook, on the page after Jean, with the date, and there is no comment
against it.
