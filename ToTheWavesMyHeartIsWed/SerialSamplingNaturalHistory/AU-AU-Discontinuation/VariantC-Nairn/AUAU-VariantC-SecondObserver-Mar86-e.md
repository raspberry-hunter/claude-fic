# Second Observer

He came to the hotel at half past four and had himself sent up for, which meant the desk rang
and Douglas came down and there was no version of it where he could pretend to be out.

Reggie was at the laboratory with the committee until six, having a building shown to him room
by room, which he would enjoy and would remember the whole of.

Pascual was standing by the window with his hat in his hand.

"I would not have come if there were another way to do this," he said. "There is not, and I have
been up since three, and I would like about an hour."

"Aye," said Douglas, and did not say anything else, because there was nothing in him at that
moment that would have come out well.

---

They stood there for what was probably four seconds and felt considerably longer.

"You would like to hit me," said Pascual.

"I would."

"Yes. I had that last night at about a quarter past nine and I did not know what to do with it,
and I have decided that the correct thing is to say so and then go on." He turned the hat round.
"I am not going to argue with you about it. I have had sixteen years to find the account that
gets me out of it and I have not found one. I would not stand in front of a committee and defend
what I did in 1966. I would do it again, which is not the same thing as defending it, and I want
you to know that I know the difference."

Douglas looked at him.

"That's a hard way to start."

"It is the only way to start. If I am polite about it for twenty minutes you will spend the
twenty minutes waiting for it, and I have not the time and neither have you." Pascual put the
hat on. "Not here. I would rather not do this in a hotel."

---

He took him to the laboratory. Not the front of it, where Reggie was being walked round by the
committee, but the far end, down a covered way and in at a side door with a key of his own that
he had evidently not yet given back.

It was a low white building with a corridor down the middle and the fans off. Wednesday
afternoon and there was nobody in it at all.

He put Douglas in a small office with a window on the yard, and Douglas sat down and looked
about him while the man found two glasses and a jug, and what he saw was this: a desk with
nothing on it, a shelf of offprints going back to about 1958 with the dust undisturbed on the
top row, a chair for a visitor, and a bench through the doorway with a cover on it and the cover
laid flat and square, the way a cover lies when nothing has been under it for some time.

He did not know on the Wednesday what he was looking at. He worked that out on the Thursday
evening at a shutter in Santurce, and when he did he was standing in a doorway with a bottle in
his hand and had to put it down.

---

"I read you last night," said Pascual, sitting down. "I want to say that plainly rather than let
you find it out. I did not decide to and I cannot help it and I have never been able to help it.
Four seconds, at about a quarter past nine, while I was asking you about the weighing.

"Most of what I got was obvious and I will not insult you with it. You do not like me, you have
decided you have no right not to, and you were being extremely correct with me all evening at
some cost. That is not a feat of observation. Ramón had it too and Ramón is not an observer."

"He's a better one than he lets on."

"He is a very good one and he has no idea. That is the best kind." Pascual poured. "What I could
not place was the other thing. There was something in you at that table that had nothing to do
with me and nothing to do with the evening, and it was old, and it was sitting somewhere it had
been sitting for years. I had it wrong twice in the night. I got it at about three."

"Get on with it, then."

"You are carrying an observation you have never been able to support." Pascual set the jug down.
"About him. One instance, no explanation, nothing on earth to measure it against and no way of
getting anything — and you are a man who does not build on a finding he cannot support, which I know because you gave me eleven
words last night when you could have given me forty and thought you were being kind."

Douglas sat very still.

"That's a fair distance to get in four seconds."

"It took four seconds to see there was something and six hours to work out what. Do not credit
me with more than I have." Pascual looked at him. "I am the only person alive who can give you the
other end of it. I have known that since about three o'clock. I have spent the time since then
deciding whether to come, and I have not enjoyed it, and here I am."

---

Douglas took a drink and put the glass down and asked the question he had promised himself since
Monday that he would not ask, and asked it in the ugliest form available, because he was angry
and because he wanted to see whether the man would flinch.

"Are you over him?"

Pascual did not flinch. He did not even take a moment.

"No. I have not been for twenty years and I do not expect to be and I have stopped expecting to
be, and it interferes with nothing. I have a man at home I would not exchange for any other
arrangement on this earth, including the one you are thinking of, and I have not once in sixteen
years wished I had stayed. Every part of that is true at the same time. It is not tidy and it is
not arranged for anybody's convenience, least of all mine."

"That's a great deal of answer."

"You asked a question. I answer questions." He almost smiled and did not quite. "You will have
noticed that runs in the house."

Douglas found that he had nowhere to put his hands.

"Right," he said. "Then I'll take my turn. I'm not sorry I asked and I'd ask it again."

"I would think less of you if you were sorry."

---

"Was he always like that?" said Douglas. "The not starting. Was it always?"

"Yes. Since he was a boy, and he will tell you so himself if you ask him, and he has told me, and
he describes it the way you would describe a squint." Pascual turned his glass round. "That is
the answer to your question and it is not the answer I came to give, and the one I came to give
begins by contradicting it."

"Then contradict it."

"Nairn. Before I say the next thing I want you to understand that I know exactly what it will do
to you, and that I have spent since three o'clock looking for a way of handing it over that does
not go through that door, and there is not one. There is no third kind of sentence. So I shall
say it badly and quickly, which is the only mercy available."

"Say it."

"In one department of his life it is not a wall. It is a difficulty. That is the whole of the
difference and it is the largest difference there is, because a wall does not move and a
difficulty can be worked at."

The fan was off. Somewhere down the corridor a tap was going.

"You will have him beginning things," said Pascual. "Saying what he wants, in order, unasked,
without hedging, arriving first. I know you will, because I had it too."

Douglas did not say anything at all.

"He did not have it in 1954. He did not have it in 1955. For two years he began nothing — not
once, and I mean that in the strict sense, not once — and I did not know how many times it was
until he told me afterwards, because he had the figure, of course he had the figure. Seventy
occasions. On every one of the seventy the first move was somebody else's, and on forty of them
he had come up the stairs on purpose in order to be where it would be made.

"In the July of 1956 he did it.

"And then he could do it, and it did not go away again."

Douglas started to say something and Pascual put a hand up.

"No. You are about to ask me what it cost him and what it was like and I am not going to tell
you, tonight or ever, and I want the line drawn where I am drawing it rather than have you find
out later where it was. That part is his. You may have it from him if you ask him, and he will
give you the whole of it, in order, with the dates, because he gives everybody everything. You
will not ask him." Pascual put the hand back down. "Neither did I. I have the figures because he
brought them to me, and I have the date because I was in the house, and that is the whole of what
is mine to hand over."

"Aye," said Douglas, after a moment. "That's fair."

It was fair, and he found he was not as glad of it as he ought to have been, and he has not gone
back over that.

---

Douglas got up and stood at the window with his back to the room, which he has not done in front
of another man since 1958, and looked at a yard with two oil drums in it.

He would like it recorded that his first thought was not a decent one. His first thought was
*that is mine,* and it went off in him like a dropped tray, and it was disgraceful and he is not
going to pretend it did not happen.

The second one took a good deal longer and it was worse.

Eleven years he has had one observation and no way of using it. The third night at Kingston in
1975 and he did not believe it, and again on the fourth and he did, and he has carried it since
as an anomaly he could not build on — one instance, one observer, no control, and no possibility
whatever of getting another, because the only man who could supply it does not know the answer
and would give him four decimal places of not knowing.

What he had was a reading with no zero on the instrument.

He has the zero now, and it is 1954, and the instrument was in another man's house for the two
years it took to come off the stop.

---

He turned round.

"You did nothing," he said. "Seventy times. That's what you're telling me."

"I did nothing seventy times. I never went at him about it. I never asked him why, I never
remarked on it, I never once let it become a subject in two years, and there were a good many
evenings when it would have been the easiest thing in the world to say something light about it
and I did not." Pascual was looking at the desk. "And when he did it in the July I said nothing
at all afterwards. Not that night and not ever. I wanted to say something very much indeed and I
did not, and that is the only part of the whole two years I have ever been certain I got right.

"I did not have a theory. I was not being clever. I was forty-five and I was in love with him
and I did not want to frighten him. That is the whole of my method and it is not a method."

"It's a method." Douglas heard his own voice come out wrong and did not correct it. "It's the
only kind that works and there's no writing it down."

He sat down again because his legs had opinions.

Seventy trials. One protocol, applied identically, no adverse event recorded, and a capability at
the end of it that exists nowhere else in the entire man — because in every other department of
that life the same difficulty set hard, and in sixty-nine years no practice effect has ever once
been observed in any of them. Not one. Douglas has been looking for nine years.

He said the sentence out loud before he had decided to, and he has not been able to unsay it
since.

"You kept it open."

"I do not know that," said Pascual.

"I do. I've the arithmetic and you've not, and I'd give a good deal to be wrong and I'm not."

---

Neither of them said anything for a bit.

"I've told him twice in nine years that it's the best of him," said Douglas. "Eleven words, both
times. I meant it and I'd say it again tomorrow." He put his hands on his knees. "And now I know
where it came from, and I can't hand it back, and I can't stop knowing it."

"No. You cannot. I am sorry."

"Are you?"

Pascual took his time about it, which Douglas noticed, because the man has answered everything
else this week inside a second.

"Some of me is," he said.

---

It was Pascual who gave him the other half, about a minute later, and Douglas has thought since
that he did not have to.

"It did not transfer," he said. "I want you to have that as well, and I want it plainly, because
you are sitting there constructing something and it is wrong.

"It was not a thing he learnt once and carried about. It went with the house. Whatever he has
with you he built with you, from nothing, at sixty and after, and I was not there and had no
hand in it and he will have found it exactly as hard the second time." He shrugged, slightly.
"What was got at in my house was not the capability. It was that the capability could be got at
at all. That is what I gave him and it is smaller than you are making it and larger than I am
comfortable with."

"That's foundations," said Douglas. "You've just told me you're the foundations and asked me to
find it a small thing."

"Yes," said Pascual. "I have. I am aware of how that sounds and I have not got a better way to
put it and I have had since three o'clock."

---

Douglas sat and looked at the cover on the bench for a while and then did a thing he had not
planned and cannot fully account for, and which he has decided, on balance, that he is glad of.

"I'll give you something," he said. "Not because I owe you it. Because it's true and you'll not
get it anywhere else in this world."

"Go on."

"He told me in the March of 1975, in writing, before I'd ever laid eyes on him, not to expect the
position to improve with familiarity. He'd got fifty-eight years of it not improving and no
reason in the mechanism why it should, and he set it out the way he sets everything out and I
believed him, because he's never once been wrong in writing about anything else in eleven years.

"He was wrong. It has improved. It took from about 1980 to about 1984 and there's a dozen things
he does now that were not available to him in seventy-five, and he found it out himself in the
January of eighty-four by going through nine years of his own file and counting, alone, on a
Tuesday, nine years late." He turned the glass round. "Now. Two trials on different sites don't
pool and I've spent thirty years telling agronomists so and I'm not going to pretend otherwise
in an office. But you had two years and I had four, and you can do that division as well as I
can, and mine was on a man who could be asked anything."

Pascual did not say anything for a while.

"Why are you telling me that?"

"Because there's nobody I can say it to." Douglas heard how that came out and let it stand.
"That's the truth of it and it's not a kindness. It's a statement about him and not about the
work, and that's a rule he made in November of seventy-five and I've kept it for eleven years and
I'll keep it till I'm dead. So the one thing that's got better in that man's life is the single
thing in it nobody's allowed to mention to him, and I've been carrying it since 1980, and you're
the first person I've ever been able to hand it to.

"That's why. You can have whatever else you like out of it."

"I shall take the division," said Pascual, "and I shall not thank you for it, because you have
just told me it was not a kindness and I do not think you knew whether that was true when you
said it."

"No," said Douglas. "I didn't."

That is not an anomaly. That is a finding, and it has been made in an empty laboratory in San
Juan on a Wednesday afternoon by a man he came four thousand miles wanting to hit.

---

"Sit down," said Pascual. "You have gone a colour I do not care for and I have not finished."

Douglas sat down.

"There is a date on the other side of it and I can give you that too, and then I am going to
stop, because past that point I have nothing but opinions and I am a veterinary surgeon and an
anatomist and I will give you what I have seen and not one inch further.

"In February of 1955 he came apart. I will not go into it; it is his and you may ask him and he
will tell you the whole of it in about four minutes with the dates. What matters is that I sat
on the edge of a bed and I had his wrist and he said four words that nobody had asked him for.
He said: *it has done it before.* And I asked when, because I ask things, and he said one word.

"He said Salisbury."

Douglas did not move.

"He was there from 1946 to 1948. You will know that; you ask him things. What I do not think you
will have, because it is not the sort of thing he would supply and nobody would think to ask, is
that he was entirely alone for the whole of it. Not lonely — alone. No one in the house, no one
outside it, nothing of any kind, for two years and some months, and he has said so plainly when
asked and thought nothing of the saying.

"And in thirty-two years I have known him volunteer information about his own condition perhaps
three times, and every one of the three was when he was in pieces."

He put his glass down.

"That is the whole of what I have. A date, an absence, and a thing that does not close. I have
not joined them up and I am not going to join them up in front of you, because if I do you will
take it as a finding of mine and it is not one. It is two observations and a name I have never
opened and do not intend to."

"You went in the room that far and stopped."

"I said *ah* and gave him a piece of bread." Pascual was looking at the desk. "I have had
thirty-one years on that four seconds and I do not know to this day whether I did the right
thing. I have decided that a man who is coming apart is owed the thing he is asking for and he
was not asking for that. It may be that I was frightened. Both are available and I cannot
separate them and I have stopped trying."

---

Douglas joined them up. He could not help it, and he did it out loud, because he does everything
out loud, and he flagged it as he went, because that is the only discipline he has ever had.

"Then it's this, and it's not supported and I'll say so before I say it. Something shut in him
between forty-six and forty-eight and shut hard. Not made — he had it from a boy, you've said
so — but set. Turned from a squint into a wall." He rubbed his face. "And whatever the mechanism
is, it can only close over what's in the building at the time. There was nothing of that kind in
his life in those two years for it to close over. So it never went near it. That's all. That's
the whole of my great theory and it's got no more standing than yours."

"It has exactly the standing of two observations and a date."

"Which is more than I had at ten o'clock this morning." Douglas let out a breath. "Eleven years
I've had one instance sat in a drawer. I couldn't build on it and I couldn't put it down and I
couldn't ask, because the only man who could answer doesn't know the answer and would give me
four decimal places of not knowing."

"He would," said Pascual, "and he would be entirely willing, and you would come away with less
than you went in with. Yes. I had eleven years of that too. I had twelve."

---

Neither of them said anything for a bit.

"Does he know any of this?" said Douglas.

"No. Not one part of it. He has no idea there is a discontinuity, and if you told him he would
enter it as a fact about himself supplied by two people who had compared notes about him in a
room he was not in, and he would thank you for it, and he would be perfectly all right, and it
would sit in him for the rest of his life doing something neither of us can predict."

"So it stops here."

"It stops here. I have never said it and I am not going to say it again, and I have no intention
of telling Ramón, who is entitled to everything and is not entitled to this."

"Aye." Douglas looked at his hands. "It stops here."

He has kept to that. He has never mentioned it, in the aeroplane or since, in nine years of
Fridays, and he does not expect to.

---

"I want to ask you something else," said Pascual, "and you may refuse and I will not raise it
again."

"Go on."

"Is he happy?"

"Aye," said Douglas. "He is."

It came out flat and it came out at once, and he watched it go into the man like a nail.

"I'll give you the grounds, because you'll want them and because I don't put a thing that size
down without them. Sleeps six and says so. Finishes his lunch, which he did not do when I met
him and has done since about 1978. Nine stone one and steady three years. Goes out on the water
with Ramnarine's boy on a Saturday for no reason at all, which is a thing he'd not have done for
any money in 1975 because there was nothing in the day about it." He turned the glass. "And one
that's not a number. He talks. Not the way you mean, he cannot start a thing off and he never
will. But he'll come in from a building he's been walked round and give you eleven minutes on a
seawater intake nobody asked him for, and he does it three or four times a week, and he has no
idea he does it, and I've never told him."

Pascual sat back and did not say anything for what must have been fifteen seconds.

"Thank you," he said. "I have wanted that for nineteen years and there was no other way on earth
of getting it."

"It cost me nothing," said Douglas. "I'll have that plain, because you're building me into
something and I'll not have it. I like being asked. I like there being an answer and I like being
the reason there is one, and I'd have told you so on the pavement on Monday if you'd asked me on
the pavement on Monday."

"I know," said Pascual. "That is the cruel part, and I do not think you can help it, and I am not
sure you would if you could."

He turned his glass round once and put it down.

"I want to be exact about what has just happened to me, because you have been exact with me all
afternoon and it would be poor manners not to. In 1966 I went without knowing whether he would be
all right. That is the whole of what I took with me. There was no way of finding out — the
letters are four paragraphs on a sluice and I wrote the questions in them myself so that they
could be answered — and I have carried it for twenty years and I had made my peace with never
knowing.

"You have just told me that he is all right, and that I am not the reason, and both halves came
in the same word. I would not have had you say it any other way. But do not tell me it was free,
because it was not free, and the bill did not come to you."

---

Then he said: "I am going to ask one more thing and it is a good deal worse than the last one,
and you may refuse and I would think none the less of you."

"Ask it."

"What was he like in 1975?"

Douglas sat with that for a moment. He would like it on the record that he did think about
refusing, and that the thinking took about two seconds, and that what decided it was not
charity.

"In his head he was in good order," he said. "That's first and it's the worst of it. There was no
misery in it anywhere. He was fifty-eight and running perfectly well and he'd have gone on
running perfectly well for another twenty years, and every man at that conference would have told
you he was the best of them and they'd all have been right.

"He was not in good order otherwise. And nobody was looking, and he'd not have noticed, and he'd
not have reported it if he had."

"Go on."

"He ate at one because the day said one, and half of it went back, and it went back all week and
nobody at the table remarked on it including me, because I'd not learnt to look yet. He'd nothing
at all after eight in the evening — that's not loneliness, it's that an evening has no heading on
it and never had, so it did not exist. Nine years of that.

"I got a figure off Jean Leslie in the February of seventy-seven, which she gave me without
knowing she was giving me anything, and I'd two of my own by then, and I put them against what I
could remember of the Kingston week and what Ramnarine would tell me." He stopped. "I'll not give
you the arithmetic. You'll do it faster than I did and you'll get further."

Pascual did it.

He did it sitting down, with his hands on his knees, in an office with the fan off, and it took
him about four seconds, and Douglas watched the whole of it go across him and land.

He did not say the number.

Douglas did not ask him for it.

The tap was still going somewhere down the corridor.

"That's what I found," said Douglas. "And I'm not putting it to you as a bill. You'd have been
dead of it by 1972 and then he'd have had nothing at all and no letters either, and I've been
over it a good many times and there is no version where anybody comes out of that house whole. I
know that. It doesn't help me and I don't expect it to help you."

Pascual had both hands flat on his knees.

"That is worse than being hit," he said, "and you know it is, and I do not think you did it on
purpose, and I am not certain."

"Neither am I," said Douglas.

---

Then Douglas did the thing he came to Puerto Rico with no intention of doing, and he did it
because there was no longer any reason on earth not to, and because it was the only thing he had
that the man wanted.

He gave him the whole of it. Standing up, at the window, for about twenty minutes.

The Salter platform in the medical room and Mrs Ramnarine reading it because she is quick and
does not talk. The March weighing and the column under it going back to 1978. The blood pressure
twice a year since the October of 1983 and the second figure sent unasked. The card against the
lamp, and what a card is for, and what a start time does that a date does not. The headings. The
Fridays. The fortnight in the summer and the fortnight at Christmas and the eleven years of
letters and what is in them and what is not. The lunch, and Mrs Ramnarine in the corridor on the
eleventh day in 1977, and what he did with that two years later. That he asks questions with
numbers for answers because a number is a thing the man can produce and an adjective is not.
That none of it requires any gift whatsoever, that anybody could run it who was prepared to keep
the paper, and that this is the entire reason it has lasted and will go on lasting.

Pascual did not interrupt once. He asked two questions in twenty minutes and both of them were
about the platform scale.

At the end of it he said, "Thank you," and then, after a moment, "That is better than what I
had."

"It's not better. It's cheaper. That's a different thing and it's the only reason it works."

"No," said Pascual. "It is better, and I have been a professional man for fifty-one years and I
know the difference between a thing that is better and a thing I would like to be worse. Yours
has no genius in it. Mine had nothing else, and it was gone in twelve years and there was no
relief in it anywhere because none could be built. Yours will still be running when you are
eighty and I will be dead."

Douglas looked out of the window at the two oil drums.

"I'd not have got there without Alec," he said, which was not an answer and was the nearest he
could come to one.

---

They walked back out through the corridor and Pascual locked the side door with the key he has
not given back.

On the step, Douglas said: "I still want to hit you."

"I know."

"It's worse than it was this morning, and that's not fair, and I'm telling you because you'd get
it off my face anyway and I'd rather say it than be read."

"That is the most courteous thing anybody has said to me this week," said Pascual, and he meant
it, and Douglas could hear that he meant it, and that was the worst part of the whole afternoon.

"Then how do we get through tomorrow?"

"You will be at Ramón's pharmacy losing money at dominoes, and I will be at home, and the two of
us will not be in a room together, and I understand that has been arranged for us by two men in
a corridor and that neither of us was consulted." Pascual put his hat on. "I have decided to be
grateful for it and I recommend it."

---

Douglas walked back to the hotel the long way and got there at ten past six, four minutes before
Reggie, who came in from the laboratory with the whole floor plan in his head and gave him
eleven minutes on the seawater intake without being asked, which is not origination and is the
next thing to it, and which Douglas has never once told him he notices.

He had a zero in his coat pocket, and a line he could draw now and had spent eleven years unable
to draw, and nowhere in the world to put either of them.

He has it there still.

## Patch to the reunion (§IV, Thursday at the pharmacy)

With the Wednesday in place, one exchange wants changing. Ramón says he thought Douglas was
being cruel at the table; in the current text Douglas explains the eleven words and Ramón takes
it. It would now run nearer to:

> "I thought you were being cruel to him at the table, though," Ramón said. "Eleven words and
> nothing after them. That's what I saw and I'll not pretend I saw something else."
>
> "You were right and I was. I put it right yesterday afternoon and I'm not going to tell you
> how, and if he wants you to have it he'll give it you himself."
>
> Ramón looked at him for a moment and then nodded and let it go, which he has been doing in
> that house for sixteen years and is better at than anybody Douglas has ever met.

Everything else in §IV holds. The anger confession, Suárez, the 1983 porch and the retraction of
*never looked round* all still work and are, if anything, harder for him now.
