# The Fourth Entry

## I

The letter from the organising committee came in the October of 1985. It set out the programme
of a two-day meeting in San Juan in the March, to mark Dr Pobil's twenty years at the laboratory,
and it explained, courteously and at some length, that they had approached him because he had been
Dr Pobil's chief at the Martin from 1954 to 1966 and it would look extraordinary to hold the thing
without him. At the foot of the second page it asked whether he would come and say something at
the dinner on the Tuesday.

Reggie replied the same day. It took him nine minutes. He said that he would come, that he would
speak for six minutes, and asked what the other speakers were to cover so as not to duplicate.

It was only afterwards that he saw what had happened, and he set it out for himself in the usual
way, because he finds it useful to be exact about his own faults even when he cannot correct them.

He had agreed to leave the station for three days in the middle of the Lent term without consulting
anybody, and the entire reason was that a question had been put to him in writing and he had
answered it. That is what he does. Ask him anything and he will give the whole of the truth at
once, without hedging, and will not mind being asked and will not wonder why. Do not ask him and
nothing whatever happens; he does not decline to speak, there is simply no mechanism, and he has
known this about himself since he was about eleven years old and has never found a way round it.
It has advantages. What it does not have is any sort of check on it, and this is the fourth time in
his life it has committed him to something before he knew he was deciding.

He looked at that for a while and then wrote to the Colonel's successor about the Lent term.

---

Then there was the difficulty of telling Pascual, and he spent four days on it and failed.

The difficulty is always the same and it comes out the same way every time. He can write the news
flat — *I shall be at the March meeting* — and that is true, and it is also not true, because a
bare statement of fact from a man who has not crossed that water in nineteen years is not a bare
statement of fact and both of them would know it. Or he can write what is actually the case, which
would be a report on his own condition, which nobody has asked for and which would be a
considerable weight to put on a man four thousand miles off who has done nothing whatever wrong.

There is no third kind of sentence. He established that in 1970, when Pascual wrote to say he had
found somebody, and again in 1977, when he tried to write and say that Nairn had come out to St
Augustine. Both times he worked at it properly and satisfied himself that the class of sentence he
wanted does not exist. A thing that does not exist cannot be found, and a man who goes on hunting
for it after he has proved it is not there is not being conscientious, he is indulging himself.

So he wrote: *I am told I am to be at the March meeting.* He put it in the fourth paragraph, after
the sluice.

---

The reply came in the January, three paragraphs on the middle pens, and then one line above the
signature: *It will be good to see you.*

He took eleven days over that line and got nowhere, which he had expected, because the line will go
two ways and there is nothing in it to separate them. Either it is what a courteous man puts at the
foot of a letter to a former colleague who is coming to his dinner, or it is the reverse of that,
and a flat line is exactly what the same man produces under either hypothesis.

He did not spend a fifth day on it. Nairn gave him the rule in 1983 and it covers the whole class:
when you cannot tell two hypotheses apart and the extra observation costs you four minutes, you
make the observation and you send it, and you let the man at the other end throw it away.

So he wrote back that he looked forward to it. He read the sentence over twice before sealing it,
to be sure it was true, and it was.

---

What he could not solve was the three days.

Every hour of his day has been under a heading since 1954. That is not a preference and it is not
tidiness; it is the mechanism by which the day is possible at all, and an hour with nothing over it
is not an empty hour, it is an hour he cannot enter. A hotel in San Juan from the Monday to the
Thursday, with a dinner in the middle of it, is a good many hours of that kind. He had the whole of
the February to think about it and got nowhere, and did not mention it to anybody, and would have
described it accurately if anybody had asked him.

Nairn said in the February that he was coming too. Reggie asked why, and was told that there was a
man at Mayagüez sitting on eleven years of cane trials who would not release them and had to be
gone at in person, and that the dates were convenient. Reggie accepted this. It was entirely in
character and there was nothing in the account that did not stand up.

It was untrue. Nairn has never been asked whether it was true and would say so at once if he were.

---

On the Sunday there was a card propped against the lamp, done in a hand you could set type from
because Nairn had had it copied rather than write it himself, and it had four lines on it.

    Mon    arrive; nothing until the evening
    Tue    the meeting; dinner 8; you speak at 9
    Wed    the meeting; free after 4
    Thu    —

Reggie read it and put it in the case with his papers and did not say anything about it, which is
what he does about that card and has done since 1978.

He looked at the fourth line for some time. Three of the four days had been made enterable by a man
who has never once explained why he does it and has twice had the card re-copied when the ink went,
and the fourth had a dash on it, and the dash is not the same object as a blank. A blank is an
oversight. A dash is a statement that the line is not yet available.

He did not ask what was going to go on it. Nairn had not been asked for a card in 1978 either.

## II

*Ramón. Monday.*

He liked the Scotchman inside ten minutes, and the reason was simple: the man told you things
without being asked.

They had ten minutes on the pavement outside the hotel while the other two were in the lobby with
the bags, and in those ten minutes Ramón learnt that Nairn was sixty-five, that he had the chair at
St Augustine and grumbled about the drive out to the station, that he had lived with a man called
Alec from 1946 until Alec died in 1958, that he had been on his own for nineteen years after that,
that he could not stand aeroplanes, and that he would have a beer if one was going and two if there
was time. Ramón gave him the pharmacy, the three girls, Marisol's husband who is a fool, and the
dominoes.

By the time the other two came out Ramón had decided that the week might be all right.

---

It was not all right. But on the Monday night Ramón did one sensible thing, which was to ask.

They were on the steps and the other two had gone up.

"Tell me what I need to know about your man," he said. "I don't want to put a foot wrong on
Tuesday and I'm no good at guessing."

"That's easy enough," said Nairn. "It's two things and they're both odd and neither of them is what
you'd think.

"First. He will answer any question you put to him. Any question at all, on any subject, including
himself, completely and truthfully, and he'll not hedge and he'll not ask you why you want to know
and he'll not hold it against you afterwards. You can ask him what he weighs and you'll get it to
the pound. You can ask him about the war and you'll get the war. There's nothing behind the door
and there's no door.

"Second. He will never start anything. Not once, not ever, not about himself and not about anybody
else. If nobody in the room asks him a question, he'll sit there all night with his hands quiet and
be perfectly content and you'll come away thinking he'd nothing to say. He told me himself, in
writing, in 1975, and he was quite clear about it: it isn't reticence, because reticence means
there's something being kept back, and there's nothing being kept back. There's just no engine in
there that starts by itself. He's known it since he was a boy and he's stopped trying to fix it."

Ramón thought about that.

"So I ask him things."

"You ask him things. As many as you like, he'll not tire of it. And here's the other half, which I
got wrong myself in 1975 and had to work out — you can praise the work as much as you want, only
he'll hand the praise straight on to whoever did that bit of it. Don't praise the man. It doesn't
land, he doesn't know what to do with it, and it stops the conversation dead."

"That's a strange way to live."

"It is," said Nairn. "You get used to it. It's nine years for me and I'd not swap."

He drank the last of his beer and set the bottle on the step.

"One more thing you'd better have from me and not off somebody else. He was your Pascual's chief
for twelve years and the two of them shared a house the whole time. 1954 to 1966. It ended because
Pascual took the San Juan post, and they haven't been in a room together since, and they've written
four times a year ever since without missing one. So it's twenty years, and neither of them will
say a word about it, and neither of them will mind you knowing."

"I know all that," said Ramón. "I've had sixteen years of the letters coming in the house."

"Aye," said Nairn. "Well. Now we both know we know."

## III

*Ramón. The dinner.*

They shook hands at the door of the dining room at ten past eight and it took under two seconds and
both of them did it properly, and there was nothing whatever to see, and Ramón had been watching
for something and was almost disappointed.

Then Reggie turned half round to bring the Scotchman in, and said:

"Pascual — Nairn."

Ramón was two feet off with a glass in his hand. He did not understand what he had heard. But he
saw Nairn hear it: the man went still for about a second and a half and looked at the tablecloth,
and then put his hand out and was perfectly pleasant.

Ramón put it away to ask about later, and he did ask, on the Thursday, and got the whole of it, and
he had the other half of it before that, from Pascual, on the Tuesday night in his own kitchen.

---

The dinner ran three hours. Ramón worked all three of them and so did Nairn, and by the end they
were handing it back and forth between them like two men carrying a wardrobe down a stair.

Some of it was fine.

Reggie and Ramón were fine, which surprised everybody at the table including Ramón. He had been
told to ask questions and he did not have to: the Englishman asked him. About the pharmacy — the
licensing, the supply, what a hurricane does to stock you cannot let get warm, whether the girls
had wanted the business and what they had gone into instead. One question at a time, the whole
answer taken, the next question built onto the end of it, for fifty minutes without any sign of
getting bored.

Ramón has stood behind a counter since 1938. He has never in his life had the whole of a man's
attention like that and he did not know what to do with it, and about halfway through he understood
that this was not politeness and was not an effort. The man was interested. He was interested in a
pharmacy in Santurce the way other men are interested in a war.

Somewhere in there Reggie went into Spanish, for the waiter and then for Ramón, and it was very
correct and very flat and about forty years old.

"Where did you get that?"

"Out of a book in 1954, and off a colleague." He nodded down the table. "My accent is Valencian and
cannot now be helped."

Nairn had no Spanish, said so, and did not mind.

---

The rest of it was not fine.

Nairn and Pascual were correct with one another. Ramón wants it said that neither of them did one
thing wrong the whole evening. Nairn answered everything he was asked, exactly, in the fewest words
the question would take, and offered nothing, and did not look at him for longer than the sentence
needed.

At about half past nine Pascual asked him how the March weighing was managed.

"Salter platform in the medical room. Mrs Ramnarine reads it, I write it down. Every March."

"And the figure?"

"Nine stone one."

"The same as 1984."

"The same as 1984."

That was all of it. Pascual said thank you and turned his glass round on the cloth.

Ramón sat there with his fork halfway up and thought: that is a man of seventy-four asking for the
only thing he has wanted for twenty years, and getting it in eleven words, and not being given one
word more, and saying thank you for it.

He thought that was cruel. He still thinks it was cruel, and he said so on the Thursday and was
corrected, and he has taken the correction and it has not entirely moved him.

What he did not do was hold it against the man, and that is worth setting down, because he had
taken to him on the pavement on Monday inside ten minutes and he does not do that often at
seventy-three and he did not care to find out he had been wrong.

He was not wrong. He worked out that much over the coffee. The Scotchman has had that Englishman
nine years and the man across the table had him for twelve before that, in a house, and nobody
alive is going to sit next to that and be entirely reasonable about it. Ramón was married
twenty-nine years and can remember precisely what he thought about a man called Suárez in 1943 who
had known his wife first and had done nothing whatever about it in his life.

So he had it in his hand — this man is being cruel, and I know one of the reasons — and he ate his
dinner. It turned out on Thursday that he had the smaller of the two reasons and not the larger
one.

---

Then there were the two of them.

In three hours Reggie and Pascual said perhaps a dozen things to one another and Ramón could give
you nearly all of them. The sluice. The eastern pens and the drainage. The Michaelmas intake, which
they have both been arranging since before Ramón had heard either of their names. A supplier in
Port of Spain who had gone under. Whether the Colonel's widow was still in Hampshire. Jean Leslie's
paper in the November.

Not one of them was a question that was not about work.

Ramón is not a clever man and would tell you so, but he has spent forty-eight years behind a
counter watching people decide whether to say what is wrong with them, and at about a quarter to
eleven, over the coffee, he got the whole shape of it at once and it turned his stomach.

They were both waiting.

That was all it was. Two men at a table, each of them willing to say absolutely anything he was
asked, and each of them sitting with his hands quiet waiting for the other one to ask, and neither
of them able to be the one who opened his mouth first. And they would have gone on doing that for
three hours a night for the rest of their lives, courteously, without a cross word, and both of
them would have got on the aeroplane at the end of it perfectly satisfied that nothing had been
held back.

Ramón got up and went out to the lavatory and stood in the corridor a minute and was angrier than
he has been since his wife died.

Nairn came out about two minutes later, which was not a coincidence.

"You've seen it."

"I've seen it. Neither of them is going to ask."

"No. And I'll tell you the worst of it — Reggie can't. It isn't that he won't. There's nothing in
him that starts a thing off, I told you that on the steps, and it applies to this the same as to
everything else." Nairn was looking at the wall. "So it's the other one that would have to, and I
don't know your man, but he's not going to either. And that's the week gone."

"Then we take ourselves out of it," said Ramón. "Thursday. My house, the evening. You be somewhere
else."

"That's what I came out here to say. I'll go over to Mayagüez, there's a man there sitting on
eleven years of cane trials —"

"I'll take you out. You don't want to go to Mayagüez."

Nairn stopped.

"No," he said. "I don't."

It took ninety seconds. Neither of them ever put it to either of the other two as a proposal. Ramón
told Pascual it was arranged and Pascual said nothing at all. Nairn went up before Reggie did and
wrote a line on a card and propped it against the lamp.

Ramón thinks about that ninety seconds fairly often. Two men who had met on Monday, settling a
thing between themselves in a corridor on behalf of two others who had not managed to settle it in
twenty years.

---

There is one more piece of the Tuesday and it happened in Ramón's kitchen at half past midnight
with the coffee going, and it is the reason he had something to give the Scotchman on Thursday.

Pascual came in and sat down and did not take his jacket off, which he does not do.

"He calls him Nairn," he said.

"He calls everybody by their surname. He told me so."

"He calls me Pascual." Pascual had both hands round the cup and was not drinking out of it. "I did
not know it was a system. That is the whole of what is the matter with me tonight and I would
rather say it now than have it come out sideways in three days.

"That man has an arrangement about names. Nineteen years of letters and every man in them is a
surname — Nairn, Leslie, Whitmore, Spilsbury, the Colonel — and it is not coldness and it is not
formality, it is how he holds a person. I have known that since about 1975 the way you know a thing
you have never looked at. I have never once asked him about it. In twelve years in that house it
did not occur to me that there was anything there to ask about, because I did not ask him things. I
looked at him. That was the arrangement and I was proud of it."

Ramón said, "And the Scotchman asked him."

"The Scotchman asked him and got three minutes of it, in 1983, and knows exactly what his own name
means and can tell you." Pascual put the cup down. "I had that man's condition off him four times a
day for twelve years and I could have told you in the dark whether he had eaten. And I never once
put a question to him about himself that was not about his health. Not one. That is not a complaint
against anybody. It is arithmetic and I have had it for about two hours and I do not care for it."

Ramón did not say anything for a bit, and then he said what he thought, which is what he does.

"You want me to tell you that's not the same thing as being second."

"It is not the same thing as being second. I know that. I worked it out in the taxi." Pascual got
up. "It is worse than that. He has a whole apparatus in him for holding people, and it was running
the entire time I was in that house, and I never asked what it was, and a man in Hertfordshire
wrote to him about a statistical appendix and asked him inside three years."

## IV

*Nairn. Thursday afternoon.*

Ramón took him out at four and did not take him to anything.

They sat at the back of the pharmacy on two crates with the fan going and a case of beer that Ramón
had plainly had put there in the morning, and the girl minded the counter. At about half past five
two other men of Ramón's age came in without being sent for and there were dominoes for an hour.
Douglas lost eleven and a half dollars and enjoyed himself more than he had expected to enjoy
anything that week. Then the two men went home and it was the fan and the beer and the two of them.

"Right," said Ramón. "The door on Tuesday. *Pascual — Nairn.* You went off like a light and I
didn't know why. Tell me why."

So Douglas told him.

"He calls people by their surnames. All of them, always, and it's not coldness, it's how he files
things. He explained it to me in 1983 and it took him about three minutes and I'd never have got it
on my own.

"I wrote to him first in 1973, out of the blue, about a statistical appendix. Thirty-one months of
letters before I ever laid eyes on him — forty-one of them — and I'd signed the first one *D.
Nairn* and never got round to putting a Christian name on any of the rest. So all he had was the
surname and what was in the letters, and what he told me in 1983 was that the surname isn't a form
of address at all. It's the heading. It's the whole of what he's assembled about me, filed under one
word. It's what he calls me when I'm not in the room and it's been that since the autumn of
seventy-three, which is two years before he knew what I looked like."

Ramón thought about it.

"That's a good thing to be told."

"It's the best thing anybody's ever said to me and I've had three years of it." Douglas turned the
bottle round on his knee. "And there's one man alive he uses a Christian name for. One. And on
Tuesday he did the pair of us in the same breath, four words apart, and never heard himself do it."

"And that hurt."

"Aye. It hurt." He said it flatly, because there was no sense being clever about it in a back room
with a fan going. "And it shouldn't. He wasn't making a comparison; he doesn't make comparisons, it
isn't in him. The name's older than I am and it came out of twelve years in the same house. I've
had that reasoned out since Tuesday night and I'll have to reason it out again in about six months,
and I've been doing that every six months since 1977, and I expect I'll be doing it when I'm
eighty."

Ramón took a drink and then set the bottle down on the floor between his feet.

"Then you'd better have the other end of it, because I got it on Tuesday night at half past twelve
in my kitchen and it's not mine to keep."

And he gave it to him — the jacket not taken off, the cup not drunk out of, the whole of it, as near
word for word as he could get. *He calls me Pascual. I did not know it was a system.* Twelve years
in the house and never once a question about the man that was not about his health. A whole
apparatus for holding people, running the entire time, never asked about. And a man in Hertfordshire
who wrote about an appendix and asked inside three years.

Douglas sat and looked at the fan for a while.

"He said that out loud. To you. On the Tuesday."

"He said it out loud to me at half past twelve at night with his jacket on, and he was not looking
for anything back, and I didn't give him anything back, because there wasn't anything."

"No." Douglas rubbed his thumb along the neck of the bottle. "No, there isn't."

He has been over that a good many times since and it has not changed and he does not expect it to.
He had spent two days holding the Christian name as evidence of something. It is not evidence of
anything except that Pascual got there in 1954 and Douglas did not. And the man who has the
Christian name spent Tuesday night in a kitchen at half past midnight finding out that he had lived
inside a system for twelve years without ever asking it a question, and Douglas had asked it one in
1983 and been answered for three minutes.

"That's the whole difference between the pair of us, then," he said. "He looked at him and I asked
him."

"That's the whole difference," said Ramón. "And he'd tell you the same. He said it plainer than you
did."

---

Then Douglas said the other thing, and it was the first time it had ever been out of his mouth in
eleven years.

"I'll tell you something else and you can do what you like with it. I've wanted to put a hand on
your man since Monday."

Ramón did not move.

"Four times in nine years I've had that and put it down again, because there's nothing in the
account that anybody did wrong, and I don't keep a finding I can't support. Then he asked me what
the March figure was and I gave it him and I sat there for the rest of the dinner with it going
round like a lad of nineteen." He drank. "That's a fault in me, not in him. I want that said
plainly. I've had the whole case off Reggie himself, in Kingston in 1975, in about forty minutes,
because I asked him and he told me. He'd not have one word said against your man and he's the only
person on this earth with a right to a view. And it comes anyway and I've no defence for it at
all."

"I know," said Ramón.

"You know."

"There was a fellow called Suárez in 1943 who'd known my wife before I did and had done nothing
whatever about it in his life, and I could not be in a room with the man for eleven years." Ramón
shrugged. "You had yours in front of you for three hours. I'd have been worse."

"No," said Douglas. "And Suárez isn't the case, and you know he isn't, because you've been sitting
opposite mine since Monday."

Ramón stopped with the bottle halfway up.

"That's the case. Not some fellow forty years back who never came near you. You had the man who was
there before you, in a chair, three feet off, for three hours on Tuesday. And you took to him inside
the hour, and you've been decent about him all week, and you're being decent about him now in a back
room where there's nobody to see it." He set his own bottle down. "That's the seat I'm in. You've
been sitting in it since Monday and you've sat in it the other way round. So don't tell me you'd
have been worse. You were in it. You weren't."

Ramón thought about that for a good while, and when he answered he was not quick about it, which
Douglas had not seen him fail to be all week.

"No. That's fair, and I'll not pretend it isn't. But it's not that I'm the better man, and I'll tell
you what the difference is, because I've had three days on it.

"Your man never knew what he was doing to mine. Twelve years he had everything Pascual had, and he
did have it, let's not be delicate about it — Pascual was fifty-five and ground down to the axle and
your man was the whole reason. And he hadn't the first idea. Couldn't have had. That's the thing
that's the matter with him, that's the illness, and you don't hold a man's illness against him. So
there's nothing there for me to forgive. It costs me nothing at all to like him. I'd have liked him
in a queue.

"Mine knew. Mine could see the whole of it, four times a day, for twelve years. He saw exactly what
he was walking out of and exactly what would be left standing in it, and he went anyway."

Douglas turned his bottle round on his knee and did not answer for a bit, because there was an
answer and he did not much want to make it, and made it.

"That's not just either," he said. "The knowing's what made him go. If he'd not been able to see it
he'd have stopped there till it did for the pair of them. You can't have it that the seeing's the
crime when the seeing's the only reason anybody got out alive."

"I know it's not just. I've had sixteen years of it and it hasn't got any juster." Ramón picked the
bottle back up. "But that's where it sits, and I've stopped trying to move it. Yours never knew.
Mine did. That's the whole of why I can like yours and you can't like mine, and it's nothing to do
with which of us is the better man, and I'd not swap."

---

"I thought you were being cruel to him at the table, though," Ramón said. "Eleven words and nothing
after them. That's what I saw and I'll not pretend I saw something else."

"I was giving him the only thing I had that he wanted, and I was giving it him straight and
stopping there, because anything I put on the end of it would have been about me. He asked for a
figure. He got the figure. If I'd have softened it he'd have had me in the answer as well and
that's not what he asked for."

Ramón turned that over.

"All right. I'll take it. It's still a hard way to hand a man his one thing."

"Aye," said Douglas. "It is that."

---

What Ramón said next took four or five minutes and Douglas has been over it a good deal since, in
the air and at his desk in St Augustine, and it is why he is glad he came.

"You've felt sorry for your man since Monday. I've felt sorry for yours since 1983 and I'd never
seen him and didn't know his Christian name.

"Eighty-three, on the porch. I asked Pascual straight out why he left the man in Trinidad. He gave
me a reason and it was a good one, and I could hear while he was saying it that it was the handsome
version. The fourteenth of July 1966, he read something about tetanus in horses and did not look up
when the man came in, and forty minutes later he could not have told you how he was — and he'd had
that man's condition off him four times a day for twelve years. So rather than be the one who
missed something he went. That's what he gave me first."

"That's what he'd give anybody," said Douglas.

"Then he stopped. He sat there a while and he gave me the rest of it, and I'll say it the way he
said it. He said: *he put it to me first. He said, you are tired of me. And I said no, straight off,
inside a second, and it was true and I would swear to it in any court there is. And then I heard
how fast I had said it. And I told him he was right. I told him what I was tired of, which was
being the only one in that house who knew anything, twelve years of it, never once asked and never
once able to be asked, and nobody's fault. I had never said one word of that to anybody and I said
the whole of it in about a minute because he had got there first and left me nowhere to stand.*"
Ramón let that sit. "And then he said he had never told it to a living soul since, and he told me on
a Tuesday because I'd asked him a question."

Douglas put the bottle on the floor.

He had had the whole of the September of 1966 from Reggie in Kingston in 1975 — the *no*, and then
the *you're right*, and then the minute that followed it, all of it written down. He had taken it as
Reggie's account of a thing that happened to Reggie, which is what it was. It had not once occurred
to him that there was a man four thousand miles off who had been carrying the same half-hour for
seventeen years and had said it out loud to exactly one person on earth.

"So he's had nobody to tell either."

"Nobody but me, and only because I asked."

"Right." Douglas sat with it. "Then I'll take some of it back. Not the feeling — I've no procedure
for the feeling and I'll not pretend I have. The finding."

"What was the finding?"

"That he built the thing and walked out of it and never looked round." Douglas rubbed his face with
both hands. "The second half won't hold. He looked round for twenty years, four times a year, and
got four paragraphs about a sluice back every time, and he answered inside the week every single
time for nineteen years, and never once put a question in a letter that would have made Reggie have
to say anything about himself. That's not a man who didn't look round. That's a man doing the only
thing that was left on the board and doing it for two decades without being thanked."

Ramón nodded slowly and said nothing, which Douglas noticed, because it was the only minute in
three days that the man was not saying something.

---

"There's a piece of that you should have as well," Ramón said after a bit. "Pascual worked out what
your name means. In 1984, when the letter came with the weight in it."

"He what?"

"He's had nineteen years of those letters and he keeps every one of them. He came out to the porch
and he said — " Ramón stopped and got it exact, the way a man does when he has been keeping
something — "he said, *in nineteen years he has never mentioned the same man in two letters running.
This one has been in every letter since 1974.* Then he said, *that is not a mention. That is a
file.* And he went and stood in the yard for a while."

Douglas looked at the floor of the pharmacy.

"He got that off the letters."

"He got it off the letters and he was pleased about it, and he was on the floor for a week, and both
of those things are true and I had both of them in my house at the same time. You get used to that."

---

The last of it came at about eight, when they were locking up, and Ramón handed it over lightly and
did not know he was handing anything over.

"Ask me a number. You're one of them, you like numbers."

"How many hours a week does he work?"

"Six. Two mornings. He stood down in the January." Ramón got the shutter halfway down and stopped
with his hand on it. "Sixteen years I've had that man in my house and he has never once had
anything hard to do, and he has never once said a word to me about it, and don't ask me what he'd
say if I asked him, because I've had sixteen years to ask him and I've never done it."

"Why not?"

"Because there's no way to put the question that doesn't come out as *am I not enough for you*,"
said Ramón. "And I'm not putting a man in a position where he has to answer that. So it sits there.
Sixteen years. You'd have the same, I expect."

"I would," said Douglas.

He did not say what else he had. What he had was the Tuesday, at half past nine, when Pascual read
the committee chairman off in about four seconds while the man was still crossing the carpet — and
then sat back in his chair and was tired. At his own dinner. At half past nine. Douglas had put it
down at the time as a man of seventy-four at the end of a long day, and he took it out again in a
back room in Santurce and looked at it properly and did not care for it at all.

---

He had it in the car and he had it at the hotel and he was still turning it over at ten.

Then he settled it, and the settling took about a quarter of an hour and he has not reopened it.

It is not his. That is the whole of the reasoning. It is a thing one man said about another man in a
back room over a beer, in a house Douglas has been in once, and there is no version of this where he
carries it four thousand miles and sets it down in front of Reggie Tar as an observation. If it
wants saying, it wants saying by somebody with a right to say it, and he has not got one.

He was pleased with himself for about four minutes in the car park, and then he went up.

## V

*Reggie. Thursday.*

The card had been altered on the Tuesday night. He found it when he came up.

    Thu    Pobil's house, from 7. Nairn out.

He looked at that for rather a long time, and what he looked at was *from 7*.

Nairn worked out in the May of 1978 that the trouble was never the company and was never the
feeling; it was that an evening which merely happens is an event, and Reggie has no procedure
whatever for an event, and can hold a state indefinitely and was got through a war on one. What a
start time does is convert an event into a state. It is not a schedule and it schedules nothing. It
is a heading with a boundary on it, and it is the difference between an hour he can enter and an
hour he cannot.

Nairn has never explained this to him and Reggie has never asked, and the two of them have been
running on it for eight years.

---

He was there from seven until eleven and there was nobody else in the house.

The first two hours went the way the letters go, which is to say very well. The middle pens. The
Michaelmas intake, which Pascual arranged nine times and which is the only part of the station that
has not been altered since 1966. Jean's November paper, which they had both read and disagreed with
in the same direction. The disturbance at week six, which Pascual had known about from the keepers
in 1955, had regarded as a nuisance rather than a result, and had therefore never mentioned to
anybody — and which Nairn and Reggie between them spent six years arguing about by post before
finding it in the daybooks in 1979.

Charles came up once, for about ninety seconds, in connection with a notebook. Twenty-two years.
Neither of them added anything to what the other said and neither of them wanted to.

At about a quarter past nine there was a pause, and Reggie was aware of it, and did not fill it.

---

He had had the finding since the December.

It is worth setting down how he got it, because he did not go looking for it and would not have
known how to look.

He had gone into the correspondence file after a drainage figure from 1979 and had not found it, and
had gone forward through the file instead of backward, which he does not normally do. What he came
out holding was a rate.

In nineteen years and seventy-seven letters, Pascual has put one question to him that was not about
work. One. It came in the October of 1983 and it instructed him to have his blood pressure taken
twice a year and to put the figures at the foot of his letters. Nairn told him at the time what that
meant, and Nairn was right and Reggie has never improved on it: a man forming a concern about
somebody of sixty-six asks three things — weight, blood pressure, whether he smokes — and Pascual
had asked for exactly one of the three and had not asked for the other two.

The anomaly is not the question. The anomaly is that the man reached for a job. Reggie did not have
that phrase for it in the December and does not much care for it now, but it is what the letter is.
There was no work in front of him and he made some, at four thousand miles, in the only place where
making some would not be a complaint against anybody.

He entered the anomaly, unresolved, and did not put it in a letter, because the only sentences
available were a lie or a wound and the class between them is empty and he has proved that twice.

He did not get on the aeroplane intending to say anything about it. He had formed no intention about
it at all.

---

What the Tuesday gave him was the second piece, and he was not looking for it either. He was
answering Ramón Vidal about hurricanes, and he had the rest of the table in the side of his eye for
three hours.

At half past nine the chairman came across the carpet and Pascual had the whole of him before he
arrived — the man was in some sort of trouble about a car and it was written all over him and it
took about four seconds.

And then Pascual sat back and was tired.

Reggie has seen that particular fatigue perhaps a thousand times and knows exactly what it is,
because he caused all thousand of them. What he had never once seen was it arriving after four
seconds of a stranger with a motor-car. That is not the fatigue of a practised movement. It is the
fatigue of an unpractised one, and there is only one way a movement gets unpractised.

That is an observation and not an inference. He went over it on the Wednesday and could not fault
it.

---

The third piece he also had in the December, and he had it out of the file, which is the only place
he ever gets anything.

Having found the rate, he went back to the entry of September 1966 to see whether the rate
contradicted it, and read that entry through for the first time in some years, and found in it a
clause he had recorded verbatim at the time and had never once used.

*Not of the work.*

Pascual said it before he said anything else and it took him four words. What Reggie did with the
rest of the sentence was correct and he does not withdraw it: he took it as an account of a load —
twelve years of being the only man in the house who knew anything, never asked and never able to be
asked, nobody's fault — and he filed it as a load, and understood the setting down of it as a
relief, and has been glad of the relief for twenty years.

He did nothing whatever with the four words at the front. He took them for a courtesy, which is
what he takes most things for.

They are not a courtesy. They are a statement about the work, made by the only man on earth in a
position to make one, at a moment when he was sparing nobody anything and had about four seconds to
do it in. And what it says is that the work was not the thing he was tired of.

Nor were they an afterthought. Pascual put them first, ahead of the rest, before he had any notion
where the rest was going; and Pascual does not say a thing to be kind unless it is also true, and
has not in the thirty-two years Reggie has known him. He knew what he was saying when he said it.
What he cannot have known, and what Reggie does not believe anybody could have known in that room,
is what would follow from it.

Which means a thing can be a load and be the whole of a man at the same time, and that setting the
load down does not hand back the other half of it.

---

The fourth piece is about himself and is therefore not admissible, and he included it anyway,
because he does not believe a man is entitled to leave a load-bearing item out of his working merely
because it is inconvenient that it should be there.

The other half had one object. Reggie is it. There is not another one.

---

He put the four pieces together in the hotel on the Wednesday night at about half past twelve. What
came out was an inference about the inside of another person, which is the class of judgement he has
attempted five times in his life and been right on twice, which he regards himself as disqualified
from, and for which he keeps a file.

He also made the objection, and it is the strongest objection there is, and nobody else would have
made it.

The objection is that he wants it to be true.

He has held one account of 1954 to 1966 for twenty years, and it is the account he was given by the
only reliable instrument in his life and he does not propose to argue with it. But if the finding is
also true, then the twelve years were not simply a thing extracted from one man by another, and the
ending of them cost them both, and neither of them is the debtor. That is a considerably easier
thing to hold. A man who arrives by inference at an easier version of an old injury ought to suspect
the inference before he publishes it.

He spent an hour on that and could not shift the four pieces, and concluded that the desirability
of a result is not evidence against the result. It is only evidence against the man reporting it.
The correct handling in such a case is to state the interest along with the finding and let the
other party weigh it.

Which is what he intended to do, and did.

---

At a quarter past nine there was a pause and he did not fill it, and then he found that he had no
procedure whatever for what came next and was going to do it in any case.

He put the glass down on the floor beside the chair.

"There is a thing I have had since the December," he said, "and I am going to give you the finding
first and then the grounds, because the grounds take about ten minutes and you should know what they
are grounds for."

He put his hands flat on his knees.

## VI

*Pascual.*

He knew what was coming before the first sentence was out, and he has tried since to establish
whether that is true or whether he has put it in afterwards, and he thinks it is true.

The contractions go. The verbs go to the end. The whole man arrives in the register he keeps for the
Colonel and for bad news and for anything that costs him something. Pascual had that perhaps four
hundred times between 1954 and 1966 and could hear it coming from the next room through a shut door.

He had not heard it in twenty years and it went through him like the cold tap.

---

"It is not about 1966. I want that first, because you will assume it is. In 1966 you told me what
you were tired by and you were exact about it and it cost you something to say and I have not
revised one word of it in twenty years and I am not revising it now. This is about afterwards.

"The finding is that you have not been used since 1966.

"You get everything you know about a person by looking at them, and you have done it for forty years
and you cannot switch it off. But it is not a general faculty and I want to be exact, because the
general version is common and worth very little and yours is not that. Yours was made and calibrated
over twelve years against one object, and the object is a man who cannot report himself, and I am
the object. There is not another one. Ramón Vidal comes in and says what is the matter with him.
That is the correct arrangement, it is the one I would have chosen for you, and I have never for one
hour wished it otherwise.

"I am not saying you are unhappy. I have no grounds for that and I do not believe it. I have watched
you in that man's house for three days and you are happy, and it is not a consolation and it is not
second best and he is not standing in for anybody. I am saying that you are not used. Those are
different things and I am aware that most people would say they are not."

Then he gave the grounds, in order, the way he gives anything.

The rate: one non-work question in nineteen years and seventy-seven letters. The October of 1983 and
the three observations a man makes, of which Pascual asked for one. And what Reggie now thinks that
letter was, which was a man reaching for a job — making work at four thousand miles, in the one
place where making it would not be a complaint against anybody in the house.

Then Nairn's ruling, and the second figure sent unasked, and *my compliments to Nairn* coming back,
which Reggie did not understand for a week and now understands to have meant *there is somebody in
the room, so I can stop.*

Then the Tuesday. The chairman coming across the carpet with something the matter about a car, and
four seconds, and then Pascual sitting back and being tired. "I have caused that fatigue perhaps a
thousand times and I know exactly what it is. What I had never seen was it arriving after four
seconds of a stranger. That is not what a practised movement costs."

Then the third thing, which he had not built at all.

"You gave me this one yourself and I did not use it for twenty years.

"In 1966, before you said anything else, you said *not of the work.* Three words. And then you told
me what you were tired of, and that was an account of a load, and it was accurate and it was the
whole of what either of us had. I filed it as a load: a thing carried and put down, and the putting
down a relief, and I have been glad you put it down for twenty years.

"I took the four words at the front for a courtesy. I take most things for a courtesy. They are not
one. They are a statement about the work, made by the only man on earth in a position to make one,
at a moment when you were sparing nobody anything and had about four seconds to do it in. And what
it says is that the work was not the thing you were tired of.

"And you knew that when you said it. You put it first, before you knew where the rest of it was
going, and you have never in thirty-two years said a thing to be kind that was not also true. I am
not telling you something about yourself that you did not have. I am telling you that I had it, in
my own hand, in my own file, for twenty years, and did not read it.

"Which means a thing can be a load and be the whole of a man at once, and putting the load down does
not hand back the other half. What follows from it is a different matter and I do not think anybody
in that room had that. I am fairly confident you did not have it until about 1972."

Then the fourth thing, which he flagged as inadmissible and gave anyway: that the other half had one
object, and that he is it, and that there is not another one.

"And Nairn is not a second one of you. I want that said, because you have been thinking it since
Monday and it is not the case. He has no faculty of that kind at all. He has a procedure. He asks me
a question with a number for an answer, and writes the number down, and keeps the paper, and once a
year a woman reads a platform scale and he writes that down under last year's. Anybody could run it.
It costs him nothing, which is why he is sixty-five and still standing, and why it will still be
running in ten years. You had no procedure. You had yourself. It took twelve years to make, it could
not be made twice, and it stopped having anything in front of it when you were fifty-five."

Then the constraint.

"I believe you have never said any of this, and that you cannot say it in this house, because there
is no form of words for it that does not come out as a complaint against a man who has done nothing
whatever wrong. There is no third kind of sentence. I know the class is empty because I have looked
for it four times in sixteen years, for a different sentence, and it is not there."

Then the objection.

"I want it to be true. If it is true, then the twelve years were not simply a thing taken out of one
man by another, and the ending of them cost us both, and neither of us is in the other's debt. That
is a great deal easier to hold than what I have held since 1966. A man who reasons his way to an
easier version of an old injury should say so out loud before anybody believes him. I have spent an
hour on it and I cannot shift the grounds. I put it to you as it stands.

"I have no remedy and I am not proposing anything, because there is nothing to propose. I am aware
that a report of this kind is a weight, and that I am four thousand miles off, and that there is a
man in this house who is owed better than to have it brought in. Nairn's rule is that when you
cannot tell two hypotheses apart and the observation costs four minutes, you take the observation
and you send it and you let the man at the other end throw it away.

"I am sending it. You may throw it away."

---

Here is what Pascual had, in the order he had it, and he is not proud of the order but the order is
what it was.

First: he did it. Reggie Tar, unasked, out of grounds he had built himself over three months,
sitting down, with the interest declared and the objection made and the whole of it laid out so that
it could be checked. Twelve years Pascual lived with a man who could not do that. Not would not —
could not. There was no engine in there and Pascual had established it beyond argument by 1958 and
had built his whole life on the finding and had left in 1966 partly because the finding was
permanent.

Second, and it arrived about a second later, and it is the one that took his legs off: he did not
build that.

And he had, in the same second, the professional judgement, which he would give under oath. The
Scotchman's method is better than his was. Not kinder — better. Pascual's version required a man who
could get a whole condition off a shoulder in a doorway, and there was one of those, and it ran him
into the ground in twelve years, and there was no relief built into it anywhere because none could
be. The Scotchman's version requires a card, a platform scale, and the habit of asking a question
with a number in it. It has no genius in it at all. It costs nothing, it can be run by anybody who
cares enough to keep the paper, and it will still be running when the man running it is eighty.

Pascual has had two weeks to find a fault in it and there is not one. That is not a thing he is
saying to be generous. It is a finding and he would defend it.

Third, and he checked it twice in the ten minutes afterwards and it is the part he is most confident
of: it is not a repair. Nothing has been mended. The mechanism has not changed. The man had just
talked for eleven minutes and had not said one word about his own condition and would not have been
able to if the house had been alight, and it had not occurred to him that this was in any way
remarkable.

What has gone is the standing conclusion — the one that said *I am categorically unable to do this
and must not attempt it.* That is all that has gone. And that is everything.

---

"Was that the whole of it?" Pascual said. His voice was not right and he let it be not right.

"That is the whole of it."

"Then you are wrong about the date, and right about everything else, and I am going to give you the
correction now because you will want to write it down."

Reggie took the notebook out of his inside pocket and put it on his knee and uncapped the pen, which
is a thing Pascual watched him do perhaps two thousand times and had not expected to watch again.

"It had work until the March of 1970. Not 1966. Hester Bevan wrote to me twice a year, June and
December, from the November of 1966 — four lines each, no more. Your weight, your hours, whether you
had been off the station, whether you were carrying anything you should not have been. She never
asked me a question and never gave me an opening to say anything about myself, and I understood that
at the time to be a kindness and I have since decided it was a judgement. I have all eight of them.
One is in the drawer in the next room with your January of 1984 folded against it.

"The last is the December of 1969. She went to England in the March of 1970 and nothing came in the
June, and I knew inside a minute what that meant, and I worked out inside another minute what would
have to be done about it, which was a note to you, or a note to Leslie, or a name given to the
Colonel's successor with a quiet word attached."

"You did not write it."

"I did not write it. I have never satisfactorily accounted to myself for that and I have had sixteen
years." He was looking at the boards. "Ramón came in the March of that year. Those two things are
three weeks apart and I have never once let myself put them in the same sentence until this minute."

Reggie wrote for a while.

Then he said: "Then the figure is sixteen years and not nineteen, and I was out by three and a half,
and the error runs in the same direction as the interest I declared. Which is what an error of that
kind does. I shall enter the correction with the finding."

"I know you will," said Pascual. "That is why I gave it to you."

---

And then Pascual asked him a question, and noticed himself doing it, having spent the previous night
in a kitchen at half past midnight telling Ramón Vidal that he never did.

"How many are in that file?"

"Four." Reggie capped the pen. "You are the first of them, and I am going to give you the whole of
it, because it is arithmetic and you are entitled to it.

"Before the twenty-second of September 1966 that file was empty. I had attempted a judgement of that
class perhaps five times in my life. Two of them I never learnt the answer to. Three of them I was
wrong about, and one of the three had a control on it and I still got it wrong. What I concluded —
at some point before I was twenty — was that I am not able to do this, that the inability is
categorical rather than a matter of practice, and that I ought not to attempt it. That conclusion
was doing an enormous amount of work and I want you to understand how much. It is the reason I have
never in my life put a thing of that kind to anybody. Not to Leslie, not to the Bevans, not to you
in twelve years, not once.

"In 1966 I put one to you. I had no business doing it and I did it because the alternative was that
you should leave on a reason less true than the one available. And you could have argued with me.
You would have been within your rights and you would have won, because you were the only instrument
in my life I trusted absolutely on a question about another person's interior, and if you had let
the *no* stand on its own I should have believed you completely and at once and withdrawn the whole
of it, and the file would have stayed empty, and the conclusion would have stood for the remaining
thirty years.

"You did not let it stand. You heard how fast you had said it and you said the other thing, and it
took you about four seconds, and I do not think you knew what you were doing.

"That is one confirmed result. One is not a rate and I have never once pretended otherwise. What one
result does is remove the word *categorically*, and *categorically* was the load-bearing word.

"I did not attempt a second for sixteen years. In the October of 1982 Leslie put a fact in a letter
that did not fit, and I had five weeks over it, and I put a judgement to Nairn about a chair at
Reading, and it was confirmed. In the June of 1985 I put one to him about Fife, and it was confirmed.
And in the December I found a rate in a correspondence file and it has taken me until this evening.

"So the arithmetic is that there are four observations in that file, and all four of them are
downstream of four seconds in a room in 1966, and the fourth one is about you."

He stopped. Then he said, in exactly the same voice:

"I had not put it in that order before this minute. I should have. It has been available the whole
time."

---

Pascual has had two weeks with that and it has not settled and he does not expect it to.

He has held the September of 1966 for twenty years as a thing done to that man, and he has been very
exact about it. He said the true thing, which he had never said to anybody, and then he got on an
aeroplane, and the saying of it did not stop him going and was not intended to and he has never
allowed himself the comfort of pretending it made any difference.

It made all the difference there is. He did not know that. It went in four seconds, out of the top
of his head, because he had been cornered by a man who had no right to corner him, and it is the
most consequential thing he has ever done for that man, and he did it by accident, and did not find
out for twenty years.

And there is the other half, which he had inside a second and which he is not going to pretend he
did not have: he gave the man the capability and then left, and it was spent twice on somebody else
before it came back to him.

He did not say that. There was nothing in it that was Reggie's to answer for and no reason to put it
in the room.

---

He asked the question at about half past ten, and Pascual had been waiting twenty minutes for it,
because he knew the form and had known it since 1955.

"Was the reading correct in every particular and not merely in substance?"

"In every particular except the date. And you have the date now."

Reggie wrote that down too, with the time, and put the notebook away, and asked whether there was
any coffee left. They talked about the drainage for another twenty minutes and it was, taking it all
in all, the best twenty minutes of Pascual's year.

---

He looked at him properly, of course. There was no version of that evening in which he was going to
stop himself, and it was the first time in twenty years the thing had been pointed at the only object
it fits.

What it returned was this.

The man is in good order. Nine stone one and steady three years running. Sleeps six hours and says so
when asked. Eyes are the eyes of a man of sixty-nine and no worse than that. He is not being handled
and he is not being managed and nobody has made a project of him. He is being maintained,
competently, by somebody who understood the specification and built to it.

It is not what Pascual would have done.

It is better.

## VII

*Nairn, and afterwards.*

Ramón came in from the porch at eleven and did not ask anything, because he told Pascual in 1970 that
he was no good at guessing and would rather be told, and has never had to say it twice.

Pascual told him. It took about ten minutes, which is longer than these things usually take in that
house. A man in a chair with his hands flat on his knees; a finding about the sixteen years since,
and a note at the top saying it was not about 1966; a date wrong by three and a half years; and a
file with four entries in it that would have had none at all if a tired man had let one word stand on
its own.

"He got all that on his own," said Ramón.

"On his own. He had it in the December."

Ramón sat down.

"I told the Scotchman this afternoon. About you not having anything hard to do. At the shutter, at
eight o'clock, and I wasn't thinking when I said it." He looked stricken. "He'll not have had time to
pass it on —"

"He didn't pass it on."

"No," said Ramón. "No, he'd not have." He looked at his hands. "I've known since about 1972."

Pascual did not say anything.

"Fourteen years. And I never asked you, and you know exactly why, because it's the same reason he
gave you tonight for the other thing, and he put it better in one sentence than I've managed in
fourteen years of not saying it." He was quiet for a moment. "And the rest of it. The four seconds in
sixty-six."

"Yes."

"So the best thing you ever did for him, you did by accident, in about the time it takes to drop a
glass."

"Yes."

"Aye, well," said Ramón. "That's most of them."

And he went in and put the kettle on, which is what he does at the end of things.

---

Douglas got it out of him on the aeroplane, over the Mona Passage, in three questions.

He had had it worked out that he was going to be told nothing, and had decided that was all right,
and had needed to decide it twice since take-off. Then Reggie put the papers down on the tray table
and said that there had been a fourth entry, and would he like the grounds.

Douglas said aye.

He got the whole of it. The December and the drainage figure that was not found. The rate. The
chairman crossing the carpet at half past nine and the four seconds and the sitting back. The error
of three and a half years running in the direction of the declared interest, which Reggie gave him in
exactly the same voice as the rest of it and with no apparent difficulty whatever.

Which means Douglas knows precisely how it was arrived at, and by whom, and when. December. A man in

The file has four entries in it now. He asked to see it in the April and was shown it at once,
without a moment's hesitation, because he had asked.

    ix.66     Pobil — tired. "Of me" denied at once, denial believed and accepted.
              Tired by the arrangement: confirmed by subject in the same minute,
              with grounds, unprompted. Both recorded. Closed 1966.
    x.82      Nairn — Reading chair; waiting to be asked. Confirmed by subject.
    vi.85     Nairn — Fife; has not been since 1977, minds it, would not say so.
              Confirmed by subject.
    iii.86    Pobil — not used since 1966; minds it; cannot say so in that house.
              Confirmed by subject, with correction: 1970, not 1966. Error of 3½ years
              in the direction of the stated interest.

Four in twenty years, and every one of them the same question, which is whether a man in a room is
carrying something he is not going to mention.

Douglas noticed that in the April and has never raised it, and he will give the reasoning to anybody
who asks him, and nobody has. It is this. A man who owns one instrument and knows its range to the
yard is better placed than most of the people Douglas has worked with in forty years. There is no
purpose whatever in telling him that his one faculty is narrow, because the narrowness is the reason
it works.

What he thought, reading the file in the April with the lamp between them and the card against the
lamp, was that a man who writes the correction into the entry in the same hand as the entry is not a
man who is bad at this.

He did not say that either. He is keeping it.

What he said was: "Nine pages of grounds for four lines of finding. There's not a journal on earth
would look at a study with an n of four."

"No," said Reggie. "There is not."
