# Discontinuation — Variant C

## I

The letter about Ramón came in the October of 1970, and it was one side, and it was not
quarterly and it was not about work.

It said that Pascual had been living since the March with a man who kept a pharmacy in
Santurce and was a widower with three daughters; that he had not looked for it and had not
expected it; and that he was writing because he had never been able to defend keeping a thing
from him and did not propose to begin.

There was no question in it.

Reggie read it at the desk and then went and stood at the window for a period he did not time.

He then spent about four days on a sentence to put in the December letter, and there is not
one. Everything he produced was either a lie or a wound. The versions expressing pleasure were
untrue and would have been detected, not by anybody else but by him, afterwards, at intervals,
for the rest of his life. The versions that were true were a report on his own condition, which
had not been asked for and would have been a considerable burden to a man four thousand miles
off who had done nothing whatever wrong and had gone to some trouble to be honest about it.

There is no third class. He worked at it properly and satisfied himself that the class is
empty, and he does not regard that as a failure. A thing that does not exist cannot be found,
and a man who goes on looking for it after he has established that it does not exist is not
being conscientious, he is indulging himself.

He wrote four paragraphs about the eastern pens and put a question at the end.

---

He also drew a conclusion, and entered it.

A man who writes to tell you he has somebody is telling you it is finished. That is not a
subtle inference and it does not require a faculty of any kind; it is what the letter is for.
He recorded it accordingly. October 1970. One result. Closed.

The September of 1966 stayed in the file, because he has never removed anything from a file in
his life. He reclassified it as historical, which is the correct handling of a result that has
been superseded, and he stopped taking it out.

He took it out in the January and again in the June and has not counted the others.

---

In the March of 1972 he wrote about the eastern pens and the drainage, four paragraphs, with a
figure Pascual had asked for; and in the third paragraph, between a note on the sluice and a
question about a supplier, without emphasis, in the middle of a sentence about the working day, he
mentioned that he had kept the practice of eating at one o'clock.

He did not remark on it. He did not know that he had said anything.

He found it on a carbon seven years later and stood in the file room holding it for a while, and
has never worked out how it got there, and it is the only sentence he has ever written that he
cannot account for.

## II

The first letter from Rothamsted came in the February of 1973 and was addressed to *R. Tar,
Esq., Wm Martin Institute, Trinidad,* which is not the form and never has been, and Reggie
opened it at the desk expecting a request for reprints.

It was four pages and it was about Appendix C.

The writer gave his name as D. Nairn and said that he was assembling something on
repeated-measures designs in vertebrate work where the number of animals cannot be got above
about a dozen; that he had come to Tar 1955 late and by accident and had then read it three
times; that the crossed design in the body of the paper was better than anything else he had
found in the decade and had been cited eleven times, all of them for the wrong result; and that
he wished to raise a matter about the appendix.

The matter about the appendix was this. The analysis in Appendix C was not in the same hand as
the rest of the paper. It was not merely better; it was differently thought. Whoever had done
it had seen the difficulty about the third week before anybody in the field had a name for it,
and had dealt with it in a footnote, and the footnote was the most useful thing in the whole of
the literature Nairn had read that year, and it was unsigned.

*I am not making an accusation,* he wrote. *I am asking a question of fact. Who did that? I
should like to cite them and at present I cannot.*

---

Reggie replied the same day, which he does not do, and the reply took him eleven minutes.

He wrote that the analysis in Appendix C was Dr Jean Leslie's; that it was entirely hers, in
conception and in execution; that she had brought it to him in the June of 1955 and he had
looked at it for a quarter of an hour and told her it was correct and put it in an appendix;
that the decision to put it in an appendix was his and was wrong; that his reason at the time
had been that the body of the paper was already long, which was true and was not the reason;
and that the real reason was that he had not understood in 1955 what he was looking at and had
therefore filed it at the level he had understood it at, which is a fault he has since found in
himself in other connections and has not corrected.

He gave her address in Aberdeen.

He added that Dr Leslie had been asked to do a great deal of that kind of thing over the years,
by more than one person, and that Nairn would be doing him a service by citing her by name and
in the text.

Then he answered the four technical questions, at length, and put a question at the end,
because a letter without a question in it does not require an answer.

## III

Nairn cited her in the text, twice, and sent her the offprint, and wrote to Aberdeen with an
apology for having got at her through a third party.

Jean answered in about a fortnight. She was pleasant about the citation and said several exact
things about the third-week problem and then, at the bottom, under her signature, she wrote:

*Whatever it is you are actually asking me, the answer is that he will not mind being asked. He
will answer anything. Nobody has ever believed me about that and it has cost two people I know
of a great deal, and one of them is me.*

Nairn read that four times and did not reply to it and has kept the letter.

## IV

The correspondence ran from the February of 1973 and by the autumn of that year it was
fortnightly and by the spring of 1974 it was not possible to say what the interval was.

It was about the work. It was entirely about the work, and it got worse.

By the eleventh letter they had abandoned the four-paragraph form. By the twentieth Nairn was
sending plots drawn by hand on the back of Rothamsted paper, with the axes labelled in a
sloping capital and a note in the margin saying *ignore the point at week 6, I have written to
the man about it.* Reggie pinned two of them to the wall of the outer office, where nothing has
ever been pinned, and a junior asked him what they were and he said that they were somebody
else's residuals.

The fourth week is this, since it runs for eleven years and nobody in the correspondence ever
troubles to define it. Seven animals, measured weekly. Two things are moving at once — the
treatment, and the animal settling into the pen — and for the first three weeks the settling is
steep and the treatment is small and a child could tell them apart. By the fourth week they are
the same size and going the same way. Reggie held, from 1956, that the staggered entry dates got
them apart. Nairn held that a stagger of nine weeks across seven animals got nothing apart at all.

It is the only thing they never resolved by post.

In the June of 1974 he wrote a letter on a Tuesday for no reason. It was not the reply to
anything. It began *I have been thinking about your point on the fourth week* and ran to nine
pages, and when he had sealed it he sat with his hand flat on it for a moment and then took it
down to the post.

Everything he sends has to have a heading. It went into the index under *Correspondence:
Rothamsted, statistical,* because there was nowhere else, and the index is the reason he can
originate letters at all, and anything with a heading can be done.

He has never queried the heading.

---

In the March of 1975 there was a letter which was not about the work, and it was a page and a
half, and it was the most orderly thing Reggie had ever been sent.

Nairn wrote that he had noticed, at about letter twenty, that Reggie answered every question
put to him, completely, without hedging and without asking why it was being asked; and that he
volunteered nothing whatever. He said that he had thought this might be his own imagination, so
he had tested it, and he set out how: from the September he had varied the number of questions
per letter between none and four, deliberately, without a pattern, and he had recorded the
length and content of the replies, and he had the table in front of him and it was not
ambiguous.

*This was not fair and I am not going to pretend it was,* he wrote. *I ran an experiment on a
correspondent without telling him. I am telling you now because the result is more use to me if
you know I have it, and because I would rather be in the wrong out loud than quietly.*

*What I want to know is whether the reading is right. If it is right I should like to know
whether it is reticence, in which case I will stop asking, or something else, in which case I
will go on asking and you may take it that I know what I am doing.*

---

Reggie answered inside the week, and the answer is the most complete statement of the mechanism
he has ever produced or will produce, and he was able to produce it because he had been asked a
direct question about how a thing works, and that is not the class of question he fails.

He wrote that the reading was correct and that the table was almost certainly right in its
particulars. He wrote that it was not reticence and that he wished to be exact about this,
because reticence implies a thing withheld, and there is nothing withheld. Asked a question, he
will give the truth to four decimal places and no rounding, on any subject, including himself,
and he will not find it difficult and will not resent it. Not asked, there is no mechanism. He
does not decline to speak. There is no engine in there that starts by itself, and he has known
this since he was about eleven, and has never found a way round it, and has stopped looking.

He wrote that the correct handling was therefore to go on asking, and that Nairn should not
expect the position to improve with familiarity, since it has not improved in fifty-six years
and there is no reason in the mechanism why it should.

He wrote: *You should be aware that this is expensive to the other party and that I have no way
of reducing the cost. I have watched it exhaust a man over twelve years. I set it down so that
you may price it before you go further, which is what I should want if I were the one being
asked to carry it.*

He put no question at the end.

Nairn wrote back by return, one line, which arrived seventeen days later on account of the post:

*Noted. Carry on.*

## V

They met at Kingston in the September of 1975, at a meeting Reggie had agreed to give a paper
at fourteen months earlier for institutional reasons and had regretted ever since.

The paper went as those things go. There were eleven minutes for questions and the room had
been treating the work as though the number of animals were large, which it is not and never
was, and the third questioner was a man about Reggie's own age standing at the back with his
hands in his pockets, who said:

"Could you say how many animals."

"Seven."

"Thank you," said the man, and sat down.

That was the whole of the intervention. It took nine seconds and it was the only useful thing
anybody did for the work that morning, and Reggie recognised the operation at once and could
not have said from where.

It was two years before he put it together. It is his own rule, the first of the six on the
card in the second drawer: *ask a question with a number for an answer.* He had derived it in
1971 from a woman who had used it on him in 1966, who had had it from a man who never wrote it
down because he did not have to; and in the September of 1975 it came at him from the floor of
a lecture theatre in Kingston, from a stranger, who had arrived at it entirely on his own from
thirty years of field trials and had never in his life thought of it as anything but the
avoidance of an error.

---

Nairn did the originating. All of it, for four days, without once making it look like work.

The meeting ran Monday to Friday.

He came up afterwards and said, "Douglas Nairn," and then, before Reggie had got anything out: "I
know. Forty-one letters, every question in all of them answered inside the week, and not one of
them about me. I'm going to talk for a bit and you can stop me."

He talked for a bit. He was a spare man of fifty-five going grey at the sides, with a Fife accent
worn down by thirty years in Hertfordshire, and he stood too close and did not appear to know it,
and he had brought the offprint with the two citations in it and put it into Reggie's hands as
though handing over a receipt.

On the second evening he said, without preamble, that he had lived with a man called Alec from
1946 until 1958, that Alec had been a schoolmaster and had died in the February, and that he had
been on his own since, and that he was telling
Reggie this at the beginning rather than the end because he had spent his whole professional
life explaining to agronomists that you cannot analyse a trial you have not described.

Reggie said, "I see."

"You can say nothing to that. I'm not fishing."

"I am not able to say the corresponding thing."

"I know. Leslie told me. Not in those words." Nairn put his glass down. "I'll ask you when I
want to know."

And he did, an hour later, and got the whole of it: 1954 to 1966, the arrangement, the reading,
the fourteenth of July, the Tuesday in the September, and the one thing Reggie had originated in
his life and had got right. It took about forty minutes and Reggie did not stop once and did not
soften any of it, because he had been asked.

Nairn listened to all of it. Then he said: "Right. He was correct to go and you were correct to
say it and I'd have done neither."

He did not say anything else about it that week, or for some years.

What he did do, for about a day and a half, was be very angry indeed with a man he had never met;
and then he took the anger out and looked at it and put it down, because there was nothing in the
account he had been given that anybody had done wrong, and because it was about a thing that had
been survived by the only person entitled to a view, and because he does not keep findings he
cannot support.

It has come back perhaps four times in nine years. It is the one thing he has never said out
loud, and he is aware of the irony and does not find it funny.

---

On the third night he said, in the corridor, in the voice he kept for reading out a column of
figures:
"I'd like to come in. You can say no and it won't make any difference to the fourth week."

Reggie found that he would like to sit down, and did not, and said that he would prefer him to
come in.

What is worth setting down about the rest of it, since he is the only person who will ever have
occasion to set it down, is that it was the one room in the house where he did not have to be
asked anything.

There is an engine in there and it starts. It has always started. He wants a great deal, and
what he wants is specific and is not what a man of his description is generally supposed to
want: he wants to be the cause of it, he wants to watch it happen, and he will work at it with
the whole of the attention he brings to everything and will not stop until it has, and the
gratification is not vicarious and is not modest and he has never once been confused about that.

Nairn said so in the morning, plainly, in about eleven words, being a man who says things.

Reggie was more disconcerted by that than by anything else in the four days. It had not occurred
to him that it was visible. He has since concluded that it must always have been visible, and
that in twelve years nobody had ever told him, and he does not know what to do with that and has
not filed it anywhere.

## VI

The two years after Kingston went in a way that Nairn had been warned about and had priced
correctly, which is the only reason there were two years.

What Douglas had was not a perception, and he would separate the two himself if anybody asked, and
nobody has. He did not look at Reggie Tar and see damage. He has never in his life looked at
anybody and seen anything, and it is one of the three or four facts about himself he is entirely
confident of. What he had was a history, taken verbally, on the second evening, in about forty
minutes, from the subject, on request; and a fortnight to think about it, on an aeroplane and at
a desk in Harpenden; and a position at the end of it he could state in a line, which is that the
man had spent twelve years being read by somebody who wore out doing it, and had then lost him,
and had drawn the only conclusion available to him, and that anybody arriving afterwards was
arriving at a site with known contamination and had better plan for it.

That is a reasoning and not a sympathy, and it is why it held for nine years. Sympathy is a
consumable.

Reggie asked the same question about once a month.

Not in the same words. He varied it, and the variation was not a device; each time he genuinely
believed he was asking a different question, because each time he had found a new place where
the reading might have gone wrong. Whether the position had been correctly understood. Whether
Nairn had intended what Reggie had taken him to intend on the third night in Kingston. Whether
the arrangement was of a kind Nairn wished to continue at the present interval. Whether anything
in the last letter should have been read as a withdrawal.

Nairn answered every one of them, in full, without ever once indicating that it had been asked
before.

Reggie has the count. It is in the second drawer with the card and the equipment log, and it
comes to twenty-six in the first two years, and the fact that he has the count and Nairn has
never referred to it is the single item from that period he has never been able to account for
and has taken out and looked at more often than he can defend.

It is also — he has been over this and the working is sound — the whole of the reason the two
years did not end. Any man alive would have said *you asked me that in the March.* The saying of
it would have been reasonable and mild and completely fatal, because Reggie would have concluded
that the asking was a fault, and would have stopped, and would have gone on being unable to
determine the answer for the rest of his life in perfect good faith.

He has never told Nairn this and Nairn does not know it, and Nairn, asked, would say that he
answered because he was asked and that he cannot see what else a person would do.

---

There were two errors in that period and both of them were Douglas's and he identified both
himself.

The first was in the November of 1975, when he said something admiring about Reggie rather than
about the work, and Reggie looked at him the way a man looks at an arithmetical error he is
about to find himself, and the conversation had to be got out of sideways. Douglas did not do it
again. He worked out inside a month what the rule is — the work can be praised without limit
and will be handed straight on to whoever did that part of it; the man cannot be praised at all
— and he has kept to it for nine years and has grumbled about it exactly twice.

The second was in the February of 1977, when he asked Reggie how he felt about the closure of
the western holding pens.

He got an answer. He got a complete, truthful, carefully constructed answer about the disposal
of the animals, the effect on the Michaelmas intake and the two members of staff whose work it
had been, and it was four minutes long and there was not one word in it about how anybody felt,
and Reggie was not evading anything and did not know that a question had failed.

Douglas went away and thought about it and concluded that the fault was in the instrument and not
in the specimen, which is what he would have concluded about a badly-worded question in a field
trial, and he did not ask that class of question again.

What he asked instead, from the March of 1977 onward, was numbers.

## VII

He came out in the September of 1977 to a two-year appointment at St Augustine, which is ten
miles off, and which he had gone after himself in the January without telling anybody, and which
he described to the Faculty as the natural continuation of a line of work and to Reggie as the
natural continuation of a line of work.

The first eight months were bad and were bad in a way neither of them had predicted, and it was
nothing to do with feeling.

It was the hours.

It was not the company. That is what took them both so long to see, because the company was the
thing they had each privately expected to be difficult, and it was not difficult for one hour.

Pascual had lived on the station. For twelve years he was in the building — continuously, under
the heading of the working day, because he was staff and his hours were the institution's hours.
There was no door. He did not arrive.

Nairn is at St Augustine and he comes. Every evening is a door opening at some point between
seven and ten, and a door opening is an event, and Reggie's day is filed. Every hour in it has
been under a heading since 1954, and the filing is not a preference or a habit; it is the
mechanism by which the day is possible at all. He can hold a state indefinitely. He was got
through a war on a state. He has no procedure whatever for an event with nothing over it, and
there were eleven of them in the first fortnight.

So for eight months he was in a condition he could not name and did not report and would have
reported accurately if anybody had asked the right question, and nobody did, because the right
question had not been thought of yet.

Douglas worked it out in the May, from a remark about the laundry.

What he did about it was write a heading. He put it on a card and propped it against the lamp:
*Evening: Nairn. From half past seven.* It is the least romantic object either of them has ever
owned and it fixed the whole thing inside a fortnight, and it is still against the lamp, and
Reggie has never remarked on it and has twice had it re-copied when the ink went.

It schedules nothing. He came at half past seven before the card and came at half past seven
after it. What it does is convert an event into a state with a start time, and it is the first
heading Reggie ever wrote for an hour that was not the station's.

It also establishes, and neither of them has ever said this out loud, that in twelve years there
was not one unfiled hour in that house. Which is why it was survivable. And why it could never be
looked at.

---

He had been in the letters to San Juan since 1973.

Not by any decision. Reggie writes four paragraphs quarterly under the heading *Correspondence:
San Juan, veterinary,* and a man at Rothamsted who takes a different view of the fourth week is a
professional fact, and professional facts go in. *Nairn at Rothamsted holds that the stagger
identifies nothing.* *I have had a plot from Nairn which I am unable to fault.* *Nairn will be at
Kingston in the September.*

The frequency climbs. He is in every letter from 1974 and in more than one paragraph of most of
them by 1976, and Reggie does not know it is happening, in precisely the way he had not known
about the sentence in the March of 1972 concerning the practice of eating at one o'clock.

In the October of 1977, with Nairn a month at St Augustine, he sat down to write the letter that
says it plainly — on the grounds that a fact of that class does not go into a paragraph about a
sluice, and that he had been on the receiving end of exactly such a letter in 1970 and knew to
the pound what it had been worth.

He spent four days on it. Everything he produced was either a lie or a wound; there is no third
class; he had established that the class was empty seven years earlier and did not need to
establish it twice.

He wrote four paragraphs about the eastern pens and put a question at the end.

It did not matter. It had been going to San Juan four times a year since the February of 1973.

## VIII

In the March of 1978 Nairn asked him what he weighed.

Reggie said that he did not know, and that he had last been weighed on the ninth of December
1969, by Mrs Bevan, in the outer room with the door open, and that the figure had been nine
stone nine.

"You can give me that to the pound from nine years ago."

"I can give you most things to the pound from nine years ago. It is not a virtue. It is the
same faculty that cannot tell you anything else."

Nairn said nothing for a moment and then said, "Come upstairs."

There is a Salter platform in the corner of the medical room, under the window, about four feet
from the chair. Mrs Ramnarine had held the chest for three weeks and was very quick and did not
talk while she was doing it. She read it off and Nairn wrote it down, and it was eight stone
seven.

Nairn did the subtraction on the corner of a requisition, openly, because he does everything
openly.

"Sixteen pounds. Eight and a quarter years. Two pounds a year." He looked at it. "That won't
show in any one year. It'll show in any eight. It has."

Reggie said that he had not been ill.

"No. I don't think you have." Nairn put the pencil down. "The eating-at-one arrangement. When
was that set up?"

"The March of 1955."

"By whom."

"By Pascual."

"And who's looked at it since?"

Reggie considered the question for rather a long time, because it was a good question and he had
never been asked it, and the answer when it came was that nobody had looked at it since,
including him, on the grounds that it had been correct when it was issued.

"It was correct when it was issued," Nairn said. "It was probably exactly right. In 1955. For a
man of thirty-eight with somebody at the table taking the plate away when he stopped." He was
not angry and there was nothing in his face; he was reading a protocol, which is his trade. "You
have been running an instrument at its 1955 setting for twenty-three years with no calibration
and no operator. In a trial I'd have thrown the whole arm out."

"Yes," Reggie said.

He was not ashamed and Nairn did not want him to be. What he was, for about a minute and a half,
was extremely interested, in the way he is interested when a fault of a specified class turns up
in his own work: it was an uncalibrated instrument, left running, producing plausible readings
throughout, which is the most dangerous condition a piece of apparatus can be in and the hardest
to detect from inside.

Then he said: "I have no procedure for adjusting it."

"No," said Nairn. "That's what I'm for."

---

The instruction that replaced it is not a diet and Nairn was careful about that, having spent
thirty years watching men set targets and then defend them past the point of usefulness.

What it specifies is a review. He eats at one, as before, and the plate is looked at, and every
March they go up to the Salter and Mrs Ramnarine reads it off and Nairn writes it in the same
notebook under the previous one, and whatever it says, they look at it in the March.

That is the whole of the intervention and it is not clever and any competent man could have
designed it, and the reason it works when the 1955 instruction did not is not that it is better.
It is that it contains a step requiring somebody to be in the room, and there is somebody in the
room.

The figures go:

    iii.78   8 st 7
    iii.79   8 st 9
    iii.80   8 st 12
    iii.81   9 st 0
    iii.82   9 st 0
    iii.83   9 st 1
    iii.84   9 st 1

The March of 1979 is the interesting one, and it is Nairn's, and he had it wrong for a week.

Two pounds up in twelve months is not what a review protocol produces in its first year. A review
produces nothing in its first year; that is what a review is for. He said so out loud and was
annoyed about it, because he had designed the thing and wanted it to be the cause. Then he went
back to the March of 1978 figure and set it against the line running down from the December of
1969, and found that it had already been about two pounds high when he took it.

The intervention was not the instruction. The intervention was the September of 1977, and it had
been running six months before either of them thought to measure anything, and the entire designed
portion of the exercise is a correction applied to a slope that had already turned.

Reggie received this as a confound and was faintly put out, on the grounds that it renders the
series uninterpretable as evidence for anything.

Nairn was not put out in the smallest degree. It is the only trial he has ever run in which he was
delighted to lose the estimate.

---

He has not got back to nine stone nine and will not, and Nairn stopped expecting it in about
1981 and has never once described this as a disappointment, on the grounds that a stable series
is a result and a rising one is a windfall and it is not the business of an experimenter to
sulk about which he has been given.

---

Hester wrote once from England, in the autumn of 1978, having had it from Jean, who had had it
from Nairn.

The letter is four lines. It gives an address in Hampshire and says that the Colonel is much the
same and that she was glad to hear the news and hopes she may be told more of it in due course.

The last line is: *How many hours are you sleeping?*

He wrote back the same day. He wrote: *Six. Thank you for asking.*

Neither of them ever referred to it again, and it is the only letter of hers he kept, and it is
in the second drawer with the card.

## IX

*Nairn. The Fourth Week, October 1979.*

The drive out is fifty minutes if the Churchill-Roosevelt is behaving and an hour and ten if it
isn't, and Douglas has done it two hundred and some times and has stopped resenting it, which he
did not expect.

The card was against the lamp. It has been against the lamp since the May of 1978 and he has
watched it be re-copied twice, in a hand you could set type from, and he has never once said
anything about it, and there is a version of his life in which he is the sort of man who could get
through nine years without mentioning a card, and he has no idea where that man came from.

*Evening: Nairn. From half past seven.*

He got there at twenty past and sat in the car for ten minutes, because arriving early is an
unfiled event and he found that out the hard way.

---

Reggie had the 1956 run out on the desk. Not the paper — the run: the original sheets, seven
animals down the side and the weeks across, in pencil, with the corrections in a different pencil.

"Sit down, Nairn," he said. "I have taken the correlation."

"You've what?"

"Between the two time-vectors. Weeks in the pen against weeks on treatment, across the whole of
the 1956 material, with the actual entry dates." He had a finger on a figure at the foot of a
worked sheet. "It is nought point nine six."

Douglas sat down.

He had said, in letters, in 1974 and 1976 and twice in 1977, that the stagger was too narrow to
identify anything and that the separation was nominal. He had said it politely the first two times
and with some heat the last. He had never once been agreed with, and Reggie had never once got
cross about it, and the thing had gone round on its mooring every eleven months for six years.

"When did you get that?"

"The August."

"It's October."

"It is. I have spent two months attempting to find a way round it." He turned the sheet so it
faced Douglas, which is what he does instead of explaining. "There is not one. You were correct.
The stagger is nine weeks across seven animals, the two vectors are collinear for practical
purposes, and I have been maintaining the contrary since 1973. The design does not separate them.
It never did. I should have taken the correlation in 1956 and I did not take it because it did not
occur to me to take it, which is a worse account than not having seen the difficulty at all."

Douglas put his head back and laughed at the ceiling.

"Six years," he said. "Six years, and you sat on it for two months to see if you could get me out
of it."

"Not to get you out of it. To establish whether the objection stood."

"Same thing from where I'm sitting and it's the nicest thing anybody's done for me this year."

Reggie looked at him with the flat unhurried incomprehension he brings to any remark of that class,
and Douglas grinned at him and knew he was grinning and let it stand, because this is the one room
on earth where a man can grin at nothing and not be asked about it.

He thought — he has this thought perhaps twice a year and it always arrives whole — that if
February of 1973 had produced that one reply and nothing else had ever come of it, no Kingston, no
card, no house, he would still put 1973 down as the best year he has had since the war.

He does not say it. He has a list of about a dozen things not to say and that one is the easiest,
because it's a statement about himself and Reggie would take delivery of it as a fact about the
working day and thank him for it.

---

What he said was: "Right. Then it's dead."

"I do not think so."

"It's dead, Reggie. If you can't get them apart there's no estimate in it. Nine weeks of stagger,
seven animals, and there's no arrangement of those numbers that gives you a standard error a
person could publish. The whole line's a write-off, it's been a write-off since 1956, and neither
of us saw it."

"That would be so if the acclimation were monotone."

Douglas opened his mouth and shut it.

"It isn't," said Reggie. "They settle over about a fortnight, and they go on settling, and at
about the sixth week they come off it. There's a disturbance. Eight to twelve days. It isn't
distress and it isn't a relapse — it's the point at which the animal has finished learning the pen
and starts testing it. Any keeper on this station could have told you that in 1956. It's in the
daybooks. It isn't in the paper because it isn't a result, it's a nuisance."

"There's a bump."

"There's a bump."

"At week six."

"At week six."

Douglas put both hands over his face, which he does, and which Reggie has never remarked on.

"I told you to ignore that point," he said into his hands. "I wrote it on the plot. I've still got
the plot. *Ignore the point at week six, I have written to the man about it.* Nineteen
seventy-four."

"You did. I did not ignore it."

"No. You pinned it to the wall."

"I pinned it up because you'd drawn it," Reggie said, "and the objection to week six I've never
once been able to sustain, and I didn't raise it because I had no ground for the objection beyond
having watched the animals, which isn't a ground."

"It's the only ground there is."

"It isn't statistical."

"It's *identifying*." Douglas was on his feet and had no memory of getting there. "That's the
whole thing. If one of your two vectors has a kink in it and the other hasn't, they can't be
collinear — the kink *is* the separation. Your treatment goes up in a straight line and your
acclimation does something at week six that a straight line can't do, and the second it does that
the design comes back. Nought nine six on the linear part and it doesn't matter, because you're
not estimating off the linear part any more. You're estimating off the eight days where they come
apart."

Reggie did not say anything for a moment. Then his mouth went, very slightly, at one corner, and
stayed there for about a second and a half before he got it back.

Douglas has seen that eleven times in six years and could name all eleven, and it goes through him
every time like the first cold mouthful of a drink, and he has never mentioned it and is never
going to.

"I threw out the only informative observation in the trial," he said. "In writing. On a plot. And
posted it."

"You did."

"You could have said."

"I did say. In the March of 1975, in about four lines, and you wrote back that it was an outlier
and gave three reasons, and two of the three were good."

"Which one wasn't?"

"The third," said Reggie, and got up to fetch the daybooks, and did not tell him which the third
was, and Douglas sat there absolutely delighted and shouted after him that he was a hard man, and
got no answer at all.

---

He was gone eleven minutes.

Douglas sat with the card against the lamp and the 1956 sheets under his hand, and had, in the way
these things arrive, a completely unarranged thought about Alec, who died in the February of 1958
and who was a schoolmaster and a kind man and never once pretended to know what a standard error
was.

Twelve years. Warm evenings, a good table, the wireless on. He had told Alec nothing whatever
about his work in twelve years — not out of secrecy, there was simply no port on that side of the
machine — and it had never for one hour felt like a lack, and it wasn't one.

It is only a lack now, backwards, for about four seconds at a time, and it is not a comparison and
he will not let it become one. He misses him. He misses him in the ordinary way, less than he did,
at intervals. He said so to Reggie in the second month, because he was asked, and what he said was
that a man of fifty-five is not obliged to have had only the one life, and he meant it then and
means it now.

He was thirty-eight when Alec died and fifty-three when the first letter came from Trinidad, and
in between he had not settled the question of whether he wanted anybody. He had stopped noticing
there was one. That is worse and he knows it is worse and he has never been able to make it sound
like anything but what it is.

Then Reggie came back with four daybooks and a ledger under his arm and put the lot down hard
enough to move the lamp, and Douglas stopped thinking about 1958.

---

They had it out on the floor by nine.

The bump is in all seven. It's in all seven and it's in the four animals from 1954 as well, which
Reggie hadn't looked at since 1955 and produced from a cupboard without comment; and the interval
isn't identical — it runs from about day thirty-eight to about day fifty and the width varies with
the animal — and that is better, not worse, because a feature that varies is a feature you can fit.

Reggie was on the floor with the ledger open across his knees and his jacket over the arm of the
chair, which happens perhaps four times a year.

Douglas was in the chair with a requisition and a pencil, doing the thing he does, out loud,
wrongly, twice.

The second time was the width. He said the variation would cost them the whole gain and put the
standard errors back where they started, and Reggie said, without looking up:

"No. The width's a parameter."

"It's noise."

"It's a parameter. You've been telling me for six years that a thing which varies with the animal
is a thing *about* the animal. If the width varies with the animal then the width is the
observation. You're arguing my side and I don't know why you've stopped believing your own."

Douglas looked at that for about eight seconds.

"Because I wanted the answer to be simpler than it is," he said. "That's the whole of it. Twice in
one night, both times the same fault, and I've been telling agronomists not to do it since 1946.
Put it in the minutes."

"There are no minutes."

"Put it in the minutes anyway." He was grinning again and could feel that he was and made no
attempt on it whatever.

And there it was again, the corner of the mouth, longer this time, and Reggie ducked his head over
the ledger to have it privately and Douglas looked at the requisition and let him.

---

At about ten Reggie went quiet in the middle of a sentence and stayed quiet.

Douglas watched him not be there for a while, and got interested, and formed a view, and then did
the thing he does instead of having views.

"Where've you gone?"

"There's a notebook. Fifty-six, the second half. I haven't seen it since the building work and I'm
trying to establish whether it went to the west store or whether it was in the office when the
office was moved, and I can't hold the two possibilities in a form that admits of resolution, and
it's been going round about four minutes."

"That's all it is?"

"That's all it is. What did you suppose it was?"

"I supposed you were sitting on the floor at ten at night minding about having been wrong for six
years."

Reggie considered this properly, because he considers everything properly, and it took him a
moment.

"No," he said. "I don't think I've ever minded that in my life. I mind not having found it myself.
That's a different objection and it's got nothing to do with you."

"Right. Good. That's what I get for guessing." Douglas got up and hunted for the torch. "Where's
the west store, we'll go and look."

"It's half past ten."

"Aye. Where is it."

---

They found it in the west store in a crate under a run of *Nature* from the war, and it was almost
entirely useless, and there were two pages in it that were not.

Reggie stood under the bulb with his glasses pushed up on his forehead and the notebook held close,
turning it to the light, and said:

"Oh — don't shut the door, the catch has gone."

Douglas caught it with his foot and said nothing at all and went on holding the torch and asked
what was on the second page.

---

He worked it out in the car.

It wasn't that sentence. That sentence is nothing; a man tells you about a catch. It was that he
went back over the whole evening at the lights on the Eastern Main Road and could not find where it
had started, because it hadn't started anywhere. It had come off him by degrees from about the
point where he got the daybooks, and by the west store there was not one uncontracted sentence left
in him, and there had been nine of them in the first four minutes, when he was handing over six
years.

That's the tell running backwards. Douglas has had the forward version off him perhaps forty times
— the formality arriving, the verbs going to the end, the man reaching for the register he keeps
for the Colonel and for bad news and for anything that costs — and he has never once seen it go
the other way, because it doesn't go the other way. There's no reverse operation. There's only the
man forgetting, for about three hours, on a floor, with a ledger across his knees, to run it at
all.

He is not going to be told. He worked that out in 1976 and it goes on needing doing about every six
months.

But he had a fight and lost it twice and won the last one and got twenty-four years of untouched
data out of a cupboard at eleven at night, and there is not a department in England that would have
given him that evening.

He got to St Augustine at ten past twelve and sat in the car outside his own house for a while
before he went in, which is a thing he has started doing and has decided not to look at.

## X

In the October of 1982 Jean wrote from Aberdeen about a paper and mentioned in the fourth
paragraph, in passing, as news of a mutual correspondent, that Douglas Nairn had been offered the
chair at Reading and that she supposed one had to congratulate him even while regretting it.

She did not know she was mentioning anything. She has produced load-bearing findings under
nobody's name three times in her career and this is the fourth and it is the only one she has
never been told about.

Reggie read the paragraph twice and put the letter down.

Nairn had not raised it. Nairn had had the offer since at least the September, on the internal
evidence of the letter, and had not raised it, and had gone on for six weeks discussing the
middle pens.

---

He took five weeks over it, which is two more than Pascual took in 1966, and he did it in the
evening, sitting down, having considered at some length whether to do it standing and got it
wrong either way.

He does not know that this is the procedure. He has never once identified where he got it. It is
simply the only model he has of a man saying a thing of that class to another man in a room, and
he was in the room when it was demonstrated, and he can run a protocol he has been shown.

He said:

"You have been offered the chair at Reading. Leslie mentioned it in her October letter and did
not know she was mentioning anything, so you need not consider that anybody has broken a
confidence.

"You have not raised it. I have had five weeks and I can produce two accounts of that. The first
is that you have decided already and do not require a view from me, in which case I am wasting
your evening and will stop. The second is that you have not raised it because you have spent
seven years arranging matters so that I am never required to originate anything, and this is the
one question that cannot be got at in that way, since an answer to a question you have asked is
not the same object as the thing itself, and you know the difference perfectly well. On that
account you have been waiting."

He had his hands flat on his knees.

"I believe it is the second. I am aware that I have attempted this class of judgement perhaps
five times in my life and been wrong on all of them but one, and that a man reasoning from a
single result to a second finding is not doing anything I would accept from a student. I am doing
it anyway.

"I should like you to stay. That is the whole of it. I have had five weeks and there is nothing
else in it and I have not been able to find a better way to put it."

Nairn did not say anything for a while.

Then he said, "Seven years."

"Yes."

"I'd got it worked out that I was never going to be told and I'd decided that was all right."
He was looking at the floor. "That's not true. I'd decided it every few months and it kept
needing doing again. It wasn't all right."

"No," said Reggie. "I do not suppose it was."

"You could have said any of that at any point in seven years."

"I could not have. I want to be exact. I have not been withholding it. I have not had access to
it." He looked at the middle of the floor. "I have access to it this evening because Leslie put
a fact in a letter and the fact did not fit, and a thing that does not fit is the one object I
can always do something with. I do not expect the mechanism to be available again."

"Once is what I needed," said Nairn.

---

Reggie asked him, before they went up, whether the reading had been correct in every particular
and not merely in substance.

Nairn said that it had.

And Reggie went to the desk and got out the file and wrote it down, with the date, in the form
he uses: the inference, the grounds it was made on, the confirmation and by whom.

There are two entries in it now. September 1966 and October 1982. Sixteen years apart, both
confirmed, both on the same class of question, both about a man in a room who was tired and
would not say so.

He does not generalise from two. That would be indefensible and he knows it. But he has been
over the file more than he can defend, and what he has is no longer a single result that might
have been luck; it is a small series, taken by the same instrument on two occasions widely
separated in time, agreeing.

That is a different object again. It is not confidence. It is the removal of the last respectable
argument for never trying.

Nairn declined Reading in the November and has never mentioned it since and got the chair at St
Augustine in 1984 instead, and grumbles about the drive.

## XI

*Nairn. February 1983.*

He'd been at it since the October — since the Reading evening, when a thing he had stopped
expecting had happened in that room and he had discovered that what it left him was greedy — and he
brought it out in the February, at about half past nine, with no preamble whatever, because four
months is about his limit and because he has never in his life worked out a good moment for
anything.

"You could call me Douglas."

Reggie looked up from the sluice figures with the expression of a man who has been asked something
in a language he has some of.

"I could."

"That's not a yes."

"No, it isn't. I'm working out whether I want to." He put the pencil down, which he does before he
answers anything properly. "I've not thought about it before. Not once. Which is odd, because I've
had your Christian name since Kingston and it's been sitting there seven and a half years and I've
never reached for it, and I don't think that can be an accident."

"Well," said Douglas. "There you are. That's why I asked."

---

"The first letter was signed *D. Nairn*. There wasn't a Christian name on any of them for
thirty-one months. No age, no photograph, no voice, nothing about you at all except what was in
the letters, and there were forty-one of them before Kingston, and I had the whole of you off
those.

"I want to be precise about the whole.

"Everything I hold about you was assembled between the February of 1973 and the September of 1975,
from the letters, and there has been nothing since that required amendment. I checked. I expected
Kingston to revise the estimate substantially — a man who has only ever had a specimen in writing
ought to expect the direct observation to move it, and if it does not move he should be suspicious
of himself before he is pleased with himself. It did not move. I went over it in the November when
you had gone back, and again in the spring, and there was nothing in it that wanted altering.

"I have never had that from any other source in my life. Not from a paper, not from a man in a
room. Not once.

"So the name is not a form of address and I have not been using it as one. It is what the whole of
that is filed under. It is what I call you when you are not here, and it has been what I call you
when you are not here since some time in the autumn of 1973, at which point I had not seen you.

"If you would prefer Douglas you have only to say so and I shall use it, and I shall not require a
reason. But you asked me why, and that is why. I should not have arrived at it on my own. I did not
know there was a question."

---

Douglas sat there.

He had heard it come in. Not at *thirty-one months* — at *I want to be precise about the whole,*
which is four words and a full stop and is the sound of a man reaching for the register he keeps
for the Colonel and for bad news and for anything that costs. The contractions went at that
sentence and did not come back, and three years ago Douglas had sat in a car outside his own house
at midnight working out that there is no reverse operation, and here was the forward one arriving
out of a page of sluice figures on a Tuesday, unprompted, at half past nine, and going on for three
minutes.

"You know what you've just told me."

"I have told you why I use your surname."

"Aye." Douglas rubbed his face with both hands. "You've told me you had me finished before you'd
laid eyes on me. That you went and checked it twice afterwards to see whether it had moved and it
hadn't. And that you've had a private name for me since the autumn of nineteen seventy-three,
which is two years before you knew what I looked like. That's what you've told me."

Reggie thought about that with complete seriousness, which is what makes it unanswerable.

"That's a fair summary," he said. "I'd not have arranged it in that order."

"But you're not taking any of it back."

"No." He picked the pencil up and turned it over once and put it down again. "No, I'm not."

---

And then, with the pencil back on the desk and the sluice figures going again across the room,
Douglas got to the other half of it.

The same thing had happened to him. Precisely the same thing, at the other end, and earlier. He was
fifty-three and in Hertfordshire and by about the fifteenth letter he was writing them on Sunday
evenings, and rereading the replies, which he does not do, and putting off the ones that were only
work; and if anybody had asked he would have said it was the best professional correspondence he
had ever had, and it was, and that was not what was going on. He went out to Kingston in the
September of 1975 to look at a man he had already settled about, and the looking confirmed it, and
he has known that about himself since roughly the Tuesday of that week.

He would never have asked. Not out of delicacy — it isn't a question you can put, there's no form
of words for it that isn't grotesque, and he'd have got a truthful answer and it would have been an
answer to something he hadn't managed to ask. He had put it away in 1975 under the heading of
things a man does not get to find out.

And he had just been handed it, for nothing, on a Tuesday, by somebody explaining a filing
convention.

---

He tried to say it.

He would like that on the record too, since he is the one keeping the record: he opened his mouth
to tell Reggie that the same thing had happened at the other end, and got as far as *The letters*,
and stopped.

Which was novel. He has been saying things to people since 1946 and had not previously come across
one that would not go. It was not shyness and it was not the company. Every version he had was
either a report on his own condition, which Reggie would have received and thanked him for and been
unable to do anything whatever with; or it was the sentence itself, which is short, and which he
was not going to put down in a room where it could not be answered, because a thing like that said
to a man who cannot say it back is not a gift. It is an invoice.

So what he said was:

"By about the fifteenth one I was writing them on Sunday evenings."

Reggie considered this.

"The fifteenth of mine or the fifteenth of yours?"

"Yours."

"That would be the June of 1974." He did not look anything up. "I wrote you one on a Tuesday that
month. It was not a reply to anything."

"Nine pages."

"Yes."

Douglas had put down one fact and got two back, and nobody had asked for the second one.

Neither of them said anything else about it, and neither of them has since, and it is the nearest
either of them has ever come.

---

Douglas got up.

On the way past he put his hand on Reggie's shoulder for a second, which is nothing, and which he
does perhaps twice a week.

And Reggie leaned into it. Not far and not for long — an inch, the head going over towards the
hand, the whole of it lasting exactly as long as the hand did — and he did not look up and did not
say anything and went back to the sluice figures when it had gone.

He has never once had to ask for that. It was there from Kingston and it has never wanted a
question put to it, and Douglas has thought a good deal, over seven years, about the fact
that the only channel in that man which opens by itself is the one nobody had ever bothered to
count.

He stood at the window for a while with his back to the room.

---

He did not ask again.

He thought about it on the drive and once more in the March and settled it and has not reopened it,
and the reasoning is not complicated and he would give it to anybody who asked, and nobody has.

He is Douglas to three of the girls in the Faculty office, and to a man in Reading who writes at
Christmas, and to a headstone in Fife that has been his to visit since 1958. He has had the word
off people for sixty-two years and it has never once carried anything.

There is one man on earth who says the other one, and he had been saying it into an empty room
since 1973, and had thought it was a heading.

Douglas had gone in that evening asking for the commoner of the two words. He came out of it having
been shown, at some length, with perfect courtesy, and by a man who did not know he was showing him
anything, what he already had.

## XII

The letter from San Juan came in the October of 1983 and was, for three paragraphs, about the
sluice and a supplier and the eastern pens, and then said:

*One other matter. You will be sixty-seven in January. Have whoever holds the medical chest take
your blood pressure in the January and again in the July, and put the two figures at the foot of
your letter with the dates. It takes four minutes.*

Reggie read it twice and put it down and went and stood at the window for a period he did not
time, and was aware while doing it that this was the second time from that address and that he
had not timed the first one either.

He worked at the paragraph for eleven days and it will go two ways and there is no feature of the
text that separates them. It is either a competent man's ordinary instruction to a colleague of
sixty-six with nobody to make him do anything, which accounts for every feature of the letter
including the flatness of it, which is considerable. Or it is the reverse of that. A flat
instruction is exactly what the same man produces under either hypothesis. He has been through it
about forty times. There is nothing in it. It is very well constructed.

On the twelfth day he put it on the table and told Nairn he could not discriminate and set out
both hypotheses in a numbered form, because he had been asked what he was doing and answered.

Nairn read it. Then he said: "That's not the one he wants."

"The instruction specifies blood pressure."

"A man forming a concern about someone of sixty-six asks three things. Weight, pressure, does he
smoke. It's not a subtle point and you could have produced the list at twenty-two." Nairn put the
letter down. "He asked for one of the three. He asked for the second one."

"I had that inside a day. It does not discriminate. Either he did not want the other two, or he
could not ask for them, and there is no procedure for determining which."

"There isn't, and you've had eleven days at it, and you're not going to get it." Nairn was
already getting up. "So here's the rule and it's the same rule I give agronomists and none of them
like it either. When you can't discriminate and the extra observation costs four minutes, you take
the observation and you send it, and you let the man at the other end throw it away. That's not a
modification. That's the standard practice."

Reggie said, "He will not read it as an over-complete return."

"No," said Nairn. "He won't."

"He will read it as an answer to a question he did not ask."

"Yes."

Reggie sat with that for about a minute.

Then he went up to Mrs Ramnarine's room, in the January, and had the cuff put on over his
shirtsleeve, and thanked her, and then asked her, having no procedure whatever for it and doing
it anyway, whether she would mind reading the scale as well.

He wrote both figures at the foot of the letter, with the date, and nothing else.

## XIII

*Pascual, San Juan.*

He had known about Nairn since the November of 1976, which is when he counted.

He had been reading the letters for content for ten years, the way you read anything that arrives
four times a year without fail. He was after a figure on the middle pens and could not lay hands
on it, so he went back through the file; and did not find the figure; and found instead that a
name had come into the correspondence in the February of 1973 and had never gone out again.

He could date it to the letter. He could date it because Reggie files by date and Pascual keeps
everything, and between them, without either intending anything of the kind, they had constructed
a complete and impeccably indexed record of the one thing neither of them was ever going to write
down.

The letter of the October of 1977 says nothing about it at all. Four paragraphs on the eastern
pens and a question at the end.

Pascual read that one twice as well.

---

It came on a Tuesday in the February and he opened it at the kitchen table with the coffee going.

Three paragraphs about the middle pens, a question at the end, and at the foot, in a hand you
could set type from:

    14.i.84   142/84
    14.i.84   9 st 1

He read the second one first. He would like that noted, wherever the noting is done: nineteen
years of a man's correspondence and he went straight past the figure he had asked for.

He had no baseline for it, or he had one, and it was in the drawer where it had been since 1970 —
a half-sheet of ruled paper, four lines, in a hand like a ledger, the last of eight, which he had
never once been able to say why he kept.

    9.xii.69   9 st 9

Eight pounds. Fourteen years and two months. It is what a man loses between fifty-two and
sixty-seven doing nothing whatever about it, and it is the least interesting number anybody has
ever sent him.

He sat in the kitchen with the two pieces of paper for some considerable time.

Because the figure is nothing, and the figure was not asked for, and a weight is not a number a
third party produces — it is a number the man takes about himself, standing on a scale, reading a
dial, writing it down and posting it four thousand miles, and there is no version of that which
is not a thing done *for* somebody.

He had spent a fortnight in the October drafting an instruction that would not say so. He had
burned one version in the sink and has never told anybody what was in it.

And the man had sent the other figure anyway, unasked, in the same hand, on the same line.

Which he could not have originated. Pascual would have staked his practice on that and would
stake it now.

So there was somebody in the room.

---

He answered within the week, because he has answered within the week for eighteen years and it
did not occur to him to do otherwise. He sent the supplier's address and the figure for the
middle pens.

At the foot, under his signature, he wrote: *My compliments to Nairn.*

---

Reggie read that in the March and did not understand it and asked, which he can do, and Nairn
took the letter and read the line and put it down and stood there for a moment with his hand
still on it.

"What does it mean?"

"It means he can stop," Nairn said.

---

Ramón came in from the pharmacy at about seven and said, "You've been somewhere else since
Tuesday, and I'm not going to guess," and Pascual told him.

It took four minutes. There was not much to it. A number he had not asked for, on a line under a
number he had.

Ramón did the sum on his fingers, openly, because he did everything openly.

"Eight pounds in fourteen years."

"Yes."

"That's just being sixty-seven."

"Yes," said Pascual. "That is exactly what it is."

Ramón looked at him for a second and then said, "Right. Good," and got up and went in and put the
kettle on, which was what he did at the end of things.

And Pascual sat on the porch in Santurce with the two pieces of paper on the boards beside him
and the instrument running for the last time on the only object it had ever been built for, and
what it returned was a reading with nothing in it at all.

He had been waiting eighteen years to be told there was nothing to do.

He did not do anything. He put the half-sheet back in the drawer with the new one folded against
it, and went in for the coffee.

There is one hour in Reggie's day with nothing filed under it.

He gets down to the eastern pens at about seven, because he does not sleep past it and there is
no purpose in lying in the dark once the machine is up, and there is an hour before the first of
them arrives, and in that hour there is nobody on the station who wants anything. The light comes
across the water from the east in a way he has been looking at since the March of 1954 and has
never once attempted to describe.

From about the April of 1984 Nairn started coming down.

He does not talk. He stands at the rail with his hands in his pockets and looks at the water and
appears to find it satisfactory, and at ten to eight he says that he supposes that is that, and
goes up for the drive.

It has not gone into the index. It has not been given a heading, there is no card against the
lamp for it, Reggie has not written it down anywhere, and it is the only arrangement in his life
of which all four of those things are true.

Douglas knows. He knows because he asked, in the June, what the seven o'clock hour was filed
under, and was told nothing; and asked whether that meant nothing in particular; and was told no.
Nothing at all.

He has not established what to do with that and does not believe there is anything to do with it.
He has it. He has the five weeks in the October of 1982, and he has an evening in the February
when he went in asking for a Christian name and came out with something else, and now he has an
hour on a rail at seven in the morning that is under no heading whatever.

He is not going to be told outright. He settled that in 1976 and it goes on needing settling.

He was told, in writing, in the March of 1975, that there is no engine in there which starts by
itself. He took that at face value and has run on it for eight years and it is the most useful
thing anybody has ever sent him.

It is also not quite true, and he has two observations, and he has never raised either.

A sentence about eating at one o'clock got into a letter in the March of 1972, and the man who
wrote it has never been able to account for how it got there. A date in the June of 1974 came out
of him in a room on a Tuesday evening, unasked for, with the second half of it attached.

Twice in eleven years. No notice. Nobody at the controls.

There is nothing to be done about an engine like that. You cannot turn it over, there is no
procedure, and the man himself is the last person on earth to ask. What you can do is keep the
records and stay where you can hear it.

Douglas was there for the second one. He fully intends to be there for the third.
