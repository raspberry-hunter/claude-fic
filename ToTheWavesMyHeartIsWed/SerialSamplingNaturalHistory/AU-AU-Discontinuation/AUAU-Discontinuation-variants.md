# Discontinuation — two variants

Both of these replace the *thank you for telling me the actual reason* exchange in §II. In the
base text Reggie accepts the report and never contests it. Here he contests it once, correctly,
and the two variants differ only in whether Pascual can take it.

---

## Variant A — "No"

*Consistent with Pascual as written. He cannot say the sentence; he has never been able to; the
inability is the flaw that has been running since 1955 and it discharges here.*

"You are tired of me."

"No."

It was out of him before the sentence was properly finished, and it was true — he would have sworn
to it in any court there is — and it was the fastest he had answered anything in twelve years, and
neither of them heard what that was.

"I am not putting it as a reproach," Reggie said. He had his hands flat on his knees. "I am putting
it because I believe it to be the case, and because you have not said it, and because it seemed to
me that if you are going, you ought not to have to go on a reason less true than the one that is
available." He looked at the middle of the floor. "You have been reading me for twelve years and
nobody has read you, and I have known that for some time and have not had a use for it. I have it
now."

And Pascual argued with him.

Warmly, at length, in good faith, for the better part of an hour. He went through the fourteenth of
July. He went through the doorway and the forty minutes and what carelessness costs in that house
and the boy in the delta and the March of 1964. He was honest in every particular; there was not
one false statement in the whole of it; he has gone back over it since and cannot find the lie.

And at the end of it Reggie was quiet for a while and then said, "Very well. I withdraw it."

And meant it. He withdrew it the way he withdraws anything — completely, at once, with no residue,
because the correction had come from the only instrument in his life he has ever trusted absolutely
on a question about another person's interior, and that instrument had returned *no*.

### What it costs

That is the whole difference, and it is not a small one.

In the base version he goes on for the rest of his life having been told a true thing kindly and at
cost, and thanks him for it, and is only wrong about the part underneath.

In this one he originated a report — the single hardest act available to him, requiring exactly the
class of inference he is disqualified from, gambled on and got *right* — and was told, at length,
with evidence, by the man he trusted most on earth, that he had got it wrong.

He does not conclude that Pascual was lying. That possibility is not accessible to him. He concludes
that he was wrong, files it, and it joins the other file, which is thick: *I am not able to do this
and should not attempt it again.* And he does not attempt it again. Not with Jean, not with the
Bevans, not with anybody, for the remaining thirty years.

He still eats at one o'clock. He also spends the rest of his life quietly certain that the one time
he ever managed to read another human being, he read him wrong.

And Pascual has that. He took a gift he had wanted for twelve years, refused it sincerely, won the
argument against a man with no floor under him, and got on the aeroplane; and in 1979 he met
somebody who tells him things without being asked, and found it easy, and was happy for sixteen
years.

He never worked out that he had been given it. That is the only mercy in the whole arrangement, and
it is not one.

## Variant B — "You're right"

*Requires Pascual to be able to do the thing he has never once done. Either because he decides he
owes it, or because he is fractionally less himself here. He still goes.*

He said, "You're right."

And then he sat there, because he had not known until it was out of him how far down it went, and
because he had never done it before and had no procedure for what comes after.

"I'm tired. Not of the work. Of you — no. Wait." Reggie had not moved. "Of doing it. Of being the
only one in the house who knows anything, twelve years, and never once being asked and never once
being able to be asked, and it is nobody's fault, and I'm fifty-five and I've stopped looking up
when you come in, and that's what tired does. So yes. You're right and I was wrong and I'd have
gone on being wrong about it in front of you for the rest of my life, which is a thing I do."

"I see," Reggie said.

"I'm still going."

"Yes," said Reggie. "I did not suppose otherwise."

### What it is worth

Stated precisely, because he would want it stated precisely.

He has, for the first time in his life, **one verified instance.** He originated an inference about
another person's interior — the class of judgement he has been failing at since he was a child, the
class that produced *insufficient* on a sheet with a control on Jean — and he tested it against the
best available instrument, and it held.

He does not generalise from it. That would be indefensible with n=1 and he knows it. He does not
walk away believing he can read people; he walks away with a single confirmed data point in a file
that previously contained nothing but null results, and he keeps it exactly as what it is, and does
not inflate it once in thirty years.

But a file with one result in it is a different object from an empty file. What it removes is not
his uncertainty. It removes the standing conclusion that he is *categorically* disqualified — and
that conclusion had been doing an enormous amount of work.

### What he does with it

Not what you would hope. Better.

He does not become able to report himself. That engine still does not start. Hester still has to
ask, and when she goes to England in 1970 the reporting still stops, and he is still in perfect good
faith about it.

What he does instead is copy the procedure.

Because he has now seen, worked through and verified, a complete example of a man reading another
man's condition, saying so plainly, and being right — and Reggie Tar cannot originate a feeling but
he can absolutely run a protocol he has been shown. So in the March of 1971, a junior called
Whitmore is six months in and going badly, and Reggie falls into step with him at the eastern pens
at eight in the morning and asks him how many hours he slept.

Not how he is. How many hours.

He does it every Friday for two years, and he never says one consoling thing, and Whitmore has told
the story in three institutions since and has never once got it across, because *the head of the
station asked me a number every Friday* is not an anecdote unless you were there.

That is what the admission bought. Not a man who can be looked after. A man who becomes, twelve
years late, at second hand, from a procedure he was shown once and checked carefully, the person who
asks.

It is also — and he does not see this, and would be very interested if you showed him — precisely
what Pascual did to Jean in 1955, at Pascual's own instigation, which is to say the sentence said
into the dark at a rail eventually reaches the person it was about, having taken sixteen years and
gone round the long way.

### Does he get anybody

Not a romance he initiates. That is permanently off the table and it is not the admission's job to
put it there.

But the conditions are now satisfiable, which they were not before, and they are specific: it would
have to be someone who does the whole of the originating, indefinitely, without resentment; who says
things in words and repeats them; who is not wounded by being answered rather than met. Rare. Not
impossible. And the crucial change is that when such a person eventually says *I should like this to
go on*, the old Reggie would have concluded he had misread it and let it pass, and this one has a
file with one confirmed result in it and no longer believes he is categorically unable to tell.

The honest odds: probably not, and possibly late, and if it happens it will be somebody else's doing
entirely, and he will spend the first two years asking whether he has understood correctly, and
being told, patiently, that he has.

### And it does something to Pascual

Because he found out at fifty-five, in a room in Trinidad, that the sentence exists and can be said
and does not destroy you.

So when Ramón asks him on the porch in 1983 why he left the man in Trinidad, this Pascual does not
give him the handsome version.

He gives him the fourteenth of July, and then he gives him the rest of it, and Ramón — who does not
say the true thing in Variant A because it is not his to say — does not have to, because it has
already been said, by the person it belonged to, seventeen years earlier, out loud, in the room, to
the man it was about.
