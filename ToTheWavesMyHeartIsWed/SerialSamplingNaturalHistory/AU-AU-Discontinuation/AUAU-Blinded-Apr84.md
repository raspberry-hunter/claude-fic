# Blinded

## I

The letter came on a Tuesday and she read it standing in the hall with her coat on.

It was two sheets. The first was a request, set out the way he set out everything, and the second
was a paragraph copied in his own hand with the identifying material taken out.

He had done the blinding properly. That was the first thing she noticed and she noticed it with
something close to admiration, because most people who think they've blinded a document have
merely crossed out the names. He'd altered the age. He'd altered the interval. He'd given the
writer as *a colleague of long standing, formerly resident*, which is a description that fits about
four hundred people and gives away nothing, and he'd rewritten one clause to remove a place-name
without disturbing the rhythm of the sentence, which is difficult and which he'd done well.

Then he'd set out two hypotheses, numbered, in eleven words each, and asked her to say which the
text supported and with what confidence, and asked her not to answer at once.

She got as far as the second line of the paragraph.

*One other matter. You will be sixty-seven in January.*

Sixty-seven. He'd altered it. She stood in the hall for a while and then went through and put the
kettle on and didn't fill it.

## II

The blinding failed in about four seconds and there was nothing whatever wrong with the blinding.

It failed because there are perhaps two men alive who would number hypotheses in a private letter,
and she'd met both of them, and one of them was writing it. It failed because the request was
addressed to her, in Aberdeen, from Trinidad, which narrows the field of subjects considerably. It
failed because she'd spent twelve years in a building with him and had a great deal of material
she had never sat down to collect.

She did not tell him any of that. He'd asked for a reading of a text and she wasn't going to
spend a paragraph explaining to Reggie Tar that his procedure was sound and his sample had a hole
in it. He'd have taken it perfectly well and it would have been of no use to him at all.

## III

She did the analysis on the Wednesday evening at the kitchen table and it took an hour and a half,
which she has since thought about.

Three features, and she wrote her working on each because he'd want it and because she'd have
wanted it.

*The interval.* Twice yearly is not a clinical interval. It isn't the interval for anything. A man
with a live concern about another man's heart asks quarterly at the outside and probably monthly;
a man with no concern at all asks nothing. Twice a year is the interval you choose when the
measurement isn't the point and the letter is — or when you've decided in advance what burden the
recipient will accept, which is a decision you can only make about somebody you know. Either way
it isn't clinical. This is the feature she'd have put first at a seminar and she suspected he
hadn't seen it, and she was right.

*It takes four minutes.* Nobody supplies that unless they're pre-empting a refusal. To pre-empt a
refusal you have to have modelled the recipient's reluctance, in advance, in some detail, and a
man who's modelled your reluctance has been thinking about you for longer than the four minutes.
She judged he'd have noticed this one and discounted it as ordinary courtesy about burden, and she
was right about that too.

*There is no question in it.* This is the one she went carefully on, because it's the one where
she couldn't be certain she was reading the text and not her own knowledge, and she said so in the
letter, at length, and it's the reason her figure is four and not forty.

But the instruction is built so as to produce a recurring reply for the rest of the recipient's
life without ever once requiring him to originate anything. It has no question in it because a
question would have to be answered and then the thing would be over. What it has instead is a
standing order. Whoever wrote that paragraph knew — not guessed, knew, and had known for a long
time — exactly what this particular man can and cannot do, and constructed the only mechanism that
would work on him, and got it right first time.

She wrote: *on the text alone I make it the second hypothesis at about four to one. I would not go
higher than that on one paragraph and I would be suspicious of anybody who did.*

## IV

Her actual estimate was not four to one.

She wants to be exact about this because it's the part that matters. She was certain. She had been
certain since about 1959 and had never had cause to revise it and did not revise it that Wednesday
and has not revised it since.

But she'd been asked for a reading of a text, blinded, on the merits, and her certainty is not
evidence about a text. It's twelve years of watching two men in a building, and if she'd put it in
the letter she'd have been handing him her opinion of Pascual instead of an analysis of a
paragraph — and her opinion of Pascual is a thing she formed in 1966 and has never softened, and
he didn't ask for it, and it would have been in there, underneath, working, whatever she said.

So she gave him the number the method produced.

Four to one is honest. It's also, she knew perfectly well as she sealed it, a great deal less than
she could have given him, and she has never been able to establish whether the difference cost him
anything or saved him from something, and there is no experiment that would tell her.

## V

On the Thursday she got out the address in San Juan.

She had it. She'd had it since 1966 and had never used it and had transferred it into two
successive books, which is a fact she looked at directly on the Thursday and then stopped looking
at.

She drafted four lines. She can still produce them.

*Reggie has asked me to give a blind reading of a paragraph of yours. He has been working on it
since October. I am telling you this because you are the only person who will know what it means
and I do not propose to explain it.*

That's all it needed. That was the whole of the intervention available to her and it would have
taken a stamp.

She didn't send it, and the reason arrived complete and immediately and she accepted it.

The reason was: he asked me to be an instrument. The moment I become an intermediary the reading
is contaminated and he'll have to discard it, and he'll be right to discard it, and the reading is
the only thing I have that's any use to him. I can help him or I can help him. Not both.

That reasoning is sound. She has been over it a great many times and it does not break.

What she noted at the time, and wrote nowhere, and did not pursue, is that a reason which arrives
that fast and that whole is generally one you had already, and that the correct procedure with a
conclusion of that kind is to go and look for what it's resting on.

She had a department, and eleven weeks of teaching left, and she did not go and look.

## VI

The reading was finished on the Wednesday. She posted it three weeks later.

What took three weeks was fourteen words.

She wrote the covering note eleven times, and the eleven drafts got shorter, which is the
direction things go when a person is removing everything she isn't entitled to say. The long ones
had explanations in them. One of them had the phrase *you needn't have gone to the trouble*, which
she cut on the Sunday because it was a criticism of the only piece of self-management he'd managed
in eighteen years. One had *I hope you are well*, which she cut because it's a question with no
number in it and he'd have had to work out what to do with it. One had four lines about Pascual
which she burnt rather than merely discarded.

She was not deciding what to say. She'd known what to say since the Tuesday. She was deciding
whether she was allowed to, and it took three weeks, and the answer she arrived at was that one
sentence would not contaminate the reading if it were placed under the signature, outside the
body, where it formed no part of the analysis.

She wrote:

*You took the names out. I did not need them.*

And put it under her name, where it belonged, and posted the whole thing on a Friday.

## VII

He didn't answer it. She hadn't expected him to; she'd made sure there was no question in it,
which was deliberate, and which she now thinks may have been the most useful thing she did in the
whole three weeks.

What she thought about afterwards, on and off through the summer, was that he'd asked her.

Not what it cost him, which she had no way to compute, though she assumed a good deal. That he had
gone through whatever list a man like that keeps and had arrived at her name, in Aberdeen, after
twenty-seven years — that of everybody available he'd picked the one who'd tell him a number and
not tell him it would be all right.

She was pleased about it. She's aware of how that reads, and it's accurate, and she was pleased
about it for months.

---

She got out the letter of October 1966 in the September, or rather the sentence from it, since she
kept no copy and has never needed one.

*I don't say you're wrong. I say you're the only one of the two of you who was ever able to be
wrong out loud, and you've spent twelve years not doing it, and now you've gone and made the one
decision that can't be retracted.*

She read it over in her head twice, in a chair, in Aberdeen, eighteen years after posting it.

It still held. It had held for eighteen years and it went on holding, and there was nothing
anywhere in the world that could have disturbed it, because the one man who could have corrected
it had received it and read it two hundred times and had never in his life written back.
