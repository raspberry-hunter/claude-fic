# Steady State

## I

The fifteenth of July, and the instruction says January and July, so he goes up at half past
eleven.

Mrs Ramnarine has held the medical chest since 1978 and is very quick and does not talk while she
is doing it, which he has always been grateful for and has never found an occasion to say. She
puts the cuff on over his shirtsleeve and pumps it and lets it down and watches the column and
says a hundred and thirty-eight over eighty, and he says thank you and writes it in his notebook
at the desk in the outer room, with the date, so that it will not have to be remembered.

"You want me to write it for you?"

"No. Thank you. It is going into a letter."

"Every January and every July." She is putting the thing away. "You never miss."

"No," he says. "That would defeat the object."

It takes four minutes, as he was told it would.

## II

He eats at one o'clock.

The practice was instituted in the March of 1955 and has held for thirty years and he has never
seriously considered discontinuing it, and it has survived two changes of cook, a year of building
work, the loss of the west refectory, and every alteration to his own duties since he took the
station. He eats at one whether he is hungry, which he is not, and whether there is anything he
particularly wants to eat, which there has not been for some considerable time.

There is an obvious account of why a practice of that kind holds for thirty years, and he has had
it in view for most of them.

He does not go into it.

This is not because he cannot. He wants that distinguished, since he is the only person who will
ever have occasion to distinguish it: he knows quite well what is in there and he has known since
about 1968. It is that going in would produce nothing he could act on, and would require him
afterwards to sit in it, and he has a station to run and no procedure for coming back out.

So the practice is a practice. It is filed under the working day. It has been filed under the
working day for thirty years and the file is accurate and incomplete and he is aware that it is
incomplete, and that is the whole of it.

He eats at one and gets up at ten past whether or not the plate is finished, which for some years
now it has not been.

## III

The machinery has never been better.

He is running on something between three and four hours and has been since about 1970, and the
rate is the highest it has been in his life, higher than 1955, higher than Salisbury, and he can
feel it going the way you can feel a well-set engine through the floor of a shed.

The output is what it was in 1952.

He worked out why in the March of 1957 and there has never been anything wrong with the working.
What a machine produces is its rate less whatever else is running on it. He had the rate up and
the overhead down simultaneously for about six years — no. That is wrong, and he has caught
himself in it before, and the fact that he keeps arriving at the wrong version is itself worth
noting and he has not noted it anywhere.

He did not have the rate up in those years. He was sleeping six hours and eating three times a day
because there was a man at the table who looked at him, and the rate over that period was the
lowest of his adult life. It was also the most productive period of his adult life by a very
considerable margin, which is the entire content of the March of 1957 and the reason the finding
was worth having, and he has now mislaid the finding twice in one decade.

The two terms are not independent. That is the part he did not see in 1957 and has had a good deal
of time to see since. The arrangement that put the rate down was the same arrangement that took the
overhead away, being in both cases one man in one building; and so there was never a combination
available to be selected from. There was one configuration in which this machine works properly and
he had it for twelve years and did not know what he had, and it is not a thing that can be
assembled from parts.

He has the overhead itemised. He does it about once a year and has stopped doing it, on the
grounds that the list does not change and running it again is not analysis, it is a man feeding
his own machine until it produces a different answer.

The largest item is the fifty minutes, which he now builds for himself, four or five times a day,
every day, and which he had for six years not had to build at all. It is expensive. It was
expensive in 1954 and he did not know what it cost because he was not paying it, and there is a
particular species of stupidity in that and he has never entirely forgiven himself for it.

The second item has no name and does not require one for the arithmetic.

The net is a man of sixty-eight running at the highest rate he has ever had and producing
respectable work at approximately the level he was producing it at thirty-three, which is not a
failure, and which he has set down once, in a single sentence, in his head, and has not developed.

## IV

In the March of 1971 a junior called Whitmore was six months in and going badly.

He did not observe this. He wants that on the record, wherever the record is: he did not look at
the man and perceive distress, because he has never in his life looked at a person and perceived
anything of the kind and the one occasion on which he did is a single result and does not
generalise and he has never once pretended otherwise.

What he did was apply criteria.

He wrote them out on the back of a requisition in the February and copied them onto a card and the
card is in the second drawer of the desk and has been for fourteen years. There are six of them
and they are all observable from across a room:

He does not finish a plate and does not remark on it.
He stands about with nothing in his hands.
He becomes polite.
He stops bringing you the thing he is pleased with.
He arrives before he needs to and leaves after he needs to and does not say what he did in between.
He answers the question you asked and not the one you meant.

He knows precisely where the criteria came from. They were used on him, at the Martin, between the
August of 1954 and the November of 1966, by a man who never wrote them down because he did not
have to, and Reggie reconstructed them from the receiving end over about three weeks and has
always assumed the reconstruction to be imperfect. It has not been. He has tested it eleven times.

What he did on a Friday in March was fall into step with Whitmore at the eastern pens at eight in
the morning and ask him how many hours he had slept.

Not how he was. How many hours. And when Whitmore told him, he said thank you, and asked what he
had eaten the day before, and Whitmore told him, and he said thank you again and talked about the
Michaelmas figures for a quarter of an hour and went back up.

He did it every Friday for two years and he never said one consoling thing, because he has nothing
in that line and has known since he was about eleven that a wrong sentence in that class does more
damage than no sentence at all, and because the woman he took the procedure from had evidently
concluded the same and he saw no reason to depart from a working method.

Whitmore is a professor in Vancouver and writes at Christmas.

## V

There have been eleven.

He keeps the list in the same drawer as the card and it is not a list of names, because he had a
concern in about 1974 that a list of names in a head of station's desk could be read as something
else by somebody who came after him; so it is initials and the dates of starting and stopping and
nothing else, and it looks like an equipment log and would pass as one.

The rules he has arrived at, none of which were given to him and all of which he has had to derive
by trial:

Start on a Friday and not otherwise.
Ask a question with a number for an answer.
Write the answer down where he can see you write it.
Do not deviate to the second question until the first has been answered.
Do not stop because the answers have improved. Stop when he stops needing to give them, which is a
different event and occurs on average about five months later.
Never once say anything kind.

He does not know whether any of it works.

He wants that stated properly, because it would be very easy to state it wrongly and he would then
be claiming something he cannot support. He has no control group. He has eleven people, over
fourteen years, none of them randomised, all of them selected by a man with a documented inability
in exactly the faculty required, and every one of them subject to a dozen other influences he never
measured. Two of the eleven left the profession. One he suspects he started on too late and one he
is fairly certain he started on for the wrong reasons and stopped correctly. He could not defend
the practice at a seminar for four minutes.

He does it anyway, and has never troubled to reconcile those two facts, and does not expect to.

He is not on the list. The criteria require an observer, and he is the observer, and the question
of who observes the observer is not a question the procedure contains, and it has never occurred to
him to look for it. There is no defect in that. A protocol does not contain a step it was not
written to contain.

## VI

The letters are quarterly and they are about work.

He originates them, which is worth remarking on since he originates almost nothing, and the reason
he can is that they have a heading. *Correspondence: San Juan, veterinary.* It went into the file
index in the December of 1966 and it has been in the file index for nineteen years and anything
with a heading can be done. He writes in March, June, September and December, four paragraphs,
and there is always a question at the end because a letter without a question in it does not
require an answer and he is not going to write a letter that does not require an answer.

The replies come inside the week. Every one. Seventy-six.

They are filed by date in the second cabinet and he has never reread one of them, and he can quote
from a good number, and he has noticed that he can and has not filed the noticing anywhere.

---

In the October of 1983 a letter arrived which was, for three paragraphs, about the sluice and a
supplier and the eastern pens, and which then said:

*One other matter. You will be sixty-seven in January. Have whoever holds the medical chest take
your blood pressure in the January and again in the July, and put the two figures at the foot of
your letter with the dates. It takes four minutes.*

He read it twice. He then put it down and went and stood at the window for a period he did not
time, which he has since regretted, as the duration would have been the only datum of any use.

There are two hypotheses and he has been unable to discriminate between them for twenty-one months.

The first is institutional. A veterinary surgeon of long acquaintance, hearing nothing but drainage
figures for seventeen years, arrives at the reflection that the head of a small station four
thousand miles off is sixty-six and unmarried and has nobody to make him do anything, and issues
the sort of instruction any competent clinician would issue to any competent colleague, and thinks
about it for perhaps ten minutes in total. This hypothesis accounts for every feature of the
letter. It accounts for the flatness of it, which is considerable.

The second hypothesis he can also state, and has, and will not write down.

The difficulty is that the letter is evidence for both to the same degree, and a flat instruction
is exactly what a man produces under the first hypothesis and exactly what the same man produces
under the second, and there is no feature of the text that separates them. He has been through it
about forty times. There is nothing in it. It is very well constructed.

He is aware that he has one confirmed result in this line. It was obtained in the September of
1966, at some cost, and it held, and it is in the file and he has not inflated it in nineteen
years. But it is one result, on one occasion, on a question that was very much easier than this
one, and a man who reasons from n=1 to a second finding is not doing anything he would accept from
a student.

So he does not know. He is not going to know. He has recorded that he does not know, which is the
only operation available to him.

---

There is one anomaly and he had it inside a day.

A clinician forming a general concern about a man of sixty-six asks for three things. He asks what
he weighs, he asks his blood pressure, and he asks whether he smokes. That is not a subtle point of
practice; it is what any competent man would set down, in that order, and the order is not
arbitrary, and Reggie could have produced the list at twenty-two.

He was asked for one of the three. He was asked for the second one.

He has worked at the omission for twenty-one months and it will go two ways. Either it is
insignificant — a busy man writing at the end of a long letter, putting down the item that occurred
to him and not troubling to be systematic, which is a thing everybody does and which is entirely
consistent with the first hypothesis. Or it is the reverse of insignificant, and the item was left
out because it was the item that would have been informative, and a man does not leave that item out
by accident.

Discriminating between those requires him to know whether his weight has moved.

He does not. He has not been weighed since the ninth of December 1969, when Mrs Bevan did it in the
outer room with the door open, and he could give the figure to the pound if anybody wanted it and
nobody has.

So the branch is untestable on the available data and he has recorded it as untestable and set it
aside, which is correct procedure and the only thing he could have done with it.

The scale is a Salter platform in the corner of Mrs Ramnarine's room, under the window, about four
feet from where he sits with his sleeve up for four minutes in the January and four minutes in the
July.

It does not occur to him. There is no instruction covering it, nobody has asked him for the figure,
and a measurement taken because a man decided on his own account to take it is not a thing he has
any procedure for at all.

---

He considered, in the January of 1984, whether to add anything.

He worked at it for some days. A line at the foot, under the figure. A sentence about the station.
Something in the fourth paragraph where the March of 1977 thing had got in without his consent —
he knows about that one now; he found it on a carbon in 1979 and stood in the file room holding it
for a while, and has never worked out how it got there, and it is the only sentence he has ever
written that he cannot account for.

He decided against, and the reasoning is sound and he has checked it.

An instruction was issued. It specified a measurement, an interval and a place in the letter. To
add to it is to modify it, and a modified compliance is an ambiguous compliance, and the recipient
would then have two things to interpret instead of one and no means of separating them — which is
precisely the position Reggie has been in since October and would not put another man in for
anything.

Whereas an instruction obeyed exactly, in every particular, without addition, without comment, and
without a single omission in the whole remaining term of his life, is not ambiguous at all. It says
one thing. It says it every January and every July. Any man who has ever received anything from
Reggie Tar will know how to read it, because it is the only sentence he has ever been able to
construct reliably, and he has been constructing it since 1954, and it has never once failed to
carry.

So he writes the figure at the foot, with the date, and nothing else.

He is aware that a man who has decided to say a thing by not saying it has left himself no way to
find out whether it arrived.

He has, on the whole, been able to live with that.

---

In the April of 1984 he wrote to Jean Leslie in Aberdeen.

The reasoning took about ten minutes and there is nothing wrong with it. He had been over the
paragraph upwards of forty times and had got nothing, and a man who has been over a specimen forty
times and got nothing has learnt something about the specimen and a good deal more about himself,
and the correct step when the instrument is suspect is not to run it again. It is to obtain a second
instrument and to blind it.

So he copied out the paragraph with the identifying material removed — no names, no place, the age
altered, the interval altered, the profession given as *a colleague of long standing, formerly
resident* — and set down the two hypotheses in a numbered form, and asked her to say which the text
supported and with what confidence, and not to answer at once.

She did not answer at once. She answered in about three weeks, which he took to be a good sign
regarding the seriousness of the reading and which he has since decided may have had a different
cause.

She said that on the text alone she would put it at the second hypothesis, at about four to one,
and that she would not go higher than that on one paragraph and would be suspicious of anybody who
did. She gave three features and her reasoning on each, and one of the three was a feature he had
noticed and discounted and one was a feature he had not seen at all, and her working is sound and he
has been over it twice and cannot fault it.

Four to one is not a fact. He knows exactly what four to one is and he has not once, in fifteen
months, treated it as anything else.

It is a different object from no figure.

That is the whole of what it is, and it is enough to be going on with, and it does not require him
to do anything, and he has done nothing.

---

At the bottom, under her signature, she had written one more line:

*You took the names out. I did not need them.*

He read that in the office at about half past four with the door shut and found that he sat down,
which he had not intended, and stayed there for a period he did not time.

He has not answered it. There is no question in it and it does not require an answer, and he has
taken the letter out of the file twice since, which he does not do with things.

---

The other thing he did, in the May, is that he went to the second cabinet and took out one of the
replies and read it.

He had not read one of them twice in eighteen years. He took the one from the April of 1977,
because the March of 1977 is the letter with the carbon in it and he wanted to see whether anything
had come back, and there is nothing in it. It is four paragraphs about the eastern pens and there is
a supplier's address in the second one and the hand is perfectly steady and it is a letter from a
veterinary surgeon to a colleague.

He read it twice and put it back in the right place and went down to the pens.

He would not be able to say what he expected to find, and he has not repeated the experiment.

## VII

The letter goes on Thursday. There is the middle pens drainage, which he has asked about before
and cannot at the moment lay hands on the reply to, and there is the Barbados supplier, and there
is a question at the end.

His collars do not sit. They are good collars and they were made in 1958 and there is something
the laundry has been doing since about the spring, and he has mentioned it twice and it has not
improved, and he has decided not to mention it a third time because the woman is doing her best
and it is not important.

The stairs to the upper store take longer than they did. He is sixty-eight. He would think very
little of a man of sixty-eight who expected otherwise.

---

He gets down to the eastern pens at about seven.

He does not sleep and there is no purpose in lying in the dark once the machine is up, so he
dresses and walks down, and there is an hour before the first of them arrives, and in that hour
there is nobody on the station who wants anything.

The light comes across the water from the east at that time of the morning in a way he has been
looking at since March of 1954 and has never once attempted to describe, and has never written
anything down about, and could not say what it is for.

It is the only hour in the day with nothing filed under it.

He would not give it up.
