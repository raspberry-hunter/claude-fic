# Discontinuation — Variant B, continued

## IX

He wrote to Trinidad in the September of 1979.

Not in the quarterly letter. A separate letter, one side, on the grounds that a fact of that class
does not go into a paragraph about a sluice, and that he had left Trinidad rather than go on being
the only man in a house who knew anything, and was not going to run the same arrangement in reverse
at four thousand miles.

He told him about Ramón. Where he came from, what he did, that there were three daughters, that
Pascual had not looked for it and had not expected it. He did not soften it and he did not
apologise for it, because either would have been an insult to a man of that description. And he did
not tell him he need not reply, because excusing a man from answering is a way of deciding for him,
and he had done a quantity of that.

There was no reply. The December letter came on time, four paragraphs about the eastern pens with a
question at the end, and Pascual answered it within the week.

He has never been able to establish whether the silence was a refusal, an incapacity, or nothing at
all. It did not occur to him for eight years that there might be a fourth thing.

---

Ramón asked him on the porch in the September of 1983, with his feet up on the rail and a glass
on the boards beside him, in the voice he used for everything, which was one setting louder than
the conversation required.

"Why'd you leave the man in Trinidad?"

And Pascual, who had spent four years finding out that he could be asked things, discovered that
there was a difference between being able to answer and having the answer to hand. He sat with
it for long enough that Ramón took his feet off the rail.

"You don't have to."

"No, I do. Give me a minute. It's not that it's hard. It's that I've only said it once and I
said it to him."

So he gave him the fourteenth of July: the doorway, and the tetanus in equids, and the forty
minutes, and the fact that he could not have said how the man was. He gave him the arrangement
and why it had no remedy inside itself. He gave him the boy in the delta and the March of 1964
and what carelessness cost in that house. He was fair to it. It was all true and he had believed
every word of it for a fortnight in 1966 and he told Ramón so.

Then he told him about the Tuesday.

"He said it first," he said. "Before I could get to any of it. I'd had three weeks to arrange how
to say it and I'd got about four sentences out and he put his hands on his knees and said, *You
are tired of me.*"

"And you said?"

"I said no." Pascual turned the glass round on the boards. "Straight off. Inside a second. And
it was true, in the sense he'd have taken it — I wasn't tired of *him*, I have never for one hour
of my life been tired of that man. And then I sat there and I could hear how fast I'd said it."

"And?"

"And I said, You're right."

Ramón thought about that.

"Out loud," he said.

"Out loud. In the room. To him."

"And then you went anyway."

"And then I went anyway."

Ramón picked his glass up and looked at it and put it down again without drinking, which is what
he did instead of pausing.

"Then he did it right," he said.

Pascual did not follow.

"He asked. He didn't sit there hoping you'd volunteer it, he didn't sulk about, he didn't get it
out of you sideways over a year and a half the way people do. He looked at you and he said the
thing and he gave you the chance to say no to it." Ramón shrugged. "That's all I've ever wanted
off anybody. I've told you that. I'm no good at guessing."

"He did it once," Pascual said. "In fifty years. He guessed once in his life and he was right and
it cost him everything he had to do it."

"Once is what I'd have needed," said Ramón, who was talking about himself, and who had no idea
what he had just put down on the boards between them.

---

They came round to the letters, because Ramón wanted to know whether there was any of it left,
and Pascual said there was: quarterly, seventeen years, work only, four paragraphs, courteous;
that he answered every one within the week and had never once missed.

"Seventeen years." Ramón was doing the sum on his fingers, openly, because he did everything
openly. "Sixty-eight letters."

"Sixty-nine. There was one in the December."

"And who looks after him?"

"There was a woman. Bevan's wife. She used to walk down to the pens on a Friday and ask him how
many hours he'd slept and write the number down."

"Good."

"She went to England in 1970."

Ramón waited to be told what had replaced her, and was not told, and did not ask again, because
he had understood the shape of the silence without needing to be clever about it. What he did
instead — and he was not thinking about Trinidad at all, he had moved on, he was a pharmacist on
a porch at the end of a long week — was start complaining about a woman on Calle Loíza who had
been on the same tablets for eleven years.

"Eleven years, twice a day, never missed. Then her sister dies and she's in Ponce for a month and
she comes back and she doesn't start again. And nobody notices. Not the doctor, not the daughter,
not me — I'm the one who counts the boxes and I didn't notice for fourteen months." He was
genuinely annoyed about it. "It's never the ones who argue with you. The ones who argue with you,
you know exactly where you are. It's the ones who say yes to everything and then one day the
thing that was reminding them isn't there any more, and they don't decide anything, they just
stop, and they're in perfect good faith about it, and nobody's watching because why would you
watch a patient who does everything he's told."

"No," said Pascual.

"Fourteen months." Ramón got up. "Anyway. You want coffee."

He went in and put the kettle on, which was what he did at the end of things, and Pascual sat on
the porch in Santurce and did in about a minute what he had done in the June of 1970 in a kitchen
in San Juan, which was work out precisely what would have to be done: a note to Reggie, or a note
to Jean, or a name given to whoever had the Colonel's chair now, with a quiet word attached.

It was the identical minute. It had not improved in thirteen years.

---

What he sent, in the October, went out in a letter otherwise about the sluice and a supplier and
the figure for the eastern pens.

He drafted it three times. The first draft asked for something else and he burned it in the sink
and has never told anybody what was in it. The version that went was the flattest one.

> One other matter. You will be sixty-seven in January. Have whoever holds the medical chest take
> your blood pressure in the January and again in the July, and put the two figures at the foot
> of your letter with the dates. It takes four minutes.

He did not say why. He did not say anything that could be answered, because there was no channel
open in which an answer could have been put, and he had closed it himself, and he knew it.

Reggie complied.

He complied exactly. He did not remark on it, did not query it, did not ask after Pascual's health
in return, did not — in the whole of the remaining two years — put a figure in any letter but the
ones for January and July, because the instruction had specified January and July.

The readings were unremarkable. Pascual has them still, in a folder, four of them, in a hand you
could set type from:

    14.i.84    142/84
    16.vii.84  138/82
    12.i.85    140/84
    15.vii.85  138/80

## X

He found out by post, three weeks after the funeral, from Jean.

That is not anybody's fault. There was no mechanism. He was a name in a correspondence file at an
institution four thousand miles off and nobody at the Martin in 1985 had the least idea who he had
been, and the ones who had known were dead or in Aberdeen, and Jean wrote the day she heard, and
she heard late herself.

He collapsed at the eastern pens at about eight in the morning on the sixth of November. A junior
was with him, whose name Pascual has never learnt and has never asked for. It was his heart. It
was quick — a matter of seconds, by the account, with no warning and nothing said. He was
sixty-eight.

Jean's letter is two sides and there is not one unkind sentence in it, and she does not repeat what
she wrote in the October of 1966 and never has. She gives him the date and the hour and what was
said at the service and who came. She tells him that Reggie had, at some point, put his affairs in
an order that made everything easy for everybody, which surprised nobody. And in the last
paragraph, because it was an administrative question and she is a scientist and she asked it
plainly, she says that the station has a file of Pascual's letters, all of them, indexed by date in
Reggie's hand, and asks whether he would like them returned or destroyed.

He did not answer within the week.

For the first time in nineteen years he did not answer within the week. He did not answer for four
months.

---

What he did that night was get out the other side of it.

He had them all. Seventy-six, from the December of 1966 to the September of 1985, in a box that had
moved house twice, and he took them out and put them on the kitchen table in order and read them
straight through, and it took him until about four in the morning, and it was the first serious use
the instrument had had since 1979.

It worked perfectly. That is what has to be got right. Six years in a field with nothing to carry,
seventy-four years old, and the thing came up and did what it was built for the moment he put it on
the only object it had ever been made for, and there was no falling-off in it anywhere.

It also found nothing at all before the January of 1984.

He went back over 1979 to 1983 four times, and then a fifth time in the morning with the light on
and coffee, looking specifically for it, wanting to find it, and it is not there. Those letters are
the same letters. Same length, same courtesy, same four paragraphs, a new subject in each, a
question at the end. Nineteen years of a man being ground down and not one quarter of it is
legible, because there was no decline. There was a man eating slightly too little, every day, at an
even rate, since before either of them had a word for it, and then a morning on which there was not
enough of him left.

That is why the letters are the same letters. They were an accurate record.

What he can read is 1984 and 1985, and he can read them the way you read a chart.

The range narrows first. From the March of 1984 the letters stop bringing anything new: it is the
sluice, the middle pens, and the Barbados supplier, quarter after quarter, three subjects working
round, where for seventeen years each letter had arrived with something in it Pascual had not
thought about that month.

And in the September of 1984 Reggie asks him for the drainage figure for the middle pens.

He had asked for it in the June. Pascual had sent it in the June, within the week, as he sent
everything. And in the September Reggie asked for it again, in the same words, in the third
paragraph, with no reference to having asked before and no apology for asking twice, because he did
not know he had.

And Pascual sent it again, within the week, without remarking on it, because he was reading his
letters for content.

Nineteen years of quarterly specimens from the one man on earth he could read, arriving four times
a year, carriage paid, and he read them for content.

The hand holds all the way to the end and is the last thing to hold, which is exactly what he would
have predicted of that hand.

---

The whole of the legible period is twenty-three months.

He has done that arithmetic more often than he has done any other. Nineteen years in which there
was nothing to see, and twenty-three months in which there was, and the twenty-three months are
almost precisely the interval during which he had a monitoring arrangement in place, running
correctly, reported on time, in writing, twice a year.

---

There is a second finding and it took him longer, because he distrusted it, because he wanted it.

He designed the test himself. He did not think of it as a test at the time; he thought of it as
knowing when to take a plate away. But he ran it for twelve years and it never once gave a false
result: when that man stops eating properly, something is wrong with him. He would have staked his
practice on it in 1965 and he would stake it now.

The test did not stop running when he got on the aeroplane. It ran every day for nineteen years
and returned the same answer.

He went at the alternatives properly, because he could see what he wanted the answer to be and
that is precisely when a clinician is worth nothing. Old men living alone eat badly. Institutional
food is institutional food. There is no woman in the house. Entropy. They all fail on the rate:
grief that resolves gives you a curve that flattens, and this is a straight line from 1969 to 1985.
He was in 1984 in exactly the condition he was in in 1967 and nothing in it ever moved.

Before 1954 that man fed himself, adequately, for thirty-seven years. Between 1955 and 1966 he
required an instruction and occasionally a hand on the plate, and got both, and did well. After
the ninth of November 1966 he never again reached the state in which he could regulate it on his
own, and on the only test Pascual has, that state is a distress state, and it was continuous.

He will not put it the other way round. He has never once said, and will not say, that the man
died of missing him; that is not a finding, it is a sentence out of a bad novel, and there is a
version of him that would like to say it and he does not permit that version the floor.

What he says is: the condition did not resolve. It is the same result, taken nineteen years
running, by the only method available, and there is no reading of it he has been able to make come
out any other way, and God knows he has tried, because he would very much rather have been wrong.

## XI

He told Ramón on the third day.

He had not intended to and there was no dramatic occasion for it. Ramón came in from the pharmacy
at about seven and said, "You've been somewhere else since Tuesday, and I'm not going to guess,"
and Pascual put the letters back in the box and told him.

He told him the whole of it. He told him about the June of 1970, when the four lines from Mrs Bevan
stopped coming and he had known inside a minute what it meant and had worked out inside the same
minute what would have to be done, and had not done it, and had never satisfactorily accounted to
himself for that, and had come as near as he ever came to an account only by admitting that writing
the note would have meant admitting that he had wanted those letters, and had read them, and had
gone to Puerto Rico and arranged for somebody else to keep the watch.

He told him about the September of 1984 and the drainage figure asked for twice.

He told him about the porch, in 1983, and Calle Loíza, and the fourteen months, and how long he had
sat there afterwards before he went in for the coffee.

It took about twenty minutes and he did not once stop or qualify or arrange it, and it is the second
time in his life he has done anything of the kind, and both times were about the same man, and the
first one was seventeen years earlier in a room in Trinidad, and it is the only thing he has ever
been able to point to as evidence that the two things were the same faculty.

Ramón heard the whole of it. Then he said, "How old was he?"

"Sixty-eight."

"That's young."

And that was all he said, and he did not get up, and he stayed in the chair for a long time
afterwards not saying anything else.

---

Pascual has thought about that sentence a good deal since.

Hester Bevan had spent thirty years looking for a sentence in that class — one that consoles
without also naming the wound — and had concluded that it does not exist in the language, and had
had the sense not to substitute a worse one, and had asked a question with a number for an answer
instead, every Friday for four years. And a pharmacist in Santurce who had never met anybody
involved, and had no faculty of any kind, and was not a clever man, produced one in about four
seconds.

The difference is not that Ramón was cleverer. The difference is that Ramón was not required to be
right. He was allowed to say an ordinary thing badly, because nothing in his construction depended
on the reading being accurate, and so the ordinary thing was available to him and it worked.

Everyone else in this had a faculty. That was the whole trouble from the beginning.

Later, in the same chair, Ramón said: "Tell me about him. You've never told me one thing about him.
Twelve years with somebody and I don't know if he was tall."

"He was not tall. Five foot six."

"You're taller than that."

"Yes."

So Pascual told him, and what came out was not a description of a man. It was the output of the
instrument, because that was the only form in which he had ever held him.

He was very neat and he never learnt what to do with his hands when they were empty. He took a
fever the way you read a dial off a wall. You could not praise him — you could praise the work and
he would take it and hand it straight on to whoever had done that piece of it, and if you praised
the man he would look at you as though you had made an arithmetical error and were about to find it
yourself. When he was tired the left shoulder came up. When he was very tired he became polite.
When he could not finish a plate he put his knife and fork together and went on talking, and if you
took the plate away he did not remark on it, and if you did not take it away he would sit in front
of it for an hour.

Ramón listened to all of it and did not say the obvious thing about a man who describes another man
exclusively in observations, because it was not his to say and because he was kind, and also
because by then he had been living with Pascual for four years and had worked out on his own
account that this was what the man had instead of the other vocabulary, and that it was not a
lesser one, and that you took what you were given and did not send it back for adjustment.

---

The obvious thing, for the record, since Ramón was too well brought up to say it and Pascual has
had to arrive at it on his own, is that those are not observations.

You do not know that a man's left shoulder comes up when he is tired. Nobody knows that. You come
to know it by watching him be tired several hundred times over twelve years and minding on every
one of the occasions. *If you did not take the plate away he would sit in front of it for an hour*
is not a description of anybody. It is evidence that somebody took the plate away.

He had made a declaration. He had made it out loud, at some length, in his own kitchen, to a man
who had asked him a simple question, and he had not the faintest idea he had done it, which is
precisely the condition Reggie Tar was in in the March of 1977 with a sentence about eating at one
o'clock.

That much took him about a week and did him no particular harm.

The rest took until the following spring, and Ramón could not have handed it to him, having never
met the man and having nothing to compare it with.

Every item in the list is a distress reading. Not one thing in it is anything else. That is the
complete inventory of a man Pascual lived beside for twelve years and loved without qualification,
produced on request, at seventy-four, with no time limit and every incentive to do him justice.

Not because that was the whole of him. Because an instrument reads change, and change is what
strain produces, and the arrangement had been built so that Reggie only ever became fully legible
to him under load.

He knew the man best when the man was worst. There was no mechanism by which he could have known
him any other way, and it was nobody's fault, and he has spent a considerable amount of time
establishing that last part and does not find that it helps.

## XII

He answered Jean in the March, and he asked for two things.

He asked for the letters. And then, because it was the third time in his life he had sat with that
minute and the first time he had done anything at the end of it, he asked her whether there had been
a post-mortem, and if so whether a copy could be got.

There had. A man of sixty-eight collapses at work in front of a witness, under nobody's care and
with no disease on record, and in Trinidad in 1985 that is a coroner's matter and there is a
document. Jean got it in about six weeks through the Colonel's successor and sent it in the June in
the same parcel as the letters, with a compliments slip and nothing written on it, which he took at
the time for delicacy and has since decided was the same judgement she made in 1966 and has never
withdrawn.

It runs to three pages. It is competent and impersonal and was typed by somebody in Port of Spain
who had never met anybody in this.

The technical findings are these, and he could recite them and has never had to.

The coronary arteries were patent. There was no atheroma of any significance, no thrombus, and no
evidence of recent or old infarction. The valves were normal. The lungs, liver and kidneys were
unremarkable for the age. There was nothing wrong with the man anywhere.

The heart weighed 244 grammes.

Cause of death was given in the usual form: I(a) cardiac arrhythmia; I(b) myocardial atrophy; II
chronic undernutrition.

An arrhythmia is not an event. Every heart in the world does it, several times a day, all its life,
and gets through it, and nobody notices and nobody is meant to. His had got through it some tens of
thousands of times since the January of 1917. On the morning of the sixth of November there was not
enough heart left to get through an ordinary one.

And on the first page, in the external examination, before any of it, in the sentence that opens
every report of this kind and is written down before the pathologist has picked up an instrument:
*the body is that of a poorly-nourished male, height 167 cm, weight 48.6 kg.*

A stranger who had never seen him alive, standing at a table, taking his condition off the surface
of him in one glance and writing it down in eleven words.

That is the faculty. That is the entire faculty. It is not rare and it is not a gift and it does not
require twelve years or love or an arrangement; it requires being in the room and looking, and
somebody was in the room in the November of 1985, and it was not Pascual, and by then the reading
was free.

---

He left it on the kitchen table and went out for two hours, which was not an accident, and when he
came back Ramón had read it.

Ramón is a pharmacist and had been one for forty-one years and does not know what a heart is
supposed to weigh. But a man who counts boxes for a living knows to two decimal places what 48.6
kilos on a frame of 167 centimetres means, and knows what *poorly nourished* is doing at the top of
a page, and knows perfectly well which measurements catch which failures, because that is not
medicine, it is his trade, and he has been correcting doctors about it since 1945.

He had the report in one hand and the folder with the four blood pressures in the other. He had got
the folder out himself.

"These are fine," he said. "All four. I'd have signed those off."

"Yes."

"You should have asked what he weighed."

"Yes."

Ramón put both down on the table, side by side, and stood there looking at them, and Pascual
watched him arrive at it — watched it happen in a face, in about six seconds, the way he had watched
things happen in faces for fifty years.

"You asked for the one somebody else takes," Ramón said.

And then, not unkindly, not as an accusation, in the flat voice he used for stock and invoices:

"Because if you'd asked him what he weighed, he'd have known why you wanted it."

He did not ask the next question. He was entitled to it and he did not ask it, and he went and put
the kettle on.

---

Here is the reasoning, which Pascual had never once set out and which took a man with no faculty
about six seconds.

A blood pressure is a number a third party produces. The figure arrives through an orderly with a
cuff, and it can therefore be requested in the middle of a paragraph about a sluice by a colleague
with a proper interest in the running of an institution. It admits nothing.

A weight is a number the man takes about himself. He stands on the scale, he reads the dial, he
writes it down and posts it four thousand miles, and the whole of it is a thing he does *for you*,
and there is no version of asking for it that does not say: I have been watching you since 1954 and
I did not stop in 1966.

That was in the first draft. That is what he burned in the sink in the October of 1983.

He did not choose wrong out of stupidity. He knew in the October of 1983 which of the two figures
was diagnostic; he has never once in sixty years been confused about what a measurement is for. He
chose the one that told him nothing because it was the one he could ask for.

---

The one o'clock instruction was working.

It was issued in 1955 by a man standing in front of him who could see him; it was right on the day
it was given; it was obeyed exactly for thirty years; and it slowed the whole thing down. It was
also short by whatever quantity of food produces a pound and three quarters in a year, and an
instruction of that kind wants adjusting about every two years by somebody who is in the room.

There was nobody in the room after 1966, and Reggie could not originate an adjustment any more than
he could originate anything else, and so it ran for the rest of his life at the setting it had been
given at thirty-eight.

The instruction of 1955 and the instruction of 1983 are the same error twenty-eight years apart.
Both real, both obeyed, both right when they were written down, neither ever looked at again,
because looking at it would have required the presence of the man who wrote it.

---

He got to the correction some time in 1987, sitting in the same chair, and it is the only part of
this he has never put to Ramón in any form.

The fiction did not work. It could not have worked and was not for one moment capable of working,
and the reason is arithmetic. A man who writes to you quarterly about a sluice for seventeen years
and then, without warning, in an October, requires your blood pressure twice a year in writing has
said one thing and one thing only, and it is audible from four thousand miles, and Reggie received
it and did as he was told.

So it concealed nothing from the person it was supposed to conceal it from. On that account it was
bought for nothing.

It was not bought for nothing.

The wall was never between Pascual and Reggie. Reggie was on the far side of an ocean and had known
since 1966 and had one confirmed result on Pascual's interior, filed, unretracted, and — Pascual
knew the man — taken out and looked at. The wall was between Pascual and Pascual. The whole value
of a blood pressure was that it came with a story he could tell *himself* while he wrote it:
institutional, third-party, four minutes with a cuff, a colleague's proper interest in the running
of a station. There is no such story available for a weight. To ask for a weight he would have had
to sit down at a table in San Juan at seventy-two years of age and write it out knowing exactly
what he was doing.

He bought the right not to have to know that. It is a real commodity. It was the only thing on
offer.

It cost two stone.

He did not pay it. That is the part that will not lie down. He has been through the transaction
looking for the place where anything at all was taken off him and there is nothing there: he had
Ramón, and the practice, and the porch, and his own weight has not moved four pounds since 1971.
The whole of the price was debited to an account four thousand miles off, in the name of a man who
was not told a purchase was being made and could not have been given the currency if he had asked
for it.

There is a version of that ledger running the other way, and he has been over it more than he can
defend. For twelve years Reggie Tar did the best work of his life on an overhead that somebody else
was carrying, and did not know what it was costing, because he was not the one being charged.

Twice, then. Once each. Neither of them told at the time. The accounts do not cancel — they were
never in the same currency, and only one of the two was fatal.

And the man would have sent the figure. That is the part he has never got out from under. He would
have known why it was being asked and would not have said so; he would have stood on the scale and
written it at the foot of the letter, in January and in July, exactly as instructed, and not
remarked on it, because remarking on it was not in the instruction.

---

What he had done, having left Trinidad rather than go on being the only man in the house who knew
anything — having gone four thousand miles precisely so as not to spend the rest of his life
holding information about somebody who could not hold it himself — was sit down, once, in writing,
at the one moment in nineteen years when it counted, and arrange for a fact about his own condition
to be present in the room and unavailable to him.

That is the exact disorder. He had spent twelve years reading around it, and he had loved it, and
it had worn him to nothing, and he had gone to Puerto Rico to get away from it, and at seventy-two
he did it himself, deliberately, in a fair hand, in a letter about drainage.

With the 1966 result in his pocket proving that he had not needed to.

---

The other thing that came in 1987 he has not put to Ramón either, and this one is about Ramón,
which is part of why.

He wrote the letter of September 1979 out of scruple. He is still satisfied that writing it was
right; he would write it again tomorrow and would not alter a line of it, and he is not looking for
a way to be sorry about it.

What he never once considered — not in 1979, not in the October of 1983, not at the kitchen table
with seventy-six letters in front of him — is how a letter of that kind is read at the other end.

A man who writes to tell you he has somebody is telling you it is finished. That is the plain
reading. It requires no faculty whatever, it is available inside a minute to anybody who can
construe an English sentence, and Reggie was entitled to it, and it was wrong.

So the position in the October of 1983 was this. A man who had been given a full stop four years
earlier — in writing, unprompted, from four thousand miles, by somebody who had gone out of his way
to be honest — received an instruction requiring him to report on the state of his own body twice a
year for the remainder of his life, from the same address, with no explanation attached to it.

Pascual did not know he was reopening anything. He had never known it was closed.

## XIII

He has the numbers now. He has kept them together and has never been able to put them anywhere
else.

The second one is off the first page of the coroner's report, converted, because he cannot think in
kilogrammes about that man and has stopped trying.

The first one is off a half-sheet of ruled paper, four lines, in a hand like a ledger, which he kept
in a drawer in San Juan for twenty-six years and never once was able to say why, and which he does
not have. Jean Leslie asked him for the figure in the November of 1986 and he posted her the paper
itself in the January.

    9.xii.69   9 st 9
    6.xi.85    7 st 9

Two stone, in sixteen years. A pound and three quarters a year. It would not have shown in any one
year and it would have shown in any three.

The figure he worked out once and checked twice and has never gone back to is what that comes to
per day, in food, taking every assumption in the direction most favourable to himself.

It is seventeen calories.

It is half a mouthful. It is less than the difference between two helpings of the same thing. It is
not a quantity any person alive could see across a table, and it is not a quantity a man could
detect in himself, and it is certainly not a quantity that produces a symptom, or a bad month, or a
letter that reads differently from the letter before it.

It took thirty years and it was enough.

He can say why he kept it. He could say it by the time he put it in the envelope, which is what
made the envelope possible, and it is not a comfort and it does not let him off anything.

He kept it because it was a baseline.

And nobody keeps a baseline except somebody who intends, at some point, by some means, to take the
second reading.
