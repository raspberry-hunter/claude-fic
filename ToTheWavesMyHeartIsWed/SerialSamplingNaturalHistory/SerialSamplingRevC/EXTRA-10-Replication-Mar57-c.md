# Replication

A replication is not the same experiment done twice. It is the same experiment done twice under
conditions asserted to be identical, and the whole of its value lies in the assertion, and the
assertion is very nearly always false.

Spilsbury comes in on the eleventh of March, 1957, off the same launch, in the same hat.

---

In 1954 he took Reggie for forty minutes with the food logs and Pascual for fifty on the
Wednesday morning and Jean for an hour and twenty that afternoon.

Reggie has those figures. He has never once been able to put them down and he does not trouble
to pretend otherwise now; he simply starts the count when the dining-room door shuts and takes
it off his watch when it opens, and he is aware that this is the behaviour of a man who has
learned nothing at all in three years, and he does it anyway.

His own is twenty-two minutes.

That is the first departure and it is a large one. Twenty-two minutes, and no logs on the table,
and Spilsbury is warm throughout and asks about the co-housing series and about the second paper
and about how the new pens are bedding in, and at the seventeenth minute he says:

"They're talking about a station at Bimini. Purpose-built, three ships, a proper establishment.
I've been asked whom I would put in it, and I've given one name, and it's yours."

Reggie says the thing about the co-housing series, which is that it is in its third year and
that moving the animals would end it, and that a man does not walk away from a three-year record
in year three.

It is entirely true. It is the truest thing he says all week and he can hear the shape of it as it
comes out of his mouth, because he has done this before, in this room, with a column of figures
and a lie about his own competence, and it worked then, and this is the same manoeuvre with the
lie taken out.

"Quite so," Spilsbury says, and does not press, and writes nothing down. "Think about it. There's
no hurry. It'll still be there in a year."

*I have not said no,* Reggie thinks, walking out into the sun. *He has not permitted me to say
no. He has left it open and he will leave it open indefinitely, and a thing left open is a thing
that has to be declined again every year for the rest of my life, and each declining is a
datum.*

He gets as far as the water tanks before he notices what he is doing, which is composing.

---

He does two days of it.

Not lying. He has not lied to Pascual and is not going to. What he does is precisely what he did
in the August of 1954 and has not done since: he takes the whole of it into his own head and
works on it there, alone, at night, and produces a design — a way of declining Bimini annually
in terms so dull that nobody could construct anything out of it, a form of words for the Colonel,
a reason to have Pascual off the station on the day of the launch — and he is four hours into
the third of these before he understands that he has arranged for a man to be sent up-coast for
a fortnight in order to protect him, and has not mentioned it to him.

He sits down on the edge of the desk.

He does not have to be caught. That is the difference and he sets it down properly, because it
is the only creditable thing about the whole two days. He catches it himself, at half past one
in the morning, with the fortnight's requisition half written.

He goes and tells him at half past one in the morning, which is not an hour at which anything
good has ever been said in this building.

Pascual listens to the whole of it sitting on the edge of the bed with his hair sticking up at
the back where he has slept on it. Then he
says:

"Bimini."

"Yes."

"And you were going to send me to Antigua for a fortnight."

"I was going to send you to Antigua for a fortnight. There's a schooner going up on Saturday."

"And tell me afterwards."

"Yes."

Pascual rubs his face with both hands, which he has been doing at Reggie in that particular way
since 1954, and says: "It's taken you two days."

"Two days and four hours. I haven't been to bed."

"In August of '54 it took you three months and a bottle." He reaches over and puts a hand round
Reggie's wrist, two fingers laid along the inside of it, which is what he does now instead of
saying things. "It's not nothing, Reggie. It's not nothing at all. Now sit down and tell me
what the man actually said, and do not summarise it."

Reggie tells him what the man actually said, and does not summarise it, and it takes until three.

And it helps.

---

Pascual's interview is on the Thursday and lasts nineteen minutes.

He comes out and is in the laboratory ten minutes later and gives Reggie the whole of it,
verbatim, sitting on the bench with his arms folded.

"He asked about your health."

"My health."

"Your health. Kindly. At length. Whether you sleep, whether you eat, whether the isolation of
the place has told on you, whether there was anything in your service that a man ought to know
about before putting him into a three-ship establishment." Pascual looks at the floor. "Which is
an entirely proper enquiry to make about a candidate for a directorship, and he made it properly,
and I've been a doctor for twenty-two years and I gave him nothing whatever, and he wasn't
surprised and didn't press, and that's the part I don't care for."

In 1954 the whole of this was a sentence in a corridor. *He wanted to know about the muscle
damage.* Nine words at a walking pace, and Reggie's hands were steady for the rest of the day on
the strength of them, and he had been in the laboratory for fifty minutes beforehand with no
account whatever of what he had touched.

That is the second departure. He has spent this Thursday morning working. Properly. He has the
notes to prove it.

That is not a thing he arranged and it is not a thing he can take any credit for. It is simply
that a man who knows he will be told does not have to construct the fifty minutes for himself,
and Reggie notes it, and files it, and does not mention it, because it would come out as thanks
and Pascual does not care to be thanked in the yard.

---

Jean's is two hours and forty minutes, which he has off her afterwards and not off his watch. That
is the third.

He does not go past the door. He is in the office with the tide sheets when she comes in at half
past twelve without knocking, which she has never done, and shuts the door behind her.

She has been offered the analytical post at his establishment. Named. Second in the section, with
a salary he makes her repeat, and first authorship on her own work as a written condition of the
appointment, which he has never in his life heard of anybody obtaining and which she obtained in
the second hour by asking for it and not moving.

She is not asking his permission and does not want his advice, and says both of those things in
the first minute in a manner he finds himself admiring against his will.

"I am telling you," she says, "because you put my name on the '56 paper and it cost you
something with the Colonel, and this is what has come of it, and you are owed the information
before anybody else has it."

*This is what has come of it.*

He has it in about a second and a half, whole, the way these things come: he made her visible.
She was a postgraduate whom nobody would trouble to interview, and he wrote three letters and
spent capital he is still paying off, and at the end of them she was a named author, and a named
author is a person one can want. Eighteen months, at compound interest, and the bill has been
presented to somebody else.

He does not say any of that. It would be an account of his own conduct offered to a woman who
has come to him with news, and he has learned, at considerable expense, what that costs the
person it is done to.

What he says is that it is an excellent post and that she has negotiated it better than he could
have, which is true, and that he will write whatever reference she requires, and that he would
like her to have the four months she is entitled to and not three.

"Thank you," Jean says. And then: "There's another thing. Not in the office."

"Agreed," Reggie says. "I have one as well."

She looks at him for rather longer than she needs to.

"The boat shed at six."

He arranges the occasion. He is aware that he is arranging the occasion and that this is
permitted; the occasion is the one thing one is allowed to arrange, and he has that from a man at
a rail, and it has taken him two years to be able to use it without checking it first.

He goes down to the pens afterwards and takes Gamma's surfacing intervals for the second time that
day, which she does not require and which he requires considerably, and he is still at the rail at
a quarter to six.

---

The boat shed at six, with the light going, and the two of them standing about six feet apart
among the drums.

"I am going to say one thing," Reggie says, "which I have no right to say and shall not repeat,
and you are not obliged to answer it, and I should prefer it if you did not."

"All right."

"It will not arrive as a demand. Nobody will ever ask you for anything. There will be a form, or
a colleague being kind, or a piece of work you agreed to in principle nine months earlier and
would look ridiculous objecting to now. It will be perfectly reasonable and it will be one of a
great many perfectly reasonable things, and you will not know which of them was the one, because
it does not announce itself, and by the time you are in a position to look back at it you will
have signed the other fifteen."

Jean does not say anything for a moment.

Then she says: "I know."

"You cannot know. Forgive me. It is not knowable in advance and that is the entire — "

"Dr Tar," she says. "I sat in that dining room on the first of September 1954 for an hour and
twenty minutes and lied to that man to his face about where Charles Cholmondeley had gone. I was
twenty-seven. I've thought about almost nothing else for three years and I've had rather
longer to look at it than you imagine, because you were looking at your own end of it and I was
looking at mine."

The light is nearly gone. Somewhere up at the house John is playing the gramophone.

"I've watched what you did," Jean says. "In '54 and since. You've made quite sure all your life
that there's nothing on you that can be pulled — and when there was something, you took a hacksaw
to it in an afternoon. Slack. Tired. Does not read his own logs. It's the cleverest thing I've
ever seen done in a room and it worked completely, and I've also watched what it cost, which I
don't think you've ever added up."

"That is — "

"I'm not going to do that," she says. "There will be things on me. There are already. I'm not
going to pretend otherwise and I'm not going to spend my life keeping myself empty so that nobody
can get a grip. I'm going to let them stand where they are, and I'm going to make it cost more
to pull them than pulling them could possibly be worth. He will never spend me on you, or on
Dr Pobil, or on anybody, because inside two years I intend to be the most expensive thing in that
building, and nobody breaks up the good apparatus in order to get at something small."

She says *or on Dr Pobil* in precisely the tone in which she has said *or on you*, and does not
look at him while she says it, and goes on to the next clause without any sort of pause.

Reggie does not react and neither does she, and the thing is done, and after three years on a
station this size he supposes it was done a considerable time ago and that he is the last to be
told, as usual.

Reggie looks at her.

He has, at this moment, about four things available, and the first of them is that being worth
something is not a defence, it is the selection criterion. He has that from the inside. He has it
from a building outside Salisbury and from two years of doing what he was extremely good at, and
every man he watched go into a room with a paper went in on the strength of being valuable, and
the two airmen who walked out of that court in March of '54 walked out because somebody had costed
them and found them worth more as instruments than as convictions. Value is what puts a man on the
list. It has never in his experience been the thing that takes him off it.

He says none of it, because she has asked him not to answer and he asked her not to answer him,
and because she is thirty and has just negotiated a written condition out of Alan Spilsbury,
and because there is nothing to be gained by telling a young woman on the eve of the best thing
that has happened to her that her plan is the plan that fails.

"I hope you are right," he says.

"And I should like my name on things," Jean says. "I'm not going to pretend that isn't most of
it."

"No. I didn't suppose you would."

She puts her hands in her pockets and looks out at the water for a bit, and then she says: "There's
one more thing, and it's the reason I didn't want the office."

---

She has been looking for Charles since the autumn of 1954.

She sets it out the way she sets out anything, properly and in order with the weak parts marked
weak. Three years of it, on her own money and in her own time, and she has kept a file.

She had one inference to work from and it is the only one available to a person who was never
given the husbandry data: that the animal came from somewhere to the south, because everybody on
the station knew that much, and that a man who comes ashore anywhere at all comes ashore among
people. So she has canvassed people. Harbour masters from St Lucia down. The Port of Spain
shipping registers, copied and sent up at four shillings a page. Two mission stations. The
Trinidad police returns for the last quarter of 1954, which took her nine months and a lie about a
family matter to obtain. Two letters to a man at Icacos, who answered the first and not the
second, and whose silence she has spent two years declining to interpret and interpreting.

Fourteen hundred miles of coast, on a research student's salary, and a list of every place on it
where a white man could come ashore and be remarked upon.

"And I have one thing," she says, "and I know exactly what it's worth, which is nothing. There is
no body. There is no unidentified European male in any return I have been able to get for that
quarter or for the two after it, and no effects, and no gear, and nothing put ashore between
Tobago and the Paria peninsula. That is not evidence. I'm perfectly aware that it isn't
evidence. It's an absence, and I've been living on it for three years, and I can do the whole of
the argument against it in my sleep."

"Why did you not — "

"Because I asked you in the March of 1955," Jean says, "on the steps, and you said that you had no
information, and you said it in the voice you use for the Colonel, and I haven't asked you again."

Reggie stands among the drums with his hands at his sides.

"You never asked me anything," she says. "Not once. In three years. He was your student, and you
put him on that beach with a chart, and I lied to a government man for eighty minutes to keep it
where you'd put it, and you haven't said his name in my hearing since. I've spent three years
trying to decide whether you're a coward or simply cold, and I couldn't get it to come out either
way, and I'm leaving in three weeks and I'm not going to spend the rest of my life not knowing."

The light is gone off the water now.

"I have the course," Reggie says.

Jean does not move.

"I have had it since the third of September 1954. The tide, the set through the passage, the
distances, the figure for the animal. It came whole at about three in the morning on the fifth day
and it has never once left me and I have never written any part of it down."

"You — "

"I did not give it to you because there was nothing you could have done with it in 1955 that was
not dangerous," he says, "and because you were the only other person on this station who had lied
about him, and I took the view that you were carrying enough. I was wrong about which of the two
was worse for you. It has taken me until this evening to see that and I am not going to pretend it
was kindness, because I have had it explained to me at some length that a man does not get to call
a thing kindness merely because it cost him something."

He makes himself go on, because the next part is the part that is actually owed.

"Your file is the wrong file. Not badly worked — it could not have been better worked on what you
had. It turns on the husbandry, and you never had the husbandry, and there was no route by which
you could have got it: what the animal was, what water he required, where he was taken from and
what he would have gone back to. Nobody had that but me. And on that material, there is no coast
anywhere in your fourteen hundred miles where he comes ashore and is remarked upon, because the
place he was going is not a place where anybody is remarked upon by anybody. You have spent three
years writing to harbour masters. There are no harbour masters."

Jean stands quite still in the dark among the drums.

"Is there any reason at all," she says, "to think he's alive."

"No."

"Then — "

"There is no reason to think it," Reggie says. "There is also no point at which the arithmetic
fails. The passage is inside what the animal could do carrying a man, at that state of tide, at
that season, with a margin. The water they were going to is water in which a man can be kept alive
provided there is something in it with an interest in keeping him so. I have run the whole of it
perhaps four hundred times in three years looking for the step where it comes apart, and I have
never found one, and I want it understood that that is not a reason and I am not going to dress it
up as one."

There is a silence.

"That's more than I've had since 1954," Jean says.

"It is a null result."

"I know what it is," she says. "It is what you have been living on."

Reggie does not answer that.

Jean says, in a voice that is not quite level, "Give it to me."

"No."

"Dr Tar — "

"You are going into that man's establishment in three weeks," Reggie says. "A thing in your head is
a thing that can be got. I have just given you the fact that I have it, which was more than I had
any business giving you, and you may make what use of that you like, and I am aware that it is now
a second thing you will have to lie about and that I have handed it to you three weeks before you
go. I have thought about that. I decided you were owed it more than you were owed protecting."

There is a long silence.

"And if I get out," Jean says at last.

"While you are there," Reggie says, "write to me about the work. From the first month, and often,
and about nothing else whatever." He stops. "And when you are out — when you are actually out,
and not before — you will put one sentence in one of them that is not about the work, and I shall
know it when I see it, and we shall see."

"That could be years."

"Yes."

Jean stands there for a while.

"I had worked out the letters part," she says. "I had not worked out the rest of it." And then,
after a moment, entirely steadily: "I am still angry with you."

"Yes," Reggie says. "I should think considerably less of you if you were not."

---

She sails on the fifth of April.

Jean Leslie's letter to the Colonel is a model of its kind: professional, warm in the correct
proportion, complete, and it tells him nothing whatever about anything. Reggie reads it in the
office with the Colonel standing over him being pleased about the credit to the station, and
recognises the technique in the third sentence, and has to put it down.

The Colonel, who has been building up to it since Christmas, chooses the following week to ask
what precisely the veterinary and pathological expansion is for, in the long term, given that
there is now an assistant on the establishment and a second refrigerator on the estimates.

Reggie gives him a perfectly good answer. The Colonel accepts it. Neither of them regards the
matter as closed and neither of them says so.

---

That night at the rail he tells Pascual what he did not say in the boat shed, all of it, and why.

"I could have stopped it," he says. "Not honestly. I could have delayed the reference, or put it
to the Colonel that she was in the middle of the intake series, or written to the editor. Three
weeks and it would have gone off. Spilsbury does not chase."

"And?"

"And that would have been arranging an outcome for somebody without telling her I was doing it,"
Reggie says, "and I have been informed at some length what that is."

Pascual is quiet for a while.

"You are certain you did not decline to stop it because you have learned a rule about not
stopping things."

Reggie turns that over. It is a good question. It is precisely the question he would have asked
himself if he had thought of it, and he did not think of it, and Pascual did.

"No," he says at last. "I am not certain. I have been over it and I cannot get to the bottom of
it and I do not think I ever shall. She would have gone in the end regardless, which is true, and
being true does not make it my reason."

"No."

"There is the other thing," Reggie says. "I told her I have the course."

Pascual takes his hands out of his pockets.

"Not the course. That I have it."

"Reggie."

"I know."

"Three weeks," Pascual says. "You've given a woman of thirty a thing to carry into that house
and she'll be carrying it for as long as she's in it, and she can't put it down, and she can't
tell anybody, and if they ever come at her sideways she'll have to be quick." He is not shouting.
Pascual not shouting is a good deal worse and has been since 1954. "You know what that is. You've
done it. You did two years of it."

"Yes."

"And you did it anyway."

"She asked me what I was, in so many words, and she has been asking it for three years, and she was
going to leave this station with the answer she had made up," Reggie says. "I have had it put to me
that the thing a person wants is to be told. I was told that by you, in about January of last year,
and I have been applying it since, and this is the first occasion on which applying it has cost
somebody who is not me."

Pascual looks out at the pen for a long moment.

"Ay," he says. "Well. That's what it's like. Welcome to it."

"That is not an answer."

"No," Pascual says. "There is not one. There is not an answer to that and there is never going to
be, and you will do it again, and sometimes it will go badly, and the alternative is what you were
in 1954." His hand arrives flat between the shoulder blades and stays.

---

**November 1960.**

The letter comes with the morning mail on the fifteenth of November and is dated the eighth of
October, from Aberdeen, and is four pages, and three and a half of them are about herring.

She has left Spilsbury's establishment. She left in the summer, on her own terms, at a time of her
choosing, and Spilsbury wrote her a reference which she encloses a copy of because she thinks it
will amuse him, and it does not amuse him at all, because it is the reference of a man who has
been comprehensively got the better of and knows it and is too good a scientist to lie about the
work.

Three years. Fourteen papers, first author on nine. The osmoregulation series that Reggie has been
quoting since March.

Then, in the last half-page, without any particular emphasis and in the middle of a paragraph
about the Aberdeen tanks, she mentions that she was asked twice for things she did not care to
give, and gave neither, and that on the second occasion the matter was dropped inside a
fortnight.

And then, at the bottom of the fourth page, in a hand that has not altered in six years:

*I am out. I have been out since June and there is nothing of his in it anywhere. You said that
we should then see. I am asking.*

Reggie sits with the four pages in the office for a long time.

It worked. That is not the difficulty. The difficulty is why it worked, and he has it in front of
him in her own hand and has been refusing to have it for three and a half years.

She was not protected by being careful and she was not protected by being small. She was protected
by being expensive. By the second year there was no transaction available in which spending her
returned more than spending her cost, and the reason there was none is that she had put every hour
since 1957 into becoming a person no sane administrator would break up — openly, in print, under
her own name, with the levers left standing exactly where they were.

He has had two techniques in his life and he has never had a third.

The first is not to build the lever. Own nothing, want nothing, be attached to nobody, and there
is then nothing on a man that can be pulled. He settled on that at twenty-nine and ran it for eight
years and it held perfectly, and what it cost is a question he has always declined on the grounds
that the alternative was Salisbury.

The second is to break the lever once it exists, which he did in one afternoon in 1954 with a
column of food figures and a lie about his own competence. Not by ceasing to be useful — he has
never ceased to be useful and could not if he tried. By making himself useless *for the thing they
would have wanted him for*, which is a narrower operation and a good deal cleverer, and he has
been quietly proud of it for six years.

Both techniques take the lever to be the danger. Everything he has ever thought about this
proceeds from that and he has never once had a sentence in his head that did not assume it. And
what she has done, for three years, in a building belonging to Alan Spilsbury, is the third thing:
leave the lever standing, in the open, unconcealed, and make it too expensive to pull.

Which brings him, sitting in the office at two in the afternoon with the herring in front of him,
to the fact that there is a lever on him now and has been since the August of 1954.

He did not build it deliberately. He was three whiskies in and it was got out of him, and by every
rule he had ever held he should have ended it inside a week, and he has instead spent six years
making it larger and more legible — visible enough that a research student named it out loud in a
boat shed without troubling to look up from the water. It is first-hand, it is unbreakable, and
there is no version of the food-logs manoeuvre that touches it. It is the single most usable thing
about him and it will be there for the rest of his life.

He has understood the whole of it, privately, since the second night, as a debt. A thing taken on
that will one day be presented.

It is not a debt.

Bimini was *offered*. Four times, in a warm room, with no hurry attached, and it will be offered
again in the spring; and a man is offered a thing when compelling him would cost more than
compelling him could return. Reggie Tar has become expensive. He has become expensive over six
years, in print, under his own name, doing the best work of his life — and he has been able to do
the best work of his life because he has spent those six years in a room with somebody who tells
him things, and because a man who is told things does not have to build the fifty minutes for
himself.

He has suspected for years that the machinery and the body it runs in are one system; that
starving the body of food or sleep feeds the rate of the machinery. This may well be true; but the
rate was never the variable. What a machine produces is its rate less whatever else is running on
it, and a machine that is not building fifty minutes on a regular basis can produce more than one
that is.

He did not arrive at her method. He fell into the mechanism, drunk, in a room he had no business
being in, and has spent six years mistaking it for the bill.

It is not a law. She was lucky as well as right and she would say so first; being expensive fails
the instant somebody wants a thing more than they want the apparatus, and neither of them will
know whether it holds until it is tested. But it is the arm of it he never looked at, and it has
been standing in his own station since 1954.

And she is out, and she is safe, and she got there by the route he could not see, and the last
thing she has ever asked him for is now the one thing he is at liberty to give.

---

He writes it there and then. It takes five lines and he has had it for six years and two months.

*Springs on the twenty-ninth of August, two days past the full. Westerly set through the passage
of something over a knot at that state, holding the first seventy miles. Four hundred miles to the
Serpent's Mouth. Past the Mouth the water is brackish a long way in. An animal in condition does
eighty in a day unencumbered and something under fifty carrying; call it ten days at the outside.
There is no figure for a man and I have never attempted one.*

Then he sits with the sheet in front of him for a while, because it is the first time any part of
it has existed outside his own head in written form, and it is in his own hand, and it is going
into a bag on a boat.

He posts it.

---

Afterwards he goes and finds Pascual, who is in the small laboratory doing something to the
refrigerator and swearing at it, and stands in the doorway.

"Jean is out. I have sent her the course."

Pascual comes out from behind the refrigerator very fast for a man of forty-nine, and looks at him,
and then puts the spanner down.

"Good," he says.

"There is a second thing, and it is going to take rather longer. I have been wrong about something
since 1946 and I have only this afternoon been able to see the shape of it, and I should like to
do it properly, and I have not got any of it written down."

"Then I shall put the kettle on," Pascual says, "and you will start at the beginning, and you will
not summarise."
