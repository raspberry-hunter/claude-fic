# Differential

The noise Tar makes on the verandah isn't a noise a man makes about a colleague.

Pascual has spent twenty years listening to animals make sounds they didn't intend to
make, and the useful thing about such a sound is never its content, which is nothing; it
is that the animal didn't decide to produce it. He has heard Tar say *no* to the Colonel.
He has heard him say *no* to Pascual with a knife in it. He has never in six months heard
him make a sound he didn't first approve.

So. *Do you know him?* And: *We have worked together.* And then the man takes the boy away
with him and when Pascual asks, later, he is told: half past nine.

At half past nine Pascual goes to his room to ask about Spilsbury.

He does not examine why he he is insistent on going at half past nine rather than at breakfast, when the
question would keep perfectly well until breakfast. He has a rule about examining things
for which he has no instrument.

He has been going to that room, on one errand or another, since February. He disliked him
on sight, and that is the first entry.

---

Twenty minutes off the launch, and a small neat Englishman on the step of the office with
his collar done up in that heat and his shoes very proper and not a mark on him anywhere.
Public school and the good sort of degree and a war he'd have had indoors. Pascual had met
the type at every establishment he'd ever worked at and had never yet been wrong about
one of them. He put him in a drawer inside four minutes and went off to look at the
animals.

Then the man wouldn't stop. About the work; only ever about the work, at a pitch Pascual
had heard out of perhaps three people in his life, none of them English and all of them
exhausting. He'd corner you in the yard over a figure. He'd corner you at half past nine
at night over a figure. Pascual found it insufferable, and found himself going down into
the yard at hours when he might be cornered, and did not put those two facts side by side,
and hasn't since.

And then, a fortnight in, the lagoon at low water.

He'd gone down to see what was living in the bottom before the new intake went across it,
so that when the works changed it there would be something to compare it against. That is a
sensible thing to be doing. Pascual had heard it explained at supper, at length, and had
thought: yes, and you'll send one of the postgraduates down to do it — somebody who can't
say no to you — even though it's your work, because you'll never get your hands dirty yourself.

He was in it to the shoulder. Sitting in it. The black stuff a foot under the grass that
stinks of eggs the moment you turn it and doesn't come out of anything, and it was up both
arms and across the front of the shirt and there was a quantity of it in his hair, and he
was going through the sieve into the trays, picking the things out one at a time with two
fingers, and he had ninety-one of one kind of worm out of a single haul and was pleased
about it in a manner Pascual had not previously believed an Englishman capable of.

He looked up and said the figure, to whoever was standing there, which happened to be
Pascual.

And then the hands. Pascual looked at the hands, and went on looking at them, because the
hands are where a man keeps his history — and these were short in the nail and scarred across the back of one of
them and not in the least careful of themselves, and had been inside
a great many things, and were holding a worm now. He had been on this station a fortnight
and had not once looked at the man's hands. He had decided about him, in four minutes, on a
step, and then not looked. He has thought about that a good deal since and does not care
for any part of it.

That's the entry. Pascual has never done anything with it. He knows where he was standing
and what the tide was doing and what was said, and he has never allowed it to be the answer
to any question, on the grounds that an afternoon isn't a start.

---
 
 And then, nineteen days ago, the tarp.
 
 He had said the thing about the government labs on the verandah the evening before, in front of Jean and John and the Colonel and two of the boat crew, at a volume the whole station could place, and he had meant it, and he would have said it again in the morning. He had been saying a smaller version of it since the third day: that the animal was going down at a rate anybody could read off the sheet; that the one thing on that station which had ever moved the sheet was a boy who was being kept from the pen for reasons Tar would not put to Pascual in plain English; and that a design which requires an animal to get worse in order to be a design is not a design, it is a wager with somebody else's stake. Tar had gone white about the mouth and hadn't answered, and that was the eleventh evening.

 In the morning there was a tarp on the concrete three feet from the pen, sodden, with blood in the folds of it; a quantity of epidermis on the ground where the animal had lain for some number of hours nobody was going to establish; and a stain, dried, the colour of port. The animal was back in the water and swimming, stiffly, with a look on him Pascual had no professional word for. And the boy asleep on the pad beside the pen without his glasses, who had got him up off the concrete and back over the side on his own, in the dark, and had then lain down and slept.
 
 He read it the way he reads any presentation he has arrived too late for, off the ground and backwards. What it came to was muscle damage of a duration he couldn't establish, kidneys he couldn't see, no figure for that animal taken when he was well, and a boy who had done all of it alone and hadn't come for him.
 
 He got the samples. He got them with the boy standing at the animal's head, because the animal would hold still for the boy and for nobody else living, which was the finding of the morning and which he had four minutes to be pleased about.
  
 Then Tar came down and stood at the rail and listened to the whole of it.
  
Pascual didn't let him say anything. He got the boy kept where he was. He held the man's eyes across the rail until they came down, and said in front of Jean and in front of the boy that it was a medical matter now, and every part of that was correct and he would do it again. None of it was what he wanted, which was to take Reginald Tar by the collar in front of everybody and ask him whether he saw what he had done.
 
 Jean took the boy up for breakfast. Pascual had said he'd stay at the pen until he came back, so that is where it was had: at the rail, over the animal, with the blood tubes in his hand the entire time because putting them down hadn't occurred to him, and twenty minutes at the outside before the boy came down the path again.
  
 "I'm going to give you the trajectory," he said. "You like figures. You shall have all of them."
 
 "Pascual —"
 
 "Day one, intake down to eleven pounds from twenty-two. Day three, nine and a half, and refusing the second feed. Day five he stopped coming up for them and I put them in front of his face. Day seven, surfacing at two minutes forty and shortening. Day nine the breathing went irregular and I stood in your doorway at eleven at night and told you so, and you said you had it. Day eleven I said what I said on the verandah. Day twelve I came down at six and read it off the ground, because there was nobody awake to tell me any of it, and what I've got is a tarp and a stain and an animal I can't lay against any figure taken when he was well, because he has not been well since the day he came here. I couldn't sedate him. Sedating him would have finished him. He may be dead on Thursday, and I'm saying that out loud in front of him. He may be dead on Thursday and I won't know until Thursday, and neither will you."
 
 "You are right," Tar said quietly. "You were right on the ninth, and on the verandah, and standing here twenty minutes ago. I was wrong. I am sorry. I do not expect that to be worth anything to you. I am saying it because it is owed."
 
 He said it at the rail, in front of the animal, with hardly any pause at all.
 
 Pascual had a further nine minutes of anger to get out and went on and got it out, because he was right, and he wasn't going to be stopped just because the other man had admitted it.
 
 "There's a second column and you haven't asked me about it once. The boy. He's lost weight I could give you a figure for if anybody in this place weighed anybody. He was sick over the drain every day in the last week and didn't tell me any of it; I had it off Juan. On the fifth night he said something to Gamma that I wasn't meant to hear and I'm not going to repeat it to you. He is twenty-four. He's been here five months, and the person whose good opinion he cares most about put him where he could watch a thing he cared about go down, and told him it was a design.
 
 "Understand the order I'm giving you these in. The animal is first and there's nothing near it, because the animal is what I'm for, and because the animal can't stand at this rail and say any part of it. The boy can. He hasn't, and I've let him not, and that half is mine and I'll carry it. But you are the reason he thinks he can't. He had my door and he had yours, and instead he got that thing up off the concrete all by himself at some hour of the night, and lay down and went to sleep. So tell me. Was it worth it?"

"No," Tar said. "I knew it last night, when you said what you did. I did it because —"

 He stopped. He started again and got no further the second time.
  
 "Say it."

"Because I was not thinking of either the animal or the boy, but of the result. It is what I am."

 And then, before Pascual had got anywhere at all with that, Tar blew out a breath and looked at him. "Tell me what you require."
 
 "That isn't —"
 
 "It is what I have," Tar said. "You may have anything on this station that is mine to give, and I would rather you named it than left it to me, because you have the last eleven days' evidence of what I do when it is left to me."
 
 So Pascual gave it to him, in parts, standing at the rail, and heard his own voice go into the register he used for instructions to a groom. <i>One. Nothing is done to either animal, or to the arrangements round either animal, without Pascual being told before it is done and not after. Two. If Pascual says an animal is compromised, that is the end of it, and there is no appeal to a design. Three. The boy stays with Beta.</i>
  
 Tar took out his notebook and wrote all three down, at the rail, in the sun. Then he asked whether the first was to be understood as covering the feeding schedules, since he did not wish to be in breach of it by accident, and Pascual said that it was, and Tar wrote that underneath and ruled a line under the lot.

 What Pascual had come to that rail to do — he wouldn't have put it that way at the time and hasn't put it that way since — was to confirm what he had thought in four minutes on a step in February. To confirm that what that man was, was precisely and exclusively a man who would do that to an animal and to a boy for a result. It was a good diagnosis. Tar had even said it himself. It accounted for everything except Tar's apology.
 
 The boy came back down the path, and both of them stopped talking.
 
 Tar kept all three requirements for nineteen days. Pascual checked.
 
 He didn't look at him for five of those days.
 
---

There is one lamp, and the shutter closed, and the level in the bottle is down three
fingers, one of which is in the glass in the man's hand, and Pascual does the sum on the
way to the chair because he can't help doing the sum. Three fingers is a bad number. Three
fingers is not a man having a drink, and it is not a man drinking to be drunk either, which
takes longer and is untidier, and generally involves others the whole way through or not at all. Three fingers in an hour and a half, with the shutter closed and one person invited in at this point,
is a man taking a dose.

Then Tar tells him, and he tells it in order, because he is constitutionally unable to tell
anything in any other order, and Pascual sits and lets it come.

Fleming; the Navy; the visit already on the books and now carrying a second parcel.
Salisbury, two years, which Pascual has been guessing at for months and had guessed lower.
And then: he has told the boy to take the animal tonight, and given him a chart, and they
are gone by now or they aren't.

"You have put a compromised animal into open water." Pascual says. "Without informing me."

Tar ignores the second part, and gives him the figures.

He gives them without hesitating and without reaching for anything, urine and intake and
surfacing intervals and full lengths of the pen since the twenty-third, and he says *I have
watched him do it every evening since,* and then he stops, because he has heard himself.

The twenty-third to last night is six nights. Pascual does that sum before he has decided
to do it, because he can't help doing the sum, and then he does the other one, which is the
number of those six evenings on which the man standing at that pen turned round and asked
him anything whatever about the animal he was standing there looking at.

Six evenings at that pen, holding the samples Pascual took, and not one question to the man
who took them. And there it is: the other thing that is the matter with him tonight, that
has nothing whatever to do with an animal in open water.

Six months of it now, even though half the time he cannot stand the man. Six months and no warrant to do anything with it, and holding it has become a
species of physical effort, like carrying something up a stair.

He says the thing about the samples anyway. It comes out with more in it than he intends.
Tar looks at him as though the sentence has been handed to him in a language he doesn't
read, and reaches for the glass, and Pascual lets it go.

And then the man tells him he knows he has broken the first requirement Pascual gave him, and still asks him — carefully, having plainly had the sentence ready since the
verandah — to stay away from the pens until after breakfast, and explains that this is so
that Pascual will be able to say he was told nothing and saw nothing, which will be true,
and that Tar was drunk in his room, which will also be true.

It is very neatly done. It is the sort of thing a man builds when he has been building
such things since he was young and has never once been able to stop.

And it is a lie. Not the content; the content is exact. Tar is sitting in a room at ten
o'clock at night with a witness and a dose because he can't be alone with what he has
done, and he has produced, at short notice, a procedural reason for it, and Pascual would
put money on his believing the reason entirely.

The man is blinking. Quickly. Pascual has known what that means for five months and has
never told him he knows.

Pascual almost leaves, then, to go down to the beach. It will take the boy a long time to drag the animal to the water, on the sand, and he could probably intercept them. But he doesn't. Later he won't be able to say why: whether it's because he didn't want to stop the boy, or whether he was worried that he would help, and so be culpable in whatever their fate was. He will always wonder which it was, and what that says about him. 

Instead he says, "And the boy. He drags the animal down the beach, and he gets him past the
breakers, and then he walks back up on his own, does he."

"I have no data on that."

Pascual has data on that. He has months of it. He has a boy who wouldn't leave that pen
to eat, who had to be sent to his hut like a child to sleep, who came back grey and got in
the water again; he has a boy whose hand goes to that animal's face when he thinks nobody
is looking and who has never once been able to explain how he knew to go down there in the
night. He has watched him and been unable to name what he was watching, and he can name
this. He hasn't needed a design for it. He needed one morning, and he has had that since
the tarp.

The boy stays with Beta. It was the third condition, and it is not going to be broken.

He does not say any of it.

He sits in the chair with it and lets the man opposite him keep the sentence *I have no
data on that*, and he does not ask himself why, and gets no answer to the question he does
not ask. 

He sits in the chair, and he has now been in this chair some minutes with the whole of it in him and has not got up; so that whatever [culpability] is there is not solely Tar's at all.

---

Then Tar begins to talk about *him*, and everything else goes out of the room.

He stands closer than the argument requires; a foot, measured. He came to this room three
times when a note would have done and twice had nothing to say. He didn't look at Tar for
five days after Beta was found outside the pens and a man who thinks he was right doesn't do that. He
touches Tar only when angry and everyone else without meaning. He says the name
differently. That is five; there are eight.

*Eight,* Pascual thinks.

And in April, the plates, across the shoulder — Pascual remembers the plates, remembers the
whole afternoon, has in fact taken it out and looked at it more than once and put it back.
What he has out of that afternoon is the hands, which are small and quick and never once
uncertain, and the back of the neck when the man bent over the tray. He has never counted
these. It hasn't occurred to him that they could be counted.

And then Jean, in the same room, at the same hour, under the same lamp.

"You ran a control," Pascual hears himself say. "On me. You ran a *control* on me."

"Of course I ran a control. Without one it is an impression."

There is a thing happening in Pascual's chest that he would, in an animal, describe and
sample and not attempt to interpret. He is aware of being angry. He is aware that the anger isn't about the plates. It is that the man built an
apparatus to find out something he could have found out by asking, and then sat on the
result for four months, and has produced it now only because he has taken a dose of whisky
in order to stop being able to think about where a boy has gone.

He does not act on impressions. He does not act on results either. Pascual would like very
much to know, as a matter of professional curiosity, what the man does act on.

Four months. April to August, while they screamed at each other on the verandah in front
of everyone, while Pascual told him he might as well have stayed with the government labs
and then couldn't look at him for five days and hated himself for both halves of it.

*You have known since April,* he thinks, *and you let me say that to you.*

He says none of this. What he says is, "And what am I, in your arithmetic? Now. Tonight."

And Tar opens his mouth, and nothing comes out of it.

"I don't know," he says.

*Coño*, Pascual thinks, and gets up, and takes the fourth glass off the desk and puts it
where the man can't reach it.

There is a fifth of a bottle gone and a hand going back to it every ninety seconds, and
he would take the glass off any man on this station in that state, and he has taken it off
this one. Both are true. Nothing he has would separate them; the two conditions have never
once occurred apart. He is not declining to look at it. He has looked at it. It does not
come out.

He does not go to the door.

He asks how much. He has the level in the bottle and doesn't need telling; you ask anyway,
because the figure a man gives you is a different piece of information from the figure in
the bottle.

He is told: three, and that three is insufficient, and that the figure will have to be
revised.

And then Tar stands up.

He stands up out of the chair like a man whose name has been called, and then he is simply
standing there with his hands at his sides, and doesn't do anything else. There is no next
thing. Pascual watches him not do it for what is probably four seconds and is not a short
four seconds, and understands, with perfect clarity and no charity whatever, that the man
wants something and has no procedure for getting it and will stand there until somebody
else supplies one.

He has seen animals go like that in a crush. He has never seen a man do it standing up in a
room.

---

He has the man by the shirt before he has decided anything, and after that there is a
period he couldn't give an account of.

What he retains, afterwards, is small and clinical and won't leave him: that Tar's
glasses go askew and he does not put them right; that his hands come up into Pascual's hair
without any of the deliberation that governs every other thing he does with his hands; that
he makes a sound, the second of the evening, and that Pascual is almost certain the man is
counting them.

Then Pascual has both hands on his face and has stopped, and is standing between his knees
breathing like a fool, and the whole of the last year is available to him for the taking,
and he says no.

He says it in the only frame he has that will hold. *I don't put a needle in a compromised
animal.* Which is true, which he has said to this man about Beta and said it in front of
Jean, and which is the one principle of his that Tar has never once been able to argue
with. It is also convenient. He notes that it is convenient and declines to work out what it is
convenient *for*.

Taking his hands off the man's face is the hardest piece of work he has done since he came
to this station. He does it slowly, because doing it quickly would be a different message
and he has run out of messages he is prepared to send tonight, and he is aware the whole
time of exactly where the jaw sits in his palms, and that he is going to have it there at
four in the morning and again at breakfast and for as long after that as it cares to stay.

He puts that out of his head for now and gets the man a glass of water instead, and stands over him while he
drinks it the way he stands over the boy at meals, and Tar looks at the floor.

Then Pascual takes the chair.

"You are not staying."

"I'm staying," Pascual says, "because I'm not going down to the pens before breakfast,
and I'd rather be somewhere I can see the man who asked me not to."

Which is true, and isn't the reason. The reason is that there will be Americans on this
station inside two days, and the last thing on God's earth he intends to be is a man
who can be found on his own in a separate room and asked about a separate thing.

Tar lies down in his clothes and is asleep inside ten minutes, which tells Pascual more
about the state of him than the bottle did.

Pascual doesn't sleep much. He fetches the blanket at about two. Putting a blanket over a
sleeping man isn't touching him; he establishes this on the way across the room, and it
holds for about as long as it takes to do, and then he goes back to the chair and sits in
it three feet off for four hours. He does not go to the window; there is nothing at the
window he needs. He knows what is happening down there and he has known since the man said
*tonight*.

---

Breakfast is the hard part.

He eats it. He eats all of it, at the table, while John talks about the Americans and what
Fleming will make of it all, and the Colonel rumbles, and Jean sits with her hands round
her cup and is a great deal calmer than a woman ought to be who hasn't seen Cholmondeley
since yesterday.

*Ah,* Pascual thinks, and looks at her for perhaps two seconds, and puts it away unexamined,
because it isn't his and because he hasn't the evidence.

At nine o'clock he walks down.

The pen is empty. The water is flat and green at the sides where the algae grows and there
is nothing in it at all. The tarp isn't in the stores; he checks. The morning fish are in
the bucket where Juan left them at six, and the sun has been on them for an hour, and they
will have to be thrown out.

The boy's cot is where it has been for months, four feet from the ledge, and his
things are on it. His razor. Two shirts on the line, dry. A book. His log, squared up on
the box he uses for a table, with yesterday's entry in it, which says *No change.*

Pascual stands there for rather a long time.

Then he does what he does with any presentation he cannot resolve, which is to make the
differential; and the differential has one thing on it, and he writes down two more,
because a differential with one thing on it isn't a differential, it's a conclusion.
