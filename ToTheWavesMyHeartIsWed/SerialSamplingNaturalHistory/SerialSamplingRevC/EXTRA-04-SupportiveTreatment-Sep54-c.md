# Supportive Treatment

On the third afternoon Spilsbury finds him at Gamma's pen and stands beside him for a
while, quite companionably, watching an animal who has been on the bottom since July, and
says: "Your Dr Tar has got slack."

Pascual doesn't answer at once, because his first three answers are all unusable.

"He was the best of them, you know," Spilsbury says. "Eight, nine years ago. There was a
period when whatever room you put him in, he had the thing worked out before you'd got
your coat off. It's a pity to see it. Too much on his plate, I expect."

"Very likely," Pascual says.

And that is how he learns what Reggie has done, from the man Reggie did it to, on a concrete
apron in the sun, with a dying animal in front of him.

He goes on watching the water for a while afterwards. He can hear his own pulse. He has a
number of things he would like to do and every one of them would cost the man the whole
week's work, so he does none of them, and at four o'clock he gets a bucket and tries her
with fresh sprats, and she doesn't come up for them.

---

His own turn is Wednesday morning, in the dining room, fifty minutes, door shut.

He decides in the first thirty seconds that his method will be to be tedious, and to be
completely honest, and that these are the same thing.

So he gives Spilsbury the muscle damage. He gives it to him at a length no living man could
want: the presentation, the stiffness, the colour of the urine, the equine analogue and why
it is only an analogue, the bicarbonate, the reason he would not sedate, the serial samples
and the whole trajectory day by day with the figures, which he has, and which he recites.
He offers to fetch the samples. He does fetch them. He is prepared to go on about
myoglobin until one of them dies.

Spilsbury lets him run for about half an hour and then says, pleasantly, "Why did the animal
leave the pen?"

And there it is, and Pascual has been ready for it since Sunday.

"I'm a veterinarian," he says. "I can tell you in detail what leaving the pen did to him.
I can't tell you why he did it, and neither can anybody else, and I'd be careful of any
man who tells you he can. They do things. Horses do things. Ask Dr Tar about the
husbandry; it isn't mine."

It is entirely true. It is the truest thing he says all week and it is a lie by construction
and he is going to have it in his mouth for a long time.

Then, at about the forty-minute mark, Spilsbury changes his manner very slightly, and
becomes warm, and says that he has read the sample work and that it is first-class, and that
it is being done by one man with a hand centrifuge and no assistant on a station four
hundred miles from anything, and that this is a waste of a career. He describes a facility.
He describes it accurately and without exaggeration, which is the professional thing to do,
and it takes Pascual about ninety seconds to understand that he is being bought.

He says no.

"Think about it," Spilsbury says, and writes something on a card and leaves it on the table.

On Thursday the Navy put the new one ashore and Spilsbury has him in the small pen by the
afternoon, and he refuses food, which is what they all do, and which Pascual notes and does
not yet mind.

Pascual thinks about it for two days. He doesn't admit this to anybody, and he is careful
not to admit it to himself in any form that could be written down, and on the Friday he
goes and stands at Gamma's pen and looks at an animal who has been dying in front of him
since April of a thing he can name and cannot treat, and who is going to finish doing it
here, and he understands that the facility is this, with better glassware.

He puts the card in the stove.

---

The other business he settles on the first night, lying on his back with his hands behind
his head, and the settling takes about ten minutes and is entirely sound.

It is finished. He stopped it himself, in his own room, with his own hands on the man's
face, on grounds he still holds and would give again in front of anybody. Nothing has
happened since. Tar has been in the laboratory all day doing what he was doing in July, and
hasn't looked at him for longer than an exchange requires, and has had about him the whole
day the look of a man who has been hit across the back of the head and is upright out of
habit. Whatever was got out of him on the twenty-ninth was got out of him by whisky and by a
boy walking into the sea, and a doctor who mistakes that for anything else is a fool.

So: finished, and filed, and he is forty-three and has had things be finished before.

He settles it again on the Tuesday. He settles it again on the Wednesday, in about four
minutes, the reasoning having been got down by then to its working parts. He settles it
twice on the Thursday. Each time it is entirely sound.

The difficulty with the settlement is that it holds perfectly during working hours and is
worth nothing whatever after six.

He gets the mouth back about once an hour. He does nothing to fetch it; it simply arrives,
and it arrives complete, and what it is is the moment the man stopped holding still and
started taking part — which wasn't gradual, which Pascual would put at somewhere under a
second, and after which nothing that happened was anything he had planned for. He has the
hands going into his hair. He has the sound. He has the whole of it in order and can start
it from any point and does, at supper, with the man three feet down the table eating
nothing.

And it doesn't stop at the mouth, which is the part he objects to. It goes on into a set of
propositions about what he would do and in what order and how long he would take over each
of them, several of which are detailed, one of which he has now had four times and has begun
to think of as a fixed idea. He is a professional man of forty-three and he is conducting,
in his own head, at intervals, a programme of work on a colleague who has told him nothing
and asked him for nothing and who is, this week, not eating.

He puts it down. It comes back up. He puts it down again. By Thursday he has stopped
pretending that putting it down is the same as its being down, and has gone over to a policy
of not making it worse, under which he does not go near the man's room, does not stand about
in that corridor, and does not at any point permit himself to work out what he would say if
he did.

On the fourth night he gets up at two and goes out into the yard and stands by the water
tanks for twenty minutes in the dark, under a window with the shutter closed, and doesn't
look up at it, and goes back to bed.

By Friday he has arrived, without ever putting it into words, at the position that nothing
further is going to happen, that this is correct, and that he will be all right in about a
month.

---

At nine o'clock on the evening the launch goes, Reggie knocks on his door and says, "I've
no procedure for this."

He looks like a man who has been standing outside for some minutes. He looks, more to the
point, like a man Pascual would want a look at in daylight.

And then he says the other thing — *I do not know how this is done* — and he says it in the
voice of a man putting down something extremely heavy that he has carried further than he
should have.

*Ay, Dios,* Pascual thinks. Six months.

Everything he has settled in five days goes over in one movement. He notes it going. He does
not put out a hand.

He stands back from the door.

---

He will not give a full account of the next part, on the grounds that a man is entitled to
something.

What he will say is that he has had it in his head in a particular order for five days, and
that he gets to do it in the order, and that at no point is he asked to slow down or to
explain himself, and that he hadn't known in advance that the not being asked would be the
thing.

He will say that a man who arrives at a door with empty hands and no procedure turns out,
once there is a hand on him, to be nothing whatever like a man being careful. That the
difference between Reggie Tar holding still and Reggie Tar not holding still is under a
second wide. That he has now been back over that second more times than he intends to admit
to, and would go back over it again tonight.

He will say that he had it wrong about the intensity. He has spent six months watching that
faculty pointed at charts and mortality tables and the drainage of a pen, and had assumed,
so far as he let himself assume anything, that a man built like that would be up in his own
head somewhere running the commentary. He is not somewhere else. There is nothing in the
room but the two of them and no commentary of any kind, and the whole of the apparatus that
makes the man impossible on a verandah is turned, for the better part of an hour, on
Pascual; and Pascual has to stop twice and get his own breathing in order, and can't
afterwards give any account of why being attended to like that should be so tiring, or why
he would arrange a good deal of his life around having it again.

He's watched this man across a yard since February. He knows the neat cuffs. He knows the precise part in his hair, always in the same place. He knows that the pen goes back on the desk parallel to the edge
of it. Dry and in his own office, the man has never at any hour had one thing out of order
on him.

What he gets is the hands first — the hands stop being deliberate, and it is the hands that
go first and he hadn't expected that. Then the breathing, which goes before the voice does.
Then the voice, which stops being a voice and becomes breath with a shape in it. Then the last of it, whatever it is that holds the face still on a verandah while
a man is being contradicted in front of his own students, going out of the face and not
being sent for.

He looks at the man below him, hair mussed and forehead beaded with sweat, eyelids fluttering, hands and breath and face undone, and something in his chest clenches, hard. He tries not to think, *This man is mine*, because he knows how it sounds, even silently in his head, but he still thinks it.

---

But he is a doctor before he is anything, so he notices what he notices, and what he notices
is that there is less of Reggie than there was in April. Not much. Enough to find with a
hand in the dark; enough to be a figure, if he had a figure, which he does not, because
nobody has ever weighed the man. Five days at most, and probably more like eight. A flat
hand on a ribcage is a poor instrument; he has spent twenty years being able to put two
fingers on any living creature and know exactly what is happening inside it, and what he
wants is the wrist, and he can't think of a decent reason to ask for it. And he lies there
in the dark afterwards with his hand flat on the man's chest and thinks, with a calm that
he recognises as the calm he uses on other people's emergencies: *nobody is looking after
you at all, are they. Nobody has been for years.*

And he has a thought, and it arrives whole, and he can't put it back.

*That is what the whisky is for.*

He turns it over. The commentary was off for the better part of an hour — not slowed, not
governed; off — and there was no bottle in the room, and he has never once seen it off
without one. Which makes it a finding, and a good one, and he turns it over twice more and
discovers that he has no particular feeling about it beyond a general and shameless
satisfaction, and that he couldn't at this moment say what would be wrong with it.

---

At three in the morning the body beside him goes rigid.

Pascual has been asleep about an hour. He comes up through it the way he comes up for a
foaling, all at once and entirely.

The breathing is wrong. Not distress; worse than distress; it is the breathing of a man
holding perfectly still inside his own head while something goes on in there without him.

"Stop it."

"I haven't said anything."

"You've gone stiff as a board and your breathing has changed and it isn't from what we've
been doing." He puts his arm across the man's chest, flat and heavy, the way one puts a
hand on a beast to keep it down. "Whatever it is. In the morning."

"It is that he went with him."

And Pascual lies there in the dark with this at last spoken in the room, five days late, and finds that he has no
professional frame for it whatever, and that he is going to have to say something as himself.

"Yes," he says. "He went with him. He was always going to go with him. Now go to sleep."

It isn't much. It's the only thing he has that is both true and a kindness, and he has
noticed over twenty years that there is usually only one such thing in a room and that it is
generally very short.

Reggie doesn't go to sleep. Pascual knows it, hour by hour, from the breathing. He doesn't
say anything further, because there is nothing further, and because a man who has been sent
to sleep twice has been told he is a nuisance.

At about four he feels the whole body change — a small settling, a going-out of something —
and understands that whatever machinery has been running has arrived somewhere.

He waits to be told and is not told.

---

In the morning he gets up first, which he hasn't done in years, and goes out and comes back
with tea and bread and a tin of the condensed milk and half a papaya, and puts it all on the
chair, and says, "Sit up."

"I don't want it."

"No. Sit up."

Reggie sits up, and looks at the plate, and Pascual watches him arrange his face into the
one he uses on the verandah when a thing has been proposed that he intends to refuse
politely, and then watches the face fail, which is when he understands how far along this
is.

"When did you last eat properly?"

"I've been eating."

"That's not what I asked you and you're a precise man, so don't do that with me." He sits
down on the edge of the bed and takes the wrist without asking, and Reggie lets him, which he wouldn't
have done in April. The pulse is fast and thin and exactly what Pascual expected and he says
so, in numbers, because numbers are the thing that will be believed.

"Five days," Reggie says at last. "Perhaps six. It goes off. It's done it before."

"When?"

"Salisbury."

"Ah," Pascual says, and doesn't go any further into that room, and hands him the bread.

He makes him eat all of it and drink the tea sweet, which he objects to and drinks anyway,
and about two-thirds of the way through, colour comes into the man's face and he stops
holding his shoulders quite so high, and Pascual has to look out of the window for a moment.

Then Reggie says, "I should give you an account of what I did with Spilsbury on Tuesday.
You will have heard some of it and it will not have been accurate."

"I heard it from him at Gamma's pen on the third afternoon, and it was accurate, and I've
been carrying it about since." Pascual takes the cup. "You will not do that again."

"It was the correct decision."

"It was the correct decision and you will not do it again," Pascual says, "because I'm not
prepared to stand next to a man's grave while he's still in it, and I've now done it once
and I didn't care for it."

Reggie looks at him with an expression Pascual can't read and wouldn't attempt to.

---

At the door, with his hand on it, Pascual stops.

He has been turning it over since four in the morning, and it is a bad idea, and he is going
to do it anyway.

"You worked it out. In the night. Where they went."

Reggie doesn't answer, which is an answer.

"Then tell me," Pascual says.

He watches it land. He watches the man understand exactly what is being offered, which takes
him no time at all, because whatever else has gone off in him, that hasn't: he is being
offered a second head to keep it in, and the price of the offer is that there would then be
two of them who could be put in a room with a paper.

"No," Reggie says.

"Reggie —"

"No." And then, with some difficulty, as though the sentence has to be got out past
something: "You were in that dining room for fifty minutes on Wednesday and I did not know
what was being said in it, and I have never in my life had an hour like it, and I am not
going to arrange for another one."

And Pascual stands there with his hand on the door.

He has been told a great many things in his life by men who were fond of him. He has never
once been told anything like that, and it has been delivered as a refusal, in the flat voice
of a man declining a request on operational grounds, and there is no part of it that is
about feeling and it is entirely about feeling, and he is forty-three years old and has to
stand quite still for a second in a doorway.

"All right," he says.

"All right?"

"All right. Keep it." He opens the door. "Eat at one o'clock. If you're not in the dining
room at one o'clock I shall come and find you, and I shall do it in front of John."

Which is, on the whole, the most he can say in a corridor.
