# Titration

In July it was the eighth time and he wasn't told.

Pascual knew on the Thursday morning. The man came down to breakfast and there was a thing
gone out of his shoulders that had been in them since April, and he ate what was in front of
him without having to be watched, and he was mildly rude to John about the drawings, which he
hadn't been in weeks. Any fool could have read it. Pascual isn't any fool; he's been reading
this particular animal for two years and he could have put a figure on it.

He'd had it some hours before the shoulders, in any case, in the dark, with two fingers laid
along the inside of a wrist — which is a form the man can't dispute and doesn't know he's
supplying, and which is the most truthful thing anybody has ever told him — and he isn't told.

It isn't that the thing has suddenly turned good. It has been coming up since April by degrees,
unevenly, with reversals in it, the way a temperature comes down; the July night is the best
of them and the June ones weren't much worse and in May there was a fortnight no better than
April. Pascual has the whole curve. He has never been handed one point of it.

---

Because the trouble isn't the result. He has the result. He got the July one twice over, off a
wrist at midnight and off a pair of shoulders at eight in the morning, and it cost him nothing
and told him everything, and that is exactly the difficulty.

He waited to be told. He wasn't told. That is now the fourth occasion he can list and he has
stopped pretending that he keeps the list for professional reasons.

He has spent twenty years getting everything he knows by looking at it. He's extremely good at
it. It has never once in his life been *given* to him.

That is the whole of the trouble and he has never said it out loud, because there's no way of
saying *tell me things* to a man who has never lied to you that doesn't sound like an
accusation of dishonesty.

---

The coffee starts in December.

It's simply on the bench one morning, a tin with a San Juan merchant's label, and there's no
note with it, and nobody says anything. Pascual makes a pot and stands in the doorway of
his own laboratory drinking it and has to put the cup down for a moment.

It isn't merely Puerto Rican. It's the right one. He has said in this man's hearing,
perhaps twice, in the course of complaining about something else entirely, that the Colombian
they get through the stores is a hotel coffee.

He doesn't ask. He's learnt that much: if he asks, the man will give an account, and the
account will be procedural and modest and will explain the whole thing away, and Pascual
would rather have the tin.

There's another in February and another in April.

---

There's also, in January, a requisition.

He finds it because he's looking for something else in the estimates and his own name is on
it, in the column, under a line item for a bench centrifuge and a paraffin refrigerator and
the salary of a laboratory assistant at the junior rate.

He stands in the office with the sheet in his hand and does the arithmetic and it's a good
deal of money, and it's been put in as *veterinary and pathological, expansion*, which is
true, and which is also a man arranging for somebody to be looked after and concealing it
inside a form.

He doesn't mention that either. What he does is go and stand at the small pen for a while,
where he stood in August 1954 and understood that he was being bought and put the card in the
stove, and he finds that the two things don't resemble each other in any way he can set out,
and that he couldn't defend the distinction to a committee, and that he's entirely certain of
it.

---

The rounds are the failure.

On the twenty-second of January he comes out at seven in the evening to do the pens and finds
them done. Feeding, condition notes, the lot, in Reggie's hand, correct in every particular,
which is the worst of it.

Pascual's angrier than the occasion can possibly bear. He knows it while it's happening. He
stands on the apron with the bucket in his hand and can't get at the reason, and what comes
out of his mouth, when the man appears, is short and unpleasant and about the log format.

He gets at the reason at about two in the morning, lying in the dark, and doesn't care for
it. It's that a man of forty-four who is relieved of his evening work by a man of thirty-nine
has been told something about himself, and it doesn't matter in the least that this isn't
what was meant, because Pascual has spent twenty years telling other people that what is meant
isn't the thing that operates.

He doesn't explain. That's his own fault and he has it down as his own fault. He's angry for
two days and formal for a third and by then it's too late to say anything without making
a scene of it, and Reggie goes about the station in that period with the particular blankness
of a man revising something.

---

In February he takes a thing at face value.

It's nothing. It's a remark about the Gamma figures, offered across the yard, plainly meant
well — *you were right about the ration and I wasn't, and I've had to move the estimate* — and
he takes it, and is pleased, and goes on with what he's doing, and it's a good four minutes
before he notices that the half-second didn't arrive.

He stops in the middle of the yard.

He has been waiting for the half-second every time for a year. It was the last piece of the
fifteenth of January he still had in his possession, and he'd understood it to be permanent,
and had made his arrangements accordingly, and it has gone off quietly at some point without
telling him, the way a fever goes off in the night.

He finds he's sorry. He works at that for a while and gets it in the end: while it was there
he was a man who had been injured and was managing it, which is a position with some dignity
in it. Now he's simply a man who's fond of somebody. There's no dignity in that at all and never
has been.

*Ay,* Pascual thinks, and picks up the bucket.

---

He finds the sheet on the fourteenth of March, in the drawer, under the mortality tables, where
he has been sent to get the mortality tables.

There are three papers under them and he reads all three, and he's aware while he's doing it
that two of the three have nothing to do with him and that he isn't going to stop.

The first says *3 — insufficient*, and a date in August 1954, and the date is in different ink
from the figure.

The second says *4*, and the ninth of March 1955, and nothing else.

There's no third of that kind. He looks for it. He looks for it twice, and there isn't one,
and Pascual stands there in another man's room holding the absence of a piece of paper and has
to breathe for a moment, because in a year of watching he hadn't been able to establish it,
and here it is established, and nobody told him this either.

The third paper is longer. It's undated at the head and dated all the way down and it's in the
clipped hand he uses for field notes, and the first line of it is:

*Coffee. Ponce or Yauco, not Bogotá — the Colombian is "a hotel coffee" (his phrase, 6.ix,
in the course of a complaint about the stores). Wrote San Juan 3.xi. Arrived 19.xii. Not to be
mentioned.*

And then:

*Does not care to be thanked in the yard. Twice; on both occasions became formal for the
remainder of the day. Thanks are to be received indoors or not at all.*

*Journals — not on a night he has been up with an animal. On those nights he re-reads what he
has. Bring nothing.*

*Gamma: he likes to be asked in front of Jean. Not established whether this is the asking or
the Jean.*

*22.i — rounds. Wrong. Two days. I have not established what part of it was wrong and he will
not say, and I am not to attempt it again on the same reasoning as before.*

And, near the bottom, with a rule drawn under it:

*15.i.55.* And beneath that, numbered one to six: *the offprint itself. That it was Aberdeen.
That it was carried across the yard rather than sent up. That Jean was present. That I did not
stay. That it was a Saturday.*

And under the six, in the same hand, smaller:

*Insufficient. No means of testing any of them and none available.*

---

He sits down on the end of the bed.

The anger arrives on time and it's the whole of it, undiminished, exactly as it was in the
February of the year before: *he's still doing it, he has a sheet, there are numbered factors,
God in heaven, there's a rule drawn under the fifteenth of January* — and it lasts about ninety
seconds, and then it goes, because he's a diagnostician before he's anything and he has read
the sheet properly.

There's no condition on it. There's no outcome column. There's nothing anywhere on the paper
that is being compared with anything else. The failures are written down as failures and
left there, unexplained, with no attempt to recover them. A man running conditions doesn't
record a null result and then write *I am not to attempt it again.*

And there's the sixth factor, which is *that it was a Saturday*, and which is on the list for
no reason on earth except that Reggie Tar can't leave a variable out of a list merely because
it's absurd.

It isn't a design. It's a fence round one.

Pascual sits with that until he's certain of it. The man forgets nothing; that's never been
his difficulty and Pascual has watched him produce a figure from the previous March across a
dinner table. The difficulty is that nothing he keeps in his head will lie where it's put. It
sorts itself. It sorts itself the one way it knows, into conditions and outcomes and quantities
that want varying, and it does this whether or not he's agreed to it and largely while he's
asleep.

So he's taken it out of his head and put it on a sheet of paper, where it can't get at it. The
whole of the page is an act of restraint by a man with one tool who's been told to put the tool
down.

Except at the bottom. At the bottom, under a rule, the fence has been got over — six numbered
factors and the word *insufficient* — and it's on the one item on the sheet he has never been
able to leave alone.

Pascual puts the three papers back in the order he found them and takes the mortality tables
and goes out.

---

He says it that night, at the rail, because he isn't going to be a man who knows a thing and
keeps it. That road is well signposted and he's watched somebody go down it.

"I read what's under the mortality tables."

Reggie doesn't move. "Yes."

"All of it."

"Yes." And then, after a moment, in an entirely level voice: "I should have moved it. I've
been aware of that since November."

"Then why didn't you?"

"Because moving it would have been a decision about whether you were to see it," Reggie says,
"and I could not establish which of the two outcomes I wanted, and while I could not establish
it I preferred not to act. I am aware of how that sounds. It is the same fault. It is always
the same fault."

Pascual watches the water for a while.

"The list isn't a design," he says.

"No."

"Say the difference. I want to hear you say it."

There's a long pause, and when it comes it comes badly, in pieces, which is how Pascual knows
it hasn't been rehearsed.

"An experiment varies a condition and takes a reading against an outcome and the whole of its
value is in the reading. There is no reading on that sheet. There is no question I am asking. I
have not got a hypothesis about you." He stops. "The rest of it is that I cannot leave the
material in my head."

"You forget nothing."

"I forget nothing. That is not the difficulty and it has never once been the difficulty." He
has both hands on the rail. "It is that it will not lie still. Anything I hold arranges itself,
and it has the one arrangement, and if I keep six facts about what pleases you in my head for a
fortnight I shall have them in conditions by the Tuesday, and I shall not have decided to do it,
and I shall very probably not notice that it has been done. So I put them somewhere they cannot
arrange themselves. That is the whole of what the paper is for. I have written a great many
things about a great many animals in exactly that hand and it did not occur to me until
November what it would look like lying on a desk."

"And the fifteenth of January."

"That is the exception," Reggie says. "That is a design and I have known it was a design since
I wrote it and I could not bring myself to take it off the sheet."

"Why."

"Because I ruined the best day you had that year," he says, "and I know that I ruined it, and I
have understood since April that I cannot ask you about it, because asking would hand the whole
of it back to you spoiled a second time. So there is a good thing in there somewhere that I did
by accident and I would like to be able to do it again on purpose, and I have six candidates
and no assay, and I have had them for fourteen months."

---

Pascual has been in a great many rooms in his life where somebody was waiting to be told
something, and he's generally been the one who had to decide how much of it to hand over.

He has never once been in one where the thing being waited for cost him nothing at all.

"It wasn't the offprint," he says. "It wasn't Aberdeen and it wasn't Jean and it wasn't the day
of the week. Take all six off."

"Then — "

"It was that you thought of it when I wasn't in the room." He keeps his eyes on the pen.
"You were somewhere with the post, on your own, and you saw a thing and the thing had me in it.
That's all it was. That's all it's ever been, with anybody, and it isn't a variable, and you
wouldn't have got to it in a hundred years, because it doesn't happen where you can observe
it."

Reggie doesn't say anything for some considerable time.

Then he says, "That's usable."

"Is it."

"It's the most usable thing anybody has ever told me and I haven't had it in fourteen
months." He's quiet for a moment. "It also cannot be acted upon. The whole of it is that it
was not arranged. If I arrange it, it is not the thing. I have been given the variable and I am
not permitted to set it."

"Yes."

"Then I am to do nothing whatever, and it will happen or it will not, and I shall find out
afterwards."

"Yes."

"That is intolerable."

"Yes," Pascual says. "It's how it is for the rest of us."

And he hears the man breathe out beside him, once, in the dark, and doesn't turn his head,
because there's a limit to what either of them can stand at a rail.

---

The letter comes on the fourth of May and it's dated the eighth of April.

His brother Efraín, in Ponce, who was fifty-one, and who had been ill since the autumn, and
about whom Pascual received no word whatever, because Rosa didn't write until it was over and
then the mail did what the mail does.

He reads it standing at the stores in the sun and puts it in his shirt and goes and does the
morning pens, because the animals don't know and have to be fed. He's quite steady. He notes
that he's quite steady and files it where he files that sort of thing, which is with the
observation that men are frequently steady for two or three days and then are not.

He tells nobody. There's nothing to be done and no boat until the twentieth and the funeral was
a month ago; a man doesn't make an announcement out of arithmetic.

Reggie finds him at the small pen at about four.

He doesn't say that he's sorry. He stands there for a moment and then he says, "There is a
schooner at English Harbour on the ninth that would put you into San Juan inside a week. I have
the money for it and there is nothing in the programme in May that cannot be moved. I want to be
clear that there is no operational reason for any of this. I have arranged it anyway."

"There's no point in going," Pascual says. "It's finished. It was finished before I knew of
it."

"Yes," Reggie says. "I thought that was probably the case. The schooner's there until Friday."

And he goes away, and that's the whole of it, and Pascual stands at the rail and finds that he's
been given the one thing available, which is the fact that it was offered.

---

For the three days after that, the pens are done when he gets to them.

He comes out at seven on the first evening and it's been done, feeding and condition notes,
correct in every particular, in that hand, and it's the identical act that made him so angry in
January that he couldn't speak.

He stands on the apron with the empty bucket and understands that the act was never the
variable.

It was the occasion. It's always been the occasion. There's no list in the world that could
have got at that, and the man has got at it anyway, in sixteen months, by some route Pascual
can't reconstruct and doesn't intend to ask about, and it has cost him God knows what.

Nobody is told why. John assumes an administrative reorganisation and is pleased with himself
for spotting it.

Jean says nothing at all. On the second afternoon she finds him sitting at the small pen at four
o'clock with nothing in his hands and nothing to do, which isn't a thing he's ever done in two
years, and she goes into his laboratory and makes a pot from his own tin and brings it out to him
and sits on the wall and talks for twenty minutes about the Gamma weight curve, which she doesn't
need his opinion on and has already got right.

Pascual has known for two years that she's the cleverest person on this station, and knowing
it has never once put her name at the top of anything.

---

On the third night Reggie comes up and doesn't say anything at all, which is correct, and sits
on the edge of the bed with his shoes still on, which isn't like him.

Pascual's lying on his back looking at the ceiling and has been for some hours.

And then a hand comes over and takes his wrist — not the arm, not the hand; the wrist, two
fingers laid along the inside of it and the thumb round the back, in the place — and stays
there.

Pascual doesn't move.

He has never told anybody. He has had the place on a wrist since he was twenty-four and for
twenty-one years it was an instrument and there was nothing in it worth keeping from anyone.
What he has never told a living soul is what it turned into in the December before last, in
this room, in the dark, with a man in it who couldn't say a word and both of them perfectly
aware that he couldn't; he has thought a hundred times that there's no form of words for it that
wouldn't sound like a lunatic's; he has taken the entire account off this man's wrist every
night they've been together, the going-fast and the steadying and the long slow coming-down,
and has believed the whole time that it was his and that nobody knew.

He was observed. Of course he was observed. He has been in a room for two years with the best
instrument in the hemisphere and it doesn't switch off, and it's been running on him the
entire time, and there's no version of this in which he wasn't being read.

He waits for the half-second.

It doesn't come.

What comes instead, at about the third minute, with his pulse going along under another man's
fingers and telling him the truth about himself whether or not his head has agreed to it, is
that the faculty was never the injury. It was only ever what it was pointed at. And it's
pointed here now, at four in the morning, in a room with nothing in it to be got, by a man who
has no possible use for the reading and took it anyway and has been sitting on it for a year
working out what it was for.

"You'll want to take your shoes off," Pascual says, eventually.

"Yes," Reggie says, and doesn't, for some little while.
