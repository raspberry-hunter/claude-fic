# Compliance

He has his hand round the man's wrist because he likes it there.

That's the whole of the reason and it's worth putting down plainly, because of what comes
after. He's a doctor and has been one for twenty years and there's a place on a wrist where
a body tells you the truth about itself whether or not the head has agreed to it, and he found
in December that he could keep two fingers there and have the entire account: the going-fast,
the place where it steadies, the long slow coming-down afterwards. It's become his favourite
thing in the world. He has never told anybody. He wouldn't know how to say it without
sounding like a lunatic.

So he's holding a wrist, in the dark, at some point in a night that has been going reasonably
well and of which he has more hope than he'd care to write down.

That is the state to have in mind. Not happy. Hopeful, which is smaller and more foolish and a
good deal harder to put away afterwards. The man came up at half past ten in a state Pascual
could read perfectly clearly from the doorway, and said almost nothing, and put his forehead
against Pascual's shoulder near the beginning in the way he does, and there has been nothing
in the first part of the evening that he will afterwards be able to point to.

Because he keeps hoping he will not have to think about the nineteen observations. He has had them by degrees
since the middle of January and has done nothing with one of them: four, and he waited; six
the following week, and he waited; nine by the end of the month, and the nine went into a
drawer. He could have had
it in January off the nine. He has taken a hundred thousand pulses and read a fever off a
man's colour across a ward and he could have had this in January, and he didn't have it in
January, and he has been carrying the whole lot about the station since in the deliberate
condition of a man who hasn't looked at them.

And under his fingers the rate does something that isn't physiological.

It hitches. Twice. In a pattern.

And Pascual, who has taken a hundred thousand pulses and could do it in his sleep, understands,
before he permits himself to understand, that the man is counting.

---

The rest of that night he won't set out. He said what he said and he meant all of it.

But the thing he was actually injured by he doesn't manage to say, and he turns it over
afterwards for weeks.

It isn't the bed. He's forty-three years old, he has been used before, and he isn't a girl;
if Reggie Tar wanted to come up here on Thursdays for an hour of not being able to think, he
could have had it, he could have had it with a signed statement and no questions asked, and
Pascual would have thought it a sad way to live and wouldn't have thought it an injury.

It is the fifteenth of January.

Because on the fifteenth of January the man walked across the yard in front of Jean with the
Aberdeen offprint under his arm, and put it on the bench, and said, *I thought you would want
this.* And Pascual carried that about for the rest of the day like a boy. He was *happy*. He caught himself whistling at the centrifuge.

And it was a condition. It was Saturday's condition, run to see what Saturday night would do,
and Saturday night did nothing, and so it wasn't repeated.

That is the injury. He was given the one entirely good day he has had in a year and it was an
instrument reading. And the worst of it, which he doesn't say to anybody and will be carrying
for a long while, is this: he can't get the day back. It isn't that it has been spoiled going
forward. It has been spoiled backwards. He can't now remember standing in the yard with the
offprint without also knowing what it was, and there's no operation that separates them.

And under that there's a second thing, which he doesn't get at until some time in the small
hours and doesn't care for in the least.

He found it first.

In September, in this room, with no bottle anywhere in it: the commentary off for the better
part of an hour, not slowed, not governed, off. He turned it over the next morning with his
coffee and was pleased with himself, and what he felt about it was satisfaction, and he could
not at that hour have told anybody what would be wrong with it.

He knows now what would be wrong with it. Not the thing itself — the thing isn't wrong, he
doesn't think tonight that it's wrong, and he isn't going to stand here pretending he didn't
like being the reason for it. What is wrong is that four months later a man got hold of
the same finding and bolted apparatus to it; and Pascual is being shown his own observation
with the apparatus still on, and there's no way to condemn the apparatus that doesn't pass
through the observation on the way.

---

He holds him for half an hour on the floor of his own room and doesn't manage to stay angry,
and is furious about that as well.

The shaking isn't distress. He knows distress; distress has a shape and comes down inside
twenty minutes. This is the other thing, which he has seen twice in his life, in men who have
had something taken out from under them that they had been standing on without ever knowing
they were standing on it. Both of the others were in a war. It takes as long as it takes.

He says the diagnosis out loud because it's true and because it will be believed — nothing
sentimental is ever believed by this man and everything clinical is — and he says the rest of
it too, that a diagnosis isn't forgiveness, because it isn't, and because he isn't going
to be lied to in his own room by himself.

And then he sets the condition.

He has about four seconds to design it, and he designs it well, and he knows exactly what he is
doing, and it's the only piece of cleverness he's proud of in the entire business.

*Nine words. I've counted them for you.*

Because there's no possible way for Reggie Tar to execute a sentence like that mechanically.
He can't run it as a protocol, because the first thing a protocol wants is that the subject
not be told what it is, and this one is nothing but the telling. He has to say the words,
aloud, sober, to a man's face, and mean them, and then stand there. And by giving
him a number Pascual has put the whole thing into the one language he trusts, which is the
nearest thing to a kindness available at two o'clock in the morning.

*That is not a procedure.*

*No. It isn't. You'll have to manage.*

---

He doesn't come.

Six weeks.

---

They go down to the pen every night. That continues, because it was a good idea on its own
merits and because neither of them is going to be the one who stops it, and so from the end
of February Pascual spends an hour of every night standing three feet from him at a rail in the
dark, taking observations on two animals, and going up.

It's exquisite, in the sense that he'd write on a chart and not in the sense people use it
about puddings. He wouldn't have believed it could be done to a man of his age.

An hour. Every night. Just the two of them. Three feet, in the dark, from a man he wants so badly that it has become
a physical fact about the evening, like the tide: a thing he comes down the steps carrying and
stands next to for an hour and carries back up. He keeps his hands in his pockets. Or rather
he finds them on the rail, at intervals, without having put them there, and jams them back
in.

It isn't the wanting that's the hard part. It's that at some point around the three-quarter
hour Reggie shifts his weight, always the same way, every night, and Pascual's entire body has
turned towards it before anything has been decided, and he stops it, and stopping it takes
something out of him that isn't back by the following evening.

And underneath that, and worse than any part of it: he's in love with the man.

He could produce the list. He has never written a word of it down and he could produce it on
demand, at length, in order of discovery. The back of the man's neck when he's bent over a
tray and has forgotten there's anybody in the room. The particular way he sets a thing down
when he's being careful, which is with two fingers, and which he does with a slide and with a
whisky glass and with a man's jaw and doesn't know he does. That he says *quite* in four
distinct tones and means four different things by it and has no notion of it. That he has
been wrong in public twice this year and said so inside the minute on both occasions, which
Pascual has seen perhaps five men do in twenty years of medicine and has never yet managed
himself. That in March, at the rail, into the dark, Pascual told him he'd been hard on that
boy then, and he's always been hard on Jean, and he didn't argue and didn't answer and
hasn't mentioned it since — and has been laboriously kind to Jean ever since, so unpractised
about it that she has twice asked Pascual whether Dr Tar is ill.

It's a long list for a man who didn't know until December that he was keeping one. He can
date that part exactly: the end of December, over a tray of slides, for no reason that has ever
been established. What he can't date is when the thing itself started, and he's given up
attempting it, and for a man who dates everything that is its own kind of information.

Which means he can't say whether it was there before December or whether December made it, and
he never will be able to, and he has an hour a night at three feet in the dark to think about
it beside the person who did the thing.

---

Reggie is scrupulous. That's the word for it. He's courteous in the yard, he's exact about
the data, he asks after Gamma every single day — every day, without fail, having been told in
February that he had stopped — and he doesn't touch him and doesn't come up and doesn't say
the nine words.

And Pascual works out, about three weeks in, standing at the rail with his hands in his pockets,
what is actually going on: the man isn't sulking and isn't punishing him. He has been given a
rule and can't establish the point at which he's permitted to invoke it. He's waiting to be
certain that when he says it, it won't be a manoeuvre. He'll wait for ever on those terms,
because certainty of that kind isn't available to anybody.

Pascual understands this completely and doesn't go to him.

He wants to. He is at the foot of the stairs twice in March and turns round in the corridor both
times. Because if he fetches him, then whatever happens next is a thing Pascual arranged, and he
will spend the rest of his life not knowing whether it would have happened otherwise; and because
he has just spent ten weeks inside somebody else's design and he's damned if he'll start
running one of his own; and because — this is the real one, and he doesn't care for it — he told
the man that a diagnosis isn't forgiveness, and he isn't certain, if he goes up those stairs,
which of the two he would be carrying.

So he waits. Six weeks. He doesn't think much of it as a method. It's simply the only thing
available that isn't another experiment.

---

The twelfth of April. Late. He has been asleep and isn't now, because there's a knock.

Reggie is in the corridor with his hands at his sides.

He looks bad. He has been at the typewriter — it was going when Pascual went up, and Pascual
has no idea when it stopped — and Spilsbury's letter came on Friday saying the paper is first-class
and asking two questions about the eleventh of November, and Pascual has watched him carry that
letter about for four days.

He gets about half the sentence out.

"The typewriter has been going," he says, "and I —"

And then he stops, and stands there, and can't do the rest of it. Pascual can see it costing him,
physically, in the jaw. The hands have gone flat to the sides in the way they do when he is
standing to attention in front of the Colonel. He's thirty-eight years old and he can't say *I
cannot stop* to a man he has been to bed with twenty-three times.

Pascual stands back from the door.

"Seven of nine," he says. "Come in."

---

What happens after isn't what he expected and he isn't, at first, certain that it's better.

He gets him sitting down, and gets the letter out of him, and the two questions about the eleventh
of November, which are good questions and are the questions of a man who intends to go on asking.
And then he makes him say the rest: that Gamma came off the bottom for an animal she had never met
and put her hand flat on his face, and that there isn't one word of it in the paper and there
never will be, and that somewhere in the Gulf, if the arithmetic holds, there's a thing that
explains it and a man who could explain it, and that Reggie will publish this and be thanked for
it and it will be, in the sense that matters, a lie he tells four times a year for the rest of his
working life.

It takes about an hour and it isn't pleasant, and at the end of it Reggie says, "That hasn't
helped at all."

"No," Pascual says. "It doesn't. Not for a long time, and I'm not going to tell you how long,
because you'd count." He puts the letter back on the table. "It does eventually. I've watched
it. You'll have to take that from me on faith, which I'm aware is the worst thing I could ask
you for."

And then he takes him to bed, and it isn't what he was expecting.

The man's eyes are open.

That's the visible part and it isn't the part that signifies. The part that signifies is
underneath it and he has it inside four minutes, and it's this: he has got him back.

Because this is how it used to be. This is October. This is the way the whole thing was before
December, and until this moment Pascual hadn't known there was a before December — not about
this, not about the bed.

His nineteen observations are all round the outside of it. Whether the man spoke coming in.
Whether he stayed. What time he left.

The counting is the twentieth. It is the first observation he has ever taken from inside, and
it ended everything, and he hasn't been back to look at it since.

He is looking now. The ten weeks are sorting themselves, while he's in the middle of something
else, into two kinds of night with a line drawn between them, and he can see the line perfectly
well from this side of it.

He couldn't see it at the time. That's the thing he's going to be carrying, and he starts
carrying it at about half past eleven on the twelfth of April with a man's face inches
from his own.

The one difference is that it's harder for Reggie than it used to be. He's working at
something, visibly, and it's costing him, and Pascual can't make out what it is.

He has to stop once. Not because anything is wrong. Because being looked at like that turns
out to be a condition he has no procedure for either.

Afterwards Reggie lies with his forearm across his face for a long time, which is new, and doesn't
say anything at all; and Pascual doesn't ask, because whatever is being done under there is
arithmetic and it'll keep until the morning.

He sleeps. Not for hours; but he does, at about four, with a hand shut round Pascual's wrist —
his wrist, the other way about — and Pascual lies awake in the dark being held on to by a man who
has never in his life held on to anything.

---

In the morning the verdict arrives before the coffee is poured, flat, with a figure attached to
it, and he's ready for it, having had four hours in the dark to work out what happened and
having got most of the way.

The man was working. All night, at something, in a room where there's nothing on earth to work
at. Pascual can say that much, and can say that it will go on costing until it stops being work,
and he says both; and then hands over a prognosis with no procedure in it whatever, and watches
it go into the same place the nine words went.

He knows exactly what he has done. Reggie will take *stop working at it* away with him and make
a method of it and comply with it beautifully, and the complying will be the work. There was
nothing else available to hand him. It's the second time in six weeks he's given this man an
instruction that can't be executed, and he's begun to suspect that this is simply what
treating him consists of.

---

In May, Reggie comes across the yard with something in his hand.

It's the Copenhagen number, which Pascual has been trying to get since January and has twice
complained about within his hearing.

And Pascual takes it, and says thank you, and turns it over, and thinks — before he can stop
himself, in the half-second before anything else — *what is this a condition for.*

There's nothing. Of course there's nothing. He knew it before the thought had finished.

But he had it. And he's going to have it again, probably for years, possibly always. The man put
a hand into that part of him and it doesn't appear to be closing up.

So he stands in the yard with the journal and looks at the back of Reggie's head going away towards
the pens, and makes the only decision actually available to him, which is to take the thing at its
face and be pleased with it and say nothing whatever about the half-second.

That isn't forgiveness either. He isn't sure what it is. It's what he has got, and it's what he
does again on the fourteenth of June, and on the second of August; and he becomes, over about a
year, rather good at it.
