# Case Series

The establishment is eleven miles out of Salisbury on a road the bus does not take, so there is a van at half past seven and a van at six, and a man who misses the second one walks. Reggie misses it twice in the first fortnight and then does not miss it again for eighteen months.

It is chalk up there, and beech, and a wire fence with a gate and a hut at the gate, and behind that a red-brick house with a bad roof that somebody's family had until 1940, and four huts, and the annexe, which is new and is the only building on the site with a proper floor.

Spilsbury meets him off the van himself, which is not usual, and says, "You're younger than the file."

"The file has my date of birth in it."

"The file has a great many things in it." Spilsbury takes his case off him, which is also not usual, and reads out of a card as they go up, in the tone of a man reading a menu: "*Possibly the best field observer of his year. Will require handling. Do not put him on anything he can see the end of.* I wrote that in September. I'm told I've a gift for it."

"Who told you that?"

"Me, chiefly." He gives the case back at the door of Hut 2. "Supper's at seven and it's dreadful. Come and find me afterwards; I've a bottle and nobody to talk to and I've read your paper on the Faeroe material twice."

Nobody has ever read that paper twice.

---

He does animals for the first three months.

He is good at it inside a week and very good at it inside a month, which is neither a boast nor a surprise; he has done this before, at the other place, in the war, on material he cannot name to anyone here and does not have to, because it is all in the file and the file is why he is here.

The animals are macaques and there are cats. The technicians name them, which Reggie objects to in writing on the grounds that a name in a record is a source of error, and he is overruled in writing by a man in London he never meets, and the memo and the reply are the first two documents of the four hundred. So there is a Tosca and a Bunty and one with a torn ear who is called the Alderman because of the way he sits. Reggie declines to use the names for eleven weeks and then uses them, and cannot recover the day he started.

The animals do not come out. That is not a thing anybody has to be told; it is the shape of the work and it is on the establishment list in the column headed *Consumption*.

He is careful with them. He goes slowly, and he goes on going slowly some way past the point at which the job could be called done, and one of the technicians says to him in November, quite kindly, "You want to not do that, sir," and he says, "Do what," and the technician says, "Nothing, sir," and goes back to the sluice.

---

He is put on the ward in the January because he is wasted on the animals, and because Spilsbury has read four hundred pages of his in three months and has arrived at the correct conclusion, which is that the man's gift is not his hands. It is that he can hold everybody else's separate facts at once and see the shape none of them can see alone.

So: assessments. Before, and after, and at intervals. It is the largest single component of the returns and it has been done badly by three people in succession and the establishment cannot read its own results.

He rebuilds it in five weeks. The schedule he produces is thirty-one items, takes fifty minutes to administer, and is the first instrument in the building that will give the same answer twice. Spilsbury reads it standing up in the corridor with his coat on and says, "Christ," and then, "Type it," and Reggie types it, and it goes at the head of the returns under the form of words Spilsbury chose in the October, which is *Special Studies*.

The ward is eleven beds in what was the long drawing room. There is a stove that does not reach the far end. The men have been in hospitals for years, most of them, and have been sent here from other places by people who ran out.

Sister Renshaw runs it and is the best nurse Reggie has ever seen work and is not told anything.

---

S/11 is Sidney Alderton, thirty-eight, and he has been in one place or another since '43.

He was a fitter at the Works at Swindon and mended watches on the side, and his hands are the hands of a man who has done that: short in the nail, absolutely steady, and never anywhere they are not wanted. In the drawer of his locker there is a tobacco tin with a roll of tools in it, and by the second week of Reggie's being on the ward the tin has come out for the wireless in the day room, and Sister Renshaw's clock, and a pair of spectacles belonging to a man in bed four, and none of these has been asked for.

He has attacks. That is the word the ward uses and Reggie has not been able to improve on it. He goes somewhere. It is twice a month and sometimes worse, and it lasts under an hour, and for two or three days afterwards he apologises to people who have told him repeatedly that there is nothing to apologise for.

In the intervals there is nothing whatever the matter with him. That is the entire difficulty of the case and Reggie has it on the second morning.

"You're the new one," Alderton says.

"Yes."

"You've got a list."

"I have a schedule of thirty-one items."

"There you are, then." He is doing something to the innards of the clock with a jeweller's screwdriver and does not look up. "What do I call you? You don't look old enough for Doctor."

"I'm twenty-nine."

"Professor," Alderton says, and that is the end of it, and it is what he is called on that ward for fourteen months.

---

He starts the list in the October and does not know he has started it.

That is not the same as not noticing. He notices; he simply does not classify. The items go into the back of the working notebook in the same hand as everything else, at the ends of days, without a heading, and it is the third week before he turns back and sees that there are nine of them and that they are all about one man.

*Mends what is put in front of him and also what is not.* Nobody put the wireless in front of him. It had been broken since before he came and the day room had got used to it, and one afternoon it was going.

*Makes tea for Dando four times a day.* Wally Dando is in bed eleven, is much worse than Alderton is or ever was, does not speak, and has nobody. Nobody has asked Alderton to do this. He does it at half past six, eleven, three and eight, and he says the man's name every time, and he says it as though he expects an answer, which after four months of this Reggie finds he is unable to sit through.

*Will not be got at through the intervals.* In November they wanted a signature on something and the sister put it to him in the good interval, which is the correct time to put anything to anybody, and he read it and said he would sign it when Dando's had been read out to Dando. Which was absurd, and cost him three weeks of privileges, and he did it anyway, and when the three weeks were up he had not altered his position by a hair.

*Knows the orderlies' children.* All of them. Names and ages and which one is the difficult one. Reggie has been on the ward five weeks and knows two surnames.

*Gets things out of people that I could not get with the schedule.* This one he wrote down and looked at for some time.

*Angry only where it is warranted, and then not to be moved.*

*Gave me half the tin on the ninth. Did not mention it again.*

*Counts what Dando eats. Has never said so to Dando.*

*Asked me a question.*

The last one is the one that ends it, and he can date it, because it is in the notebook between two intake figures. On the fourth of December, at about a quarter to five, with the light gone and the stove not reaching, Sidney Alderton put the screwdriver down and said, "Here — what's it like for you, then? Up here on your own with the monkeys and the rest of it. Who've you got?"

And Reggie, who has answered a great many questions in a great many rooms, produced nothing at all.

He goes back to Hut 2 and writes the ninth item and rules off under it, because a list that has been ruled off is a finding, and he has the finding.

---

He has one other.

He made it in the spring of 1945, at the other place, on a scientific officer named Wren, and it ran to eleven items and ended, and he could not at the time say what it was a finding about, and he put the notebook away and did not think about it again for a year and a half.

He gets the notebook out on the Sunday.

The two lists are not the same in content and are identical in construction. The same order of approach. The same fault at the same place, which is that both of them go on some way past the point at which the point has been made. Both of them begin with a thing a man did that nobody had asked him to do.

One of anything is nothing. Two of anything is a series.

It takes him about the length of the corridor between Hut 2 and the house. He does not write it down. It is the only finding of his life that he does not write down, and he is aware, standing in the wet at the corner of the house at half past nine at night, that he is not writing it down in a building whose entire business is the writing down of what people are.

He is twenty-nine. He has three weeks of being twenty-nine left and he does not know that this is going to matter, and it does, because he settles the whole of the rest of it inside them.

---

The bottle is a standing arrangement by then. Twice a week, sometimes three times, in the office in the house with the fire on one bar and the blackout curtains still up because nobody has taken them down.

It is the best work of his life. He should like that recorded and would not like anything constructed out of it. Alan Spilsbury is thirty-nine and running his first programme and has an appetite for the thing itself that Reggie has never met in another administrator and will not meet again; and Reggie can put a half-finished shape in front of him and have it come back with the missing quarter attached, and can do the same for him, and they can go at it for four hours and neither of them has to slow down for the other one. He has waited his whole life to be in a room with somebody who does not have to be waited for.

In the December Spilsbury explains to him how the establishment gets people.

He does it because they have got on to it sideways off a staffing question, and because he is candid by construction, and because he is talking shop at eleven at night with the one man on the site who is worth talking shop to.

"It isn't blackmail," he says. "Blackmail's for amateurs and it goes off in your hand. What you want is a man who's worth something. He goes on the list because he's worth something — that's the whole of the selection, and people always get it the wrong way round; they think value protects you. Then you find the thing, and there's always a thing, and you don't do anything with it. You never do anything with it. You simply make sure he knows you have it, and then you offer him the best work in the world, and he takes it, because it *is* the best work in the world, and afterwards he'll tell you and everybody else that he took it for the work. And he'll be telling the truth."

"And if there isn't a thing."

"There's always a thing."

"But if there isn't."

Spilsbury looks at him over the glass with real pleasure, the way he looks at a good result. "Then he's no use to me. Nothing to be had. You can't spend a man you can't get a grip on, and the trouble is you can't do much else with him either." He puts the bottle across. "Have another. You've gone a very odd colour and it's the cold in here, I don't doubt."

Reggie has another.

And discovers, somewhere in the middle of it, that the arithmetic has stopped. Not slowed: stopped. He has been running two things in the back of his head since the corner of the house on the Sunday — running them constantly, the way one runs a bilge pump — and the second glass takes the edge off them and the third puts them down altogether, and for about an hour there is nothing in his head but the work and a man across a fire being funny about the Ministry.

He notes it. Three. He is careful, later, in his room, to note the number.

---

The decision he takes in the first week of January and he tells nobody, and there is nobody to tell.

It is not a moral decision and he does not dress it as one. It is a costing. There is nothing on him now and there will be nothing on him ever: nothing owned, nothing wanted where it can be seen, nobody in the world whose name could be said to him across a desk. He is not going to be the handle. Whatever the thing is that gets pulled, it will not be pulled through him, because there will not be anything at the end of it to take hold of.

He is thirty on the eleventh. He does not mention it and it is not in the file in a place anybody reads, and the day goes by as a Saturday.

---

The snow starts on the twenty-third of January and does not go for seven weeks.

The road is shut for four days in February and then shut again. There is no coal to speak of and the power goes off in the afternoons, and the ward runs on hurricane lamps from two until four, and Sister Renshaw gets the men into the day room round the one stove and Alderton mends the lamps.

Reggie writes the paper on S/11 in the last week of February by lamplight, in nine pages, and it is the best thing he has yet done in that building.

What it says is that S/11 is unsuitable. Not unfit — unsuitable, technically, on grounds. The presentation is episodic. The intervals are clean and are long and there is nothing in them to measure against; a man who is entirely himself for twenty-five days in twenty-eight gives you a baseline you cannot read an outcome off, because the outcome will be scored during an interval and every interval he has ever had has been clean. The case cannot be interpreted. He says so four different ways and he has the figures for all four.

Spilsbury reads it on the Thursday and comes and finds him in the hut, which he has not done since October.

"This is very good," he says. "It's better than good, it's the first thing anybody's written here that I'd put in front of a journal, and I want you to understand that I agree with every line of it."

"Then he comes off the list."

"He goes to the top of it." Spilsbury sits down on the end of the bed with his coat on and the paper in his hands, quite gentle about it, entirely reasonable. "Look at what you've written, Reggie. You've written that this man has twenty-five clean days a month. Thirteen of them we've done and not one of the thirteen could tell you afterwards what had happened to him, because there wasn't a man in there to ask. This one can. This one will sit up in bed and give you an account, in order, in his own words — and *that's* the thing we haven't got, and you've just proved to me that he's the only one on the ward who's got it." He puts the paper down on the blanket. "You've made the case. You made it better than I could have. That's not a criticism, it's the opposite."

Reggie sits with his hands on his knees and goes through it looking for the fault.

There is no fault. That is the finding. It is correct all the way down; it is what he would have said if it had been anybody else's paper and anybody else's ward, and he can see the whole design and it is a good design, and if it comes off it will be worth more than the previous thirteen put together.

"Yes," he says.

"You'll be in for it. I want you in for it. Your hands are the best in this building and everybody knows it and nobody says it because you're twenty-nine."

"Thirty."

"Thirty," Spilsbury says. "Many happy returns. When?"

"The eleventh."

"You might have said." He gets up. "Fourth of March. Bring the schedule down the day before and do the whole thirty-one; I want it complete and I want it in your hand."

---

Alderton asks him on the Monday.

He asks it the way he asks everything, sideways, with something in his hands: he has the sister's clock apart again on the counterpane and he does not look up.

"They've told me it's tomorrow."

"Yes."

"Nine hours' worth of it they've told me, an' I couldn't tell you a word." The screwdriver goes round. "So I'll ask you, Professor, and you'll give it me straight, because you've never once talked to me like I was the bed. Would you have it? If it was you."

Reggie has the whole of it available. He has thirteen sets of before-figures and thirteen sets of after-figures and he has spent five months building the only instrument in England that can read them, and he goes to it now the way he goes to everything, and what comes back is true.

"Of the thirteen before you," he says, "nine are better than they were."

Alderton stops the screwdriver.

"Nine out of thirteen," he says.

"Yes."

"That'll do me." He laughs, once, and puts the back on the clock. "That'll do me. Twenty-two years I've been mending things, and I'll tell you what nobody tells you: you can't never mend nothing without you take it apart first. Here — hold that."

Reggie holds it.

He goes back to Hut 2 and sits down to the returns and gets through none of it. He is at the table until two in the morning with three lines written and he cannot at any point in those hours establish what is stopping him, and it does not occur to him — not that night, not that year — that a man who has just been given a figure and has taken a decision off it has been given the figure by somebody, and that the somebody is in the room.

---

He does the thirty-one items on the Monday evening and they take fifty minutes and he writes it all in his own hand.

The fourth of March is a Tuesday and the road is still bad.

It is twenty-three minutes. He has the figure and he keeps it and it is the only thing he takes out of that room.

---

They have him in the annexe until the end of the month and then he comes back on to the ward, and the Avon is out over the meadows by then and you can see the water from the top windows, silver, in three fields, all the way to the spire.

Reggie goes up on the Thursday with the schedule.

Alderton is sitting up. He looks well. That is the first item and Reggie writes it down that evening in exactly those words, because it is true: he looks better than he has looked in five months, the colour is good, and the tightness that used to sit round his eyes on the bad days has gone out of his face and has not come back.

"Hullo, Doctor," he says.

Reggie stands at the end of the bed for a moment with the folder.

"Hullo," he says.

He does the thirty-one items. They take fifty minutes, as they are designed to, and Alderton is pleasant throughout, and answers everything, and is not slow, and gets four of the harder items faster than he got them in October. On item nineteen, which is a recall series, he is better than he has ever been. Reggie writes down the figure and writes down the previous figure beside it and sits looking at the two of them for some while.

Then he puts the folder away and stays.

The tobacco tin is in the locker and the locker is shut.

At about half past four Reggie takes his own watch off and puts it on the counterpane and says, "The balance is off on this. It's been gaining since the autumn."

Alderton picks it up, and opens it, and gets the tin out of the locker, and does it in eleven minutes, and hands it back with the hands set right. His fingers have not altered in any particular. They are the same hands, absolutely steady, never anywhere they are not wanted.

Then he sits with them in his lap.

At six the tea comes round. At half past six the girl puts Dando's on his locker in bed eleven, where it goes cold, because Wally Dando does not take anything unless it is put into his hand.

Alderton looks at it. Reggie watches him look at it. He looks at it the way a man looks at a thing on a shelf in a shop.

Then he looks away.

Reggie sits there until a quarter to eight. Nobody asks him to leave and he is a member of the scientific staff and may sit anywhere he likes. He sits for an hour and a quarter and he is not waiting for anything else.

At a quarter to eight he gets the tea and takes it over and puts it into Dando's hands and says the man's name, and does it badly, and goes out and down the stairs and stands in the passage by the dispensary for some considerable time with his hand flat on the wall.

---

He gets the notebook out that night and goes down the list.

*Mends what is put in front of him.* Yes. Eleven minutes, and better work than the clock.

*And also what is not.* He puts a line through that half of it.

*Makes tea for Dando four times a day.* No.

*Will not be got at through the intervals.* There are no intervals. There is one condition now and it does not vary, which is the thing the design was after, and it is in the returns in his own hand and it is a good result.

*Knows the orderlies' children.* He tests this on the Friday, obliquely, and finds that he does. Every name. Every age. Asked, he produces the difficult one without a pause.

That is the one that does it. Not the tea. He has been braced for the absences all week and the absences are survivable in a way he could not have predicted; what is not survivable is the ticks. Everything is there. It is all there, the whole of it, in perfect order, and every single item on that list was a thing the man did because nobody had asked him, and now Reggie can have any of it he likes for the asking, and only for the asking, and it turns out that having it is worse than not having it.

He does not get to the ninth item. He does not go anywhere near the ninth item. He puts the notebook in the case under the bed and goes and stands outside in the wet for twenty minutes with no coat and comes back in and is sick.

---

Edie Alderton comes on the second Sunday of the month, on two buses, from the laundry at Devizes.

She is small and quick and she has brought a cake made with the dried egg, and she stands in the passage afterwards with her gloves in her hand and Reggie is the only one of them there, because he has arranged to be, on the grounds that somebody should be, and he could not at any point in the preceding week have said what he intended to do about it.

"He's ever so much calmer," she says.

"Yes."

"You've no idea what it was like. Five years. You couldn't leave him at the top of the stairs, and after, he'd be that upset with himself, days of it, and you couldn't tell him. You couldn't tell him anything." She is not crying. She is looking at him with her chin up, and she wants something from him, and he understands what it is and cannot supply it. "He's not upset any more. He's not upset with himself at all."

"No."

"So it's worked, then. Hasn't it."

There is one sentence available that is both true and a kindness and it is generally very short, and he does not have it, and does not know that this is a thing a person can look for, and will not be told so for another seven years.

"He's better than he was," he says. "That's what the schedule shows."

She takes his hand and shakes it, and thanks him, and asks him where the bus goes from, and he walks her down to the gate because the road is bad, and comes back up the hill and gets on with the returns.

---

By the summer he is not eating and nobody notices, because it is 1947 and the whole country is thin and he draws his ration like everybody else and what he does with it afterwards is his own affair.

It goes on being the hottest summer anybody can remember. He is at the table in Hut 2 from six until eight and again from nine until two, and he takes the bread down to the ward twice a week for Dando, and he is running on about three hours.

And the machinery has never been better.

That is the part he does not say to anybody, then or afterwards, because there is no way of saying it that does not sound like a boast or an excuse and it is neither; it is an observation, and he makes it in the June and does not join it to anything, because there is nothing available to join it to.

He is producing more than he has ever produced. He is producing more than the establishment can use. In the June he does a re-analysis of the whole of the animal series from 1946 that nobody asked for, over four nights, and it overturns two of Spilsbury's own conclusions and Spilsbury takes it like a man being handed a drink. In the July he sees the thing in the twenty-two ward records that three people have been looking at for a year and have not seen, and he sees it crossing the yard, complete, with a folder in his hand, and has to stand still in the sun for a moment before he can go on. It is the best week of his professional life to that date and he cannot afterwards recall a single meal in it.

The shaking starts in the August. It is worst about an hour after he stops working and it goes off if he eats, which he knows, and it goes off if he drinks, which he also knows, and he takes it standing in the passage by the dispensary with his fingers on his own wrist and times it, because timing it is the only operation available.

The MO takes his pulse in the September and says a good deal that is entirely correct and offers him something to take at nights.

"No, thank you," Reggie says.

"You're thirty."

"I'm aware of it."

"You're the colour of a candle and I'd not put you on a horse. Take three days."

He takes three days in the October and goes to his mother's in Reading and lies on the bed in the back room listening to a lawnmower, and comes back on the Wednesday worse than he went.

---

The follow-up paper he writes in the November.

It is the best piece of work he does in that building or anywhere for the next fifteen years. It takes the S/11 material and the thirteen before it and it settles the interpretation question that his own February paper raised and could not answer, and it does it by proposing the design that would answer it, because that is what the paper wants and he is a scientist and he writes the paper the paper wants.

The design requires a subject.

He specifies the subject in section four, in eleven lines, in the way one specifies anything: presentation, duration, stability of the intervals, capacity to give an account afterwards, absence of an alternative course. He writes the eleven lines between about ten o'clock and half past eleven at night on the fourteenth of November, straight off, with no difficulty of any kind, and he is pleased with them, and he is pleased in the specific way one is pleased when a hard thing has come out clean, and he sits back and reads them through with the pen still in his hand.

They are a description of a person.

Not of a class. He has written eleven lines and there are two men on that ward who fit them and he has known which two since the fourth line, and he knew it at the time, in the sense that it was there, and he went on writing, and the writing was better because of it.

He sits at the table in Hut 2 for a long while.

What he has, at the end of it, is not what people afterwards suppose a man has at a moment of that kind. He does not discover that the work is monstrous; he has known what the work is since the January, in the only terms the building permits, which are that they do not come out, or they come out in a way their friends do not recognise, and he has typed that in a column four hundred times.

What he finds out on the fourteenth of November is a fact about himself, which is that if the work is good enough he will do it, and that there is no floor under that, and that the only thing which has ever stood between him and the whole of it is the quality of the design on the table in front of him.

He puts the paper in the drawer. He takes it out in the morning and reads it cold and it is still the best thing he has ever written and every word of it is correct.

He submits it, because the alternative is to be a man who withholds a finding, and he has not got a name for that man either.

---

He has two counts by then and he keeps them in separate places and he has never once put them on the same sheet.

The first is in the returns, where it belongs, and it is the number of subjects, and by the December it is twenty-six.

The second is not in anything. It is not a column and he has never written it down and he could give it at any hour of the day or night, and he has taken the precaution, which he has never examined, of never taking the two of them out at the same time.

---

He writes it on Tuesday the sixth of April 1948 and it is two sentences.

*I wish to resign my appointment with effect from the 30th instant. I am content that the terms of the undertaking I signed in October 1946 continue to bind me in every particular and for the whole of my life.*

There is no scene. He is careful about that and he is careful about it on grounds he could not have stated: a scene is a thing that would be remembered, and a thing that is remembered is a thing that can be raised, and he has decided about handles.

Spilsbury drives him to the station himself on the thirtieth, which is not usual, and does the whole ten miles without saying anything at all, which is very much not usual.

At Salisbury he pulls up short of the yard and puts the brake on.

"Give me a reason I can put on the card."

"Health."

"I'll put health." He does not write anything. "Now give me the other one."

Reggie looks out of the windscreen at the wall of the goods yard for some time.

"I found out what I would do," he says, "if the work were good enough. It is a fact about me. It is not about you and it is not about the programme, and I would be obliged if you did not construct anything out of it."

"No," Spilsbury says. And then: "That's the second thing you've said to me in eighteen months that was about you. I've been keeping count."

"You would be."

"I would." He puts both hands on the wheel and looks at them. "I wrote a card in September of '46 that said, *do not put him on anything he can see the end of.* I have thought about that a good deal since about the middle of March last year, and I want it understood that I read my own instruction, and I put you on it, and I would do it again, because you were the best instrument I had and I have never in my life been able to leave a good instrument in the drawer. I am not apologising. I don't do that and you would not believe it."

"No."

"Come back in a year. Not to this. I'll have something else by then and you'll want it."

"No," Reggie says.

It is the first time. There will be nine more of them, and the last of them will be five paragraphs long and will be written in an office four thousand miles away with the sea outside the window, and neither man in this car has any means whatever of knowing that.

Spilsbury puts out his hand and Reggie takes it, and then he gets the case out of the back and stands on the pavement and watches the car go up the London road, and finds that he would like to sit down, and finds that there is nowhere to sit down, and picks the case up and goes in and gets the twelve-forty.

---

In the inside pocket of his coat there is one sheet of establishment paper, folded in four, with nine items on it in his own hand and a rule under the ninth.

Taking it out of the building is an offence under the undertaking he has just confirmed will bind him for the whole of his life. He has thought about that for eleven days and he has done it anyway, and it is the only thing he ever steals.

He puts it in the back of the notebook with the other one, on the train, somewhere before Andover, and shuts the notebook.

Two is a series. Three would be an established finding, and there is not going to be a third, and he settles that between Andover and Basingstoke with the fields going past, in about the time it takes to settle a thing that has already been decided, and he holds it for six years.
