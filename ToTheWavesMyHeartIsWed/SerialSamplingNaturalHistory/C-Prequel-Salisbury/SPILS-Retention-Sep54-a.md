# Retention

The thing he is good at is not interrogation. Anybody can be taught interrogation; he has taught it, twice, to men who went on to be adequate at it.

What he is good at is pricing. Put him in a room with a man for forty minutes and he will come out knowing what the man is worth, what would be needed to move him, and whether the moving would return more than it cost. He has been wrong four times in nineteen years and can tell you the four.

He has never once failed to arrive at a figure. That is the part he'd have told you about, if he were the sort of man who told people things.

---

He asks the friends question of everybody. It is the cheapest question in the trade and it costs nothing to put, and nine times in ten it produces nothing, and the tenth time it produces a career.

He put it to the Leslie girl on the Wednesday, standing, doing his cuff, at the end of an hour and twenty minutes in which she had given him precisely nothing about Cholmondeley in six different shapes and had been magnificently, professionally, unimprovably boring about it.

He'd enjoyed her. He was already thinking about what he'd do with her in three years — she had rehearsed, which none of the others had, and she was the only person on that station who had understood in advance that an interview is a thing one prepares for.

And she got to the end of it, and relaxed, because it was over.

*Had Dr Tar many friends?*

Four-tenths of a second.

That is all it was. She then said something perfectly serviceable and said it well, and if he had not been watching her mouth he would have had nothing at all. He is aware that four-tenths of a second is not evidence and would not survive a minute in front of anybody sensible.

It is not evidence. It is an instruction to look.

He went out and looked at the pens for a while and did not write anything down, and noted, as a separate matter, in the way he notes things about himself, that he had become interested rather faster than the material warranted.

---

He does not sleep on stations. He never has. Something about the generators.

So he was at the window on the Thursday night with the lamp out, at about two, when the veterinary surgeon came out and stood by the water tanks.

Twenty minutes. Spilsbury took the time twice because he is a professional and because he could not at first believe it was going to go on that long. The man did not smoke. He did not walk about. He did not do any of the things a man does in a yard at two in the morning, and he did not look at anything except one shutter on the first floor, which had been closed since ten, and which had a room behind it that Spilsbury had been in on Tuesday.

There is no ambiguity in twenty minutes. There is a great deal of ambiguity in three, and in five, and in some of the readings he has taken off people over the years and had to argue for afterwards.

Twenty minutes of a grown man standing in the dark in a yard looking at a closed shutter is not a reading. It is a statement.

He went back to bed and did not sleep, and it was not the yard that kept him awake.

---

It was Tuesday.

Because Tuesday had been finished. He had gone into that room with the food logs and Cholmondeley's log and a fair expectation of finding a man who understood the phenomenon, and had come out with a tired administrator who did not read his own behavioural records, and he had been faintly sorry about it and entirely satisfied with it, and had begun composing the paragraph on the walk back.

And a slack man is worth nothing to anybody, which is the whole point of the paragraph, and is why the paragraph is safe.

There is one other thing about Tuesday and he did not go anywhere near it for two days.

It was in the first half-minute. Tar sat down and said good afternoon and gave him the two figures he had come for, unasked, in order, and it was all in that voice — the flat one, the returns voice, no verb contracted from one end of the room to the other, a man reading himself aloud out of a ledger. Nobody at that station finds it remarkable. It is simply what Tar sounds like. It is what he has sounded like to everybody in the Western Hemisphere for six years.

Spilsbury knew a man in October of '46 who argued for four hours about a cliff face and laughed twice.

He is the only person alive who has both readings. There is nobody else on the earth who could tell you there had ever been anything else, and there is not going to be.

He got through the first half-minute and found that he had stopped writing, and started again, and put the second question rather faster than he had meant to in order to be past something, and put the whole of it down at the time — because he keeps notes on himself, in the same hand, on the same paper — as *close in there; slept badly.*

He found that note again on the Thursday night. It is four words in pencil and it is perfectly legible and he knew, reading it, exactly what it had been for.

Now go back through it.

A man sits down opposite you and, without hesitating, without any rehearsal — and Spilsbury would stake a good deal on there having been no rehearsal, because rehearsal has a sound and there wasn't one — hands you his own professional negligence. Volunteers it. Offers it in writing. Watches you take it. Does not once try to soften it, does not blame the postgraduates, does not mention that he had two specimens and four juniors and was doing the acoustic work himself except as a list of facts against himself.

Spilsbury had read that as a clever man finally being honest about having got slack.

It reads rather differently at three in the morning with a man standing in your yard.

Nobody hands over four months of incompetence to a government officer for nothing. It is much too expensive. You do it when the alternative costs more, and there is only one question in that room that could have cost more, and it was *where did your man go*, and Tar had answered it truthfully and been believed and had then, immediately, spent everything he had to make sure the conversation did not go on.

He was not slack. He was buying.

And Spilsbury had bought it — had taken the transaction, priced it, written it up in his head, and gone away pleased. It is the neatest piece of work he has ever had done on him and it was done by a man who cannot lie, using the only method available to a man who cannot lie, which is to tell you the truth about something else entirely and let you do the rest yourself.

He lay in the dark being got round by that for some time.

And here is the finding, and he did note it, because he notes things about himself the way he notes things about other people, on the same paper, in the same hand:

He was not angry.

He should have been angry. He is professionally required to be angry; being got round is the one thing that costs him money. What he was instead was something he has not had cause to identify since about 1938, and which took him until nearly four o'clock to name, and which he is not going to write in a file.

---

So then he did the work.

He priced it, sitting up in bed with a pad, because that is what he is for and because doing it is a comfort.

The material: a foreign national, dependent on a work permit, deportable at a stroke and with no standing to object. A station head with government work and a security file and a career that ends the same afternoon, in this jurisdiction, on considerably less than twenty minutes. The offence carries what it carries. The thing is unbreakable — it cannot be paid off, disproved, aged out, or lived down, and unlike money or a woman or a drink it does not stop being true when the man reforms, because there is nothing to reform.

Permanent, then. He wrote *permanent* and underlined it, which he does not usually do.

Value: very high. Against a scientist of that quality, in a programme of that importance, at a moment when nobody in London can be made to take the phenomenon seriously — very high indeed.

Cost of use: low. That's what makes it good. You don't spend it; you let the man know you have it, once, and then you never mention it again for twenty years, and he does everything.

He had a figure by about half past four.

Then he lost it.

He went back over the working, found nothing wrong with it, and got a different figure. He did that four times before it was light. The material did not change; the arithmetic did not change; the figure would not sit still. He had never had that happen. He has priced men he liked, men he'd served with, one man he'd been at school with, and got a clean number every time and slept afterwards.

It took him until nearly the end of it to see that his system was not failing. It was working perfectly and had nothing to work on.

Everything he has ever priced, he has priced as an instrument: worth, leverage, cost of use, expected return. There is no other column. There has never needed to be one. And the material in front of him at half past four in the morning was the only man he has ever met who was not one — which he knows for certain, and knows on evidence, because he met him for one evening before the conversion and then carried out the conversion himself.

He is not being obstructed by sentiment. He is being handed a quantity in a unit he does not stock.

An instrument you cannot get a reading off is not an instrument. It is a piece of glass. And it is very much worse to discover that the glass is sound and that you have been holding it up to something that was never going to register.

---

At about five he stopped pricing it and started rehearsing it, which is the other thing he always does, because compulsion is not conducted by memorandum. It is done in a room, to a face, in about nine words, and the nine words have to be said by somebody, and the somebody is him.

He is good at the rehearsal. He can run a room twice through before breakfast and know where every silence is going to fall.

He put himself in the room with Tar and got as far as the sentence.

And could not tell what his own face was going to do.

He tried it four or five different ways and it was the same every time: a blank, precisely where the read on himself ought to be. He has gone into rooms for nineteen years knowing exactly what he was going to look like in them. It is not a talent. It is the minimum equipment. A man who cannot predict his own face does not go into the room, and does not send anybody else in either, because the file has his initials on it and the file is him.

He sat with that for a while as the light came up over the tanks.

---

The first evening was the fourteenth of October, 1946.

He'd written the card in the September, off the file, before he'd ever laid eyes on the man: *possibly the best field observer of his year; will require handling; do not put him on anything he can see the end of.* He has always been able to do that. It is the same faculty and it has never let him down.

He met the van himself, which was not usual, and took the case off him, which was not usual either, and by seven he had him in the red-brick house with the bottle, on the grounds that there was nobody up there to talk to and that he'd read the Faeroe paper twice.

They went until two in the morning. Not about the establishment; there was nothing to say about the establishment and Tar had not been cleared for most of it. They talked about counting things that move. About the impossibility of a census on a cliff face, and what you are actually measuring when you measure a colony, and the difference between a bird that is absent and a bird you did not see — and Tar, who was twenty-nine and had not yet acquired the voice, argued with him for four hours and contracted his verbs and laughed twice and forgot entirely who he was talking to.

Spilsbury has had a great many evenings. He can count the ones he did not want to end on the fingers of one hand and have fingers over.

He walked him back to Hut 2 across the chalk with a torch, because the ground is treacherous up there in the wet and because there was no other reason to be going that way. The annexe stood off to the left in the dark with its proper floor. Tar said goodnight, entirely unwarily, and went in.

And Spilsbury stood in the wet grass with the torch off and thought, in exactly the tone one uses about the weather: *what a waste.*

He put that in the drawer marked professional regret — a first-class field man about to be spent on a piece of work with no end he could see — and shut the drawer, and did not take it out again for eight years, which is not what he does with material. He takes material out. He turns it over. It is the whole of his method.

In the January he put him on the ward.

In the March of the following year there was the thing that is not going to be gone into at four in the morning on a concrete island four thousand miles away, and about which the only surviving verdict is a man walking out of a building.

And in the April of '48 he drove him the ten miles to Salisbury without saying anything, and pulled up short of the goods yard, and told him he'd read his own instruction and put him on it anyway and would do it again, because he had never in his life been able to leave a good instrument in the drawer.

Every word of that was true and he'd say it again tonight.

What he did not say, having not got it — and it has taken him six years and five months to get it, and he has got it in bed, in the dark, on somebody else's station, off a veterinary surgeon standing in a yard — is that he has disregarded a written instruction of his own exactly once in nineteen years.

Nor is that the whole of the correction available on a Thursday night.

He has believed since 1948 that he lost a good instrument to the service through his own error, and has said so, and has meant it. It is not true. The instrument is in perfect order. It was in this room on Tuesday and it took him apart in forty minutes with a set of food logs and he did not notice until three o'clock on Thursday morning, and it is the best thing he has ever built.

What he lost was the man in the wet grass. That man was never the instrument. He was the raw material, and there is no line anywhere in the establishment's returns into which that goes.

And there is one more, which he arrived at somewhere about half past four and which is the reason he stopped writing.

The doctrine is his. He teaches it; men have failed the course for getting it the wrong way round. You do not spend the thing you have on a man. You make certain he knows you have it, once, and then you offer him the best work in the world — and he takes it, because it *is* the best work in the world, and afterwards he tells you and everybody else that he took it for the work, and he is telling the truth.

He gave that lecture in the red-brick house in the October of '46, over the second glass, to a man of twenty-nine, in the tone of a professional explaining his trade to somebody who would appreciate the elegance of it.

He has been putting the Bimini post four springs running, warmly, in a good room, with no hurry. The best work in the world.

He would spot it in four seconds in anybody else's file.

---

So then, because he is honest in the one place he is honest, which is on paper at five in the morning with nobody else awake, he wrote out the second sentence.

The first sentence is *Tar is that sort,* and the first sentence is an instrument.

The second sentence is *and it is not for me.*

Which was not a discovery, and that is the part he had not expected and did not care for at all. He had had it in the wet grass outside Hut 2 in 1946 and had filed it under the wrong heading, and the wrong heading had held for eight years, and it was holding as recently as Tuesday.

*What a waste.* Of what, and to whom. He had never asked either question, and he has now asked both, and there is a third behind them that he asked at about ten past five and did not care for at all, which is that the waste was not a prediction.

He carried it out himself. He had it in writing beforehand and he did it anyway.

He has read that file a great deal more often than the work has ever required. He can give you what is in it without opening it. He has said *he was the best of them* out loud on this station once already this week — to the veterinary surgeon, at Gamma's pen, in the afternoon, apropos of nothing, for no operational purpose whatsoever.

He had thought that was warmth. It was, in fact, precisely the sound a man makes when he wants somebody to ask him a question, and he had made it to the one person in the hemisphere who could not possibly have answered it.

---

He wrote it up on the Saturday morning at the small table in the guest room with the door open.

He wrote that the station head had allowed the behavioural record to go unchecked for four months, that this represented a serious lapse of supervision and had cost a specimen and a research student, that in his assessment it proceeded from the man's having attempted to be both chief of station and principal investigator, and that with the co-housing trial completed and published the programme could be considered sound.

And then, in the standing section, in the column that has been there since 1947 and which he has filled in some four hundred times:

*No dependencies identified.*

He looked at that for rather a long time.

It is not a slip. He does not make slips. It is four words in his own hand in a file with his initials on it, and it is false, and he knew it was false while the pen was moving, and there is now a thing about Alan Spilsbury in a registry in London that is exactly as permanent and exactly as unbreakable as the thing he declined to put in it about Reginald Tar.

He'd have preferred not to have understood that quite so clearly. He understood it entirely.

He blotted it and closed the folder and went down to breakfast and was, as he had been all week, extremely pleasant.

---

In the spring of 1957 he offered Tar the Bimini post, warmly, in a good room, with no hurry, and was refused, and was pleasant about it.

He has done that four times now. He will go on doing it.

He can afford to. There is nothing on the man.
