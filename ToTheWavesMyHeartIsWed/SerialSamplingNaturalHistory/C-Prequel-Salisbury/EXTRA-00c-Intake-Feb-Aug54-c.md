# Intake

The specimen ledger is on its second page and the second page has one line on it.

*Beta. Male? Adult. Netted 4.ii.54, Barbuda bank, two men. Intake wt. 181 lb (in net, spring balance, ±4). Laceration L. flank/prox. tail, edges granulating, not attributable to capture. Untreated.*

It is a correct line. Every figure in it is one Reggie would defend at a committee, and the phrase that does the work is the one about attribution, which is on the form because the form requires it, and which is there because the men who netted him are on a contract and the wound is three weeks old and was not made by them.

So the second entry in the ledger of this institution records that the Martin is not liable for an injury it is not treating.

The animal will not let anybody within eight feet. That is not in the line. There is nowhere in the form to put it, and Reggie drew the form.

He has had the mortality table for five years. Four hundred years, ninety-five intervals, mode at twenty weeks. He put Alpha in it in the January and it did not move, and he ran it anyway.

---

Dr Pobil settles in three weeks, which is two and a half weeks faster than Reggie expected and rather faster than Reggie would have preferred.

The difficulty is not competence. The competence is not in question and was never in question; the man's myoglobinuria paper is better than it needs to be and his hands are better than his paper. The difficulty is that he arrived on a Thursday, read the whole of Miss Leslie's post-mortem record on Alpha by the Sunday, came into the office on the Monday morning and said that the type description was wrong.

"On what evidence."

"On her drawings and her notes and thirty years of opening things."

"That is not evidence, it is an impression."

"It's an impression from a man who has been putting his hands inside animals since you were at school." Pascual had both palms flat on the desk. "I can't prove it without a body. I'm telling you now so that when I get one you'll remember I said it first."

"When you get one," Reggie said, "we shall see."

He has been over that exchange since. What he has, on review, is that the objection was raised in the correct place at the correct time by the correct person and was refused on the grounds that it had not yet been proved, which is not a ground; and that he had read the Victorian paper as a young man in a place he cannot name, with the specimen in front of him, and had read it the way the literature told him to, and has never once gone back.

He does not put that anywhere. There is nowhere to put it.

---

What the station actually does, in the months when the animal is sitting in the pen doing nothing that can be written up, is fisheries.

Landings returns for the Fisheries Officer, four settlements, twice a month; market sampling at St John's on Saturdays; gear returns. It is dull and it is paid and it is what keeps the diesel running. Miss Leslie has a brackish-water fauna of her own that she has costed at eleven pounds and has been asking for since November, and Reggie has not funded it, on the grounds that the money is not there, which is true.

There are the hydrophones, which are war surplus and which he got for nothing, and the tag catalogue, which is his and which he has been adding to since 1949.

And there is the other thing, which is not on any establishment paper anywhere, and which is that when he is at a settlement for the landings he stays afterwards and asks.

---

He takes Pascual to Parham on the twenty-seventh of February because Juan has a bad hand and somebody has to hold the other end of the tape.

It is the last time he takes him for that reason.

They do the landings in two hours. Then Reggie starts, and he starts the way he has started a hundred and forty times since 1949: he asks about the boat first, and the season, and the run of the fish, and he does not write while they are talking, and he goes back a second time. By four o'clock he has one record and it is thin, and it is about the right rate.

Pascual, meanwhile, has got down on the ground in the dirt behind the shed with somebody's donkey.

He is there for the better part of forty minutes. Reggie can see him from the beach: the animal's off fore between his knees, the hat off, talking the whole time and not to the donkey. He does not appear to be asking for anything and he does not, so far as Reggie can establish afterwards, ask for anything at any point.

At half past five an old man called Simeon Dalso, who has not spoken to Reggie in two years of Saturdays beyond good afternoon, comes and sits on the gunwale next to the doctor and says that his father took one in 1931 off Guiana Island and that the other one would not go away.

Reggie is standing eight feet off with the notebook shut.

"Would not go away," he says.

"Three days," Dalso says, to Pascual, not to him. "Follow the boat in. Come inside the reef, where they don't come. My father say leave it, and they leave it, and it stay, and the one they had died in the pen at Willikies inside of the two month and then the other one gone." He shrugs. "So."

"Did anybody see the second animal after that."

"No, sah."

Reggie writes it in the boat going back and it takes him four lines, and he heads it, as he heads all of them, with the settlement, the informant, the date given, and the date taken.

The folklore says the same thing in eleven languages and he has had it since 1949 and has always filed it as folklore, because a thing that four hundred years of frightened people agree on is a thing to be suspicious of. What he has now is testimony from a named man with a year and a place attached, and he files it in the same place, under the same heading, and rules off.

Pascual, in the stern, sunburnt across the nose, says: "So they mind about each other."

"That is a very large word for two animals in the same water."

"It's the smallest word I've got. What would you like me to call it?"

"I should like you to call it nothing at all until there is something to call it."

Pascual laughs at him. It is not unpleasant and it appears to be a thing he does, and Reggie is halfway into the next sentence when he hears the one he has just said, from outside, the way anybody else in the boat would hear it. A man in an open boat at six in the evening forbidding another man a word. And the word is *mind*, which is four letters, and which is in point of fact the smallest one available.

It is, all round, funny, and he smiles a little.

Pascual stops laughing.

He does not say anything for some while after that. He is still not saying anything when they come round the point, and Reggie assumes he has been disagreed with, and puts the notebook away.

He takes him again on the thirteenth of March, and on the twenty-seventh, and after that it stops being a thing that requires a reason. Pascual gets four in a month. Reggie has been getting two in six.

He does not, at any point in the whole of that spring, sit down and work out why he is taking him. There is no need to. It is in the returns, in the column headed *records obtained*, and a man does not have to account for a fourfold improvement to anybody.

---

Cholmondeley arrives on the eighth of March and Reggie forms a view of him inside a week and does not revise it for five months.

The view is: twenty-four, a good second, soft, does not hold a look, will do as he is told and will do it thoroughly. There are a great many of them and this one is quieter than most. He puts him where such men go, which is on the empty pens and the fish delivery and whatever Juan wants doing, and he does not think about it again.

In April he picks up the residue book off the bench because it is in his way.

It is not a residue book. It is an intake and output series: mass of fish in, by species, net of the diseased and the refused; mass of residue out, sorted; the two ruled against each other by day, with the balance carried. Nobody asked for it. Nobody on this station has produced a quantitative series on a living merfolk since the place opened, because the only man who could take a weight is the one the animal will not let near it, and this is a measure of intake that does not require touching the animal at all, and it has been running since the second week of March.

Reggie stands in the doorway of the laboratory with it in his hands for some little while.

Then he puts it back on the bench where it was, and does nothing whatever about it, and he can give the reason and the reason is exact: a junior who has found himself useful work is a junior who does not require managing, and there are two support staff going on leave in a fortnight and Gamma is coming.

That is the whole of it. He would like that recorded and would not like anything constructed out of it.

---

Gamma lands on the twenty-first of April and the rota does not close.

He does it on the Thursday night with the sheet in front of him. Gamma is a fresh capture and a fresh capture is where the work is, so that is himself and Miss Leslie. John draws, and John has been gone after with teeth once already and will not go back. Juan and Everton are on leave from the Monday. Dr Pobil cannot feed an animal twice a day and run a laboratory.

What is left in the second column is one name, and he writes it in, and the whole operation takes under a minute.

He speaks to Cholmondeley at the end of lunch the next day, and tells him the animal is dangerous, and tells him the truth, which is that they have not the staff; and the boy says *of course I can* before the sentence is finished and then says it again.

It was not a decision. It was a subtraction. He has costed a death eight months in advance in his life and he gave away the only animal that has ever mattered to this institution on a staffing sheet in fifty seconds, and he is going to have thirty-three years to consider that.

---

From about March there is a list.

He does not permit the list. It comes up when he is tired, and he rules it off, and it does not stay ruled off; and it is the third of its kind, and he had settled at twenty-nine that there would not be a third.

The other thing takes one evening at the end of April. He has it inside a week and does nothing with it for four months.

The plates across the shoulder, the transfer held two seconds past what the transfer wanted, the count. Then Jean, in the same room, under the same lamp, at the same hour. Then both again, twice, because one of anything is nothing.

He has the result by the twenty-ninth. He puts it in the drawer with the mortality tables and he does not take it out again until the twenty-ninth of August, and he wants it understood — he wants it understood by somebody, at some point, though there is no one to want it of — that he did nothing with it, and that doing nothing with it was not restraint. Restraint implies an alternative that was available.

---

Beta stops declining in the second week of May.

Not improves: stops. The trajectory goes flat, which is a thing Reggie has seen four times in ninety-five intervals and every one of those four was a plateau on the way down.

At the end of June it is not a plateau any longer.

He gets it in three places at once, on the verandah, on a Tuesday evening, and this is what makes it a finding rather than an impression: Miss Leslie has the graph and is doing the arithmetic in her head and frowning at it; John has the plates fanned out on the table and the animal in them is unremarkable, which for an animal in a pen is astonishing; and the food figures are the boy's and they are stable and have been stable since the first week, and went up after the first week, and there is nobody in the world but that boy who could have produced them.

"Beta's already beaten the mortality tables," Jean says. "Has any other mermaid in captivity lasted that long?"

Reggie is doing her sums and one other. The one other is that the flattening dates from the third week of April, and that the third week of April is when he wrote a name into the second column of a rota under two men's leave.

"Not that I know of," he says.

He looks at Cholmondeley, and Cholmondeley looks at the table.

---

Pascual gets his hands on the animal in July because the boy holds his head, and comes off the apron pleased in a way Reggie has not seen in him about anything.

"He's nine pounds up from his intake weight. Agrees with his food logs. Surfacing intervals of four minutes, five."

"Not declining, eh?"

"Such a pleasure to be working with an animal who isn't declining."

Jean winces on Gamma's account and Reggie does not correct any part of it, because it is a rebuke and it is warranted and there is no version of the reply that is not worse than the silence.

Then, later, with his eyes half shut: "Any hypotheses as to why, Pascual?"

He asks it in the ordinary voice, at the ordinary hour, on the verandah, in front of four people, and Pascual takes about eleven seconds and says he expects it was Charles.

Which is what he wanted, and is now on the record in another man's mouth, and cost him nothing.

---

In the June and July he starts sending the boy to the settlements.

It begins as an economy. There are four landings a month and a market Saturday and a station of six people, and the boy has a bicycle and no other claim on his afternoons. And by the third week of June Reggie has read three of his returns and has had to put them down and go and do something else.

The boy is better at it than he is. That is not a difficult thing to be and Reggie has known for five years how bad he is at it and has been perfectly clear-eyed about the whole business, and it is still not a comfortable set of pages to read. The boy gets given things. He is given a 1912 sighting inside forty minutes at Urlings by a woman who has told Reggie twice that she does not remember anything. He writes down what people actually said instead of what they meant, which Reggie has been telling himself for five years is a distinction without a difference and is not. And he asks the wrong questions, in an order that has nothing to do with the subject and everything to do with the man in front of him, which Reggie has known since 1949 is the only order that works and has never once been able to make himself use.

He is also very good with a hydrometer, and better in the water than anybody on the station, and on the turtle transects he lays the tape himself and calls the counts up, and he is quick and he is accurate and he does not come out when he is cold.

Reggie holds the boat off the line so as not to put the animals down. It is four hours at the tiller in a chop, and it is constant, and from beginning to end of it he does not look at a turtle. John sits in the stern with the tally sheet and thinks it a punishment, and says so on land, where it is safe.

Reggie enjoys it a great deal. That is a fact about himself he has never examined. Four hours in an open boat with nothing to write and nobody requiring anything of him is the only part of the year in which he is entirely all right, and he has never once put those two observations side by side.

He does not promote the boy. He does not mention the returns to him. He does not mention them to anybody. What he does is send him again, and again, and he could have given a reason at any time in those two months and the reason would have been staffing and would have been true.

---

He draws the design up in the last week of July and it takes four evenings.

The question is whether the effect is the man or the handling. If it is handling, it transfers, and a merfolk in captivity becomes a soluble problem for the first time in four hundred years. If it is the man, it does not transfer, and the finding is very much larger and cannot be published by anybody.

Withdraw the handler from Beta. Substitute Jean. Put the handler on Gamma. Two weeks. Take everything.

He writes the objections himself, in the same hand, on the same paper, because a design that has not been argued against has not been designed.

*Beta's baseline is contaminated by four months of a single handler and there is no clean pre-period to score against.* Correct. *Beta is the only animal on this station not declining, and the withdrawal arm therefore puts at risk the one animal that is working.* Correct, and the graver of the two. *Jean is not a naive handler and the substitution arm is accordingly not a control.* Correct.

Three objections, all sound, all his, and the design is uninterpretable and he can demonstrate it in nine lines.

He runs it.

He can give the argument that carries it and it is not a bad argument. It is that an uninterpretable case is precisely where the value is, because the whole of the difficulty in this field for four hundred years has been that the subject cannot afterwards tell you anything, and here there is one that can. The boy can sit down at the end of it and give an account, in order, in his own words. That is the thing nobody has ever had.

He puts it to himself in those terms, on the fourth evening, and finds it good.

Somewhere behind it there is an older proposition which does not present itself in words and which he does not go and look at, and in the morning it is not there.

He tells the boy on the verandah on the first of August. He says a week or two. He has designed fourteen days and he says a week or two, and both are true, and he gives the smaller one, and the boy nods and does not argue.

The boy does argue, once, and it is the only good idea anybody has that month: put Gamma in with Beta. Reggie explains, slowly, why one does not put two territorial animals in one pen.

He has had the Dalso record in the notebook since February.

---

Day one, intake down to eleven pounds from twenty-two.

He has the figures. He has had every figure of it as it came, daily, in his own hand, and there has never been a moment in the fortnight when he did not know precisely where the animal was on the curve.

Day three, nine and a half, refusing the second feed. Day five he stops coming up for them. Day seven the surfacing intervals are at two minutes forty and shortening. Day nine the breathing goes irregular, and Pascual is in his doorway at eleven at night saying so, and Reggie says he has it.

He does have it. That is the part that will not go anywhere afterwards. Every night of those nine he stood at the rail after the others had gone up and read the animal off the water and knew what he was looking at, and went to the office, and wrote it down accurately.

And the boy, in the other pen, cannot get into the water at all and is sick over the drain twice and goes grey, and gives him an account every evening in order and in his own words, which is the thing nobody has ever had.

*Day 3: No change. Day 4: No change. Day 5: No change.*

On the eleventh day it is on the verandah, in front of the Colonel and Jean and John, at volume, and it goes on for some considerable time.

Pascual is entirely correct throughout and Reggie has an answer to every part of it. He gives them. He gives them calmly and in order and without raising his voice once, which is doing him no good whatever with the room and a great deal of good with himself.

There are two questions on that verandah. One of them is whether the thing he is doing is a monstrous thing, and he has no instrument for that and never has had. The other is whether he is conducting himself correctly, which is answerable; and he is answering it continuously while he speaks, in the back of his head, item by item, and it keeps coming back yes.

A thing done properly is a thing a man is not answerable for. He settled that a long time ago in a building he does not name, and it has held for six years, and it is holding now.

Then Pascual says that if he is going to kill the first specimen they have managed to keep alive, he might as well have stayed with the government labs.

The generator stops.

It does not stop; he stops hearing it, which he has not done in two years and would have told anybody was not possible. He is aware of the chair under him and of the boards and of the fact that his glass is on the arm of the chair and that he has not moved it. He has the reply. He has had the reply since the second sentence and it is a good one, and there is nothing whatever between his head and his mouth and it does not come out.

Somebody says something. It is probably Jean.

"That will do," Reggie says, and gets up, and says goodnight to the Colonel, and goes in.

On the stairs he puts a hand on the rail. He has gone up those stairs perhaps two thousand times.

What he does not do, at any point that night, is stop the experiment. He has it in front of him for four hours. He has the intake series and the surfacing intervals and two days of the design left to run and he takes it out and reads it three times, and it is uninterpretable and he has always known it was uninterpretable, and he does not stop it.

He puts it down at about three and lies on the bed in his clothes and does not go and get anything, because it does not occur to him that there is anything to get.

---

At six in the morning Pascual reads it off the ground.

Reggie is not there for that. He has it at ten past seven, from Jean, in the doorway of the office, and she gives it him in about forty seconds and does it well.

He asks two questions. Whether the animal is in the water. Whether Pascual is with him. Then he thanks her and asks her to wait.

He writes the minute sitting down, which takes longer than standing would have. It is two lines. *The crossed design (Beta/Gamma, substitution of handler) is terminated at day twelve. Handler returns to Beta with immediate effect.* He signs it and puts the time on it and folds it into his breast pocket, and he takes Jean down with him, because Jean is the substitution arm and it is her result as much as anyone's and she is entitled to be told by the man who started it that it is over.

That is seven minutes. He has had a good deal of time since to think about the seven minutes.

On the apron the tarp is on the concrete with the blood on it and there is a port-wine stain by the drain and the animal is in the water and the boy is in the water with him, and Pascual is wet to the shoulder and has already had blood off him.

"Ah, Reggie, Jean. Showing up just when the work is over."

"What happened?"

Pascual tells him. It is a clean account and it takes some minutes and there is not one figure in the whole of it, because nobody was there. Reggie flicks his eyes to the boy, once. There is nothing on his face and there is nothing to be got.

Then Reggie opens his mouth, and his hand is already at the pocket, and Pascual says:

"You certainly see it's a medical issue now. He's not doing any good with Gamma, and he's needed here with Beta." And then: "You have your results, Reggie. You don't need more."

They look at each other.

It is not a long interval and it is not, in any sense Reggie would put on paper, a contest. What it is, is that there is a paper in his pocket with a time on it, and the time on it is a quarter past seven, and the animal was on that concrete for some hours before that. Produced now, it is a defence. It will be believed, because it is signed and because he does not lie. And it will stand thereafter as a claim to a decision.

He did not make the decision. The animal made it, by getting himself up a ladder and across nine yards of concrete in the dark, and everything Reggie has done since ten past seven is arithmetic upon a completed event. He had four hours the previous night with the whole series in front of him and he did nothing whatever.

He drops his eyes.

He has not done that. Not in a corridor, not in a committee, not once, not to anybody, and he does it on a concrete apron at twenty past seven in the morning in front of two of his own juniors.

"Right," he says. "Charles, you can stay with Beta for now."

"Go eat," Pascual is saying to the boy. "You're no use to him if you keel over from hunger." And then, without turning round: "Reggie. I'd like to have a word with you —"

---

The word is behind the generator hut and it goes on for eleven minutes and Pascual does the whole of it.

He does it by days, which is the correct way to do it, and every figure he produces is a figure Reggie gave him. Day one, intake down to eleven pounds. Day five he stopped coming up for them. Day nine in the doorway. Day eleven on the verandah. Day twelve read off the ground because there was nobody awake to tell him any of it.

Reggie stands and takes it and does not interrupt, and at the end of it Pascual says, "Well?" — wanting the fight, wanting it badly, having come round the hut for it.

"You are right in every particular," Reggie says. "The design was uninterpretable and I wrote down that it was uninterpretable before I ran it. I have the paper. I will show it to you if you want it."

Pascual looks at him for a moment.

Then he says, "No," and walks off.

That was the wrong answer. Reggie is aware of it within the minute and cannot construct the right one, not that day nor at any point in the following week, and what he settles on eventually is that there was not a right one available, which is the sort of finding he distrusts in other people.

Pascual does not look at him for five days.

He is clear about what that means and he is clear about it at the time. A man who believes he was right looks at you rather more, not less; he wants it again and he wants it in front of somebody. Five days of a man not looking at you is a man who has put the thing somewhere and is not going to take it out.

He would rather have had the fight.

The minute goes into the file that afternoon, because it is a minute and minutes go in the file. It is in the Martin's records still. Nobody has ever read it.

He goes down to the pen in the evenings, and he goes when Pascual is not there, and stands and takes the readings, and it is by a distance the most interesting week of his professional life. The animal is recovering from muscle damage that ought to have killed him and is doing it in the water with a man in there, and there is a plot in the office with a line on it that Reggie would send off tomorrow if the thing could be sent.

It cannot. Not on grounds of n; he has published off one animal before and would do it again and has never much minded what was said about it. It is that an honest write-up of that line requires a methods section, and the method is a man. The moment the method is a man, the man is the finding, and there are people who read the journals whose entire business is establishing where a thing like that is kept.

So the plot stays in the office. He has known that since about the second day of it and has gone on taking the readings every evening regardless, which he could not defend to anybody and does not have to, because nobody has asked him.

He stops eating at some point in the third week of August.

He is aware of this in the way one is aware of the tide. It is not a decision and there is no distress attached; it simply does not present itself as a thing to be done, and the dining room is at one o'clock and he is generally elsewhere at one o'clock, and Jean asks him once and he says he has had it, and she says *ah* and does not ask again, and Pascual is not looking at him.

The work is going well. He notes that, because it is the sort of thing he notes. He noted it once before, in a hot summer six years ago, when he was also not eating, and he does not join the two observations to anything.

---

On about the fifteenth the boy stops asking him for things.

Reggie is exact about this, because it is the sort of thing he is exact about. Cholmondeley has asked him for something on nineteen occasions since March — the loan of the hydrometer, a second bucket, leave to take the bicycle on a Saturday, the use of the small boat, four hours — and they are on a page at the back of the daybook, because Reggie keeps a page like that for everybody and has done since 1952. There has not been one since the twelfth.

He has also: that the boy has not been off the apron in six days. That he has been to the stores twice and drawn nothing that appears on any indent. That on the Monday he asked Juan about the set of the tide on the north side, which is not a question anybody asks about a pen.

Reggie sets those down and does not draw the conclusion. He could have the conclusion inside eleven seconds if he permitted himself to want it, and he does not permit himself to want it, because a man who knows a thing can be asked; and instead he goes and finds Jean and asks her for a fair copy of the southward chart.

"What's it for?"

"The survey schedule."

Jean looks at him for slightly longer than the question needs. "Right," she says.

She brings it in on the Wednesday with nine annotations on it in her own hand. Six of them are the ones he told her to put on. The other three are where the reef opens, and where there is fresh water, and one that reads *Keep clear — ordnance*, and there is no version of a survey schedule anywhere on earth in which a man needs telling where the ordnance is.

Reggie looks at that one for rather longer than he looks at the other eight.

"Thank you, Jean."

"Mm," she says, and goes.

He rolls it and puts it in a screw-top tin out of the stores and puts the tin in the second drawer of the desk on the eighteenth of August, and does not open that drawer again for eleven days.

There is no survey schedule. There is not going to be one for another eleven years.

---

Hester comes to him about the co-housing on the nineteenth.

She does it properly, which is to say she does it in the office, sitting down, with the door open, and she does not mention 1951 and does not have to.

"There's a condition you and I agreed to before there was a laboratory," she says. "I've never once raised it and I'm not raising it now. I'm asking you for a different thing, which is to put two of them in one pen, and I'd like it done before the Navy come."

"They are territorial. There is a fifty-year record of what happens."

"There's a fifty-year record of animals dying alone in twenty weeks, and the one that hasn't is the one that's had someone in the water with him since April. I've read the same table you have, Reggie, I typed most of it." She puts her hands on her bag. "Colonel Bevan does outrank you, and I'd rather not go that way round."

"You would go that way round."

"Yes," Hester says.

He looks at the wall for a moment.

"I will try it once," he says. "The Navy are bringing us another animal when they come, so we shall be able to do it immediately. I want it minuted that I have said it will not work."

"It's minuted."

She goes. He sits with it for about a quarter of an hour, and then takes the Dalso record out of the notebook and reads it again, and puts it back, and does not do anything else about it, and he has no account of that either.

---

The verandah, the twenty-ninth of August, after supper.

The Colonel says something about writers. John says the name Fleming, and says he is an old family friend, and says it the way he says everything, which is with the whole table listening and enjoying itself.

Reggie says that he would prefer results not to be publicised before review.

And John waves a hand and says that the Americans were very interested, and that they are sending another observer during the visit, a chap named Spilsbury.

The sound is out of him before he has any part of it.

It is not loud. It is short and it is low and it is in the chest, and it is the first uncontrolled sound he has made in front of another human being since he was a boy, and there is no getting it back, and Pascual and Jean are both looking at him.

"Do you know him?" Pascual says.

"We have worked together," Reggie says. And then, because he can hear his own voice and it is not doing what it is told: "John. Let us speak in my room."

---

John is pleased with himself in the room, and stays pleased with himself the whole way through, and Reggie takes it in order, because there is no other way to take anything.

What was said. To whom. When. Whether in company. Whether the animal was named. Whether the man was named. Whether anything of Jean's was in it, or Pascual's, or the Colonel's. What John had himself observed at the pen, and on what date, and whether he had written it down, and where that is now.

It takes nineteen minutes and the boy answers everything, promptly and accurately and with a certain relish, and there is not one item in it that he had any business having and he assembled the whole of it out of an overheard argument and a fortnight of his own eyes.

At the end of it Reggie has the entire shape of what is going to arrive on the second of September and he has it complete and he could put it down on paper in ten lines.

John says, "It'll be splendid for the Institute, honestly."

Reggie considers, for about a second and a half, telling him.

He does not. A man who has been told what he has done is a man in possession of a fact, and a man in possession of a fact can be asked; and John Godfrey will be sat in front of somebody within a fortnight and will answer everything promptly and accurately and with a certain relish, and there is exactly one thing in this business that is still not in his head and Reggie intends to keep it that way.

"Thank you, John," he says. "That is all."

Nobody is ever going to tell him. Reggie settles that in the room, in about the time it takes to stand up, and he is right about it, and he will go on being right about it for the rest of both their lives.

---

Then Cholmondeley, in the office, with the door shut.

He gives it to him in order. What John has assembled and how. Who Fleming is. Who Fleming's people are. What is coming ashore on the second and what the second parcel of business is.

The boy asks him why he is telling him.

And Reggie says that what they are doing is not science, which is true; and says the other thing, about labs that are not cruel but are curious and impatient and very fond of knives, which is also true and which he has not said aloud to a living soul in six years and did not intend to say now.

He watches the boy take it and he watches the boy relax, and he understands, at the time, exactly why: he has been given a reason that is about the work, and a reason about the work is a reason that can be believed, and if Reggie had said any of the other reasons the boy would have known he was being handled.

He is not being handled. That is the part Reggie cannot get across and does not attempt, because attempting it would be the handling.

He gets the tin out of the second drawer.

*Reef opens here. Fresh water. Keep clear — ordnance.*

He gives it to him. He tells him about Gamma, and about Hester, and about the Colonel outranking him. He says he shall be quite cross if it does not work, and it is meant as a kindness and comes out as itself.

And he says the last thing, which is that the boy need not worry about the government men when he comes back, and that the Colonel has dealt with those types and so has he, and they will take care of them.

There is no version of that which is true. He knows that while he is saying it. He says it anyway, in the earnest voice, and the boy says *yes* and takes the tin, and neither of them believes it, and it is the last conversation they will have for ten years.

---

He goes out on to the verandah at about a quarter to eight.

Pascual is still there and is going to ask, because he is a man who asks. He asks. Reggie says, come up at half past nine.

Then he goes up.

The room faces the yard and the tanks, which he has complained about twice in writing. He shuts the shutter and lights the one lamp and stands for a moment with his hand still on the catch, because there is a distance in yards from the pens to the water and there is a tide table in the drawer and he is not going to be able to keep those two things apart for very long, and there is one instrument he has ever found that interferes.

He takes the case down off the top of the wardrobe.

There is a bottle in it, with the winter things, and it has been there since the November of 1950, and in four years he has not once had occasion to want it.

He pours the first at eight.

Three is the dose. He established that in a cold room with a bottle of somebody else's whisky in the December of 1946 and he has had no occasion to revise it. Three inside twenty minutes would put him somewhere he cannot afford to be at half past nine, so it is one now, and one at nine, and one after that; and he works that out standing at the wardrobe with the bottle in his hand, and it is the last piece of arithmetic he does that evening which comes out.

He is slow over it. He has never liked the taste and does not like it now, and that makes no difference to anything at all. The dose is the dose whether a man enjoys it or not.
