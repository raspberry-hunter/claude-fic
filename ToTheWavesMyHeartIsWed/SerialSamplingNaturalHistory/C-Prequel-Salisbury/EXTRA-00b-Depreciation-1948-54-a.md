# Depreciation

The reference Spilsbury writes for him in the April of 1948 is the best document of its kind he will ever have his name on, and it is unusable, because a reference from that establishment is a statement about where a man has been.

He learns this in five months and eleven applications. Nobody is rude about it. Two of them are extremely kind.

He takes, in the September, a temporary assistant's post at a provincial museum in Exeter, cataloguing a bequest, at two hundred and eighty pounds a year. It is a post for a man of twenty-two and he is thirty-one and he takes it because it is offered and because the collection is of a kind nobody else in England wants to be near, and he has one room over a bakery in Longbrook Street and a gas ring and a case under the bed.

He drinks in the evenings. He keeps the count in the front of the notebook because a thing that isn't counted isn't controlled, and the counts for the autumn of 1948 are what they are, and he isn't going to write anything about them here.

---

The bequest is a colonial official's, and it is nine hundred and forty objects of no discernible principle: shells, an ivory, four bad watercolours, a Benin bronze that is not, and a quantity of natural-history material assembled by a man who bought what pleased him.

Object 611 is in a deal box under a lot of newspaper from 1911.

It is thirty inches long. It is the upper half of a young macaque sewn at the waist to the tail of a grey mullet, with the join packed out in papier-mâché and painted, and the arms wired into a posture of surprise, and glass eyes, and the teeth are the animal's own and have been filed. Japanese work, probably 1860s, of which there are a hundred and fifty in cabinets in this country and about four thousand in the world.

The fur on the top of the head is worn down to the hide.

That is the item he sits with. Not the join, which is competent, and not the teeth. It is that this thing has been in a house for eighty years and people have put their hands on its head, over and over, the way one does to a dog, and the maker never saw that coming and neither did the man who bought it.

He writes the card. The card requires a line of reference. He goes down to the library and gets the type description out — 1841, one paper, one corpse, a naturalist who never saw a living one — and reads it through twice, and it is wrong.

He knows precisely how wrong and in which particulars and he cannot state the grounds. He has had his hands inside three of them and there is no sentence in the English language he is permitted to build out of that.

So he writes the card with the reference in it, and initials it, and puts the thing back in the box.

And then goes and stands outside in the yard in the rain for a while, because it has occurred to him — and it has occurred to him complete, the way things do — that there exists on this earth an animal with one paper to its name, and that paper is wrong, and there are four thousand forgeries, and no institution anywhere is going to fund a man to correct the ratio.

He has never in his life wanted anything he could not be given.

---

He starts in the January of 1949 in Bristol, in the merchant archives, and what he is after is not an animal. It is a date of death.

The literature has nothing. But the animal is four hundred years in the record of people who were not scientists, and those people wrote things down for reasons of their own: a customs man clears a cargo, a governor reports a curiosity to the Colonial Office because it costs him nothing, a menagerie keeps an inventory because animals are stock. And every one of those documents has two entries in it if you know to look, which are the day the thing arrived and the day it stopped being on the books.

Nobody has ever done this because nobody has ever thought the sources were worth the time. He is prepared to think so, and he is prepared to say why, and the why is that a consistent observational record across five centuries and eleven languages is data whatever one thinks of the observers, and he'll go to the stake on it.

By the June he has forty-one intervals. By the December he has ninety.

They are all the same number.

---

The other half of it is people, and the people are worse, because people cannot be filed and have to be sat with.

Captain Absalom Neagle is eighty-four and in a home outside Weston, and he saw one off the Grain Coast in 1892, and he is deaf, and Reggie has come on two buses.

For an hour and fifty minutes he gets nothing whatever. He asks the questions in order and the old man answers a different question each time, and the daughter comes in twice, and there is a clock. At about half past three Reggie closes the notebook and thanks him and stands up to go, and Neagle says, to the window, in an ordinary voice:

"We didn't put a boat down."

Reggie sits back down.

"No, sir?"

"There was seven of us on that rail and not a man said a word for I don't know how long, and then Mr Beddoes said we'd put a boat down and take it, and we didn't." The old man's hands are on the blanket. "I couldn't tell you why. I've had sixty years to think what I'd say if anybody asked me and nobody ever has, and I still couldn't tell you. It went down and we come away and we never spoke of it again, not one of us, not the whole of that voyage."

Reggie writes it down in the bus shelter in the rain, because it will not keep.

He notes that his hands are unsteady, and puts it down to the cold, and it is April.

The daughter had put a plate in front of him at one o'clock and he had eaten the whole of it, because refusing would have finished the interview inside a minute. He notes that too, in the same entry, in the same hand, as though the two observations were of a kind.

---

There are a hundred and forty of them over the four years and they are all like that. Two hours of nothing, and then, at the door, one sentence.

He is bad at it. He is bad at it the whole time. He does not become a man people warm to; he becomes a man who has learned, item by item, by rote, what such a man does — how long to leave a silence, that the tea must be drunk, that you ask about the ship first and the animal fourth, that you never write while they are speaking, that you go back a second time. He assembled the items off a list he made about somebody else in another county, and he could not tell you that, and does not know it.

In the March of 1950 he sits down at the gas ring and builds an instrument.

It takes him an evening. It is thirty-one items, it will take fifty minutes to administer, it will produce comparable and scoreable data across the whole of the remaining sample, and it is the best thing of its kind that exists, and he reads it through at eleven o'clock at night with the pen still in his hand.

Then he folds it and puts it in the back of the notebook, where the two other things are, and does not use it.

He does the remaining ninety by hand, one man at a time, badly, without an instrument, and the data are measurably worse for it. He keeps them anyway. He is going to keep them for thirty-five years and he is never once, in all that time, going to give anybody an account of the decision, because there is no account that does not require the first sentence.

---

The whisky stops in the November of 1950 and he doesn't notice for some weeks.

What he notices, on a Tuesday, doing the accounts in the front of the notebook out of habit, is that the last figure in the column has a date on it in February.

He works it out sitting on the bed. The instrument was never the drink. The instrument was interference — a means of stopping a thing that ran in his head whether he wanted it or not — and a day that begins at half past six with a bus and ends at eleven with three hundred words of another man's speech to get down before it goes is interference of a far better quality, and is free, and produces a paper at the end of it.

He notes the substitution as a finding, in one line, and rules off under it.

He does not throw the bottle away. It goes in the case under the bed with the winter things.

---

The only thing with his name on it between 1948 and 1953 is two pages in a museum journal in the November of 1950, on the composite figures — the join, the wire, the filing of the teeth, the four thousand — and it is initialled and not signed and it does not appear on a publication list anywhere, because catalogue notes do not.

It is, he is aware, a paper about forgeries by a man who has stopped publishing. He is aware of it while he writes it and he writes it anyway and does not soften a line.

He has by then a fair idea of how the whole of it reads from London, and the answer is that it reads correctly. A first-class man of thirty-three, out of a government establishment, in a provincial museum, on two hundred and eighty a year, doing folklore. Nobody has to be told anything. The shape does the work and the shape is not a lie, and there is no version of the truth he is permitted to substitute for it, and he has stopped minding in the way one stops minding a noise.

---

Mrs Bevan writes to him in the October of 1951, on paper with no letterhead, having got his address off the two pages about the forgeries.

They meet at a hotel in South Kensington. She is not what he expects, and what he expects is a benefactress. She is about forty-five, in a good coat that has been turned, and she has done her reading, and inside four minutes he understands that she is the one who will decide and that the Colonel is a signature.

"There's a laboratory on the north coast of Antigua," she says. "It doesn't exist yet in any sense that would satisfy you. There's an endowment from 1946, a disused seaplane station, and a great deal of war surplus. It wants a scientific director and it can pay him six hundred a year, which is derisory, and there's a house."

"What is it for?"

"That's the question I want to ask you, actually, and I'd like you to be exact." She puts her cup down. "I've written to eleven people in three years. Every one of them has told me what these animals *are*. Where they sit, what they're related to, what they ought to be called. Not one of them has been able to tell me what happens to them."

"They die," Reggie says. "Within twenty weeks of being landed. It's remarkably tight — the whole distribution is between fourteen and twenty-six and the mode is at twenty. It's been that number since 1560 and it doesn't move for latitude, for handling, for feeding, for whether the people concerned were kind to them or not. I have ninety-four intervals and I'll have three hundred."

Mrs Bevan does not say anything for some while.

"You're too good for six hundred a year," she says at last. "I know exactly why I can get you and I have no intention of asking. I want that stated, because you'll be four thousand miles from anyone and I don't propose to have you sitting in a house waiting for me to raise it. I shan't raise it. Not this year and not in twenty."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me, it's entirely selfish. A man who's being managed does bad work." She takes a gloves-on grip of her bag, which he will come to know as the thing she does when a matter is concluded. "There's one condition and it's mine and not the Colonel's. You'll not take an animal for the sake of having one. If we hold one it will be because it was going to be held by somebody, and I'd rather it were us."

"That is a condition I would have proposed myself."

"I know," she says. "It's why I wrote."

---

He sails in the March of 1952 and he pays for his own passage out of two years' salary, most of which he has not spent on anything, and he is thirty-five.

The station is a seaplane apron, four Nissen huts, a slipway, a diesel that was in a landing craft, and two hundred and ten yards of harbour wall that the sea comes over in October. There is no laboratory. There is a room with a bench in it. He has a Colonel who is decent and out of his depth and a foreman who is worth six of anybody, and by the end of the first year there are pens.

He designs the pens himself off the mortality table, which is to say off four hundred years of other people's dead, and they are the best pens that have ever been built for the purpose, and they are still not going to be enough. He knows that when he draws them.

He is not unhappy. He would not have used the word and would not have understood the question. What he would have said, if anybody had asked, is that the work is the correct work and there is a great deal of it, and that he is asleep by eleven, and that he eats at one o'clock because the whole station eats at one o'clock and a director who does not come in is remarked upon.

Miss Leslie arrives in the September of 1953, twenty-six, from Bangor, and is the first person he has appointed who is better at the work than the post requires. He does not tell her so. He gives her the whole of the acoustic side inside a month, which is the only language he has for it, and she reads it correctly and neither of them ever mentions it.

---

Alpha is landed on the eleventh of August 1953, in a net, off Barbuda, by two men who have no idea what they have.

She is the first one he has ever seen alive.

He is thirty-six years old and he has had his hands inside three of them in a building he cannot name and he has ninety-four intervals and four hundred years of testimony, and he has never once, in his life, seen one move.

He stands on the apron for a long time.

That week he writes the requisition for a veterinary officer. Not after; that week. He has twenty weeks in front of him with a mode at twenty, and he wants the man in post before the interval closes, and he says so in the paper — not in those terms, since one does not requisition a post on the grounds that a thing in one's charge is going to die on schedule, so it goes up as *anatomical and clinical capacity, permanent*. The Colonel signs it. It goes to London in the September.

It takes six months.

Alpha dies on the twenty-second of January 1954, at twenty-three weeks, which is inside the distribution and rather better than the mode, and he had not permitted himself a figure and had one anyway and it was twenty-one.

Miss Leslie does the post-mortem, because there is nobody else, in the heat, in three days, with instruments that are not for it. She does it thoroughly and writes it up honestly and the record is what it is, and the fault is not hers in any particular and he tells her so twice and she does not believe him either time.

He sits down that night and reads the whole of it through, and what he has at the end is a great deal of gross description and no answer to a single question he has been carrying since 1949.

---

He is not able to give a full account of the following week and does not attempt one.

What is in the daybook is that the pens were stripped and limed, that the intake sheets were closed off and ruled, that the specimen ledger was opened at the second page, and that the director was in the office from six to eleven for six consecutive days, and that on the Thursday the whole of the mortality table was recalculated with the interval for Alpha in it.

Ninety-five.

The number does not move. It was never going to move; one interval in ninety-five moves nothing, and he ran it knowing that, and he ran it anyway, and he has no account of why he ran it that survives being written down.

He does not go to the bottle in the case on top of the wardrobe. It does not present itself as available. He is aware, on about the Wednesday, that it has not presented itself, and files that under things that are true and of no use.

---

The letter from Spilsbury reaches him in the February, forwarded twice, and is two lines.

*The table is right and the method is the thing, not the table. You have done the only piece of work in the field in fifteen years and you have published it in a place where nobody who matters will see it, and you know that, and I should like it recorded that I have understood what you are doing.*

*A. S.*

He reads it standing on the step with the mail bag still open.

Then he takes it inside and puts it in the drawer of the desk, which is a decision, and files nothing else about the matter at all.

---

The launch comes in on the eleventh of February at about half past three, and he goes down, because a director goes down.

Dr Pobil is on the foredeck with his hat off. Forty-three; qualified at Cornell in 1935; nineteen years, of which eleven in practice and the remainder split between a race-track and a government abattoir, which is an unusual pairing and is the reason the file came to the top; two papers, one of them on equine myoglobinuria and considerably better than it needed to be. Reggie has read them both twice and can give the figures.

He notes, coming up the steps: that the man is a good deal larger than the file suggested, and audible at the far end of the apron before he is at the head of the slipway; that he has brought a great deal of his own equipment, which no appointee has ever done; and that he has stopped, at the top, to say something to Juan about the boat, which takes eleven seconds and which Reggie times without deciding to.

That is the whole of the arrival record. There is no more of it. He has been six years making certain that there would be no more of it about anybody, and it has held, and it holds now, and it is going to hold for another six months.

"Dr Pobil. You will want an hour to put your things down. Supper is at seven; I shall want to go through the standing orders with you tonight rather than tomorrow, because there is a good deal of it and I would rather you had it in order before you see the pens. There are four heads. The first is the specimen ledger, which is now on its second page and which I shall explain, since the manner in which it is kept is mine and is not usual. The second is —"

"Are you all right?"

Reggie stops.

He has not been interrupted in the middle of an enumeration in six years. It is not that people do not dare; it is that he does not, as a rule, give them the opening, and he has just given one, and cannot at this moment establish where.

"I'm sorry?"

"You've lost one," Pascual says. "Three weeks ago. I read the file on the boat." He puts his bag down on the concrete, which is wet. "You've a laboratory here that doesn't exist yet and you buried an animal nobody in the world knows how to keep, and you've been on your own with it. So I'm asking. That's all."

The apron. The generator. Somebody dropping a length of pipe down at the slipway.

"The specimen ledger," Reggie says, "is now on its second page."

"All right," Pascual says.

He picks up the bag and goes on up, and Reggie stands where he is for a moment before he follows him, and puts it down to the heat, and it is February and the heat is nothing.
