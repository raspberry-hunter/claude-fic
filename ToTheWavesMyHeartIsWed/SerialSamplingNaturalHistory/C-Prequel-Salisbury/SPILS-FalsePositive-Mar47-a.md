# False Positive

It was the middle of March and he'd had him in the room with the bottle because the man had come up to the house at eleven at night about a discrepancy in the fortnightly return, which was not a thing that could not have waited until eight.

They did the discrepancy. It took nine minutes.

And then Tar sat there with the glass not drunk out of and said, "I haven't eaten since Tuesday."

Flat. The way he'd give you a temperature. He didn't put anything after it and he didn't look up, and he plainly regarded it as an item and not as a confidence.

Spilsbury had been in that man's company most days for eighteen months and had never had one sentence out of him that was about himself. He knew the size of what had just been put on the table. He had it correct, immediately, to the ounce.

He then made the only error of magnitude he has ever made in his life, which was not an error of magnitude at all. It was the sign.

He put his glass down and leaned over and kissed him.

---

And Tar did not jerk away.

That is the part Spilsbury has never got out from under. A younger man, a frightened man, any of the four hundred men Spilsbury has read across a table, comes off the chair — and this one sat perfectly still for about a second and a half, so that when he did move his head it could not be mistaken for flinching, and moved it perhaps four inches, and said:

"No."

Spilsbury began to say something about the hour and the whisky.

"No — understand what I am saying." Uncontracted. Spilsbury had never heard him do that and would hear it for the rest of his life. "You will go over this at four in the morning and you will decide that I meant the other thing. I did not mean the other thing. I mean: not you."

The fire went, and neither of them attended to it.

"You realise," Spilsbury said, when he could, "that you have just handed me your career."

"Yes."

He did not say it quickly. There were some seconds in it; and Spilsbury, whose entire trade is watching men put a price on a thing, watched him work out to the pound what he had just given away, and arrive at the figure, and pay it.

"Why?"

Tar looked at the grate for a while, and then said, in precisely the voice he used on the ward for the assessments:

"Because you were not wrong. You were wrong in one term of it. If I leave you the other reading you will have decided by Friday that the whole of it was an error of yours, and it was not an error, and I am not going to go out of this room leaving a man a bad figure when I have the correct one in my pocket."

"That is a kindness," Spilsbury said, "and I had rather you did not do me one."

"It is the correct figure."

"Tar."

"You may have it either way you like." He put the glass down, still not drunk out of, and stood up. "I would take the figure."

Then he said goodnight and went down to Hut 2, and was on the half past seven van in the morning, and did the ward.

---

It took Spilsbury some days to work out the whole of what had been done to him, and when he got it he did not care for it at all.

He had been given an assessment: the exact thing the establishment employed the man to do, performed at midnight, unpaid, out of his own ruin — which is to hand somebody a true account of himself when nobody else in the building is competent to produce one.

That much he had in the room. What took the week was the rest of it, which is that Tar had chosen the form.

Sympathy Spilsbury would have thrown out of the house. Discretion he'd have taken as pity and resented for a decade. Silence would have finished them both. And he had been handed, instead, a correction to the record, in the flattest voice in the establishment, by a man who then declined to have it called anything else and told him which of the two readings to take — because a correction to the record is the only form of kindness Alan Spilsbury has ever in his life been able to accept, and Tar had known that, and had had about four seconds to know it in.

He wrote *will require handling* on a card in the September of '46.

He has had eight years to consider which of them did the handling.

---

That is the first time the instrument failed.

He has sat opposite murderers and bishops and a man who sold a sonar programme to three governments in one year, and he has always, always known what the thing in front of him was worth. And a scientist of thirty in a bad chair handed him an unbreakable lever, for nothing, at no return, so that a man he had no reason on earth to protect should not go away with a wrong figure — and Spilsbury could not price it, then or since. He has tried perhaps a dozen times. It will not hold a number.

It is one of the four. He can tell you the four and this is the only one of them that was not about somebody else.

---

Nothing was ever said about it again.

Not in the thirteen months following, in which they worked side by side and the returns got better and the man got thinner. Not in the car at the goods yard, where either of them could have used it and one of them had every reason to. Not in nine refusals. Not in a good room in Trinidad in 1957 with the sea outside the window and no hurry at all.

The lever is still there. It has been there since March 1947 and it is exactly as permanent and exactly as unbreakable as any of them, and the man who holds it has never once put his hand on it.

And it was a clean no. Tested, held, and correctly priced at the time.

He has never in nineteen years re-put a question that got one of those. There is no percentage in it. He knows it better than anyone living; he teaches it; he has failed men on the course for less.

He has re-put this one about ten times, in two different shapes, to the same man, and has called it recruitment on every occasion, and had never until this week been obliged to look at the figure.
