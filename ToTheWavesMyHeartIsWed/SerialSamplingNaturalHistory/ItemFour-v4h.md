Spilsbury arrives on the Thursday and Reggie is ready for him, in the sense that there is nothing to be ready with.

That is not luck. He built it himself, deliberately, in his own room, at night, in about four minutes, and it is the most economical piece of design he has ever done. He knows what was on the chart. He does not know whether the chart was any use, which is a different quantity, and it is the only quantity Spilsbury wants.

He did not ask for a date. He did not ask for a signal, or an address, or a name to write to, or a way of being told. He did not ask what the man intended to do when he got to the water, which he could have asked and which the man would have answered, because the man answered everything.

So he sits in his own office for three hours with a person he has been in a room with before, and they talk like colleagues, and it costs him nothing, because he has nothing.

What it costs him is elsewhere. Spilsbury reads the file. Spilsbury reads the crossed experiment -- the design, the withdrawal, the substitution, the negative both ways -- and Reggie watches him get to the end of it and understand precisely what has been demonstrated, and precisely what has been demonstrated is that there existed, on this station, until eleven days ago, a phenomenon of the first importance, in two halves, and that the director of the station has managed to let both halves out of the building.

Spilsbury does not say *treason*. There is no treason in it. He says, in the corridor, pleasantly, on his way to the boat: "Bad luck, Tar."

Reggie is written down as incompetent, and it is the only thing in the world he cannot bear, and he pays it, and never mentions it to anyone as long as he lives.

<hr>

He keeps the promise in October, when the new specimen comes.

He does not do it because he promised. He does it because by then there is nothing left to lose by trying it, and because of a question he was asked in July, in the Administrator's office, with the fan going and the shutters half down.

Hester Bevan had the mortality table in front of her. She had asked for it and he had brought it up himself, and she read it the way a person reads a thing they have read already.

"Nine months," she said. "And they are all of them alone in there."

"They are housed singly. It is standard practice with animals of unknown temperament."

"Yes." She put the table down and squared it against the edge of the blotter. "Dr Tar. What happens if you put two of them in together and nobody dies?"

Reggie said that he was not in a position to answer that.

"No," she said. "I don't suppose you are."

That was the whole of it. He went back down to the pens and thought no more about it than a man thinks about a stone in his shoe, which is to say continuously, and without once taking the shoe off.

It works in four days. Feeding, weight, the thing he writes down as activity, which is not activity.

He knows inside a week what he is looking at, and he knows inside a fortnight what it will be used for, and he writes it up anyway and publishes it in 1956, because a true thing that is going to be used badly is still a true thing, and because he has never once in his life been able to sit on a result.

The paper does not say why he tried it.

There is no version of that paper which says why he tried it, and he goes over the discussion section nine times to be sure of it, and what he removes is not a sentence. It is a shape. Anybody with the mortality table and the husbandry paper side by side could get from one to the other in an afternoon, and Reggie writes it so that they will have to do the afternoon themselves.

That is the whole of what he can do for them and he is aware of how little it is.

<hr>

He takes the school in the winter of 1955 because it is what is available.

He has a first, and a doctorate, and eleven years of work he may not describe to anybody, and two referees who cannot be applied to, and a third who can be and would tell the truth. He has no teaching certificate and no intention of getting one. The Ministry is crying out for schoolmasters and every school in England worth the name still wishes to know where a man has been for eleven years.

The school is not worth the name. That is not unkind; it is in the prospectus, more or less. Two hundred and ten boys between eleven and eighteen, a chapel with a leaking roof, fees that a provincial solicitor can manage if he is careful, and a headmaster who is delighted to have letters after a name on his staff list and does not ask a single question about why they are going begging.

There is a senior science master, who is a chemist, and who has the laboratory and the ordering and the timetable and the sixth form, and who is not handing any of the four to a man with a doctorate, no certificate and no hour of a classroom behind him. Biology at the school stops in the fifth form and always has. Nobody regards this as a want. A boy who intends to read medicine goes somewhere else, and there has not been one in nine years.

Reggie takes the lower forms. Biology to the fourth, general science to the second and third, and for the first eighteen months he is, by any measure he would accept, bad at it.

He teaches the way he was taught, which is to state the thing correctly, once, in the right order. It does not work. He can see it not working. There is a particular expression a boy of thirteen produces when he has stopped receiving and has not yet decided to say so, and by the end of his first full term Reggie can identify it at forty feet and cannot do anything whatever about it.

So he does the thing he does, which is to treat it as a problem.

<hr>

The offers come in the spring of 1956, on the strength of the paper, from two institutions and a man in Washington who does not say which department.

By then he has been at the school for four months.

He answers all three. He answers them properly, at length, on good paper, with a fountain pen, sitting at a desk in a room that smells of boys, and he declines all three, and he does not tell anybody he was asked.

He has one reason and he can state it in a form he can live with. He is thirty-nine years old and he has been given, twice, a building with animals in it, and on both occasions there came a morning when a result was available and the cost of it was somebody else, and on both occasions he took the result.

The first time he did not have a choice. He was twenty-four and they put a knife in his hand and it was wartime and someone had to.

For thirteen years he could not get to the bottom of what he would have done if there had been a way out of that room, and told himself, on the few occasions he told himself anything, that a man who was not asked does not get to award himself the answer.

Then he was asked. In daylight, at his own station, with the door open and a colleague shouting at him and nothing on earth compelling him, over eleven days.

So he does know now. He would like it noted that the result is clean, and properly controlled, and that the design was his.

There is an objection to that and he puts it to himself at the desk, in order, at length, because he is not a man who declines to hear an objection.

For ten of the eleven days he did not have the magnitude of it. He had one refusal on the first morning, which he overrode. After that he had a man who went where he was sent, and got nothing whatever out of the second animal, and had to be watched at night in case he went back to the first. It was costing him. Both of them held that it was costing him and that it could be borne a while longer, and one of them was in a position to say so and did not.

Pascual shouted from about the third day. Pascual was shouting about the animal.

On the eleventh day he had the magnitude. He was standing in front of it with nothing in the way, and what he did was suggest dinner, which is not a thing he does.

That evening Pascual took him apart on the verandah in front of everybody. That night the animal went over the wall. By the following morning there was a result on the ground larger than the one he had spent the fortnight trying to obtain, and then the trial stopped itself.

He turns it over for some time and rules that it is not admissible. The question is whether he would take a result when the cost of it was somebody else, and the answer is that he did, for eleven days.

<hr>

There is a third thing available to him at that desk and he does not pick it up.

Weeks after the verandah he built himself, in about four minutes, a way of not knowing where a man had gone; and gave up the only phenomenon of the first importance he will ever have his hands on; and has been paying for it ever since in the one currency he minds.

He does not enter it. It does not occur to him that it could be entered. He has it filed as the bill, and a bill is not evidence about the man who pays it.

<hr>

So he does the only thing available, which is to go on declining, and to remove himself from the class of person to whom a building with animals in it is offered at all.

He would not call any of that a decision about his character. He would call it a fair assessment of a piece of apparatus with a known fault.

<hr>

The problem of the fourth form turns out to be tractable, and the first serious result comes in the summer of 1957, and it is about osmosis.

They can all do osmosis. Every boy in the set gets it right, every time, and Reggie does not believe them, and the reason he does not believe them is that they get it right too fast and in the same words.

So he sets the question again with the diagram drawn the other way up.

Ten of fourteen fail it. Not partially: they produce the reverse of the correct answer, confidently, in his own phrasing, because what they have learned is not the movement of water. What they have learned is the picture, and which arrow he pointed at, and the noise he makes when he is pleased.

He sits in an empty room with ten wrong papers and understands that he has been teaching it wrong for two years and that nobody would ever have found out, including him, if he had not gone looking.

Withdraw the variable and see what stops. It is the only method he has ever had and he has never once used it without something being destroyed by it.

The variable is not the diagram. It takes him a fortnight to get to that and it is not a comfortable fortnight. The variable is the noise he makes when he is pleased.

So he designs it properly, which takes a week, and runs it in the second half of the summer term with potato cylinders and a balance, that being the whole of what the room can offer and enough.

Three beakers -- distilled water, weak brine, syrup -- and fourteen boys, and each of them writes a prediction, in ink, with a reason, before anything goes into anything and before a single cylinder is weighed.

Seven of them write it with Dr Tar at the front of the room.

Seven of them write it with Dr Tar standing out in the corridor with the door shut, which he does for nine minutes and finds a great deal harder than he has any business finding it.

Withdrawal and substitution, negative both ways, and he has the result on the bench by four o'clock.

The seven written with him in the room are right. They are right in the same words and the words are his. The seven written in the corridor are wrong five times out of seven, and no two of them are wrong in the same way, and every one of the five contains a reason that Reggie has never said out loud in his life.

He sits with those five for a long time. They are the only pieces of work anybody in that set has produced all year.

He has never once used the method without something being destroyed by it, and he has just used it, on fourteen boys, in a room he took precisely so as not to be in a position to use it on anything.

Here, nothing is. That is what takes him time to trust -- not the technique, which is his own, but the arithmetic on the other side of it. In every building he has worked in, a result was a thing produced, and it was paid for, and it was paid for by whatever was in the room that could not leave. Here the apparatus goes in one end -- the diagrams, the hours, the ten wrong papers, his own two wasted years -- and what comes out at the other is a boy of fourteen who can do it without him.

Nothing is consumed. He checks this repeatedly for about three years and it continues to hold.

From the Michaelmas term of 1957 his mark-book has a fifth column in it, and what goes in the fifth column is not the boy's answer.

It is what Dr Tar said in the ten minutes before the boy answered.

He rules it himself, in pencil, at the kitchen table, thirty-four pages of it, in the last week of every August, for twenty-five years.

<hr>

Jean Leslie comes in the autumn of 1960 and he is glad to see her, and the gladness is not complicated by anything at all, which surprises him.

He shows her the labs, such as they are -- a bench, a cupboard of jars, a fume cupboard that has not worked since 1951 -- and she is polite about them in a way that is not condescending, and she looks at the boys' drawings on the wall for longer than politeness requires, and says nothing about them, and he understands that they are not good and that she is not going to say so in front of him. He had forgotten she was like that. He had forgotten a number of things she was like.

"Where are you now?" he says.

"Aberdeen. Herring, mostly. The Ministry pays for herring."

"Any good?"

"The herring are excellent. It is the only animal anybody has ever given me enough of." She turns one of the jars round to read the label, and puts it back with the label facing the way it was facing. "There is a paper coming in the spring on the winter shoal. I did every hour of it."

"Whose name is on it?"

"Guess," Jean says.

He does not guess. There was no call to ask in the first place; he had it before he opened his mouth, which is the trouble with him, and he asked anyway, which is the other trouble with him.

"The winter shoal work is yours," he says, "and I should be prepared to say so to anybody who asked me."

"Nobody is going to ask you."

"No," Reggie says. "They are not."

They do not discuss the Martin. He does not raise it and she does not, and by the time they are walking back along the corridor to his room he is aware that she has been waiting all afternoon and that the afternoon is nearly over.

He has had the sentence for six years.

He built it in the first winter, and rebuilt it twice, and had it right before he left the island, and used it once, on Jean Leslie, in a corridor at the Martin, with nobody about, and was told no. He does not intend to do another piece of experimental design on an adult again. It has to be askable of a person who does not want to answer. It has to be answerable in one syllable. It must not contain a name, or a place, or a species, or a date, and it must not oblige the respondent to tell him anything he could subsequently be made to give up.

*Is there any news of our friend.*

He says it in the corridor, with a bell going somewhere and boys coming past, and he does not say which friend, because that is the point of it and always was.

"Yes," Jean says.

And Reggie stands there.

He does not ask where. He wants to ask where the way a man in the desert wants water; he has wanted to ask for six years; there are eleven questions and he has them in order, and he ran that station and built that experiment and read that log, and he is entitled to every one of them.

He does not ask, because he knows exactly what he is.

He is a man who is given three facts and has the fourth by Tuesday. It is not a habit and it is not a discipline and he cannot switch it off; it is the shape of his head, and it has never once failed to operate, and if she tells him a place he will have a district, and if she tells him a district he will have a village, and if she tells him a village he will have the man, and he will not be able to stop himself, and he will then be a person who knows where he is.

And one day, in some room, somebody will ask.

"Good," he says. "Good."

The second one is not for her.

Then he nods, and goes into his room, and shuts the door, and stands just inside it with his hand flat on the desk for rather a long time.

Two syllables, across six years. No. Yes. And because he cannot stop synthesising, not about this, he knows that it must mean that the man was alive, because a junior researcher who dies in the sea is not news, there would be no news about that, not after a no at six months.

And context. Jean enjoyed the afternoon, and at the same time she knew he would ask; he could see all of that in her expression, which has never been much good at hiding anything. So: he has more than two syllables. Spilsbury did not find the man, because she would not have come if that had been the news, or if she had come it would have been to throw it in his face. He remembers that much about her. Therefore the man must have made it out, and the data from the experiment he ran suggest the animal made it too --

This won't do, this won't do at all. After a moment he fetches the bottle of whisky from his cupboard. Three drinks: that blunts the apparatus. Then one more he has to force down: that should disable the apparatus for enough time for him to get hold of it.

The next morning he is wrecked and also realises he has miscalculated; he did not factor in having to teach the whole day, not having had to the other two times he has used this mechanism. He manages to get through it, but it is a near thing a couple of times and he is rather shorter with the boys than he usually is. At least his earlier sets have clearly warned the later ones, which eye him uneasily and give him no trouble at all. He does hear Firkin say to his friends, in a voice that the boy thinks is pitched too quiet for him to hear but is not, that in any other master he'd think it was the drink, but not Tar. Reggie almost snorts and gives it away, and oddly feels a bit better despite himself. And by the end of the school day the apparatus has no energy left to work with, which is useful. 

The day after that he has got hold of himself, at least enough so that he is capable of writing a note to Jean Leslie in which he thanks her for coming and apologises for leaving abruptly and says nothing at all about why, and does not ask any questions. The note does not invite a response, she does not respond, and there's an end of it.

Pyke is one of the quieter boys in the third form and has probably said two words to Reggie over the course of the term. So Reggie is surprised when Pyke stops by his desk after class and says, "Sir, the carol service is tonight."

"I am aware of that, Pyke," Reggie says, in a neutral voice, wondering what the boy will say next.

"I, er. Are you coming?"

"I might," Reggie says, amused. It is the first time a boy has invited him, if it counts as an invitation.

"Ah. Well. I hope you do," Pyke says, reddening, and then all but runs out of the classroom.

He is not himself musical, nor is he especially religious. He has not been before, and was not planning on it this year either, but he looks at the marking and decides it will wait an extra day. He sits in the very back, so that if he decides to leave in the middle it will not be obvious.

He had worried it would be a chore, but halfway through realises he has stopped following the order of service and has not looked at his watch the entire time. Part of it is that the school is better supplied with musicians than its reputation would suggest, which he supposes he should have guessed; the school always has a few of the boys win music scholarships, and the music master is always mentioned in the prospectus. Part of it is that he knows these boys but has not seen them in this context before, so that Key's angelic visage is interestingly at odds with Reggie's experience of him, and Palmer's frowning at the music master exactly what he would have expected.

Pyke himself is the only boy not a sixth former given a solo, and Reggie understands why when he starts to sing. It does not seem possible that such a voice could come out of a third former. He is caught out of time, not able to think or do anything but listen, and he could not have said how long it was, afterwards. He notices then that his hands on the programme are shaking, and stills them.

The next day the thirds come tumbling into his class as they always do. Pyke is surrounded by a few other boys who are nudging him and saying things that make him laugh, and then he sees Reggie and his face goes blank and he drops his eyes.

It does not take any sort of great facility to know what is going on. Reggie waits until a few minutes through the lesson, then calls on Pyke, who answers in a low quiet voice, as usual.

"Really, Pyke," Reggie says, making his voice extremely dry, "you could speak up a little more. I know you can be heard at the back of a chapel sixty feet long with a roof that swallows sound."

He turns away to write on the blackboard so that he can plausibly pretend he does not see the sudden delighted smile on Pyke's face before he says, "Yes, sir," in a voice that is stronger than before.

<hr>

Whitcombe in the lower fifth.

Whitcombe cannot do equations and can do everything else, which is not a shape that occurs naturally. Reggie collects, over five weeks, without asking him anything: that he writes the working left-handed and the answers right-handed; that he can do the same operation aloud, at speed, if nobody is looking at the page; that his prep is immaculate and his class work is not; that he was at a preparatory school in Shropshire where, according to a remark he made once about somebody else, they used to strap you for it.

Three facts and the fourth by Tuesday. And the fourth fact is not about Whitcombe.

There is nothing whatever the matter with Whitcombe. There was a room in Shropshire with a bad instrument in it, and the instrument did what bad instruments do, and the boy has been carrying the result about for five years in the form of a hand that writes and a hand that thinks and no way on earth of getting the two of them into the same operation. The fault is in the apparatus.

He says none of it to Whitcombe.

He cannot. The boy has five years of training which holds that the left hand is a punishable act, and no boy of fifteen is going to test that on a master's word, in front of a room, on the strength of a kindness. Anything said aloud can be quoted; anything quoted has to be defended; and the defending would fall to the boy.

So Reggie puts him on a slate, standing, at the side of the room, where the page is in nobody's line of sight. And on the second occasion he hands him the chalk into the left hand, once, without comment.

And never refers to it. Not that term, not that year, not once in the three years the boy is still in the building.

It is permission issued in a form the recipient cannot be made to have received. Nothing was said. Nothing can be quoted. Nothing has to be admitted by anybody, ever, and if it comes to nothing the boy has lost nothing at all and picks the chalk up in the other hand on Thursday and no one will have seen a thing happen.

It is the corridor sentence built the other way round. Same designer, same specification -- one syllable or none, the respondent discloses nothing, the thing must survive being overheard -- except that this one is for getting something in rather than getting something out.

<hr>

The cost lands where costs land.

A boy of fifteen doing his working left-handed at a slate is positioned not to be seen, but eventually he will be, and other masters have views, and in the Lent term Ockley, who has the mathematics, puts it to him in the common room in front of four people, pleasantly, as a joke about modern methods.

"Tar. I am told you have young Whitcombe standing up at a slate like something out of 1880."

"I have."

"Left hand and all."

"Yes."

"Is it a system?" Ockley says. "Are we to have systems?" One of the others laughs, because it is pleasant and it is meant to be laughed at. "In my day a boy wrote with the hand he was given and got on with it."

"He gets them right," Reggie says. "I am not going to argue about the hand."

"I am not asking you to argue about the hand. I am asking what it is in aid of."

"He gets them right."

Ockley waits for the rest of it. There is no rest of it. After a moment he says that he supposes it takes all sorts, and that the fire is very good this evening, and somebody agrees with him about the fire.

That is the whole of it. Not a word about Shropshire. No mention of a strap. No case entered on the boy's behalf, because a case entered on a boy's behalf is a case that can be repeated, and repeated in front of him, and there is no version of that which does not end with Whitcombe discovering that a master worked it out.

He says it again in the summer, when it has stopped being a joke.

It holds, and the reason it holds is that there is nothing about him anybody in that room wants to open. They had his school and his voice and his college inside a fortnight, and the letters after his name are on the staff list, and not one of them has ever gone on from there to the eleven years, because a man that easy to place is taken to have been placed. It is the only capital he has, and he does not have to spend it, only to let it go on being there.

What he is written down as, in that room, is a man with a theory about hands.

He does not correct it. He cannot correct it, because correcting it requires saying why, and he is still down as it when he retires. It is the second time in his life he has been recorded as something he is not and has paid it without a word. And this one bought something worth buying that he can see.

By Easter it has gone away entirely. Whitcombe takes the mathematics prize in the sixth and leaves the school in 1965 believing that he grew out of it.

There is an entry in the fifth column of a mark-book for the Lent term of 1961 which reads, in full: *chalk.*

<hr>

The senior science master retires in the summer of 1962, having been at the school since 1934. The present is a decanter and the speech runs nine minutes and mentions nothing whatever that happened in the laboratory.

Reggie gets the laboratory. He also gets the ordering, and the timetable, and the sixth form, which in the case of biology does not exist and never has. He keeps the second and third forms as well, because at a school of that size everybody teaches everything, and he would have kept them in any case.

He starts it in the September with five boys, four of whom want medicine and one of whom does not want anything in particular and is the best of them.

The syllabus is the one he was taught himself and it has not moved since: the types, in order, on the board and under the knife -- amoeba, hydra, the earthworm, the crayfish, the dogfish, the frog, the rabbit -- and the plant types, and Mendel, and a physiology paper, and a practical in which the dissection is examined. There is nothing in it later than about 1910. He does not mind.

Every Lent term he stands over a dogfish with five boys round it and puts the point of the seeker on the snout, where the pores are: small, jelly-filled, arranged in fields, running back under the skin to the ampullae. They are asked to draw them. They are told that the function is not established. The ones who draw the field as it lies, and not as the plate has it, are the ones he can do anything with afterwards; and a boy who can write *not established* under his own drawing, and not mind it, has learnt something that is not on any paper he will ever sit. A boy who can take a dogfish apart properly can be taught anything at all afterwards.

By 1966 the school has sent four boys to medical schools and one to read Natural Sciences at Cambridge, which is four and one more than in the whole of its existence, and it goes into the prospectus.

<hr>

The osmoregulation comes in the Michaelmas term of 1963 and he has seen it coming since about the June.

The marine elasmobranch holds urea and sits level with the sea and spends nothing. The freshwater teleost bails, continuously, against the gradient, for the whole of its life. It is the potato cylinders with a bill attached; and the five boys in front of him had the potato cylinders from him in 1959, at fourteen, and there is an entry against each of their names in a fifth column ruled at a kitchen table that August which says what he supplied them at the time.

So he gives them the marine case and asks them for the freshwater one, in ink, with a reason, before anything is opened.

He writes down first, in order, why it is not an experiment, because a result a man has already decided to want is not a result. Five boys. No control, since he cannot withhold the marine case from two of them and would not. Four years in between, and three other masters, and adolescence. And no arm without him in it, because he taught all five of them at twelve and at thirteen and at fourteen, and he has had a hand in every one of them, in a proportion he cannot state.

Three of the five get it. Two do not.

The two are not the two he had predicted.

He has the 1959 entries beside him and they are accurate -- he can still hear himself saying the sentences -- and they do not predict this, and they do not predict the reverse of it either. They predict nothing. What the fifth column has in it is what left the front of the room. Whether any of it arrived is a different quantity, and he has not measured it once, in six years, in thirty-four pages a year, in the last week of every August.

He could build the other instrument. He has the design inside four minutes, without wanting it: two intakes matched on the entrance papers, one taught by Dr Tar at twelve and thirteen and fourteen and one not, the same question in ink four years later, and the difference between the arms is what arrived.

It would answer the question. He does not run it.

He writes down why, in order, and the reasons are sound. Two matched intakes cannot be got at a school of two hundred and ten. Four clear years cannot be got with three other masters in the middle of them. A man cannot withhold himself from twenty-eight boys and go on being the biology master. He checks each of them twice and they hold.

He cannot tell whether they are the reason. A man who would not have run it and a man who could not have run it leave the same empty page.

He rules the column again in the August of 1964. The heading is the same heading.

<hr>

Reggie is not a master that is loved, as some of the others are. He prefers it that way. He is not the master that they are chummy with, or come to with confidences, and he would be quite uncomfortable if they did.

There is a certain type of boy whom he gets along with rather well, one that thrives on learning, and who is self-confident enough to enjoy a dry sense of humour and the possibility of being incorrect.

There is another type of boy who is intimidated by him. He notices this and understands it is a problem.

He cannot solve it, not entirely. But he gathers data, as he does. Longitudinal observation only, but the correlation is clear enough that it does not matter. A patient master working with a boy on his own is not necessarily profitable, but an impatient one working with a boy on his own almost never works, and on the rare occasion it does work, the boy does not seek out help again, which comes out to much the same thing. So he decides on patience as a tactic without running a clean experiment on it, for here he sees what would be consumed by the experiment.

At the end of one somewhat long session with Jaynes of the lower sixth, Jaynes finally gets the expression on his face that makes Reggie relax: the boy has understood the calculation.

"Thank you, sir," he says, sounding relieved. "Croft was right."

"Croft?"

"He said you were much nicer by yourself -- oh -- er, no offence meant, sir --"

"No offence taken, Jaynes," Reggie says gravely. "But haven't you got nets right now?"

Jaynes scrambles up with the air of a boy who realises that the games master may not be entirely happy with him, and with a flurry of limbs is out of the door, with another hasty thank-you appended.

Reggie's lips twitch, and he gathers up the scrap paper and goes to the staff meeting that he himself is late for.

<hr>

Reggie has one rule about the apparatus and for fourteen years it is absolute.

He does the arithmetic on boys. He does not do it on anybody else, on any subject, and when a fact arrives about an adult he puts it down where it lands and walks away from it, and he has become rather good at that too, and acquired the skill the way other men acquire a limp.

But on a fifteen-year-old he is permitted, and he is very fast, and it has taken him until his forties to discover that the fault in the apparatus does not signify in a room where the thing being assembled is a boy who is going to walk out of it afterwards.

<hr>

The Colonel writes once, in 1967, three lines and a black border, to say that his wife has died in March and that he thought Dr Tar would wish to be informed. There is nothing else in it. No account, no invitation, no sentiment. It is entirely correct and Reggie recognises it as the work of a fair man doing a thing he considers owed.

He writes back three lines, which is the right length.

He does wonder, for about a week, why she wanted them housed together, and why she was so particular about it, and why a woman who never once came down to the pens had asked him a question in July 1954 with the answer already in her face.

Then he puts it down. He is not permitted that one.

<hr>

The Havill business begins in the common room, which is where Reggie hears everything and says nothing.

Havill is twenty-six and in his second year and teaches English, and the lower fourth have him. Not just for a bad period or a bad week. They have him, the way a form of thirty gets hold of a man and does not put him down again, and it has been going on since about the second week of the summer term.

The account of it is unanimous. He is soft. He wants to be liked. He came in wanting to be liked and they smelt it on him in a fortnight, and there is no cure for it, and the kindest thing is a quiet word in the spring.

Reggie does not believe it, and the reason he does not believe it is that it is unanimous and it is in the same words.

There is also the fact that the man is not otherwise incompetent, which nobody has entered anywhere. He gets work out of Bell, who has produced nothing for anybody in three years. He has been cataloguing the library since 1967, unpaid, in his own time, and is a third of the way through what was previously a cupboard with a rumour attached. Neither of those is examined and neither is counted.

The headmaster asks him for a view in the second week of November, in the passage, pleasantly, as a man clearing a small item.

"A word about young Havill, when you have a moment."

"I have one now."

"Then: everybody in that common room has given me a view. There are eleven of them and it is the same view eleven times." He is looking at the noticeboard while he says it. "You have not given me one."

"No."

"You have been in that room since 1956 and I have never yet known you not to have one." He straightens a drawing pin that does not require straightening. "Tuesday, perhaps. After four."

<hr>

In 1962 he would have done it and told nobody.

He has the timetable. He could put Havill's lower fourth in another room from the January and never mention it, and if the thing came right nobody would know a variable had been moved, and if it did not come right nobody would know that either.

The trouble is that it would be a guess. A guess moved in a timetable is not a result, and if he is wrong the man goes in the spring having never been told there was a question.

So on the Tuesday he goes to the headmaster and takes himself out of it.

"I cannot give you a view on Havill."

"Cannot."

"I believe the fault may not be in the man. I have not established it. A master should not be reported on by somebody who is halfway through finding something out about him."

"And when you have established it?"

"It will not be going in either. Either way. Whatever comes of it."

The headmaster does not answer at once. He is a classicist and a decent one and has never in his life read a scientific paper, and Reggie has therefore never regarded him as material.

"You are taking your own hands off the scales," he says.

"Yes."

"Well." He turns a sheet over on the desk, and turns it back. "Then I shall have to settle it myself in the spring, shan't I. I hope you find whatever it is you are looking for."

Reggie puts it down to the theory about hands. It does not occur to him that afternoon, nor that year, nor at any point in the thirteen years that are left to him in the building, that he has been given until the spring on purpose.

It costs him the only lever he would have had. It is done by four o'clock and he goes back and teaches the third form the earthworm.

<hr>

Then he asks Havill, on paper, because paper can be refused without a man having to say the word.

Two sides. What he thinks is happening: that the collapse is in Room 4 and not in the boys, Room 4 being the one that can be heard from the study passage, and Havill being audible in it and knowing that he is audible. What would be required to establish it. Four weeks, two rooms, two forms crossed, the bad arm first, because a bad arm is worth nothing second. And the prediction, in ink, dated and signed at the foot, handed over for Havill to keep.

That is the part he does not enjoy. He has never once run anything in which being wrong was recoverable by anybody but himself.

Havill reads it twice, standing up, in the laboratory, on the Thursday.

"Four weeks," he says.

"Four is the minimum I can defend."

"Four weeks of going on as I am."

"You would not be worse. You would be precisely what you are now. That's the whole of the point."

"Yes," Havill says. "I follow the point." He puts the sheets down on the bench and squares them, which is a thing he does. "And in four weeks' time, when you move me, what do the boys have?"

"Their timetable altered. They will not notice it."

"They'll have had four more weeks of it. It doesn't come off them in the holidays, you know. They've had two terms of a man they can do as they like with, and you're proposing to give them a third, on purpose, and write it down."

"It is a fortnight either side of Christmas and boys forget --"

"Do they?" Havill says. "You've had thirteen-year-olds since 1955. Do they forget?"

Reggie does not answer immediately, which is itself an answer.

"No," he says.

"Then no."

Reggie begins to put the case again, because the case is sound and he can state it in four sentences, and he gets about half a sentence into it and stops.

He does not finish it and does not offer a reason for not finishing it, and Havill has the manners not to ask.

"You may have the other thing," Havill says. "Move me now, if you like. I'll go wherever you put me. But you won't know whether it was the room, will you. It might be the room. It might be that I shall walk in there on the first day knowing that one man in this school sat down and thought about it for a fortnight instead of settling it in the common room. You'll never separate them."

"No."

"Can you bear that?"

"No," Reggie says.

"Well," says Havill.

<hr>

He moves him immediately. Room 4 goes to the fifth and sixth, who cannot be got at by being overheard, and Havill goes to Room 9, and nothing else in the building alters and nobody in the common room remarks on anything at all.

By half term the lower fourth do not have him. Conclusively.

Reggie has nothing whatever. No control. No negative arm. One man, one room, one term, and the subject informed and altered by the informing, and no way on earth to say which of the two things did it, or whether either did, or whether the boys simply tired of it in the ordinary course.

He hates it, flatly and durably, which is how he does everything. He is fifty-two and he has spent his working life on the proposition that a result you cannot separate is not a result, and he still holds it, and it is still true, and he has run this one anyway and would run it again.

The headmaster keeps Havill in the spring. The reason he gives, to anybody who asks him for one, is that the young man found his feet.

At the kitchen table in the August, in the back of the book, where it does not belong, Reggie writes: *No control. Negative arm declined by the subject, with reason. The reason was sound and I could not answer it. Result uninterpretable and not offered as one.*

Havill finishes the catalogue in 1974 and is still in post when Reggie retires.

<hr>

Metcalfe, in the upper sixth, in the Michaelmas term of 1971, stops sailing.

That is all. He hands in his prep on time and it is shorter. He has moved to the back of the room, which for that boy is not laziness but distance. He drops the Wednesday set he has been in for two years and gives a reason, and the reason is a good one, and Reggie does not believe a word of it.

He does not ask him. There is no version of asking a boy of seventeen a direct question that produces anything but a shrug and eight further weeks of silence, which is a fact about the material and not a complaint about it.

What he does instead is give him a job. The beginners' group on Saturdays is a nuisance and needs a second man on the bank and there is nobody.

"Metcalfe. You sail."

"Sir."

"There are twelve beginners and one whistle, and I cannot be in two places at once." It is a lie. Reggie learned the trick of it his second year and has been in two places at once for fifteen years. "Saturdays, from two."

Metcalfe says that he supposes he could, sir, if it is really wanted, in the voice of a boy who has been handed something and does not yet know that he has.

"You would be doing me a considerable favour," Reggie says, letting himself sound just a bit relieved.

Metcalfe does it for two terms, badly at first and then not badly, and shouts at twelve-year-olds in a capsized dinghy and comes up the bank afterwards wet through and complaining, and by the spring he is sailing again himself and has never once been asked anything. By then Reggie knows about the father, but it makes no difference.

That is the whole of it, and it is the best work he has ever done, and there is no paper in it and never will be.

It is on the bank of a reservoir in the March of 1972, with a whistle round his neck and a boy in the water swearing at another boy in the water, that Reggie notices he has been thinking of him as Luke for about a year.

He does not make anything of this. He puts the whistle in his mouth.

<hr>

He does not intend to tell anyone, but the headmaster brings it up. "Metcalfe. I'm pleased he's doing better," the headmaster says, at their monthly meeting in the headmaster's rooms.

He pours Reggie a measure of whisky. Reggie takes a sip for politeness; it is very good, but he is not fond of whisky. "Yes," Reggie says.

The headmaster nods. "I was concerned. You heard about his father, I presume?"

"Eventually."

The headmaster is quiet for a minute, then smiles. "Well. It was well done." He swirls the whisky around in his tumbler and says musingly, "You're fond of the boys, aren't you, Tar?"

Reggie is appalled. "Absolutely not."

The headmaster laughs, and Reggie gets the distinct impression that he thinks Reggie is joking. He is not. Two hundred and ten boys are noisy and smelly and involve rather a lot of wet clothing.

It is only that they will not stay in the aggregate.

<hr>

The offers from schools had begun in 1966, and they do not stop.

A minor public school in Dorset, then a rather good one in Yorkshire, then, in 1969, a very good one indeed, with a laboratory built in 1961 and a headmaster who takes him out to lunch and tells him with some warmth that he is wasted.

Reggie declines all of them, politely, and the reason he gives to them is his commitments here, and the reason he gives to himself is that a name at a school of that kind gets into print. It goes in *The Times* under appointments. It goes into the Old Boys' letter and the Head's annual address and the obituary of somebody else entirely. There are perhaps four men living who would notice it, and one of them has a desk in a building in London and an excellent memory and no reason at all to have stopped wondering.

He is not hiding. He has never hidden. He is simply not available to be read, and it is restful, and after about 1965 he stops giving himself the operational reason and starts admitting the other one, which is that he does not want to go.

He does not have a word for what the school is. If he were required to write it down he would write that the place is unusually well run, which is not what he means and is the nearest he can get to it.

He knows the names of the groundsman's three children and the order they came in. He goes to the carol service every year. At the summer thing in 1974 the bursar's wife puts a plate into his hands and talks to him for forty minutes about her greenhouse, and he answers her questions about tomato blight, which he happens to know a great deal about, and afterwards he walks home and notices that he did not mind any of it and in fact would do it again.

<hr>

On the speech day of 1976 a father in the marquee looks at the prize card in his son's hand and says, quite loudly: "Good Lord. *Tar?* Is that R. Tar? How on earth did you get Reggie Tar to teach for you?"

The headmaster -- the third one Reggie has served under -- has plainly no idea what the man is talking about.

But he has been asked a question, and he is a courteous man, and he answers it.

"Oh, yes," he says. "He's the best teacher we've got."

Reggie is thirty feet away with a jug of orange squash in each hand, going somewhere with them, and he hears both halves.

He takes the squash where it is going.

<hr>

A few of his sixth formers write to him, after they leave. Pyke sends him Christmas cards with a short awkward scrawl -- the boy never did learn to write legibly -- about his work; after reading zoology he is now a tenor of some standing. Reggie can follow perhaps half of what he writes about music and enjoys it a good deal. Rossiter writes long but infrequent missives filled with enthusiasm for the research he is doing, and Reggie cannot help but smile when he opens one of them up and sees the runaway nested parentheses.

He writes them all back, even the dull ones, closely spaced pages in blue ink, and he keeps all the letters in a box in his office.

<hr>

He retires in 1982, at sixty-five, and stays on for the Saturdays, because the sailing club is not the sort of thing you hand over.

He is on the bank with a whistle. He does not go in. He has not been in the water since the war, and nobody at that school has ever once thought to ask a biology master why.

<hr>

The letter comes in October 1986 and is addressed to *Dr R. Tar*, care of the school, and has been forwarded twice.

It is from a doctoral student at St Augustine, in Trinidad, who apologises for writing out of the blue, and who has been sent to Icacos to look for baseline catch data on something else entirely, and who has been handed a cardboard box by a woman in the village.

Nine notebooks, ruled in four columns. And under the notebooks, fourteen ledgers, and under the ledgers some other things which the student does not describe and has no questions about.

The student encloses two photostats and a list of questions. He is writing to Reggie because the only published work on the acoustic tagging is Tar 1953, and because he cannot find anybody else alive who has heard the tapes.

Reggie takes the letter into the empty biology room, because the common room has people in it, and sits down at the bench.

Item four in the second notebook is a hard descending buzz with a click at the front of it.

He can hear it. He can hear it in the room. He put his finger on the reel in a hut on Crabbs Cay in 1954 and said *there, that's the interesting one*, and he was right, and it has taken thirty-two years and another man's whole life to find out what it meant.

But he does not spend very long on the acoustics, which is not what he would have predicted of himself.

What he does is read the notebooks the way he has read nine hundred boys.

The hand: block capitals throughout, which is a decision, and a decision taken by somebody who expected to have to read it back years later. The capitals are the same size for twenty-eight years and then in the eighth notebook they get larger and the pencil goes through the paper in three places, and there is a gap of ten weeks, and afterwards the entries resume in a smaller hand and never recover the old size, and from that page onward there is one of them for every single day. Reggie can date the bad years off the photostat without being told a thing.

He reads a man in a bed.

It is a complete and coherent reading and he has it in under a minute: a clerk in a room behind a shop in the summer of 1983, too ill for ten weeks to sit up to a table, and then well enough, and then dead in the March. He is right about the illness. He is right about the animal, and right about what a daily interval means after twenty-eight years of nine days and nineteen.

He is wrong by two hundred yards, and there is nobody who is going to tell him, because the only two people alive who could are a woman in a village who has never heard his name and a woman in Scotland who does not know that he has been written to.

And the columns.

*What came. Circumstance. What followed. What I supplied.*

He had that problem in 1954. He had exactly that problem, from the necropsy onward, for fourteen weeks, and he never solved it, and in the end he solved it the only way he could think of, which was to take the man out and put a stranger in and see what stopped.

He has been solving it every term since 1957.

It is the whole of teaching. It is the only real question in the trade: whether the boy has understood the thing, or whether he has learned the diagram and the arrow and the noise you make when you are pleased. There is no instrument for it. You build one every time, out of nothing, and set the diagram the other way up, and find out that you have been the source of the answer all along.

Charles built one and ruled it into every page for twenty-nine years.

The first notebook is dated June 1955. Reggie ruled his fifth column in the September of 1957.

He was second by two years and alone in nothing, and he gets that off the front of the photostat without wanting it, and it takes him no time at all, and he has no intention of putting any of it in the letter.

And Reggie, who has spent three decades working out what is the matter with a boy from what the boy has stopped doing, sits at a bench in an empty classroom with two photostats and gets the fourth fact by about half past four.

The column is not scruple.

A man does not rule a column for *what I supplied* on every page of nine notebooks for twenty-nine years because he is being rigorous. He does it because he is checking. Every day, at a table, alone, with no colleague and no library and no instrument and nobody in the world to ask -- checking whether he had made the whole of it up.

Charles never once let himself be answered.

Reggie sits with that for some while. It is the second piece of arithmetic he has done on an adult since 1954. The first was in 1969, and the subject had the design in his hand and could stop it, and did. This one is done on a man three years dead, and he does it now because there is nobody left it can be used against, which he is aware is the arrangement he made for himself and about which he has no complaint to enter.

Then he takes a sheet of school paper and answers the questions.

He answers all of them, in order, accurately, over five pages, and confirms item four from the tape, and gives the reel number and the date and the fact that the animal it was recorded from was designated Beta and was male, and was called a mermaid on the bracket card and in every minute of every meeting for nine months, which was nobody's fault in particular and was wrong.

And at the end, because the student has asked whether the four-column method has a name in the literature, he writes the last of perhaps four thousand references he has produced in his working life, and he knows the form of them better than he knows his own signature.

*It has no name. It ought to have one. It was devised by C. Cholmondeley, who worked under me at the William Martin Institute for Marine Studies in 1954 and who was the finest field observer I have ever had, and you may quote me to that effect in print.*

He signs it, and seals it, and puts it in his pocket for the post.

Then he goes down to the lower fourth, who are not his any more and who have him on Thursdays regardless, and who are waiting.
