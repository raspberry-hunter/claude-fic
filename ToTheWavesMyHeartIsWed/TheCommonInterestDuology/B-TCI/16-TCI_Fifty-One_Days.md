# Fifty-One Days

Kingston in November is hotter than the cay and has no sea in it, which Pascual had not expected to mind and minds a great deal.

They are a week early, because the Colonel will not travel to anything at the last minute, and for the first three days he will not let Pascual open the trunk. They have two rooms at the Blue Mountain, which is a temperance hotel with brown walls and a fan that goes round at about the speed of a man thinking, and the trunk is under Pascual's bed because the Colonel said so and because neither of them has any intention of arguing about it.

On the seventeenth he unpacks the four box files and lays the whole of the bundle out on the floor of a hotel room in the order Hester indexed it, and goes through it on his knees for six hours.

On the eighteenth he does it again.

On the nineteenth the Colonel takes him out and makes him eat a large lunch and does not mention the hearing once, and talks instead about a horse he owned in 1934, and Pascual understands about halfway through that he is being handled by a professional and lets it happen.

---

The package comes down on the Monday on Mr Alleyne's boat, which runs to Kingston for ice on Mondays and Thursdays and which Hester has evidently talked into carrying something else, and it is at the desk when they get back at four.

It is a fibre case with a rope round it and a covering letter in Hester Bevan's hand, and the letter is two pages, and the first page is entirely about how it is to be entered.

The second page begins: *Now the rest of it.*

He reads it standing at the desk and then sits down in a chair in the lobby of a temperance hotel and reads it again.

Accession number one hundred and ninety-seven. Male, four years, six feet two. Removed by Dr A. Spilsbury from the applicant's holding pen at four o'clock on the morning of the eleventh of February 1956 and kept by him for fifty-one days in a stone fish pound at a place he declines to name. Daily record: feed offered, feed taken, weights, activity. Refused cut fish from the first day. Nineteen days of adequate intake on live fish. Then nothing, for thirty-one days, on the bottom at the seaward end with his face against the stone. Dead the second of April. Post-mortem by Spilsbury over three days: nothing whatever.

One of a pair.

*He knew the animal was one of a pair,* writes Hester. *He knew what solitude does to them; he tells me he was told it at our own rail in May 1954, by you. He kept the animal alone regardless, in the belief that decline was a matter of feeding and that he was a better feeder than the men in Nassau, and the record is the record of a man trying to prove that and failing. He did not have the bond until the fourth of July, when Reginald said it on the verandah. I am satisfied that this is true, and I have satisfied myself in the way you would expect.*

*By you.*

Pascual sits in a lobby chair and remembers a rail, and a calf butting an old female in the shoulder, and his own voice, louder than he meant it: *It is not pneumonia. It is solitude.* He said it to that man's face to get rid of him, and every word of it was true, and the man wrote it down and went and took an animal anyway.

---

He goes up to the room and puts the case on the bed and does not open it for about an hour.

Because he has understood something in the lobby which has not yet been said out loud by anybody, including Hester Bevan, who will have understood it on the fifteenth and left it off the page on purpose.

The record in that case is the only proof in the world that Reggie is right.

And the man who produced it is finished the moment it goes in, and will be finished by Pascual Pobil, in a room, on a Tuesday, in front of the Colonial Secretary's office and a consignment agent from Bay Street.

He sits on the end of a hotel bed with his hands between his knees.

---

There is nobody to ask.

That is the whole shape of it and he sees it perfectly clearly at about six o'clock: the Colonel is next door and would give him a straight answer in four seconds and it would be *use it*; Hester has already given hers by sending the case; Jean would say *use it* and be right; and the one man whose answer he wants is four hundred miles away and would say *use it* faster than any of them and would mean it.

And in September he had a thing in a drawer and nobody to ask, and he made a decision alone, and it was good reasoning, and it cost this Institute the licence.

He is not going to do that again. He is also going to use the record. Both of those are true at once and he sits with them for a while and does not find that they cancel.

Then he works out what the difference is, and it takes him about nine seconds, and it is not complicated at all.

*The difference is that he tells him first.*

---

The cable office on East Street shuts at seven and he gets there at twenty to.

He writes it out four times on the counter form and the fourth one is nine words and costs him rather more than he expected, and he pays it and does not ask for the change.

> SPILSBURY MARTIN INSTITUTE
> PRODUCING 197 RECORD AT HEARING TUESDAY ELEVEN OCLOCK
> POBIL

He is aware, walking back up East Street in the dark, that it is not consent, that it cannot be answered before eleven o'clock tomorrow, and that there is no possible arrangement by which the man could stop him.

He is also aware that this is the first time in six months that he has told anybody anything before he did it, and that he has done it for a man he swore at on a wet concrete deck in 1954 and has not forgiven and does not intend to, and he walks the last two hundred yards rather fast.

---

He does not sleep.

At about two he puts the light on and reads Hester's second page again, and then the daily record, all fifty-one lines of it, twice. At about half past three he finds himself doing a thing he has not done since he was a student, which is to write the argument out longhand on hotel notepaper, in order, so that he can look at it.

It comes out at nine lines and the ninth is the only one that matters.

*Every line of it was written by a man who expected the animal to live.*

Then, because he is forty-three years old and alone in a brown room in Kingston at twenty to four in the morning, he writes one more line underneath, which has nothing whatever to do with the hearing.

*Tell him all of it. In order. Nothing left out. Do not wait to be asked.*

He looks at that for some while.

Then he tears the bottom off the sheet and burns it in the ashtray, because he has learned a great deal this year about what becomes of pieces of paper, and goes back to bed and lies there until six.

---

The hearing is in a room on the first floor of the Secretariat with a long table and six chairs and a window that does not open.

Mr Averill is the Assistant Secretary. He is about fifty, dry, and has read the submission; Pascual can tell within four minutes because he asks a question about the salinity ranges that nobody asks who has not read the appendix. There is a Mr Hoyte from the Department of Agriculture who says perhaps nine words all morning and misses nothing.

And there is Mr Lecky, for the applicant, in a linen suit, who stands up when they come in and says he is delighted.

They offer tea at five past eleven.

Pascual takes the tea.

---

Mr Lecky is extremely good.

That is the thing Pascual will want to say to them all afterwards, and will say, because it is the useful part: he is not oily and he is not a fool and he does not overreach once in ninety minutes. He has a client with a station, a cold store, three qualified men and a veterinary surgeon on call from Nassau. He has the Institute's own published paper, from which he quotes section four accurately. He is warm about the Martin. He says twice, and means it as a kindness, that nobody doubts the Institute's devotion to these animals, and that devotion is not the same as capacity.

At twenty past twelve he says: "Four scientists, sir. One generator. And a licence written for a station that had one animal."

And Pascual, sitting with a cold cup of tea, thinks: *he is going to win this, and he ought to.*

---

Mr Averill turns to him at half past twelve.

"Dr Pobil. You are the veterinary officer."

"I am."

"You wrote to this office in September."

"I did."

"Warning against a procedure."

"Yes."

"Was that procedure proposed by anybody at the William Martin Institute?"

Nineteen days in a hard chair in Hester Bevan's sitting room.

"It was raised," says Pascual, "by a visiting scientist, in a meeting, on the thirty-first of May. It was refused by the head of the station in a second and a half."

"And subsequently?"

"Raised again and refused again on the fourth of July. And refused a third time in writing, in a document which is item forty-one in the bundle in front of you."

"Item forty-one is a protocol."

"Item forty-one is fourteen sheets of a protocol of which the last two pages are headed *Grounds upon which the study cannot be performed*, and are initialled, and are in the hand of the man who would have had to do it."

---

"And your letter?"

"I wrote it. I'd do it again."

"Why did you write it?"

"Because a veterinary opinion attaches to the animal and not to the address, and I wanted it to travel with them." He puts the cup down. "I did not show it to the head of my station first. I was wrong about that, it cost this Institute its licence, and I've had two months to think about it."

Then he stops talking.

The fan goes round. Mr Hoyte writes something. Mr Averill waits, in the way men in that chair wait, for a further twelve or fourteen seconds, and Pascual sits in a hotel-brown room in Kingston with his hands on his knees and does not say one more word, and it is the hardest thing he has done since the twelfth of September.

"Thank you," says Mr Averill, at last, and moves his pencil, and Pascual understands that he has just been given something.

---

He produces the record at ten past one.

He does it exactly as Hester's first page instructed: hands it to the clerk, not to Averill; states what it is in one sentence; gives the provenance in the second; and then gives the number.

"Accession one hundred and ninety-seven, in the applicant's own ledger."

Mr Lecky's face does not do anything at all.

"Male, four years old. Taken out of a pen containing two animals on the eleventh of February this year, and held on his own for fifty-one days."

"With what record?"

"A daily record of feed offered, feed taken and weight, kept by hand." He does not look at Mr Lecky. "He refused cut fish from the first day. He fed adequately on live fish for nineteen days."

"And then?"

"On the twentieth day he stopped. He did not eat again. On the fifty-first day he died in good bodily condition with nothing whatever on the post-mortem."

"And who kept the record?" says Mr Averill.

"Dr Alec Spilsbury."

"Who is?"

"The man who took the animal," says Pascual.

---

Mr Lecky is on it in four seconds and he does it beautifully.

He does not raise his voice. He observes that the Institute has handed in a document compiled by a man who removed a specimen from his client's establishment without leave; who concealed its existence for six months; who kept the animal in conditions he will not disclose, in a place he will not name; and who allowed it to starve.

"He did not allow it to starve," says Pascual.

"The animal took no food for thirty-one days."

"The animal was offered food every day for thirty-one days and the weights are on the page." He keeps his voice exactly where he put it. "You may call him what you like, Mr Lecky, and a good deal of it will stick. I'm not here to hold him up."

"Then what are you here for?"

"For the paper."

---

"The paper is his."

"The paper is fifty-one lines in a man's own hand, dated daily, by somebody who thought he knew what he was recording." Pascual puts his palm flat on the table, which he had promised Hester he would not do. "He took that animal to prove it could be kept on its own, if the feeding was right."

"That is an admission."

"It is. It's also the only reason the book's worth anything. He wasn't keeping it for us. He was keeping it to prove himself right, and he kept it straight, because he keeps books, and the book says he was wrong."

"That is an argument about his character."

"No. It's an argument about what he was expecting when he wrote it." He takes his hand off the table. "The feed's taken for nineteen days. The weights hold. The husbandry doesn't fail once, and on the twentieth day the animal stops anyway."

"And?"

"And the hypothesis it supports wasn't stated by anybody in the world until the fourth of July. The last entry is the second of April. He couldn't have written it to fit ours, and it doesn't fit his."

The room is quiet.

Mr Hoyte, from the Department of Agriculture, says his ninth word of the morning.

"Yes," he says.

---

They come out at twenty past two.

Mr Averill will not decide today. He reserves; he says the objection raises a question of welfare that has not previously been before this office in any form; he says the inspector will go to the Martin in the new year; and he says that pending that inspection the existing licence will be extended, on its existing terms, to the thirty-first of March.

Pascual is on the steps of the Secretariat before he understands what has been said.

"That's four months."

"That is four months," says the Colonel. "And an inspection, and a man in an office who has written the word *welfare* in a minute where it has never been written before."

"It isn't a win."

"It is not a win. It is the first thing that has gone our way since the twenty-sixth of April." He puts his hat on.

"Sir —"

"And they are not going in a lighter in January, which my wife told six people in August was certain. I intend to say so to her myself, and I should be obliged if you would let me."

---

Mr Lecky catches them on the steps and it takes about ninety seconds and there is nothing in it at all.

"Dr Pobil."

"Mr Lecky."

"That was extremely well done, and I am not being pleasant about it." He has his hat in his hand and the sun full on him. "The point about the dates was the point. I did not have it, I ought to have had it, and I shall have it in March."

"There's going to be a March."

"There is always a March." He smiles, and it is a perfectly good smile. "May I say one thing that is not business?"

"You may say it."

"Your Dr Spilsbury was with my client's establishment four months and was thought a very great deal of." Lecky puts the hat on. "There is nobody there who wishes him ill. I mention it because I understood from that room this morning that you may have supposed otherwise, and because in my experience the people one is afraid of are hardly ever the ones doing the thing."

"Good afternoon, Mr Lecky," says the Colonel.

"Colonel."

And he goes down the steps into the sun and gets into a car.

---

Pascual stands on the pavement and works at it for the whole of the walk back to the hotel, and gets nowhere.

It was a courtesy. It was a warning about somebody at the Martin. It was a piece of ordinary professional decency about a colleague. It was an invitation to look at Dr Spilsbury again. It was all four, and there is not one sentence in it that anybody could put in front of anybody, and he cannot tell which.

He is about two hundred yards up the road before he understands what has just happened to him.

*This is it,* he thinks. *This is what Reggie has been standing in since 1943.*

Four readings of a thing and no way on earth to choose between them. Four times a year, in rooms, for thirteen years. And Pascual Pobil has sat across a supper table and watched a man fail to pick one of them, and has thought, more than once, that he was being slow about it.

He stops in the street.

Then he walks on rather faster, because there is a cable office on East Street and he is not going to be able to send what he wants to send from it.

---

There is a cable waiting at the Blue Mountain.

It was handed in at the Martin at twenty to nine that morning, which means that a man walked down to the harbour office before breakfast to send it, and it is four words, and Pascual reads it standing in the lobby with his hat still on.

> USE IT ALL STOP
> SPILSBURY

He looks at it for some time.

Then he folds it along the crease and puts it in his breast pocket, where it stays for the whole of the voyage home, and he is aware of exactly what that is and does not do anything about it.

---

Alleyne's boat goes back on the Thursday, the twenty-second, and it is ten hours to the cay.

He sits in the stern with the trunk between his knees and does not sleep and does not read, and the Colonel, who has been a soldier for forty years and knows precisely when to leave a man alone, sleeps like a stone from Port Royal to the last hour.

Pascual does what he does, which is to take it in order.

Four months. An inspection in the new year. A word in a minute book. A dead animal in a fish pound with fifty-one lines under his name. A man on a slipway who sent four words at twenty to nine in the morning.

And a promise made on a cot on the twelfth of November, about three days and nowhere either of them has to be, which he asked for properly and which has been sitting in him ever since like a stone in a shoe.

At about four in the afternoon the cay comes up out of the haze, and there is a figure at the end of the jetty who has plainly been there for some while.
