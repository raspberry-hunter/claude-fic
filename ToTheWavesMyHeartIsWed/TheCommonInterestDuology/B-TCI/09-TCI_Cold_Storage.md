# Cold Storage

The thing nobody at the William Martin Institute has said out loud is that the bickering has stopped.

Jean has been here since September 1953 and she has never in her life worked anywhere so noisy. Not loud — noisy. Two men arguing about a gate schedule from opposite ends of a verandah; Reggie saying *that is not what the figure means and you know it is not what the figure means*; Pascual saying *ay, listen to him, he has read one paper about seals*; forty minutes over supper about whether a mermaid can be said to prefer anything; a running quarrel about the microtome that has been going on since before she arrived and that she assumed would outlive all of them.

It stopped on the twenty-second of June and it has not started again, and it is the twenty-sixth of July.

What there is instead is *thank you*.

They are extremely polite. They pass each other things. Reggie says *thank you* for the salt and *thank you* for a plate and, on the Tuesday, *thank you very much indeed* for a section that Pascual had cut at five in the morning, and Pascual says *not at all*, and John Weald put his fork down and looked at his own hands until it was over.

Nobody asks. That is the rule at this station and it is a good rule and Jean invented most of it herself, and she is beginning to wonder whether it is the stupidest rule she has ever obeyed.

---

John says it on the Wednesday, in the dark room, with both hands in a dish.

"Are they all right?"

"Which they."

"Don't." He does not look up. "You know which they. I've been drawing in that building since I was twenty-four and I know what it sounds like, and it doesn't sound like that."

Jean puts the tongs down.

"I don't know," she says.

"Right."

"I'm not going to ask them, and neither are you."

"I wasn't going to ask them." He turns the plate over in the dish. "I'm asking you. You've been here longest of the three of us and you're the one who'd know."

"Pascual's been here since 'fifty-two."

"Yes," says John, "but you notice things, and they don't."

And Jean stands in a room the size of a cupboard in the red light and has to concede that this is entirely correct.

---

What she is actually doing in July is forty tons of fish.

It comes out of the same bad afternoon as everything else: Reggie standing at a verandah table on the fourth saying *you buy herring by the ton because it is cheap and it comes in a box of a known weight*, and Jean sitting on the rail thinking, with the part of her mind that does the quarterly return, *yes, and then what*.

Because a ton of frozen fish is not a fact about fish. It is a fact about a building.

Frozen herring out of the North Sea or the west of Scotland does not arrive in the Caribbean in a man's boat. It arrives in a ship with insulated holds, and it is landed at a wharf with a crane, and the moment it leaves the hold it has to go into cold store within the hour or it is forty tons of ruined fish and a claim on somebody's insurance. And there are not very many places between Cuba and the Windwards where you can do that. There are the big ports, and there are three or four fruit companies with their own plant, and after that there is nothing at all.

So she stops doing the current work for a fortnight, which she does not tell anybody, and starts writing to shipping agents as a fisheries station enquiring about landing facilities for research material requiring refrigeration, which is the exact truth and is why it works.

---

It takes nine days and it is the dullest work she has ever done and she loves every minute of it.

Lloyd's. The *Fish Trades Gazette*, which the Institute takes because the Institute does the returns and nobody has ever read it but her. Four shipping agents, two harbour offices, the Colonial Secretary's office at Kingston for the published wharf schedules, and — this is the one that takes four days and is worth all the rest — the insurance schedules, because a cold store has to be surveyed and a surveyed cold store is a published tonnage.

By the twenty-fourth she has a sheet of foolscap with nine entries on it and a column for capacity and a column for whether the wharf will take a ship of the necessary draught.

She reads it through twice. It is not an answer. It is a list of nine places, of which she would guess seven are perfectly innocent.

But it is nine, out of the entire Caribbean basin. On the twenty-sixth of April they had the sea.

---

She does not go to Reggie with it.

She thinks about that afterwards and is not sure she can defend it, and defends it anyway: she goes to Hester, because Hester keeps the bundle, and because Jean has spent three weeks watching Reggie Tar carry something across a verandah four times a day with his shoulders up round his ears, and has decided, without consulting anybody, to stop adding to the load until she has to.

Hester reads it standing up, which is how she reads everything.

"How firm?"

"Firm on the tonnage. The tonnage is surveyed and published and it isn't a guess." Jean has her hands behind her back and makes herself stop. "Soft on the draught in three of them because the harbour office gave me a figure from 1949."

"Then we put a query against those three and we do not pretend it is not there."

"Yes."

"Miss Leslie." Hester puts the sheet down on the pad. "This is the best piece of work anybody has done in this building since April, and I include the bone."

"It's a list."

"It is nine," says Hester. "On the twenty-sixth of April we had the Atlantic Ocean."

---

They put it on the table at the Friday meeting and nobody prepares Spilsbury for it, because Hester says that preparing him would be the single stupidest thing they could do and that he should come into the room and find it lying there like a pair of gloves.

So it is lying there, at the end of the long table, face up, when he comes in at ten past nine with his slide box.

Jean watches him.

It is worth watching, and she is the only person in the room who is not pretending to do something else, because she has decided that if she is going to be in this then she is going to be in it with her eyes open. He picks up the slide box he has just put down, which is the first thing. Then he reads the list — she counts; it takes him about nine seconds for nine entries and a capacity column — and his face does nothing whatever, which is what his face does, and he puts it back on the table at a very slightly different angle from the one it was lying at.

"Whose is this?"

"Mine," says Jean.

"How?"

"Insurance surveys. A cold store has to be surveyed and the tonnage gets published. And the wharf schedules for the draught, except for three where the figure's old and there's a query on it."

"Mm," says Spilsbury.

And then he says, in exactly the voice he uses about a decalcification artefact:

"Six of those are wrong."

---

There is a silence in the long room of a kind Jean has not previously experienced.

The Colonel does not move. John's pencil has stopped. Reggie has gone absolutely still in the way he goes still, which is not dramatic and which she has learned to read as the opposite of calm. Pascual is at the end of the bench with his arms folded and is looking, not at Spilsbury, but at the list.

Two people move for the list at once, from opposite ends of the table, and their hands stop about four inches apart above it, and neither of them picks it up.

Hester picks it up.

"Six," says Hester.

"Six."

"Which six?"

"No."

"Dr Spilsbury —"

"You will notice," Spilsbury says, "that I have already told you a great deal more than I intended to when I came through that door, and that I did it before I had sat down, and that nobody in this room asked me a question."

"Then why —"

"Because it is a bad list and I could not leave it lying there." He pulls a chair out. "That is the whole of the reason. You may also have my advice, which is to write it down, because it is the most useful thing any of you know about me and it will not occur to you again for months."

Hester picks up her pencil and writes it down.

---

What is astonishing — what Jean will think about for years, on and off, usually at inconvenient moments — is that he then sits down and does the meeting.

Ninety minutes on Mr Lecky, who is in Kingston in a fortnight and who has had no reply and is not going to get one, and on whether the Colonel should be off the island when he calls or conspicuously on it; on the second set of photographs, which are done, forty of them, and which go to a dull honest solicitor in Bristol in two consignments by two routes; on the licence in November and what a renewal actually requires and who signs it and whether Hester can get the signature moved to somebody younger before the Colonel's name becomes a liability.

He is good at all of it. He is better at the Lecky problem than anybody, because he has read that firm's correspondence for months and can say what they will do, and he says it in the same flat voice he uses to say that six entries out of nine are wrong and he will not say which.

At the end of it he goes out with his slide box and his hat and does not say goodbye, because he does not.

"Well," says the Colonel.

"Three," says Jean.

"Three," says Hester, and draws a line under it.

---

She finds Pascual at the north pen at six, doing the Tuesday measurements on a Thursday, which is not like him.

Chumley is up against the wall of the pen with his tail under him, letting it happen, six years old and nearly seven feet and about as tractable as a large dog and very much less tractable than a large dog if he decides otherwise. Gamma is out in the middle where she goes.

Jean sits down on the concrete with her back against the rail post.

"You've done the tail root twice."

"I know I have."

"Then you're doing it a third time and you're going to write it down wrong."

Pascual looks at the tape for a moment, and then lets it go, and sits down on the concrete beside her with his forearms on his knees, which she has seen him do perhaps four times in three years and never in daylight.

"Twenty-one and a half," he says.

"That's what I've got."

"You weren't measuring him."

"No. But he's twenty-one and a half because he was twenty-one and a quarter on the first, and you've told me what he does in July every July since I got here."

Pascual almost laughs, which is the first sound of that kind she has heard out of anybody in five weeks.

"He's putting it on faster than last year."

"Is that good?"

"It's good. It's the only good thing I've got in a book at the moment and I've read that page about nine times."

---

They sit there for a while. Chumley comes over and hangs in the water four feet away with his chin on the shelf, being extremely interested in nothing.

"I'm not going to ask," Jean says.

"I know."

"I'm going to say one thing and then I'm not going to ask."

"*Ay, Dios.*"

"You can have it or not." She is looking at the water and not at him, which is deliberate and which she thinks he will notice and let go. "When I came here in 'fifty-three I was twenty-eight, and I had a supervisor at home who had put it in writing that field stations ruin women."

"*Ay.*"

"And the two of you spent three years arguing about a microtome in front of me, and neither of you ever made me feel like a visitor for one minute of it."

Pascual does not say anything.

"That's the whole of it," says Jean. "I'm not asking, I'm not taking a side, and I'd like the noise back, and if you tell Reggie I said any of that I shall deny it under oath."

"Jean —"

"That's the one thing. I've had it."

He sits there for about four seconds.

"It's going to take a while," he says.

"Right," she says. "Good. That's better than what I thought you were going to say."

---

She goes to Reggie on the Friday morning, on her own, and shuts the office door behind her, which she has done twice in three years.

"Miss Leslie."

"I've got four years of water figures on those two animals," she says. "Temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen, feed weights, and the growth series with Pascual's measurements against mine as a check. Every month since December 1952."

"I know what you've got."

"It's about six hundred entries, it's in three books in my hand, and nobody else alive can read my hand."

Reggie puts his pen down slowly.

"Yes."

"You're not having them."

The office is very quiet. Out through the window John is doing something with the derrick and singing.

"I've said no twice," Reggie says.

"I know you have. I was in the room both times." Jean keeps her hands at her sides and her voice exactly where she put it. "On the thirty-first of May you took a second and a half. On the fourth of July you took four."

"Jean —"

"I don't know what you'll take in November, and I'm not going to be standing in a room in November finding out. If it ever comes to that, I want it over already, and I want it to have been over since July."

"Jean —"

"I'm not accusing you of anything."

"You are, rather."

"No." She is surprised by how steady it comes out. "I'm taking a thing off you. There's a difference and you know there is, because you've spent three years teaching me it."

---

He gets up and goes to the window and stands there with his back to her for a while, and she lets him, because she has just done a thing to a man she likes very much indeed and is not going to pretend it was nothing.

"You could have done that to Hester," he says. "She'd have minuted it, and it'd have been an Institute decision, and I'd never have had to know it was yours."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you."

"Because you'd have found out in October and you'd have known I couldn't say it to your face." Jean looks at the back of his head. "And because everybody in this building has spent five weeks arranging for things to be said by somebody else, and I've watched what it's done, and I'm not adding to it."

There is a long pause.

"No," says Reggie. "Quite right."

He turns round. He looks, she thinks, about nine years older than he did in April, and he is smiling, and it is not a very good one, and it is entirely real.

"Would you put it in writing?"

"What?"

"Put it in writing. Two lines, dated today, to me, saying the figures are yours and that you decline to release them for any experimental purpose involving either animal."

"You want it on paper."

"And a copy to Hester for the bundle." He comes back to the desk. "Not because I don't believe you, Miss Leslie. Because I should like it in a box in October, where nobody has to be brave about it twice."

Jean stands there.

"Right," she says.

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me," she says, before she can stop herself, and then, hearing it, and hearing what it has sounded like in this building for five weeks, she goes red to the ears and says, "God. Sorry. I didn't —"

"I know you didn't," says Reggie.

---

She writes it that afternoon. Two lines, dated the twenty-seventh of July, in her own hand, and she takes it to Hester and Hester reads it and puts it in the box file at the back where the standing instructions go and does not say anything about it for eighteen days.

What Hester says, eighteen days later, when it matters, is another thing.

Walking back down the path in the last of the light, Jean passes the long room.

The door is shut. It has been shut since the twenty-second of June.

Both of them are in there. She can see the lamp under the door, and she has just come up from the pens and neither of them is at the pens, and the stools have been scraped back from the bench because she can hear where they aren't.

There is no sound at all coming out of it. Not an argument. Not a word. Two men in the same room at nine o'clock at night, working, in silence.

She stands there for about four seconds, which is four seconds longer than her own rule allows.

Then she goes up to the house, because it is not hers; and because she has done the one thing today that was hers to do; and because if she knocks on that door she will have to have a reason, and she has not got one.
