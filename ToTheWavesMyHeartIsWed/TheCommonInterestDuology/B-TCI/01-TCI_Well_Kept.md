# Well Kept

Juan's brother comes up the path at a run, and Pascual is off the step of the sick bay with the bag in his hand before the man has got the whole sentence out, because in three and a half years Ramón has not run anywhere.

"How big?"

"Big. Bigger than the boat."

"That's not a size, that's a boast. Get Miss Leslie, she's at the ponds. And tell her to bring the long tape."

He goes over the neck of the cay at something between a walk and the other thing, in the flat grey light before the sun is properly up, with his shirt already sticking to him because it is the end of April and this island has not had rain in nine weeks.

Four of them have got him above the wash on the seaward beach, on a tarpaulin, and they have done it properly, which is to say they have done it the way Pascual taught them to do it with the calf two winters ago: one man to a corner and nobody pulling on an arm.

From thirty yards it could be a porpoise.

It is not a porpoise.

---

He is an adult male and he is enormous, and Pascual is on his knees in the wet sand with both hands on him before he has thought anything at all.

Cold. Properly cold, the cold that comes up out of the middle of an animal and not off the sea. The eyes have gone. The jaw is slack and moves under his hand without resistance, which means the stiffness has been and gone, and the belly is not yet up, which means it has not gone far. A day and a half. Two at the outside.

Then he sits back on his heels and looks at what he has got.

The hollows are not there.

That is the first thing and for a moment he cannot get past it. Every one of these animals he has ever had his hands on, alive or dead — Alpha in two boxes, Gamma in the corner with her tail under her, Beta with his ribs standing out through the water like barrel hoops — every one of them has been a diagram of what a mermaid ought to be with the meat taken off it. This one is not. There is a layer over the ribs and a proper mass on the shoulder and the tail is heavy right down to the fluke, and when Pascual puts his thumb into the flank it goes in and comes back out again the way flesh does.

He has not had his hands on one of these animals in this condition, alive or dead, and that includes the two in the water at the end of his own path.

"*Ay,*" he says, to nobody.

Then he gets to the tail, and finds the belt.

---

It is round the animal at the narrowest part, where the body goes into the tail: three inches of woven webbing with a padded lining sewn to the inside of it, and a steel buckle, and the buckle is at the back, in the one place on that body a man could not have reached with either hand.

Made. Not improvised. Somebody cut that lining and stitched it and set those holes.

There is a brass ring worked into the webbing opposite the buckle, and on the ring a swivel, and on the swivel about four feet of braided line, and the line has parted. He runs the end of it through his fingers. It has not been cut. It has gone under load and it has gone all at once, and there is a hand's width of it that has been chafing against something for a very long time, because the braid there is furred and flattened and pale.

Pascual crouches on a beach at half past six in the morning with four feet of line in his fist and finds that he has nowhere to put any of it. He has been in this trade twenty-one years and his temper is a reliable instrument and it has always had somewhere to go: a man who left an animal on concrete, a man who did not fetch him at two in the morning, a man running an experiment on the only pair of hands on the station. There is no such man on this beach. There is only Juan's brother holding his hat, and the sea going about its business, and this.

He puts the line down. He goes back up the body to the head and takes the jaw in both hands and opens it.

Then he stops moving for a while.

---

Reggie comes over the neck of the cay at ten to seven with his shirt buttoned and his glasses on and no hat, which means he came the moment somebody told him, and he does not ask a single question. He walks up the beach and looks, and then he crouches down in the sand in a clean shirt and looks some more.

"Dr Pobil."

"Front teeth. Eight of them, upper and lower. Drawn."

"Broken?"

"Drawn." Pascual has the jaw open and the light coming over his shoulder. "No fracture. The roots are out entire and the sockets have gone over."

"Not a bite."

"Not a bite, not a rock, and nobody did it with a hammer." He tips the head so the light gets into it. "Somebody put an elevator in there and took them out one at a time, and knew what he was doing, and did it a long while back."

"How long."

"Six months. A year. I'll do better when I've got him inside."

Reggie says nothing at all.

"Reggie."

"I heard you."

"Then say something, because I've been on this sand for half an hour on my own."

"He's fat," Reggie says.

"Yes."

"He's in better condition than either animal in my pens."

"Yes."

"Who has he been with," Reggie says, and it is not really a question, and Pascual watches the man's face do the thing it does about twice a year, which is nothing whatever, very fast.

---

It takes them until half past nine to get him up.

He goes eight feet seven inches on Jean's long tape, forty-eight round the chest and thirty-four at the tail root, which is where the belt sat, and there is no scale on this island that will take him, so Jean does it off the length and the chest against the formula she worked up for Gamma and gets three hundred and ninety pounds, and says she is prepared to be wrong by twenty either way and that anybody who wants a better figure may go and buy a weighbridge.

There is no sensible route off that beach. The tide is wrong for bringing the launch round and the truck cannot come nearer than the top of the slipway, which is a hundred and forty yards of loose sand away, and it is going to be ninety degrees by ten o'clock.

"Roll him," says Juan.

"You can't roll four hundred pounds of dead animal up a beach," says Reggie.

"Not roll him. Roll the tarpaulin. Under, so." Juan makes a shape with his hands. "With the poles."

They take the two spare pen gates off their hinges, which nobody has thought of in four years, lash them into a sort of raft, get the tarpaulin under the animal in three lifts with eight men and a great deal of shouting, and drag the whole arrangement up the beach behind the truck at walking pace with John sitting on the front end as ballast and drawing the whole time, one-handed, on a board on his knee.

Halfway up, the near gate catches a rock and the raft slews, and Reggie — who has got himself in under the low corner of it with his shoulder, in the sand, in what was this morning a clean shirt — says something that Pascual does not catch over the engine and Juan does, and Juan laughs so hard he has to stop the truck.

"*Qué dijo?*" Pascual says.

"He says he will pay for the gates."

"He will not pay for the gates, the Institute will pay for the gates —"

"Get *on*," says Reggie, from underneath, "and stop conducting a review."

They get him onto the slab behind the sick bay at twenty past nine with the sun coming off the concrete, and every man there is wet through, and nobody is talking, because they have had an hour and a half now to look at what they are carrying.

---

"Ice," Pascual says.

"How much?"

"All of it. Every pound they've got and then whatever the boat can bring after." He is stripping his shirt off and reaching for the apron. "I've a four-hundred-pound animal in the open at the end of April and I'll have him opened by noon or I'll have nothing. Pack him while I work or I'll be reading soup."

"The fish house has ice," Reggie says.

"The fish house has ice for the fish house."

"Yes." Reggie takes his glasses off and does something to them with his cuff, which does not improve them, and puts them back on. "I'm going over with Juan and I'm going to be charming at Mr Alleyne for approximately four minutes, and I should think Juan can have the first of it back to you by half past ten. Consider yourself told."

"Noted," Pascual says. "Thank you."

"It's two years and you still say thank you."

"I'll stop when it stops mattering."

"Mm," says Reggie, and gets as far as the door.

"Reggie. Don't do him for more than you need. He's been decent to us about the bait."

Reggie stops.

"That is the first useful instruction anybody has ever given me on the subject," he says, and goes.

---

He works until four, with John.

It is a very long time since he has had anybody in the room with him for a gross, and he had forgotten what it is like to say a thing out loud as it comes up instead of writing it down twenty minutes later. John is twenty-six now and has a paper with his name on the plates and a small unbearable moustache, and he has his board and his tape and he does not once look away.

"Where do you want me?"

"Where you can see. Everything I say, you put a picture to it. If I say something twice, that's because I want two."

"Two views or two plates?"

"Two plates. Don't be clever."

What they get, over six hours, is this.

*Condition.* Blubber at the standard site an inch and three-eighths. Good musculature, no wasting anywhere, nothing at all like Alpha and nothing at all like Gamma in the bad winter. The animal has been eating well and moving enough to keep what he ate.

*Stomach.* Fish. Perhaps three pounds of it, partly gone. All of it in portions — headed, tailed, cut across the body with a knife, the way a man cuts fish into a bucket. Not one whole fish in him. No crab, no shell, no weed, nothing he would have taken himself out of open water.

John stops drawing for a moment when Pascual says that, and then starts again.

*Fluke.* Along the dorsal edge, on the left, an area about the size of a hand with healed punctures in it. Pascual counts forty and gives up counting. They are in rough columns. Some are silvered over and old and some are inside a year, and they are exactly and precisely where he puts a needle into Chumley every second Tuesday, because it is where the vessel runs and there is nowhere else sensible to go.

He stands with his hands on the edge of the slab for a moment.

"Dr Pobil?"

"Write it down. Forty plus, dorsal fluke edge, left, healed, in series." He clears his throat. "Somebody has been bleeding this animal twice a month for a very long time, and he let them do it, because there's no tearing round any of them. He held still."

*The belt sites.* Under where the webbing sat, a band of scar the whole way round, healed and broken and healed again, with the hair gone and the epidermis thin and shiny. Nothing acute. Nothing infected. Whoever kept him has been looking after that, too.

*Internal.* And here is the thing Pascual keeps arriving at and going away from and arriving at again.

The lungs are clean. There is no pneumonia. There is not so much as a patch of it, and in four years and four animals and a literature going back to Labat, the answer has always been pneumonia. The heart is a good heart. The liver is a good liver. The kidneys are unremarkable and he takes a great deal of trouble over them, because he knows those kidneys better than he knows his own, and there is nothing.

The adrenals are a little heavy. Only a little. He weighs them twice and writes both figures, and thinks of a cortex from 1952 that was twice the depth it had any business being, and this is not that. This is an animal that has been mildly and chronically put upon, in the way any animal in anybody's care is mildly and chronically put upon. The thymus is where it should be and has what it should have in it.

At four o'clock he puts down the knife and steps back from the slab and pulls his mask off his face.

"Well?" says John.

"Nothing killed him," Pascual says.

---

Reggie comes back at five with the rest of it, which brings the day's ice to fourteen hundredweight in sacks, and a note from Mr Alleyne saying that he is glad to help the Institute and enquiring after the Colonel's wife, and he takes one look at the slab and the trays and Pascual's face and says, "Tell me the order you found them in."

So Pascual tells him in the order he found them in, which takes twenty minutes, and Reggie does not interrupt once.

At the end of it he says: "Say the lungs again."

"Clean."

"There's nothing in them."

"There's nothing in them, Reggie. I went through both of them on the slab and I've got the blocks coming and I'll cut them Monday and I'll tell you again on Monday. They're clean."

"And the adrenals are ordinary."

"Slightly heavy. Ordinary."

"So by every criterion this Institute invented," Reggie says, "that animal was well kept."

"Yes."

"He was well kept and he's dead on a beach at eight feet seven."

"Yes."

The ice is going into the tank next door with a noise like shingle. Somewhere down the path the generator changes note.

"Then say the rest of it," Pascual says, "because you've had it since ten to seven this morning and you've been carrying it about all day, and I'd rather have it in the room."

Reggie takes his glasses off.

"Somebody's been keeping *N. aestuarius* successfully," he says, "at a size that wants open water. On a tether. Fed cut fish out of a bucket and bled twice a month."

"A menagerie."

"Nobody at a menagerie takes blood." He puts the glasses down on the bench. "That's a programme. It has a schedule and it has a book, and it's been running long enough to have a routine."

"And nobody's published."

"Nobody's published a line. I'd know; I read everything in this field and there isn't very much of it."

"How many have asked you?"

"Three institutions, since the paper came out. Wanting to know how it's done." He picks the glasses back up. "I answered all three, in full, with the plate numbers, because that's what one does."

"And?"

"And one of them I'd never heard of and couldn't find in any directory, and I answered it anyway, in March of last year." He puts them on. "I've thought about that letter perhaps twice since."

"You couldn't have known —"

"I could have found out," Reggie says. "It would have taken a morning."

"Reggie. The paper was right."

"The paper was entirely right," Reggie says. "It's the best thing I've had anything to do with and I'd write it again on Monday."

"You don't look like a man who'd write it again."

"What it did," Reggie says, looking at the slab, "was tell every man alive with a tank that the losses are survivable. That's the whole of what it did. Before it you took four to keep one for eight months. Now you take four and you keep four."

"You don't know that."

"No," Reggie says. "There he is, though."

There is a silence in the sick bay that Pascual does not like at all.

"Right," Pascual says. "Put the glasses on."

"I —"

"Put them on and come here and look at his mouth, because you haven't looked at his mouth and I'm not having you sit in a corner subtracting for the rest of the day."

---

Jean comes in at six with a chart, her hair tied up in a scarf, and puts the chart on the bench without asking anybody's permission and puts the parted line on top of it.

"A day and a half dead," she says. "That's yours, is it?"

"Thirty-six hours. Forty at the outside."

"Right. Then the drift's nothing." She has a pencil out. "The set's been west-nor'-west all week and it's been light all week, and thirty-six hours of that carries a floating body about eighteen miles, twenty-five if you're feeling generous. So he died within twenty-five miles of that beach and probably nearer."

"And before he died?" says Reggie.

"That's the part I can't do and neither can you." Jean puts the pencil down. "A grown one does twenty-five miles a day and can keep it up as long as it likes."

"So."

"So if he broke off that line a week before he died, he came from anywhere between Cuba and the Windwards. And if he broke off it yesterday morning he came from just over there." She taps the chart without looking at it. "I'm not going to draw you a circle. You'd only frame it."

"Jean —"

"I'll do the current work properly if you want it, and I'll want a fortnight and the launch." She is already rolling the chart. "I'd rather do that than guess at supper."

"Do it," Reggie says.

"Then I'll want the launch on the eighth and the ninth as well, and I'm not asking, I'm telling you now while you're pleased."

"She's had that off you," Reggie says to Pascual, after she has gone.

"She has."

"You taught her that."

"I taught her that in the summer of 'fifty-four," Pascual says, "and I'd do it again."

---

Hester receives them at half past seven with the tea already poured, and Pascual puts the belt on her table.

She does not touch it. She reads the length of it with her eyes, the webbing and the padding and the stitching and the buckle at the back, and then she does put a finger out and move the brass ring half an inch, once, the way you turn over a card.

"Where do you want to send it?" she says.

"Somewhere," says Reggie. "Anywhere. There's a dead animal of an undescribed genus on my slab that's been held by persons unknown for years, and I'd like to tell somebody about it whose business it is."

"Nobody's business."

"Hester —"

"I am not being clever with you, Reginald. Have the position in four sentences."

"Go on."

She sets the pot down. "They are not fish, so the Fisheries Ordinance does not reach them. They are not game, and there is no close season, because nobody has ever put them on a schedule. There is no treaty in the world with the word in it. And they belong to nobody until a man has one in his hands, and to him the moment he does."

"That can't be right."

"It is exactly right. This Institute has a licence four lines long and I wrote three of them myself."

"Four lines."

"Four." Hester picks up her cup and does not drink out of it. "I have been asking for twenty years for somebody to make them anything at all. A bird. An otter. A seal. Anything that is on a piece of paper somewhere."

"And?"

"And you cannot get a Colonial Secretary to schedule an animal that most of his office believes is a sailor's story. The ones who know better would rather it stayed one."

"And if I write to the Colonial Secretary anyway?"

"Then you will have written to him," she says, "and in nine months a clerk will regret that the matter does not fall within his department. And somebody clever in that office will have learned that the William Martin is very interested in who keeps merfolk." She puts her cup down. "Do you want that?"

Reggie does not answer.

"I thought not."

"Ma'am," Pascual says. "The belt."

"Yes."

"Somebody made it. Cut it and lined it and stitched it and set the holes. That's a saddler or a sailmaker or a harness shop, and that's an order, and an order's a piece of paper with a name on it."

Hester looks at him for a moment over the top of her cup.

"That," she says, "is the first sentence anybody has said in this room tonight that I can do anything with. Leave it with me and don't tell me what you think it means."

---

They bury what is left of him at nine, behind the store, in the sand above the high line, in a pit the length of a man and a half that Juan and his brother and John dig by lamplight while Pascual stands there with his arms folded and does not help, because he has been on his feet since twenty past six.

The skeleton will be up in two years and it will be the most valuable object in the building. The head is in the paraffin. The blocks are in the fixative. Everything that can be kept is kept, and it is a great deal, and it is all going into a day book under a heading that Pascual has not been able to write.

Because the Martin numbers its animals in Greek letters and there are four of those and this is not one of them. He is not a specimen. Nobody consigned him. There is no manifest with his name on it and no licence that covers him and no register anywhere in the world with a line in it that he fits, and Pascual stands over an open hole in the sand at nine o'clock at night and finds that he is angry about a heading in a book.

In the end he writes: *Adult male, N. aestuarius, found dead on the seaward beach 26.iv.56, provenance unknown.*

Then he sits and looks at *provenance unknown* for a while, and does not cross it out, because there is nothing to put instead.

---

He comes into Pascual's room at twenty past ten and shoots the bolt and does not say anything, and stands there with his back against the door.

"Come here," Pascual says.

"In a moment."

"No. Now, and do the thinking afterwards, because you've a face on you like a man reading a bill."

Reggie comes, and Pascual takes hold of him by the front of the shirt and puts him against the wall the way he has been doing for two years, and does not do anything else for a bit; just holds him there and puts his mouth against the side of his jaw, where the muscle is standing out, and waits for the muscle to stop standing out.

It takes a while. It does, when he has been subtracting.

"You've been in the sea," Reggie says, into his shoulder.

"I went in at half past nine. I'd been in an animal for six hours and I was not coming to bed like that."

"You went in at night."

"I went in off the slipway up to my waist with a torch and a bar of soap, Reggie, I did not swim to Cuba —"

"You went in at night," Reggie says again, and Pascual understands, rather late, that this is not about hygiene, and gets both hands up under the man's jaw and holds his head still.

"Listen to me," he says. "Nothing is in that water. Look at me and tell me you've heard it."

"I've heard it."

"Say the other thing too."

"I don't believe it," Reggie says, "and I'd like you to go on saying it anyway."

"*Ay, Dios.*" He kisses him. "Every night, then. Twice on Sundays. Now take that off."

---

There is something in this that he has never got used to and does not want to: that this man, who spent eleven days in 1954 running an animal into the ground and could not be stopped by anybody, will stop dead in the middle of a room because somebody has taken hold of the back of his neck.

He gets him to the cot and gets the rest of it off him and takes his time about it, which he has learned to do, because the first six months were all hurry and they neither of them needed it to be. The room is hot. Everything is hot; it has not rained since February and the iron has been holding the day since noon.

"There," Reggie says. "Go on."

"I am going on."

"You are conducting a *survey* —"

"I'm looking at you," Pascual says. "I'm allowed."

He puts his mouth to the hollow under the collarbone, and then lower, and takes the ribs one at a time with the flat of his tongue, slowly, counting, because he is what he is; and the noise he gets at the fourth one is worth all of the rest of the day.

"Pascual."

"Mm."

"Say something."

"About what?"

"I don't care. About the microtome." Reggie's hands are in his hair and shutting there. "About anything at all that isn't an animal."

So Pascual talks. He talks about the microtome, which is still a disgrace; and about Juan's brother's boat, which is a worse disgrace; and about the way Reggie got himself in under the corner of that raft this morning in a clean shirt with eight men watching, and how he had to stand there holding a rope with his back to the lot of them until he was fit to be looked at — low, into the throat, into the hinge of the jaw, in two languages and not in any order, while his hands go on doing what they are doing.

"That isn't true."

"It is entirely true. I stood there for half a minute with my back turned —"

"You *didn't*—"

"— and Juan asked me if I had hurt my shoulder, and I said yes." He puts his teeth, very carefully, into the place at the join of the neck where the man has no defences at all. "So now you know what I am."

Reggie says something that is not a word.

"Again?"

"*Yes.* God. There —"

And after that there is not much talking for a while that would go into a book; only the iron cot doing what it has done since 1943, and the generator, and a hand flat in the small of a back holding somebody precisely where he is, and one man saying another man's name twice in a register that does not get used at a table, and then not saying anything at all.

Afterwards Reggie lies half across him, going heavier by the minute, with his forehead against Pascual's collarbone on the winch scar and one hand flat over the ribs where the heart is, and does not move it.

"Pascual."

"Mm."

"He was on that line for a long time."

"Yes."

"The chafe on the braid. That's a year's chafe. That's not a month."

"Go to sleep."

"Yes," Reggie says, and does, almost immediately, which he does perhaps once a fortnight and which Pascual has learned to treat as a piece of good weather.

---

He wakes at ten past four because he always does.

He lies there in the dark for about a minute, and then he gets up very carefully and gets his trousers on and goes through into the sick bay, and puts the lamp on, and gets the belt out of the tray.

He has been not doing this since seven o'clock last night.

It is buckled, as it came off the animal, at the fourth hole. He measures it: thirty-four inches, which is right, which is what he took off the body himself with Jean's tape.

There are three more holes in the strap beyond it.

The leather punch marks are old. The webbing round them has that pale scuffed look the buckle tongue leaves when it has sat in a hole for a long while, and it has sat in every one of them: thirty-four, and thirty, and twenty-six, and then the innermost, which is twenty-one.

Pascual sits down on the stool.

He does not need the book for this and he goes and gets the book anyway, because he is not going to say a figure like that out of his head. Chumley, who came out of a tank in May 1954 at four years old and has been measured on the first of every month since, in Pascual's own hand, down the left-hand side: girth at the tail root, nineteen and a half; twenty; twenty-one, in the November.

Twenty-one inches is a calf of about five.

He sits in his own sick bay at twenty-five past four in the morning with a dead animal's belt across his knees and works it forward: twenty-one to thirty-four, on his own growth curve, on the only growth curve anybody has, which is four-year-old Chumley's and is not enough and is what there is.

He gets eight years. He tries it twice more to make it be less. It will not be less.

---

Reggie is awake when he comes back, sitting on the edge of the cot in the dark with his glasses on, which means he has been awake for some time.

"Tell me."

"Come through. I'll want to show you."

So they go through, the pair of them, half dressed at half past four in the morning, and Pascual lights the second lamp and puts the belt out flat on the bench with the buckle to the left, and puts his finger on each of the holes in turn, and says the numbers.

Reggie does not touch it.

"Twenty-one," he says.

"Twenty-one. That's a calf of five. That's Chumley in the November before last, and I have got Chumley in the November before last in a book in my own hand, so I'm not guessing at it."

"And he came off that beach at thirty-four."

"He did."

"How long."

"Eight years," Pascual says. "I've done it three times and I can't get it under eight, and if the curve's wrong — and it is wrong, it's one animal — then it's wrong in the direction of longer. Nine. Ten. Not less than eight."

Reggie stands there with both hands on the edge of the bench.

Pascual watches him do the subtraction, because he cannot not do it, and because it is the simplest piece of arithmetic either of them has done today and it is the only one that has anywhere to arrive.

"Forty-eight," Reggie says.

"Reggie —"

"Forty-eight. Forty-seven, if the curve's out."

"Yes."

"I was there."

"I know where you were."

"No," Reggie says. "You do not know where I was. You have had it out of order, and that is my doing. I left that place in the autumn of 1950. In the spring of 1948 I was in the room at the end of the corridor, and I was extremely good at my work."

"Reggie."

"And somebody down that same corridor — or somebody at a place I could have found out about in a morning, if I had been the sort of man who asks — was fitting a belt to a five-year-old animal and starting a book."

He has stopped contracting. He does not know he has stopped. He did not know the last time either. It comes off him like a light going up in a window, and Pascual has seen it exactly twice in three and a half years.

"And I do not know," Reggie says. "That is the whole of it. I was in the building, and I was good, and I do not know, and I should."

The lamp does what lamps do to that face, which with him is nothing very much at all.

Pascual does not say *it was not you*, because it may have been, in the way that matters least and stings most. He does not say *you could not have known*, because the man has already knocked that one down himself at five o'clock in front of a slab. There is nothing to hand him and no point in handing him anything.

So he does the only thing there is, which is to put his hand flat on the back of Reggie's neck, warm, and leave it there, and say the practical thing.

"Then we find out," he says. "Not tonight. Get the kettle on, and we'll want Hester at seven, and you'll write your list of the three institutions before you eat anything, because you'll have it wrong by breakfast and right now you've got it."

"Pascual."

"What?"

"I shall have to write to somebody who can find out," Reggie says, "and there is exactly one man on this earth who can."

Pascual takes his hand off the back of his neck.

"I know," he says. "I've known since the fish came out of his stomach in portions."

The generator changes note. Out beyond the store, above the high line, there is a pit in the sand with a marker on it, and forty yards the other way there are two animals asleep on the bottom of a concrete pen with one hand loose across the other, and nobody in the world is coming for them tonight.

"Kettle," Pascual says.

"Yes," says Reggie, and goes and puts the kettle on.
