# Paragraph Five

Mr Sturrock's report comes on the fourteenth of February and runs to nine paragraphs, and Hester reads the whole of it out on the verandah at six o'clock, in order, without commentary, which is the only way she does anything.

Paragraphs one to four are the pens, the intake, the feed and the water, and they are the best paragraphs about this station that anybody outside it has ever written. Paragraph three has the phrase *maintained to a standard which I have not met elsewhere in the colony*, and the Colonel makes her read it twice.

Paragraph five is the drug book.

*Two entries in December 1956 are uncountersigned. The veterinary officer states, correctly, that he was the only qualified person on the premises at the time. Where a station holds a schedule of this kind, the licensee should nominate a second signatory who need not be medically qualified, and should do so in writing. I recommend that this be made a condition.*

"That's fair," says Pascual.

"It is entirely fair and I am extremely annoyed about it."

"Ma'am, it's fair. I've been signing that book on my own since 1954 and nobody's ever asked why."

"Because nobody's ever looked," says Reggie. "It's the first time anyone's inspected this place properly since the Colonel took it over."

"Since 1948," says the Colonel, with a certain amount of relish. "Nine inspectors. Sturrock's the first one who found anything."

Paragraph seven names Mr J. Weald, draughtsman, and says the plate collection is of a standard which the Department would be glad to see deposited. John goes out onto the path and does not come back for ten minutes.

Paragraph nine says that in the inspector's opinion the two specimens are better kept at this station than they would be anywhere else he has visited, and that he would view any proposal to move them with concern.

Nobody says anything at all for a moment.

"That," says Hester, "is worth more than everything in the objection put together, and it is in a departmental file, and it will be there in ten years."

---

The executed withdrawal comes back on the twenty-seventh, signed for the applicant by a man whose name nobody has ever heard, with a covering slip of two lines.

Pascual is on the jetty when the bag comes up, and he stands there while Jean sorts it, and he does not say anything, and Jean says, "It's the Bay Street envelope," and hands it to him instead of taking it up to Hester, which is the sort of thing this station does now.

He takes it up to Hester unopened, because it is hers.

She opens it standing, reads it, and sits down.

"They've signed it."

"Both parts?"

"Both parts, simultaneous, neither at liberty to renew." She puts it flat on the desk and smooths it with the side of her hand, once. "Gamma and Chumley are ours. Nobody is ever going to apply for them again."

Pascual finds that he has to put his hand on the back of a chair.

---

He goes down to the north pen afterwards and stands at the rail for about half an hour.

He is not a man who cries about things and he does not. What happens instead is that his hands start shaking, at the rail, with nobody about, at eleven o'clock in the morning; and Chumley comes up at the near end and looks at him, and puts the flat of one hand on the stone coping the way he does, and Pascual puts his own hand next to it and leaves it there until the shaking stops.

"Nobody's taking you anywhere," he says, out loud, to an animal.

He is aware, saying it, that it is true of these two and untrue of nineteen others, and that this is what the whole of it comes down to and always was.

---

Reggie finds him there twenty minutes later and does not ask.

He puts his forearms on the rail next to him, about four inches away, and looks at the water, and after a while says:

"I've been standing in the office doorway for ten minutes not coming down here."

"Why?"

"Because I thought you'd want it on your own, and then I thought that's the sort of thing I'd have got wrong last year, so I've come down to ask." He turns his head. "Do you want it on your own?"

"No."

"Good."

And they stand at the rail for a while not saying anything, in the ordinary way, and then Pascual says, "Your paper's going to be late if you keep standing about," and Reggie says, "It's going to be late anyway, it's got four animals in it and one of them's stolen," and goes back up.

---

The second Bay Street letter comes on the eleventh of March.

Hester brings it out to him at the pens, which she never does, and he knows before she opens her mouth.

"He has worked it out."

"How?"

"The Zoological Society circulates the titles promised for its summer meeting. Reginald sent yours in on the second." She has it in her hand. "*Mortality in captive Nereopithecus following removal of a paired conspecific: four cases.* There is not a word in that title that anybody could object to, and if you are Mr Lecky it is the only thing you have thought about since Friday."

She reads it to him at the rail.

> We are instructed to say that our client considers the arrangement of the 2nd January to have been concluded on a basis which the Institute did not disclose, and that while our client makes no complaint of any breach of its terms, it regards the spirit of the arrangement as at an end.
>
> Our client will not correspond further with the Institute.
>
> We are instructed further that our client has written, and will continue to write, to such institutions and societies as it considers appropriate concerning Dr A. Spilsbury and the circumstances of his departure from its employment.

"That last one's the bill."

"That last one is the bill, and it was always going to be sent, and it is now going to be sent to everybody."

"For us."

"For us." Hester folds the letter. "For the paper he is not the author of, out of an arrangement he had no part in drafting, after evidence he gave against his own interest in front of a magistrate."

---

Pascual goes and sits in the long room that evening, which he does not do.

Spilsbury is at the end bench with the fourth jar of wet tissue and John's new pens laid out beside him in their case, because John leaves them there now for whoever needs them, and he does not look up.

"Mrs Bevan's had a letter."

"Bay Street."

"You knew."

"I have expected it since the second of January and I have been mildly surprised by the delay." He sets the forceps down. "How many institutions?"

"It says *such institutions and societies as it considers appropriate*."

"Then all of them. It is not expensive to do, and there is no hurry about it, and a man in Nassau with a typewriter can finish me in about four afternoons." Spilsbury picks the forceps up again. "It is precisely what I should do in their position, and I have no complaint whatever."

"You've not got a complaint."

"No."

"You'll be fifty in six years and you'll not work again."

"I am aware of the arithmetic, Pobil. I did it in November, on a Wednesday night, in this room, with a pencil, and I have not revised it since."

And he goes back to the tissue, and his hands are perfectly steady, and Pascual sits on the stool at the other end of the bench for twenty minutes without saying anything else.

---

The licence comes on the eighteenth.

Three years to the thirty-first of March 1960, in the Colonel's name, with two conditions: a nominated second signatory for the schedule, in writing, within twenty-eight days; and an annual return of holdings.

The Colonel reads it, and goes and gets the bottle that has been in the cupboard since the coronation and was not opened at Christmas, and pours seven glasses including one for Juan, who has come up to see what the shouting is, and makes a speech that is four sentences long, which is a record.

"Three years," he says. "In 1960 I shall be sixty-seven and I intend to be here and to be extremely tiresome about it. Mrs Bevan kept the bundle. Dr Tar was right in May and would not be moved. Dr Pobil, you are the reason paragraph three says what it says, and I have never in my life been better served."

He drinks the whole glass in one go and sits down, and his hand is not quite steady either.

---

The second signatory is settled in four minutes, at the same table, and it is Jean.

"Me?"

"You're here, you're sober, you can read a label and you'll say no to me." Pascual writes it on the form with the Colonel's pen. "It says *need not be medically qualified*. It doesn't say *must be a fool who signs what he's handed*."

"I'll say no to you quite often."

"You'll say no to me about once a year," says Pascual, "and that's the year it'll matter."

---

That night Reggie comes into his room at about eleven with a sheaf of foolscap under his arm and his glasses pushed up on his head, and says, "I've got a problem with the author line and I don't want to talk about it tonight," and puts the foolscap on the chair, and gets into the bed.

"Then don't talk about it."

"I'm not going to. I'm telling you it exists so you know what I'm like for the next fortnight."

"*Ay.* All right." Pascual moves over. "Come here."

It has been better since January. Not mended; he still has the thing about doorways since the inspection, and there are four days in September that neither of them has taken out and looked at since the hut. But there is a difference between a thing that is healing and a thing that is being lived around, and he has been a veterinarian for twenty-one years and he knows the difference by feel.

Reggie puts his head on Pascual's shoulder and is asleep in about ninety seconds, with his glasses still on, which Pascual takes off and puts on the crate.

---

He lies awake.

What he is thinking about is a man three rooms down the passage who has been finished, thoroughly and by professionals, for a paper that will have Reggie's name on it and a station that is not his and two animals he has never put a hand on.

He has hated that man for two and a half years, with reason, in writing, twice.

He goes through it the way he goes through a post-mortem, in order, without deciding anything in advance. The corridor in 1946 and the four years afterwards. The dinner table in May 1954. The pit on the third of July. *Because it did not cost me anything.* The fifty-one days. The hand above the elbow on a slipway in November, which he has not forgiven and does not intend to.

And the twenty-first of November, at twenty to nine in the morning, nine words on a telegraph form that are still in his breast pocket.

At about two o'clock he gets up, very carefully, and puts the blanket back over Reggie's shoulder, and goes along to the long room in the dark with a lamp.

He sits down at the end bench with a sheet of foolscap and Hester's good pen.

He is not a fluent writer and he knows it. He gets as far as *To whom it may concern* and stops for a long time.

Then he writes: *I have worked beside Dr Alec Spilsbury for ten months and I have no reason whatever to like him.*

And then he goes on.
