# The Thirty-First

He clears the bench on the twenty-eighth and it takes him two days, and Reggie works at the other end of the long room for both of them and they hardly speak at all.

It is the least sentimental thing Reggie has ever watched.

Every jar is labelled in a hand that does not vary: contents, fixative, date of fixation, date of transfer, and the accession number down the side. Every block is in order in the tray. The four appendices are copied fair, and the copies are on Hester's desk, and the originals are in the case with a sheet on top listing what is in the case, because a man who has been in a building ten months and is leaving it does not expect anybody to trust his memory, including himself.

On the second afternoon he brings the sheet over and puts it on Reggie's bench.

"You will want to check that against the tray before I go, because after Sunday there will be nobody to ask."

"I'll check it now."

"You will check it on Monday, when I am not standing here, because you will check it properly then."

"Alec —"

"Monday," says Spilsbury, and goes back to the other end.

---

Hester draws up a deposit form because Hester draws up a form for everything, and it is two paragraphs long and it is the best-tempered document in the file.

*Deposited with the Institute this 30th day of March 1957 by Dr A. Spilsbury: the anatomical material of one specimen of N. aestuarius, being the animal designated accession 197 in the ledger of its former holders; nine blocks, four jars of wet tissue, one plate sequence, and the daily record of 51 days. The depositor retains no interest and reserves no condition, save that the material be available on application to any qualified person.*

John gives it a number out of the Institute's own series and writes the label himself, in the new pens, and it takes him half an hour to do a label that would have taken him four minutes in January.

*W.M.I. 1957/1. Male, 4 yr. Recovered from captivity, d. 2.iv.56. Full record deposited.*

"Is that right, sir? *Recovered from captivity*?"

"It's right," says Reggie, and does not trust himself with anything longer.

---

On the thirtieth, at six in the evening, Spilsbury asks him to come down to the north pen, which he has not done in ten months.

He stands at the rail with his hat in his hand and looks at the two animals for about four minutes without saying anything. Chumley is at the seaward end. Gamma is in her corner under the shade board, doing nothing in particular.

"Your female is old," says Spilsbury.

"I know."

"You do not know, because nobody knows, because nobody has ever had one long enough. I am telling you what I think and you may have it for what it is worth, which is something." He does not look round. "She was full-grown when she was taken, and that was at least six years ago. She has had years of poor keeping and three of good. Her skin is going at the shoulder and she has slowed at the turn. I should be surprised if you have her in 1962 and astonished if you have her in 1965."

Reggie stands with his hands on the rail.

"And then Chumley's on his own."

"Then Chumley is on his own, at about thirteen years of age, in a pen he has never been alone in, having made the only bond either of you has ever documented." Spilsbury turns his head. "And you will be the first people in the history of the world who saw it coming."

---

They stand there while the light goes.

"You will want a protocol before it happens and not after," says Spilsbury. "Weights daily from the day she is off her food, not from the day she dies. Feed offered at the same hours by the same person. Somebody at that rail for an hour morning and evening with a watch, writing down what he does and not what you think he means. Photographs weekly. And a second observer who does not know what you expect, which is the part you will not like, because it will have to be Weald and you will want to tell him."

"I'd tell him."

"You would tell him inside a fortnight and the record would be worth a third of what it could be. Have Leslie hold the hypothesis and let the boy keep the book blind."

"That's cold."

"It is the only thing I have to give you," says Spilsbury, "and it is worth more than anything else I own, and in 1962 you will thank me for it and I shall not be there, which is satisfactory to both of us."

---

Reggie asks him, at the end, what he is going to do.

"There is a trade encyclopaedia being got up in Aberdeen which buys anatomical descriptions at three guineas the thousand words," says Spilsbury, "and a man who can write four thousand words a week about the viscera of food fishes will not starve, and I can write six. I have twelve hundred pounds. I shall take a room somewhere it does not cost anything to be alive and I shall write it, and in about two years the letters will have stopped circulating and somebody with a technical problem and no money will remember that I am cheap."

"That's not a career."

"No," he says. "It is a method. I have had twenty-two years of a career and I spent the whole of it declining to pay for anything, and I find I would rather have the two hundred pounds a year and the record."

---

On the thirty-first the boat comes at twenty past ten.

He asked for nobody at the jetty and gets all six of them, because this station has developed a habit and there is nothing whatever he can do about it.

John shakes his hand and cannot say anything and Spilsbury tells him his hatching is now better than his own ever was, which is not true and which John will carry for forty years.

Jean shakes his hand and says, "Your appendix two is wrong about the tide and I'm correcting it in proof," and Spilsbury says, "Good," and looks, for a moment, extremely pleased.

The Colonel shakes his hand and says nothing at all, which from the Colonel is a speech.

Hester gives him an envelope with the deposit receipt in it and one other thing, and says, "You will write to this Institute at least twice a year, Dr Spilsbury, because I intend to know where the material's depositor is, and I shall be extremely tiresome about it."

"Noted," says Spilsbury.

---

Then Pascual comes down the jetty with an envelope in his hand and gives it to him, unsealed.

Spilsbury opens it standing there, because he is not a man who puts a thing in his pocket to read later.

Reggie is four feet away and can see the first line, which is in Pascual's hand and takes up most of the width of the page:

*I have worked beside Dr Alec Spilsbury for ten months and I have no reason whatever to like him.*

Spilsbury reads the whole of it, which takes about forty seconds, and Reggie watches his face do nothing at all, and then watches it stop doing nothing at all for approximately a second and a half.

"There's a copy with Mrs Bevan," says Pascual. "Anybody writes to us about you, that goes back by return."

"Pobil —"

"It's not forgiveness," says Pascual. "Don't have it as that. It's a reference, and every line in it's true, and I'd say the whole of it to your face and most of it I have."

Spilsbury folds it along its creases.

"You have written that I am the best observer in this field," he says, "and that you would have me back in your building, and that a man may be worth employing and not worth liking."

"Yes."

"The last of those will cost you a certain amount with some readers."

"It's the line that'll get you the post," says Pascual, "because it's the one that proves I'm not doing you a favour."

And Spilsbury looks at him, and Reggie sees, for the second time in fourteen years, Alec Spilsbury come up against something for which he has no apparatus whatever.

"Thank you," he says.

He says it plainly, at ordinary volume, and puts the letter in his breast pocket, and that is the whole of it.

---

He goes down the ladder last.

Reggie goes down the jetty with him, which is a thing he has thought about for four days, and they stand at the top of the ladder for a moment with the boat knocking against the piles.

"Alec."

"Tar."

There is a very great deal available and neither of them takes any of it.

What Spilsbury does instead, because he is what he is, is look past Reggie's shoulder at the north pen for about two seconds and say: "Your male came up three times while we were standing there yesterday and each time he came up on her side of the pen. That is in nobody's book. Put it in."

"I'll put it in."

"Good," says Spilsbury, and shakes his hand, once, and gets into the boat.

---

He does not look back. Reggie had not expected him to, and had thought, a little, that he might, and stands on the planks watching the boat until it goes round the head.

Then Pascual comes and stands beside him, about four inches away, and they watch the empty water for a while in front of the entire Institute.

"Well," says Pascual.

"Well."

"You all right?"

"I've no idea," says Reggie. "Ask me at supper." He takes his glasses off and cleans them on his shirt. "Thank you for the letter."

"It wasn't for you."

"I know it wasn't. That's why I'm thanking you."

Pascual makes a noise and puts his hand flat between Reggie's shoulder blades, briefly, and takes it away again, and there are five other people on that jetty and not one of them is looking anywhere else.

---

They go back up the path.

Jean is already arguing about the tide in appendix two before they reach the top. John has the deposit label to finish and has decided it wants doing again. Hester has the file open on the hall table and is writing the day's entry in her own hand, and it will say that the depositor left at twenty past ten, and it will not say one other thing about it, and in ten years somebody will read it and think it was an ordinary morning.

The licence runs to 1960. The paper will be read at the summer meeting. There are nineteen animals in a cut with a sill across it, and thirty a year going into it, and a green cloth binding on a shelf in an office in Nassau that nobody at this Institute is at liberty to go after, this year.

Reggie stops at the top of the path and looks back down at the water.

Then he goes in, and gets a fresh book off the shelf, and rules it for daily weights, and writes the date at the top of the first page, and leaves the rest of it blank.
