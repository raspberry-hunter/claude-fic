# Primary Record

He wakes at ten past four because that is when he wakes, and his hand is still where he left it.

Reggie is asleep. Properly asleep, on his side, with his back against Pascual's chest and his knees drawn up a little and his glasses on the crate where Pascual put them at eleven; and Pascual's right hand is closed, loosely, round the outside of his left arm, a hand's width above the elbow, and has been, as far as he can tell, all night.

He does not move it.

He lies in the dark and takes the inventory, because that is what he does at ten past four and it has not failed him yet.

Yesterday, on a rock, he said the thing he has been lying awake on since the thirtieth of May. He said it at a volume that could be heard at the point, and in two languages, and the man did not flinch from any of it. And then he put his hand there, where the other one had been, and Reggie did not go still. Reggie leaned. His whole weight came sideways about four inches and his face went into Pascual's neck, and Pascual has been a veterinarian for twenty-one years and knows exactly the difference between an animal that freezes under a hand and an animal that comes to it, and has not had to think about which one he was looking at.

It has not made the fear go. He did not expect it to. What it has done is give the fear a size, which is more use to him than its going; a thing with a size can be carried.

The other thing has a size as well, and he has been not looking at it since Friday morning.

He takes his hand off the arm, very slowly, and gets up, and goes out onto the rocks in the dark.

---

The file is in Kingston.

He knows it by heart. He wrote most of it. *Specimen Beta, adult male, N. aestuarius.* Eleven days of clinical record in his own hand, serum and sediment and weights, every sheet true. A last entry on the seventeenth of May 1954. A death note on the twentieth: renal failure consequent on rhabdomyolysis, the animal having twice left the pen under its own power, not recovered. And a covering letter over Reggie's name, and two of John's plates that were not in it, because Pascual had taken them out with his own hands and put them in Hester's envelope to be lost.

Every sheet of it true, and the whole of it a lie, and he knew that when he signed it and would sign it again.

Because what was not in that file was the mechanism. An animal bonded to a man, and the man taken away from him and put in the other pen, and the animal going down: off his food, off the bottom, into the concrete, for eleven days, in front of the whole station. And the man put back. And the animal on the eighteenth and the nineteenth of May, very lively, would not keep still.

Nobody has had a cleaner record of it, before or since. And he wrote it up as the kidneys.

And a man stood at the rail of Gamma's pen two days later and heard *solitude* from him, and did not entirely believe the kidneys, and did not have the rest; and twenty-one months afterwards took a four-year-old out of a pair at four in the morning, certain he was a good enough husbandman to keep it, and sat and watched it put its face against a stone for thirty-one days.

Pascual sits on the rock in the dark with his arms round his knees and does not try to make that any smaller than it is.

---

Reggie comes out at a quarter to six with his glasses on and a blanket round his shoulders, and does not say anything for some while, and then sits down on the same rock.

Then he gets up again and goes and fights the primus.

Pascual watches him fight it for about four minutes, because it is the best thing that has happened this morning and he is not going to cut it short.

"It's a design fault."

"It's a primus."

"The jet's blocked."

"The jet's fine. You haven't pumped it."

"I've pumped it."

"You've pumped it twice. It wants twenty." He does not get up. "Twenty, Reggie. Count them. You're good at counting."

Reggie pumps it twenty times, counting under his breath, and it lights with a noise like a small animal, and he sits back on his heels looking so pleased with himself that Pascual has to look at the sea.

---

They drink the coffee.

"The file," says Pascual.

"Yes."

"I wrote it up as the kidneys."

"We wrote it up as the kidneys."

"I wrote it. My hand. Every sheet." He is looking at the water, which is going grey. "And it's sat in that office two and a half years with the one thing that'd have told him taken out of it. By me."

"Pascual —"

"And he read it, and didn't believe it, and didn't have what we'd taken out. And a four-year-old sat on the bottom of a fish pound for thirty-one days —"

"Stop."

He stops. Not because he means to; because of the voice.

"You told him *solitude*," says Reggie. "At that rail. Loudly, and true."

"To get rid of him."

"I don't care why. He had it. He wrote it down."

"He did."

"And then he went down a jetty at four in the morning and took an animal out of a pair, because he was certain he knew better than the warning." Reggie has put his cup down. "We gave him a wrong mechanism. You gave him the right warning. He took the animal."

"Reggie —"

"That's three things, and you've been sitting out here since four o'clock trying to carry all of them."

"You don't know how long I've been out here."

"The blanket on your side's cold."

Pascual shuts his mouth.

---

"And there's a fourth thing," Reggie says, "and it's the one you haven't said."

"Go on."

"If we'd written it up true, we'd have given him Charles."

The sea comes in over the low rocks and goes out.

"Yes," says Pascual.

"Then it was a price. We knew there'd be one."

"We didn't know —"

"We didn't know it'd be paid with somebody else's animal, two years on, in a pound we'll never see." Reggie's voice has not risen and does not. "That's the size of it. Not bigger."

"*Ay*."

"And it's ours, both, and I'm not watching you carry it on your own for five months. I've done that once."

Pascual looks at him.

"You've been saving that."

"Since Thursday week. I've had eight days in an office."

"*Ay*." He puts the heel of his hand to his eyes for a moment. "All right. Half."

"Half," says Reggie. "Don't take more than half. I'm not having it taken off me."

And Pascual laughs, badly, into his hand, because he said that yesterday on this rock and the man has given it straight back to him.

---

It comes up out of Reggie about ten minutes later. Pascual can see it coming; he has been watching it come for four years.

He goes quiet. He takes his glasses off and does not clean them. He looks at the sea as though the sea had a number on it.

"Say it," says Pascual.

"The notebooks."

Pascual goes cold.

"Four hundred pages, in the boy's hand, where they've been since 1954." Reggie is not looking at him. "Every day of the separation. What Beta ate, what he didn't, what he did in the water, what the boy saw when he went to the other pen. And the day it ended."

"Reggie —"

"It's the best record on earth of what that hypothesis says. There's nothing else close. It'd go in front of Averill and there'd be nothing more to say."

"*No*."

"I know."

He puts the glasses back on and turns round.

"I know. I'm not going to have them. I want them," he says. "I want them very badly, and I'm not going to have them, and I wanted you to hear me want it. Out loud, before, and not find it in a drawer."

Pascual sits on the rock.

He has had four years of this man wanting things, and he knows exactly what the wanting costs when it is this big; he has watched it cost an animal nearly its life, once. And the man has just set it down on a rock in front of him in plain words, and not walked off with any of it.

"Thank you," he says.

"It's two years and you still say it."

"I'll stop when it stops mattering." He puts his hand out and gets hold of the back of Reggie's neck. "If it ever comes to the animals against the boy, we'll have it out. Both of us. In a room."

"Loudly."

"At the top of our lungs. Hester'll minute it."

---

And then something happens that Pascual has been waiting since June to see, and had begun, privately, to think he would not.

Reggie says, "Hang on."

He says it to the sea. He takes Pascual's hand off his neck, not unkindly, the way a man moves a cup that is in his road, and stands up, and stands there.

"What."

"We don't need them."

"Reggie —"

"Hang *on*." He is walking now, three steps along the rock and three back. "What did Lecky say on the steps. About the dates."

"That he ought to have had it and he'd have it in March."

"And what's the one thing the Institute's case has that Lecky can't touch?"

"The number. One ninety-seven."

"In their own ledger. Which they can't deny without producing." He turns round. "So in March they produce it, or they concede it. And if they produce it, Averill has a book with a column for *date in* and a column for *date out* for every animal they've held since 1948."

"And no column for why."

"No column for why. We don't want one." He has both hands up. "What does a station like that keep, if it's diving animals on a line for a naval contract? Depth, duration, date, and which animal. It has to. It's what they're selling. There's a dive book."

Pascual sits very still.

"Two books. Their own. Nobody's taken anything out of either of them." Reggie's voice is going up and he is not stopping it. "Every animal out of that ledger dead since March of last year. Put the date out beside the dive book. Who was in its pen. Was that one on the line that week."

"And if it was —"

"Then it's the separation, in their hand, and nobody at this Institute has laid a finger on an animal. They've run the experiment every week for eighteen months and written it down twice without knowing." He stops. "Pascual, I need a pencil."

"There isn't a pencil."

"There's a pencil in my —" He pats his pockets. "There isn't a pencil."

"There's one in the crate," says Pascual. "Under the bread."

---

Reggie gets the pencil and has nothing to write on.

He looks round the rock. Then he holds his hand out, palm up, and Pascual understands, and takes the card out of his breast pocket, and gives it to him, and keeps the other thing where it is.

Reggie turns the card over, past *Rice*, and starts writing on the back of it in a hand that gets worse down the card, and talks the whole time.

"Hester files for production. Both books. On the ground that the accession's disputed and the applicant's the only party that holds the record. Averill can't refuse it without looking as though he's —"

"Go on."

"— and Spilsbury knows what the dive book's called and who signs it, so the application can name it, which it has to, or they'll say there isn't one —"

"Go on."

"— and Jean. Jean did the ports off insurance surveys. There'll be a survey on a diving barge, there has to be, somebody insures the compressor —"

"*Go on*," says Pascual, and finds he is grinning so hard his face hurts, and does not care who could see it, because nobody can.

Reggie stops writing and looks up.

"You're laughing at me."

"I'm not laughing at you."

"You are, you're —"

"I'm watching you," says Pascual. "I've not seen that since the fourth of July. Go on. Fill the card. I'll get the bread."

Reggie looks at him for a second longer, with an expression that Pascual would give a good deal to have John draw; and then he bends his head and fills the card.

---

It is a good plan. Pascual goes through it four times in the course of the morning looking for the hole, and the hole he finds is Spilsbury, and it is a small one, and he can live with it.

It costs no animal. It needs nobody's notebooks. It asks a department to read two books that a hostile party keeps for its own purposes, and it will take Hester three weeks to make it into a paper nobody can refuse.

He says so. Reggie says *yes*, and then *do you think so*, and then *yes* again, and eats three pieces of bread without noticing, which Pascual notices.

---

In the afternoon it is too hot to do anything, and they do nothing, on the rocks, in the shade of the hut wall, with their feet in a pool, and argue for an hour about whether Chumley can be said to prefer anything. It is the first full argument they have had since the second of November and it is, on both sides, completely unreasonable.

At about five Reggie stops arguing in the middle of a sentence and looks at him.

"What."

"You said yesterday you were going to take your time."

"I did."

"It's five o'clock."

"It is five o'clock." Pascual does not move. "I'm taking my time."

"You're sitting in a rock pool."

"I'm looking at you," says Pascual. "I'm allowed."

---

He takes him in at half past five with the sun low and the window full of it, and the whole of the hut gone the colour of the inside of a shell.

He does it slowly on purpose. He has been promising himself this since a hotel in Kingston, and he has had five months of it going wrong, and he is not going to hurry one second.

He gets the shirt off him first, button by button, standing, with Reggie's hands hanging at his sides and his face doing its best to be patient and not managing it.

"You're doing this deliberately."

"Yes."

"There are eight buttons, Pascual."

"There are seven. That one's been gone since August." He gets the seventh. "You'd not noticed."

"I'd noticed. I was waiting for somebody to —"

He does not finish, because Pascual has put his mouth to the hollow at the base of his throat, and holds it there, and feels the swallow go past under his lips.

---

Then his hands.

He puts them up on either side of Reggie's face, both of them, and holds it, and waits; because the last time he did this in a room with a bolt on the door, the man turned his head away, and Pascual has thought about that every day since and will not pretend otherwise.

Reggie does not turn his head.

He looks straight up at him, in the gold light, with his glasses off and his eyes not quite focused, and he lifts his own hands and puts them on Pascual's face, one each side, the answer, the way it is done; and they stand there in the middle of a Navy hut holding on to each other's faces like two people at the bottom of a pen, and neither of them says anything at all for a while.

"There," says Pascual, when he can.

"There," says Reggie. "I'm here."

"Say it again."

"I'm here. I'm in a hut with a door that doesn't lock properly and a man who's taking a quarter of an hour over seven buttons, and I'm here."

"Say it in Spanish."

Reggie's eyes shut. His mouth moves, working it out.

"*Estoy aquí.*"

And he has got it right, the whole thing, the ending and all, and Pascual laughs out loud with his hands still on the man's face and feels Reggie's hands tighten on his.

"Who taught you that?"

"Juan. In October. I paid him in cigarettes."

"*Ay, Dios*. In October."

"I was saving it," says Reggie, with dignity, and then Pascual kisses him, because there is no other available response.

---

It goes on for a long time and he takes all of it.

He gets him down on the cot and takes the rest of his clothes off him an item at a time, and then his own, and then he does not do anything for a while except look, with one hand flat in the middle of Reggie's chest, feeling it go up and down. The light is on the whole length of him. There is a line of sunburn at the throat where the collar stops, and a white stripe across the wrist where the watch goes, and a bruise on the outside of the shin from the rocks yesterday that the man has plainly not noticed.

"You're making an inventory."

"I'm making an inventory."

"Of what."

"Of you. You've a bruise on your leg."

"I haven't —" Reggie looks. "I have a bruise on my leg."

"From yesterday. You were sitting on the rocks watching me walk away and you never felt it." He puts his mouth to it, very lightly, and feels the leg jump. "I'll have that one."

"Pascual —"

"And this."

The inside of the knee. Then the long muscle of the thigh, outside, where it runs up to the hip, and the bone of the hip itself, where it stands up under the skin and where, if he puts his teeth to it very carefully, the man makes a noise that is not a word at all.

"And that."

"*God*."

"Not God. Me."

"You're *insufferable* —"

"I'm taking my time," Pascual says, against his ribs, "as advertised," and takes the ribs one at a time, the way he does, counting, and at the fourth one Reggie's hands come down into his hair and shut there.

---

"Come up here."

"No."

"*Pascual*."

"Ask me properly."

"Come up here, please, because I want your weight, and I have wanted it since Thursday, and I'm not asking again."

"You are," says Pascual, "you'll ask me three more times," but he goes, because that is a man asking for a thing he wants with his whole face, and there has been very little of that in five months.

He gets his weight down along the length of him, slowly, forearms taking some of it, until Reggie pulls him down the rest of the way with both hands flat on his back; and they lie like that, skin to skin in the heat, and Reggie lets out a breath that goes on for a long time.

"Yes," he says. "That."

"Too much?"

"Not nearly."

---

Then Pascual puts his mouth to the outside of Reggie's left arm, a hand's width above the elbow.

He does it on purpose, and he does it where Reggie can see, and he holds it there.

Reggie laughs. It is a real laugh, winded, under all that weight.

"You're not subtle."

"I'm not trying to be subtle."

"You're marking it."

"I'm *putting something on it*," says Pascual, into the arm, "so the next time you think about that place you've something else to think about. Hold still."

"I'm holding still."

"You're not. You're laughing."

"I'm allowed," says Reggie, and then does not laugh, because Pascual has put his teeth there too, carefully, and the noise that comes out of him instead is the one from the ribs.

---

After that there is not much in it that is slow.

It is not fast, either. It is two men on an iron cot in a hut on the wrong side of an island with nowhere to be, going at it like men who have been kept from each other, and talking the whole way through: Reggie telling him, plainly and in some detail, what he wants and where and how hard, and getting it; and Pascual answering in two languages, most of it not fit for a book and some of it not language at all.

"There — Pascual, *there* —"

"I know where."

"Then *stay* there —"

"I'm staying." His mouth is at the join of the neck, the place with no defences. "Look at me."

"I can't, I haven't got my —"

"You don't need them. Look at me."

Reggie looks at him. His eyes are wet and wide and not focused on anything and entirely there.

And it is at that point, with the man looking straight at him, that the thing Pascual has been carrying since the thirtieth of May comes up his throat without asking, and he stops.

He just stops. Holds still, with his forehead down against Reggie's, breathing.

"What."

"Nothing. Give me a minute."

"It's not nothing. We've had *nothing*." Reggie's hands come up off his back and take hold of his face again. "Say it."

"I thought you'd go."

He says it into the man's mouth, very low, and it is the stupidest thing he has ever said in bed and the truest.

Reggie does not answer it with anything clever.

"I'm here," he says. "Ask me where I am."

"Where are you."

"Here. Under you. In a hut. With my arm marked." A breath. "Ask me again."

"Where are you."

"*Aquí*," says Reggie. "*Contigo*. Is that right?"

It is right. Pascual cannot tell him so, for a moment, and does not need to, because the man can feel his face.

"That's right," he says, eventually.

"Then go on."

"Reggie —"

"Go on," says Reggie, and pulls him down, and Pascual goes on.

---

Afterwards they lie in the last of the light with the sheets on the floor and the window gone from gold to grey, and Reggie is across him with his ear on the winch scar, entirely boneless, and Pascual's hand is moving slowly on his back without being told.

The sea is doing what it does. Somewhere out on the reef a bird makes one remark and stops.

"It's not mended," says Reggie, after a long time.

"No."

"I can feel where it isn't."

"So can I." Pascual's hand does not stop. "It's better than Thursday."

"It's better than June."

"It's better than June," Pascual agrees, and then, because it is true and he is tired and there is nobody within half a mile: "It's going to take a while."

"I know."

"I'm not going anywhere while it does."

"No," says Reggie, into the scar. "Nor am I. Thirty years."

"Thirty years," says Pascual, straight back, and it is the same as it was in June and in October, no half second in it; and this time, for the first time since the summer, it is not the only true thing in the room.

---

They walk back round the seaward side on Sunday afternoon with the crate between them and the card in Reggie's pocket, and Hester is on the verandah before they have reached the top of the path.

She takes one look at the pair of them and says nothing about it whatever, which is a thing Pascual will be grateful to her for until he dies.

What she says is: "Well?"

And Reggie takes the card out and gives it to her, *Rice* side down.

Hester reads the back of it standing up. She reads it twice. Then she sits down, which Pascual has now seen her do three times, and says, "John. Get the minute book. And Miss Leslie, and my husband, and Dr Spilsbury, in that order, and tell Juan's mother there will be six for supper and none of them will be on time."

---

It takes two hours and it is loud.

Jean wants the insurance survey and gets into an argument with Reggie about whether a diving barge is a vessel or a plant, and wins it. John wants to know whether the dive book will have the animals' numbers or names, and Reggie says numbers, and John says *you don't know that*, and Reggie says *no, I don't*, and John writes it down, very pleased. The Colonel says that an application for production is the most aggressive thing this Institute has ever done and that he approves of it entirely. Hester says she will need three weeks, and then, looking at the card again, says two.

Spilsbury comes last, and reads the card, and stands there with it for longer than Pascual has seen him hold anything.

"Yes," he says. "It is called the Diving Register. It is kept by the chief handler and countersigned weekly by the medical officer, and it is in a green cloth binding, and it lives in the same office as the ledger, on the shelf above."

"Obviously," says Reggie.

"It is not obvious." Spilsbury puts the card down on the table. "I sat in that office for four months with both books on the same shelf. I did not think of it."

The verandah goes quiet, briefly.

"I should like that minuted," Spilsbury says to John, "because I do not expect to say it often," and John minutes it.

---

Pascual finds him at the slipway at dusk, on the bollard, with his hat on his knee.

He does not sit down.

"You put your hand on him."

"I did."

"He told me."

"I observed on Thursday week that he would." Spilsbury does not look round.

"If you do it again, I'll put you through a window."

"I shall not do it again," says Spilsbury. "I did it once, knowing what it cost, and I have paid for it. There is nothing further on that side of the page."

Pascual looks at the back of his head for a while.

"No," he says. "There isn't."

---

Then he takes the telegraph form out of his breast pocket, and unfolds it, and holds it where the man can see.

Spilsbury looks at it.

"You have kept that."

"I'm keeping it."

"I supposed you might."

"Thank you for it," says Pascual.

Spilsbury does not say anything for some while. It is, Pascual thinks, very possibly the first time anybody has watched him try to find the apparatus for that and come up empty-handed.

"It was the correct instruction," he says at last.

"I know it was. That's not what I'm thanking you for."

"No," says Spilsbury, and for once does not ask what it is.

---

Pascual goes up the path.

Behind him the sea is doing what it does, and ahead of him, from the verandah, where the lamps are lit, he can hear Reggie and Jean at full volume about whether a compressor is fixed plant or moveable, and John telling them both to be quiet so he can write it down, and Hester telling John that he may write down *argument* and leave it at that.

Down at the north pen two animals move about in the dark, not alone.

Pascual folds the telegraph form along its creases and puts it back in his pocket, where it stays; and goes up towards the noise.
