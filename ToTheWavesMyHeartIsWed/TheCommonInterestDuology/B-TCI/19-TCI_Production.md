# Production

The objection runs to twenty-three pages and four enclosures and she writes the whole of it by hand between the twenty-sixth and the twenty-ninth of November, because she does not trust a typewriter with a document she may have to swear to.

She has done this sort of thing thirty times. Not with material like this.

*Enclosure A.* The daily record of accession one hundred and ninety-seven, fifty-one sheets, in the hand of Dr A. Spilsbury, with the weights.

*Enclosure B.* The account, in twelve sections, signed and attested.

*Enclosure C.* The condition of the adult male recovered on the twenty-sixth of April: the belt with four settings of holes; the maker's schedule from Walsall; the ninety-one sagittae and the cable from Plymouth; the seven foci of dead bone; the plate sequence, four hundred and nineteen to five hundred and fourteen, with no gap in it.

*Enclosure D.* Miss Leslie's nine ports, with a query against three of them and a note in her own hand that six of the nine are wrong and that the Institute has been told so by a witness who declines to say which.

She reads that note four times and leaves it in. A document that admits what it does not know is a document a clerk will believe about what it does.

---

Reginald reads the whole thing at her table on the twenty-ninth, for two hours, and alters nothing.

Then he says, "Page nine."

"Yes."

"*The Institute does not assert that the applicant has caused the death of any animal.*"

"That is correct as it stands."

"It's correct and it's the best sentence in the document." He puts it down. "Who's it for?"

"Mr Averill, in about four minutes' time, when he has stopped looking for the place where we overreach." She squares the page. "I have been writing that sentence into documents since 1931. It is the only reason anybody has ever read the rest of one."

---

They take Dr Spilsbury across to the Bay on the twenty-eighth in Alleyne's boat, and he is attested in the magistrate's room by Mr Purdie, who is seventy-one and has been a magistrate since before the Colonel came out and who reads every word of every document he witnesses, which is the entire reason Hester chose him.

It takes an hour and fifty minutes. Purdie asks two questions.

The first is whether the animal was in the applicant's lawful possession at the time it was removed.

"It was in their pen," says Spilsbury. "I have no opinion on the law and no intention of pretending to one."

The second is whether he was paid, at any time, by any person, for the account he is signing.

"No."

Purdie writes it down.

"And I should like the next part written down as well." He does not raise his voice, which Purdie has plainly not expected. "I have a motive. I was dismissed in February, I resent it, and I am giving evidence against the men who dismissed me."

"Doctor —"

"Anybody who reads this ought to have that in front of him at the same time as the rest."

Purdie looks at him for a while over the top of his spectacles.

"I have been doing this for forty years," he says, "and nobody has ever asked me to put that in."

"Then it is about time somebody did."

---

He signs each of the twelve sections and initials every alteration and dates the lot, and his hand does not vary from the first sheet to the last; Hester watches it particularly, because she has watched a good many men sign things.

Out on the water going back, with the case on his knees, he says: "Mrs Bevan."

"Doctor."

"When that is answered, I shall be of no further use to you as a witness."

"You will be of no further use to anybody as anything. Lecky will have your name inside a week and that letter will go to every institution you might reasonably write to."

"Yes." He looks at the water for about four seconds. "May I have the use of a bench until March?"

And Hester Bevan, who is sixty-two and has spent twenty-eight years reading what men leave off the page, sits in the stern of a fish boat and understands that she has just been asked for something, in the plainest available words, by a man who has not asked her for anything since the thirty-first of May.

"You may have the use of a bench," she says, "and the middle room at the end, and your board, and as much of Juan's mother's cooking as you can eat, until the last day of March."

"Thank —"

He stops, as though somebody had used his voice without asking him.

"Noted," he says, and looks rather sharply out to sea.

Neither of them mentions it.

---

The objection goes in on the thirtieth by the government launch, and the answering slip comes back on the fourth of December with a departmental number on it, and nothing whatever happens for nine days.

Hester has warned all six of them that nothing whatever will happen for nine days. It does not help in the slightest. John manufactures errands. Jean rereads her ports. The Colonel walks the sea wall twice a day with his hands behind his back, and on the seventh he tells her at the dressing table that he has written to his sister for the first time since 1953, which is how she knows.

She uses the nine days on the other application, which is Reginald's, which is the best thing anybody in this building has thought of this year, and which she would not have thought of at all.

---

It comes to her on the back of an index card with *Rice* written on the other side.

She has it copied out fair and then she works at it for nine days, because an application for production is not an argument; it is a hole of an exact size that a department can drop a thing through, and the size has to be right or the thing will not go.

The form of words comes by cable from her cousin in Bristol, who charges her nothing and takes four days and sends back seventeen words of which she keeps six. Jean finds the survey on a diving barge in the *Fish Trades Gazette* index for 1955, which is not proof of anything and establishes that such a vessel exists and is insured, which is all that is wanted. Spilsbury gives her the register by name, binding and shelf, and signs a separate sheet saying so, and does not ask what she intends to do with it.

The application is two pages long. It asks the Secretary to require the applicant to produce two books: the accession ledger, on the ground that the applicant's own accession number is in dispute and no other party can produce it; and the Diving Register, on the ground that the objection turns upon whether the applicant's animals are separated in the course of its operations, and that the applicant is the only holder of the record.

It does not say what they expect to find. She takes that out on the eleventh, having put it in on the ninth, and is not sorry.

It goes in on the fourteenth.

---

On the sixth she goes over about the ice, because the Institute's ice bill is hers and because Mr Alleyne likes to be asked about his wife's knee.

She is not on an errand. She wants to be clear about that afterwards, in her own head, because it is the only piece of luck this Institute has had since April and she would rather not dress it up as method.

Somewhere between the knee and the price of a hundredweight, Mr Alleyne says: "And your insurance fellow. Did he ever come to you?"

Hester puts her cup down.

"Which insurance fellow?"

"September. Antilles Mutual, the card said."

"Asking what?"

"About the station, for a survey. The plant. The generator." He is entirely comfortable. "How many men there were. Whether the doctors lived in."

"And Wilfred told him."

"Wilfred gave him what he had. The ice contract's insured out of the same office, and we'd been told there'd be a man."

"Had you been told there'd be a man?"

Mr Alleyne thinks about this for some little while.

"We'd been told there'd be a man," he says, "by the man."

---

She sits with that on the boat and it is very cold for December.

Not the island. Not a person in the village with a grievance; not Juan's brother's cousin answering a question in a shop, which is what she said on the twenty-fourth of October and has believed since. A card, and a plausible office, and a clerk who was glad to help, and nine or ten questions of which one was *whether the doctors lived in*.

It was collected. Somebody went and got it, in September, from four hundred yards away, and had it in a drawer in Nassau before anybody at the Martin knew the licence was in question.

She thinks, on the whole, that Mr Lecky did not know. She thinks that because of ninety seconds on the steps of the Secretariat in November, which she has had from Dr Pobil twice, word for word, and because a man who was doing that to you does not stop on a pavement to tell you that nobody at his client's wishes your colleague ill.

She thinks it and she writes down, in her own book, that she thinks it and that she cannot support it.

---

She tells them together, in the office, with the door shut, on the seventh: Reginald and Dr Pobil, and nobody else at all.

She has thought about this for a whole night and she is going to do it the way she should have done a number of things in June.

"It was not anybody on this island," she says, "and it was not Dr Spilsbury, and it was not an accident. A man with a card from an insurance agency asked Alleyne's clerk nine or ten questions in September, and one of them was whether the doctors lived in."

Neither of them says anything for a moment.

"*Ay*," says Pascual.

"September."

"The clerk thinks the second week."

"Before the letter about disposal."

"Five weeks before it, and seven before the paragraph." She keeps her hands still on the desk. "So you may both stop wondering which of the people you eat with did it, and I am aware that you have both been doing it since October, and so is John."

"I wasn't wondering about John."

"You were wondering about Juan."

"I was not."

"You asked him twice in October whether anybody had been down at the fish house."

"*Ay, Dios.*"

"You were wondering about somebody, Dr Pobil, and it was making you careful, and I have had a bellyful of the pair of you being careful."

And Pascual Pobil laughs out loud, once, and Reginald looks at the desk with the corner of his mouth going, and Hester has the sensation of a window being opened in a room she had stopped noticing the smell of.

---

"Then it's in a drawer in Nassau."

"It is in a drawer in Nassau and it will stay there until it is worth taking out. That is what that kind of thing is for."

"And in January there's an inspector on this island for four days."

"There is. Which brings me to the thing I am going to say once, and I shall be brief about it, and neither of you is to be delicate with me afterwards."

She sees Reginald go absolutely still, and Dr Pobil's hands go flat on his knees, and she carries on, because being frightened of this is precisely how the rest of it was allowed to happen.

"For four days in January the pair of you are two men who work together. Nothing else. Not in a doorway, not on the path, not at my table."

"Ma'am —"

"I am not asking you to be anything you are not. I am telling you what a man with that paragraph in his folder will be looking for, and that he will find it in a doorway and not in a bedroom."

"Yes."

"The inspector sleeps in the bungalow, where I can hear him. And the signals hut is a perfectly good building and Juan's brother has put a new hasp on the door."

There is a silence of about two seconds.

"Mrs Bevan," says Pascual, "you are a very frightening woman."

"I am a woman who has kept records for twenty-eight years," says Hester, "and I intend to keep the two best men in this Institute as well. Go away, both of you."

---

The reply to the production application comes on the twenty-first of December, in the same post as three Christmas cards and a bill for lamp oil.

It is not from the Secretariat. That is the first thing, and she stands in the hall with it for some while before she opens it, because a party does not write to you direct about an application unless it would rather the department were not the first to answer.

> DEWAR & LECKY
> Consignment Agents
> Bay Street, Nassau
>
> Mrs H. Bevan
> William Martin Institute for Marine Studies
>
> Dear Madam,
>
> We are instructed by our client in the matter of the Institute's application of the 14th instant.
>
> Our client is advised that the application is misconceived and that its books are its own; and is of the view that a contest of the present kind serves neither party, the Institute being small and the season for such matters short.
>
> Accordingly, and without any admission whatever, our client is prepared to withdraw its application of the 18th October for the transfer of the Institute's two specimens, upon the Institute's withdrawal of its application of the 14th instant, the two withdrawals to be simultaneous and neither party to be at liberty to renew.
>
> Our client would further be glad to discuss a standing arrangement for the supply to the Institute of preserved material, of which it has a considerable quantity, upon terms which we are confident the Institute would find generous.
>
> Mr Lecky expects to be in Kingston in the second week of January and would put himself entirely at your convenience.
>
> We remain, etc.

---

Hester reads it three times standing up, which is how she reads everything, and then she sits down on the hall chair, which she has not done in that hall since 1946.

Because there are two sentences in it that matter and she has both of them at the first reading.

*Neither party to be at liberty to renew.* Gamma and Chumley stay on this island, in a licensed pen, for the rest of their lives, and no application is ever made for them again, and the licence goes through in March with a clerk's signature on it and nobody asking a single question.

And: *its books are its own.* Which they will remain. Which means nineteen animals in a cut with a sill across it, and thirty a year out of the sea, and a column for *date in* and a column for *date out* and none at all for *why*, for as long as the chequebook holds.

It is, on the whole, a great deal better than anything she thought she could get in August. It is the best offer this Institute will ever be made. And a man who has read a bill for twenty years knows one when he is handed it.

---

She puts it on the verandah table at four o'clock with all seven of them there.

She reads it out herself, every word, because she is not going to let anybody hear it in pieces, and then she says the thing she has been arranging in her head since the hall chair, and she says it without any decoration at all.

"It is a good offer. It gives us the two animals for good, the licence in March, and the end of a fight we were losing."

She puts her hand flat on the sheet.

"As the holder of the bundle, and as the person responsible for the specimens, I am obliged to advise this Institute to take it. I do so advise."

Nobody says anything at all.

The Colonel has not moved. Jean is looking at the sheet as though she could read it through the back. John has his pencil in his hand and has not written a word.

Dr Spilsbury says, "Well. Yes. That is what I should have done in their position, and rather earlier."

And Reginald Tar, who refused a thing in a second and a half in May and in four seconds in July, and who did not refuse at all on the fourteenth of August, does not say anything either.

He picks the letter up. He reads the third paragraph again, and then the fourth. Then he puts it down and says:

"How long have we got?"

"Mr Lecky is in Kingston in the second week of January. The inspector is here on the twenty-first."

"Then we've three weeks."

"You have three weeks."

"And the objection?"

"Stands until we withdraw it."

"And the production?"

"Stands until we withdraw that too. They have not asked the Secretary for anything, you will notice. They have asked me."

---

"It's not three weeks," says Pascual.

Everybody looks at him. He has not sat down; he is at the end of the table with both hands on the back of a chair, and he is not shouting, which Hester notes, because she has been at this table with that man when he was.

"Dr Pobil?"

"It's three weeks for Gamma and Chumley." He says it perfectly evenly. "For the nineteen it's however long the ice holds, and then it's another thirty next year, and I'd like that on the paper before anybody says the word *careful* about this offer, because somebody at this table is going to."

"That is the second time you have asked me to minute that."

"It's the second time it's been true, ma'am."

Hester picks up her pencil.

Then she puts it down again, and looks at the seven of them round the table in the last of the afternoon — at her husband, who is sixty-three, and at a draughtsman of twenty-six with a pencil he has not used, and at the best man in the field, who is holding his glasses, and at a man with no career left sitting in the good chair with his hat on his knee — and she does the thing she has not done once in twenty-eight years of keeping other people's records.

"John," she says. "Write the date. Leave the rest."

"Ma'am?"

"The date, and *no decision taken*, and then put the book away and go and have your supper." She pushes the letter into the middle of the table where all of them can reach it. "Because we are going to be a fortnight about this, and I would rather the book showed that we took a fortnight than that anybody had been quick."
