# Nil

He is on the jetty at a quarter to four, which is early enough to be indefensible and late enough that he can tell himself otherwise, and he has a card in his breast pocket.

It is an index card from the plate cupboard and it has six lines on it in his own hand. *Jean — pens, Fri to Sun, John to assist. Juan's brother — oil, water, food to signals hut, Thurs a.m. Colonel — leave, three days. Cover: bone series, writing up. Hester — told. Rice.*

He wrote it on Monday. He has taken it out perhaps nine times since and put it back, and every time he has been aware of what the fifth line is for, which is the paragraph in the minute book that John read out in October; and every time he has been aware that a man who writes *cover* on a card in order to spend three days alone with somebody is a man who has been read the bill for it, over wine, in 1954, and has kept the receipt.

He puts it back in his pocket.

There is a place on the outside of his left arm, a hand's width above the elbow, that he has been aware of for a week and would very much prefer not to be.

---

Alleyne's boat comes round the head at six minutes past four, low in the water with the ice, and he watches it the whole way in.

The Colonel is first up the ladder, and says "Tar," and gets "Sir" back, and looks at him for about a second and a half, and then goes up the path at a pace nobody on this island has seen him use, which is the most tactful thing Reggie has watched a man do in a long time.

Then Pascual.

He has caught the sun across the nose and the tops of the cheeks, the way he does when he has been standing about in a town instead of working, and his face has gone in slightly under the bones, and his shirt is stuck to him down the whole length of his back. He hands the trunk up to Juan's brother. He comes up the ladder. And when he is standing on the planks, the first thing his right hand does is go to his breast pocket and rest there, flat, for a moment, as if checking that something is still in it.

Juan's brother is six feet away with the trunk. So they shake hands.

---

"Four months," says Pascual.

"I had it Tuesday night. Hester read the cable out twice."

"And an inspection."

"In the new year. I know."

"And Averill wrote *welfare* in a minute where nobody's —"

"I know. I have had it by cable and I've had it from Hester, and I'll have it from you properly at six." He lets go of the hand, eventually. "You've had Hester's second page."

Pascual looks at him.

"You were in the room."

"I was in the room."

"*By you*," says Pascual, not loudly. "She's put that in. Two words, at the end of a sentence, where she knew I'd stop."

"Yes."

Pascual looks out at the water for a while, and then back.

"Not on a jetty," he says.

"No. Not on a jetty." Reggie takes the card out of his pocket and holds it out. "I have arranged the three days."

---

Pascual reads it.

He reads it twice, which he does with everything, and on the second time his mouth does something at the corner, and on the last line it gives up altogether.

"*Rice*."

"Juan's mother. It's in the hut with a cloth over it."

"You wrote it on a *card*."

"I wrote it on a card."

"*Cover: bone series.*" Pascual is laughing, properly, for the first time Reggie has heard since about June, with his head down. "*Ay, Dios.* You've told the Colonial Secretary we're writing up femoral heads."

"If he asks."

"He won't ask."

"No. But if he does."

Pascual puts the card into his breast pocket, and Reggie sees that there is already something in there, folded small, and that the card goes in behind it and not in front.

"You'd a thing to ask me," Pascual says. "On the jetty, on the thirteenth. The other one."

"At the hut."

"At the hut," Pascual agrees, and picks up his end of the trunk.

---

They report at six on the verandah because they are not animals, and it is the best hour this station has had since April.

The Colonel tells it in order and with a certain amount of relish, and tells his wife, at the end, in front of everybody, that nobody is going in a lighter in January.

"I said in August that they would," says Hester. "I was wrong. I intend to enjoy it until at least Tuesday."

Jean wants Mr Hoyte's single word exactly, and gets it, and writes it down. John takes the minutes and underlines *welfare* twice, and then looks at what he has done and does not rub it out.

Pascual gives the hearing in about four minutes and stops talking at the end of it, and Reggie watches Hester watch him stop, and sees her nod, once, to herself.

He has seen something else already, at a quarter past five, from the office window: Pascual going up the bungalow path on his own before the report, and going in, and coming out again ten minutes later without looking round. Hester has said nothing about it. Hester has also not, at any point this evening, looked at Pascual Pobil the way she looked at him on the fourth of October, and Reggie has been in rooms with that woman for four years and knows what that costs her and what it means.

---

Spilsbury has been at the far end of the table the whole hour, with his hands flat on it, and has not said a word.

At the end, when the chairs go back, Pascual walks the length of the verandah and stops in front of him.

Reggie is at the rail with his back half turned, and stays there, because he is not going to be the man who comes and stands next to it.

"You were told," says Pascual.

"I was. At the rail of Gamma's pen." Spilsbury does not get up. "You were loud."

"And you went and took one anyway."

"Yes."

"And kept him on his own. Fifty-one days."

"Yes."

Pascual's hands have come up off his sides. Reggie watches them come up, and stop, and go flat against his own thighs, and stay there; and watches the man's shoulders go down about an inch with what looks like a great deal of effort.

"The weights were good," says Pascual.

"They were."

"I used it."

"I know. The Colonel cabled." A pause. "It was well used."

"That's all I've got to say to you tonight."

"It is more than I had allowed for," says Spilsbury.

Pascual turns round and walks back down the verandah, and Spilsbury looks after him, and then, for about a second, past him, at Reggie; at the left arm, a hand's width above the elbow; and then he gets up and goes down to the slipway.

---

The old signals hut is half a mile round the seaward side and they walk it in the dark without a lamp, because they both know the path.

It is twelve feet by fourteen and was put up by the United States Navy in 1943 and has a window on the water and nothing else worth mentioning. Juan's brother has swept it and left a lamp, a primus, a crate of food, four cans of water and a pile of clean sheets folded on the end of an iron cot, and on top of the crate there is a covered dish, and under the cloth there is rice with the fish in it.

Pascual stands in the middle of the floor and looks at the dish for rather a long time, with the face he gets about kindness, which nobody else on this island would recognise as gratitude.

"No pickle," he says.

"No pickle."

"That's two and a half years."

"I noticed in March of last year. I told you."

"You told me, and you've still bought it every month since." He sits down on the end of the cot, heavily, like a man at the end of ten hours in a boat. "Ask me, then."

---

Reggie gets the lamp going first, because his hands want something to do.

Then he turns round.

"Are you still frightened of me?"

Pascual does not answer straight away. He looks at his own hands, which are hanging between his knees, and turns them over.

"Not of you," he says.

"Then of what?"

"Of the morning." He looks up. "I'm going first. I made up my mind at twenty to four on Tuesday morning in a hotel in Kingston, and I wrote it on the notepaper and burned it, so it doesn't count as having written anything."

Reggie makes a noise that is very nearly a laugh.

"All of it. In order. Don't let me off any of it."

"I won't."

"You'll want to."

"I'll want to," Reggie says, "and I won't."

"*Bueno*." Pascual reaches for the dish. "Then eat this with me, because I've had nine days of a temperance hotel and I'm not doing any of it on boiled mutton."

---

They eat it out of the dish between them with two forks, sitting on the cot, and it is very good.

Afterwards they lie down, because there is nothing else in the hut to do, and Pascual is asleep inside two minutes with one arm across Reggie's chest, the way a man sleeps who has not slept in a week.

Reggie lies awake under the arm.

He has had eight days since the slipway to work out how to say it, and he has not worked it out, and at some point on about the Sunday he stopped trying, because a thing arranged in advance is a thing he would put the coat on to say, and he is not going to say this in the coat.

What he has instead is the order it happened in, which is the one arrangement of it he can trust.

He lies in the dark and listens to the sea, and to a heart going under his ear, and does not do arithmetic.

At ten past four the arm across him shifts, because that is when Pascual wakes, and a hand comes up and takes hold of the back of his neck, and stays there, and neither of them says anything until it is light.

---

Pascual starts at eight, on the rocks outside the hut, with coffee off the primus and the sun already on their feet.

He does not clear his throat and he does not say *well*. He puts his cup down on the rock beside him and says it the way he gives a finding: short, in order, with nothing on it.

"The page first."

Reggie waits.

"Second of September. Half past five in the morning, your office. Four sheets under the order book." He is looking at the water. "I didn't turn it over because I couldn't have asked you what it was. That isn't a reason. It's the thing that was wrong."

"Pascual —"

"I'm sorry for it."

He says it plainly and does not put anything round it. Reggie has heard that man say *perdón* in bed a dozen times and *sorry* at a table as often as anybody, and has not, he realises, heard him say this in this voice.

"I left it there four weeks," Reggie says. "In plain sight. I told myself that was the same as showing it to you."

"It wasn't."

"No. So half of that one's mine."

"Half." Pascual looks round. "Don't take more than half. I'm not having it taken off me."

"Half," Reggie agrees.

---

"Hester."

"Yes."

"Seventh of September. She stood in the long room and asked me had I anything on paper, and I said no, and the letter's twelve feet from my hand the whole time." He rubs his thumb along the rim of the cup. "I went to her last night, before the report. I wasn't waiting to be asked."

"I saw you go in."

"I thought you might." He does not seem to mind. "She's going to write a line under yesterday's date. *Correction to entry of seven nine.* In her own hand."

"And the *nil*?"

"Stays. She won't strike it out." Pascual picks the cup up. "She said you don't rub a thing out in a record. You write the right one underneath and leave the wrong one where it is, so anybody reading can see what happened."

Reggie does not say anything for a moment.

"The boy's packets," he says.

"The boy's packets." Pascual's voice goes somewhere for about a second and comes back. "She said that too. I didn't know she'd seen them."

"Hester's seen everything."

"*Ay*. I know that now."

---

"The letter itself, you've had. I'd write it again and I'd show it you first. That's not changed."

"I've had it."

"Right." He sets the cup down again. "Third of July."

Reggie goes still.

"The pit. He asked me how you're stopped. I told him what I thought, and I didn't tell you he'd asked, and you had it off him in October on the slipway, which is worse, and that's mine. All of it. Not half."

"All right."

"And there's a thing inside that one." Pascual has both hands on his knees. "I said a year to him. *You've been looking for one since 1946.* It was out before I heard it."

"I know."

"How do you —"

"He told me. Last Thursday." Reggie hears his own voice and keeps it where it is. "He had the rest of it out of those two words. You'll have that after. It's in mine."

Pascual looks at him for a long moment.

"After," he says.

---

"Thirtieth of May. I said a thing to the ceiling and wouldn't say it again when you asked."

"*I know why you stayed now.*"

"You remember it."

"I've thought about it perhaps once a week since."

"Then here it is." Pascual does not look away this time. "You stayed at that place till 1950. You'd told me what it was doing to you."

"I had."

"You stayed four years past where you'd any business staying. And on the thirtieth of May I stood in the door of the long room and watched you with him at my microscope for nine hours, and I knew why."

The sea goes in and out between the rocks.

"Yes," says Reggie.

"That's all?"

"It wasn't only that."

"I know it wasn't only that."

"It was most of it," Reggie says, and Pascual nods, once, as though a figure has come out where he said it would, and it has not made him glad.

---

"Kingston," says Pascual.

He tells it quickly: Lecky on the steps in the sun, the perfectly good smile, *the people one is afraid of are hardly ever the ones doing the thing*, and four readings of the sentence, and two hundred yards of pavement without being able to pick one.

"And I stopped in the street," he says, "because it came to me that's what you've been standing in, about him, since before I knew you. And I've sat across a table for two years and watched you not pick, and thought you were being slow."

"Pascual."

"I was wrong about that, and I'd like it said."

"It's said."

"I'd like it out loud."

"You have said it out loud," says Reggie, "on a rock, to me, and I have heard it."

---

Then Pascual reaches into his breast pocket.

The card comes out first, Reggie's card, and he puts it on the rock under the coffee cup. Then the other thing: a telegraph form folded in four, gone soft at the creases.

He holds it out, and Reggie takes it and unfolds it.

> USE IT ALL STOP
> SPILSBURY

"Handed in here at twenty to nine on the Tuesday," Pascual says. "I've had it in that pocket since. I know what that is."

"What is it?"

"It's a thing I don't want to give back." He takes it when Reggie holds it out, and folds it along its creases, and does not put it away. "And then Hester's second page, and *by you*. I said *solitude* to that man at a rail to get rid of him, and every word of it true, and he wrote it down and went and took an animal anyway."

"There's something else in that," Reggie says.

He had not meant to give it this morning. He gives it anyway, because it is Pascual's, and because the man has just gone through five things in order without once being let off.

"In the office. After *one of a pair*. He said the one animal in the world that would have given him the mechanism was written up in this building as a uraemia." He watches it land. "And that he'd never entirely believed it."

Pascual's hand shuts on the telegraph form.

"That's my name on it."

"I signed the covering letter."

"*Ay, Dios mío.*" He puts the heel of his other hand against his eyes and keeps it there. "Not today."

"Not today," says Reggie. "It will keep one day. We're both in it."

"We're both in it," Pascual says, behind his hand, and then takes the hand away. "That's the lot. In order."

He puts the telegraph form back in his breast pocket, and the card after it.

"Your turn," he says. "And I've nothing left this morning to hold anything up with, so say it straight."

---

Reggie gets up.

He goes down to the edge of the water and stands there with the sea coming over his shoes, which he does not notice for some while, and then he comes back up and sits down again on the same rock, a foot nearer than before.

"The twenty-second of June," he says. "In the long room. I was halfway through a sentence and I stopped, and you asked me what that was, and I said *nothing*."

"I remember."

"It wasn't true. I'd like it back."

"Then have it back."

"It wasn't nothing. It was the letter. There was a fifth thing."

"Go on."

---

"On the second of May I turned the envelope over on the jetty and saw his name in pencil on the back." He is looking at his own hands. "Before I'd read a word of it. Before the fixative, before the cut fish. I thought: *he's coming*."

Pascual says nothing.

"And then I thought: *he's coming because of me*."

The sea goes out and comes back.

"It was not a reading of anything. It wasn't a fact. I'd no evidence for it but an envelope."

Pascual waits.

"And a man who says in a room that he thinks Alec Spilsbury has crossed the Caribbean on his account," Reggie says, and takes his glasses off, "sounds like a fool. Or like something Hester would have to be careful about in a file."

"That isn't the thing."

"No. It's the front of it."

"Then say the thing."

He holds the glasses in both hands and looks at them for a while.

"I was glad," says Reggie. "A part of me wanted to go with him. I was not going to go, and I knew that on the jetty with the envelope still in my hand. The wanting was there regardless, and I did not tell you, and that is not the arrangement."

---

The sentence comes out flat and he hears it come out flat and does not attempt to do anything about it. It is the thing he has had behind his sternum since the second of May, and it sounds, in the open air, on a rock, very much smaller than it has felt, and exactly as bad.

Pascual has not moved.

"Did he ask," he says.

---

So Reggie gives him the slipway, in order.

The fourteenth of November 1946. The fuse box. The hand above the elbow. A man called Prentice coming up the stairs for his coat.

"That's all it was?"

"That's all it was. Nobody stopped it. A man wanted his coat." Reggie puts the glasses back on. "He had wanted me since the spring of 'forty-five. He knew it. He stood there and did the sum, and I wasn't worth a laboratory."

"You weren't *worth* —"

Pascual has come half up off the rock. Reggie watches him catch it, and put it down, and sit again, and rub a hand over his face.

"Go on," he says, through the hand.

"Nicholson was a bill. And a threat. He knew it was both when he chose the word. And it was a test of you." Reggie looks at him. "He says you held. He says he's respected you since that night."

"I don't want it."

"I told him you wouldn't."

"What else."

"He's measured us. Me with you against me with him. He says it is not close, and not in his favour." Reggie's mouth does something he does not authorise. "He says it's because we both pay for it. He says he learned that off the pair of us."

"*Learned* —"

"Yes."

"From *us*?"

"That's what he said."

"*Madre de Dios*," says Pascual, to the sky, with enormous feeling, and then: "What else."

---

"Then he came down the slipway and put his hand on my arm." Reggie touches the place with two fingers of his right hand, on the outside of the left arm, a hand's width above the elbow. "There. Where I put mine, in 'forty-six."

Pascual has gone entirely still.

"And I couldn't move. About nine seconds." He makes himself say the rest in the same voice. "He could've done anything he liked in them, and I'd have stood there, and he knew it."

"What'd he do."

"He took his hand away. And he said my name."

"Your —"

"*Reggie.* He hasn't used it in thirteen years."

Pascual's face does all of it at once, which is what that face does, and Reggie makes himself watch every bit of it and does not look at the water.

"And then I asked him whether he'd have opened that case at all," he says, "if he'd still had the laboratory."

"The case. One ninety-seven."

"The thing that finished him. Whether he'd have done it with a career on the other side of the page."

"And?"

"No."

Pascual does not say anything for a moment.

"...*Ay*," he says. "Oh."

"Yes."

"That's why he said your name."

"That's why it was a goodbye," says Reggie.

---

Pascual stands up.

He does not do it fast. He gets up off the rock the way a big man does who has been sitting on stone for an hour, and he stands there with his back to Reggie, looking along the shore towards the point, and for about a minute he says nothing at all.

Then he says:

"Thursday week."

"Yes."

"And you've had the front of it since the second of May."

"Yes."

"And on the twenty-second of June —" He turns round. "On the twenty-second of June you stood in that long room with twelve feet of bench between us, and you took me apart over a date on a letter, didn't you."

"Yes."

"*Twenty-three days*, you said. You said it three times, I've counted. *Twenty-three days.*"

"I did."

"With *that* in your mouth."

"Yes."

"You let me stand in that room as the only one of us who'd kept a thing back." His voice is coming up now and he is letting it.

"I did."

"You let me take all of it. That whole night, and the twenty-three days, and Hester's face, and John — and I took it because I thought it'd all been *mine*, and you had —"

"Yes."

"*Seis meses*, Reggie! *¡Seis meses!* Six months you had it, and I've been walking round this island since June thinking I was the one who —"

He stops.

Reggie watches him stop. It is not graceful. He gets as far as the next word and it is plainly a bad one, and he shuts his mouth on it, and his hand comes up and goes over his face and drags down it, hard, and he stands there breathing through his nose for rather a long time.

"I'm going to walk," he says, at last, quite quietly. "Along to the point. Don't follow me."

"I won't."

"I'll come back."

"I know you will," says Reggie.

---

He does not follow him.

He sits on the rock until Pascual is a figure at the far end of the shore, and then until he is round the point and not a figure at all, and then he goes into the hut and cleans the lamp glass, which does not need it, and fills the lamp, which does not need that either, and folds the sheets that are already folded.

It is not difficult to work out why he did not say it in June. He has had five months to work it out and he could give it to anybody in a sentence: that half of it would have been worse than none of it, and he had only ever had half, because he had not known the answer to his own question until eight days ago, and a man cannot hand somebody a question he is afraid of the answer to and call it telling them.

That is the reason, and it is a true one, and it is no use to anybody at all, because the man at the end of the shore did not get either half. He got *nothing*.

At about noon Reggie eats a piece of bread because Pascual would ask whether he had.

At about two he goes back out to the rock and sits on it, because that is where the man will look first.

---

Pascual comes back at twenty past four with his trousers wet to the knee and his face burned darker than it was this morning, and sits down on the rock beside him, not a foot away, and not touching.

For some while neither of them says anything.

"I'm not angry about his hand," says Pascual.

"No?"

"No. You couldn't move. I've seen you not able to move." He is looking at the water.

"When?"

"Twice a week, near enough. I've had my hand on the back of your neck and watched you stop. It's what happens when a thing's too big to do sums on, and I'm not going to be angry at you for it happening with him."

Reggie says nothing.

"I'm angry you left me in that room on my own."

"I know."

"Say it, then."

"I'm sorry," says Reggie. "For the twenty-second. For *nothing*. For letting you be the only one who'd kept something, and for letting you carry it five months as if it were only yours."

"*Bueno*." Pascual does not look round. "That's not all right. But it's said."

"It's said."

---

"Now ask me what I've been walking about."

"What have you been walking about."

"Since the thirtieth of May," says Pascual, "I've thought that if he asked, you'd go."

Reggie turns his head.

"Go."

"Go. Back. To a room next to his, wherever he's got a room." Pascual's hands are flat on his knees.

"Pascual —"

"You were glad he was coming. You'd stayed four years for him. I watched the pair of you do nine hours at my bench, and I went to bed and lay there doing that sum, and it'd come out the same every night he was on this island."

"I was never going to go," Reggie says.

"You've just told me a part of you wanted to."

"The wanting and the going are not the same thing." He says it carefully. "I've had six months of failing to put that in a sentence that doesn't sound like a man excusing himself, and I'm going to fail at it again now, and you'll have it anyway. A part of me wanted to be asked. The whole of me was staying."

Pascual does not say anything.

"And it didn't occur to me," Reggie says slowly, "that you could think otherwise."

"I know it didn't." It is not said unkindly. "You feel a thing all the way through and you think it shows. It doesn't, Reggie. Not from outside. It occurred to me every night he was here."

---

Reggie sits with that.

What he would like is a sentence that settles it, and there is not one, because a man who has just said *a part of me wanted to* does not get to say *never* on the same rock and be believed for it.

So he gives him what there is, which is what happened.

"He told me he would take it if I offered. To my face."

"*Ay*."

"And he told me I wasn't going to offer it, because he'd established that on the first of June, and he's the best observer either of us has met." Reggie takes a breath. "Then he put his hand on me, and I couldn't move, and when it came off, the first thing I did was step back."

Pascual turns his head.

"Before I could think. Before anything." He holds the man's eyes. "And when I could think again, the first thing I thought was that you were in Kingston, and that I'd have to tell you every second of it, and that I wanted to."

"I'd rather have had it in June."

"I know."

"I'd have been angry a fortnight and we'd have had the whole of the summer."

"I know that too," says Reggie. "That's the whole of what I've done, and it took me six months to do it, and I'm sorry for it."

---

Pascual looks at him for a long time.

Then he holds out his hand, palm up.

"Give me your arm."

Reggie gives it to him.

Pascual takes it by the wrist, not hard, and turns it, and puts his other hand flat on the outside of it, a hand's width above the elbow, exactly there; and leaves it.

And Reggie does not stop.

That is the thing he will have afterwards, and have in his body rather than in his head, which is where he keeps most things that matter: that he does not stop, and does not go still, and does not feel anything go through him like current. What happens instead is so ordinary that for a second he does not recognise it. He leans. His whole weight goes a few inches sideways without being asked, and his forehead ends up against the side of Pascual's neck, where it is hot from the sun and smells of salt, and stays there.

The hand on his arm does not move.

"There," says Pascual, very low.

"Yes."

"That's not nine seconds."

"No," says Reggie, into his neck. "That's not anything like."

---

They sit like that until the sun goes behind the point.

Then they go in and eat, and neither of them says very much, and they lie down on the iron cot with the lamp out and the window open on the water.

It is not mended. Reggie can feel exactly where it is not mended; it is in the way Pascual's arm comes across him and then stops, for about a second, before it settles, as though checking the arm is allowed.

"It's allowed," says Reggie.

"I know." The arm settles. "I'm checking anyway. Let me."

"Check, then."

"Tomorrow," says Pascual, into his hair, after a while.

"Tomorrow what."

"Tomorrow I'm going to take my time about you. All of it, start to finish, and you'll talk to me the whole way through, and I'm not having one word about him in it."

"Agreed."

"And in the morning we'll do the other thing. The one in my file with your name on the covering letter."

"In the morning."

"*Duérmete*."

Reggie does not sleep for some while. But at about eleven the hand that has been resting on the outside of his left arm, a hand's width above the elbow, shifts in the dark, and closes, not hard; and he finds that he has gone to sleep under it without noticing when.
