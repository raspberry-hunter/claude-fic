# Number 197

There are four scientists at the William Martin Institute for Marine Studies and Alec Spilsbury has had five and a half months in which to form a view of each of them.

Weald is the best technical draughtsman he has ever worked beside and will be wasted anywhere else. Leslie has the most orderly mind in the building and will run an institute inside fifteen years if nobody marries her to a man who wants dinner at seven. Pobil is a first-class gross anatomist, a better histologist than he thinks he is, slow, thorough, and possessed of the only working instrument for reading other people that exists on this island.

And Tar is the best man in the field, and is at present operating at about sixty per cent, and has been since the twenty-second of June, which is a waste that Spilsbury has now watched for twenty-one weeks and has been unable to do anything about.

That is the assessment. He has written it out twice, in his own notebook, once in June and once in October, and the only line that changed was the percentage.

---

They go to Kingston on the thirteenth, Bevan and Pobil, with a locked trunk and a case of photographs.

Spilsbury does not go down to the jetty, because he has nothing to contribute to a departure and because he has observed that people at this station make a ceremony of them. He watches from the top of the path instead, which affords a better view, and notes that Tar and Pobil shake hands, and that the handshake lasts about four seconds longer than a handshake, and that neither of them appears to notice this, and that Jean Leslie does.

Then he goes back up and works, because the boat is gone and there is no other useful response to a boat being gone.

---

On the fourteenth Mrs Bevan asks him to come to the office and shows him the arithmetic.

She is extremely good and she does not dress it up. The objection period closes on the thirtieth. The Institute's grounds of objection are that the applicant's facilities are unknown, that the applicant is unidentified beyond a post office box, and that the transfer would be against the welfare of the specimens.

The first two are procedural and will be answered by the applicant in a paragraph. The third rests on the mortality hypothesis.

"And the hypothesis rests on what?" says Mrs Bevan.

"One animal."

"One animal, found dead, of unknown provenance, with no history and no observations of any kind before it came ashore. And an account from a witness who declines to explain six weeks of his own movements."

"Yes."

"Then be so good as to tell me how we win."

"You do not," says Spilsbury.

---

He goes and works until half past nine and then he stops working, which is unlike him, and sits in the long room and does the arithmetic properly.

The position is this.

One: without a second animal the objection fails on the thirtieth and the transfer is granted in December, and Gamma and Chumley are in a lighter by the middle of January, and the second of them will be dead inside a year of whenever the first is taken out of the pen for a dive series, which will be in the spring.

Two: there is a second animal. It is in nine blocks, four jars of wet tissue, one badly drawn plate sequence and a daily record of fifty-one days, in a fibre case under the bed in the spare room of this Institute, and it has been there since the thirtieth of May.

Three: if he produces it, the Institute has an independent series, with a known provenance and an accession number, showing the whole of the decline from the first day.

Four: if he produces it, he is a man who removed a specimen from the establishment against which he is testifying, kept it alone knowing what that meant, and lost it.

Five: it is the fourteenth of November.

---

He has been doing this arithmetic since the eleventh of May and it has come out the same way every time, and what is different tonight is not the arithmetic.

What is different is that he can now put a figure on the other side.

He writes it down, in the notebook, in the long room, because he writes everything down:

*Objection fails 30.xi without second series. Probability ~0.9.*
*Second series destroys witness. Certain.*
*Witness now worth ~nothing in any case (see 22.vi).*

And then, underneath, in the same hand:

*Therefore produce.*

He looks at the word *therefore* for about four seconds, because it is not doing all of the work and he is not in the habit of letting a word do less than it appears to.

So he writes the rest of it down as well, since nobody is ever going to read the notebook:

*Also: I do not intend to sit on this island in January and watch Tar's face when they put those two animals into a lighter.*

That is not a moral consideration and he does not mistake it for one. It is a preference, he has had it since about the fourth of July, and he considers himself entitled to one preference at forty-four.

Then he looks at the date he has written at the top of the page, without thinking, at about ten o'clock, and he sits with that for rather longer than four seconds, and does not write anything about it at all.

---

He gets them into the office at eight the next morning: Tar, Mrs Bevan, Leslie, Weald. He has the case on the desk before anybody sits down.

"I am going to give you an account," he says. "It will take about twenty minutes. You will want to interrupt and I would rather you did not until I stop."

"Alec —"

"Twenty minutes, Tar."

---

It takes rather longer than twenty minutes, because they do interrupt.

On the ninth of February he was told his engagement was concluded. On the eleventh he left the station. At about four o'clock on the morning of the eleventh, before he left, he went to the holding pen at the end of the jetty and took an animal out of it.

"You took —"

"I took accession number one hundred and ninety-seven. Male, four years, six feet two, in good condition." He does not stop. "I put him in a boat and I took him off the place, and nobody attempted to prevent me, and nobody has written to me about it since."

"Why?"

"Because he was the best material in the building and they were going to waste him."

"That is not a reason, that is a —"

"It is the first of my reasons. You will have the other directly."

---

He kept the animal in a stone fish pound on a coast he does not name, for fifty-one days, with one man to help him and an arrangement about money.

He fed him. That is the part he has gone over more than any other and he gives it to the room flatly, in order, because it is a record.

Cut herring, offered daily. Refused from the first day.

Whole herring: refused.

Whole fish of four local species: refused, refused, taken and dropped, refused.

Live fish, netted by the man, offered in the water: taken. Nineteen days of adequate intake on live fish only, weights recorded, and Spilsbury has the weights.

And on the twentieth day the animal stopped.

"Stopped what?" says Weald.

"Stopped eating. Sat on the bottom at the seaward end with his tail under him and put his face against the stone."

"For how long?"

"Thirty-one days. I could not get him off it. On the second of April, at about two in the afternoon, he died."

Nobody says anything.

"There was nothing whatever on the post-mortem," says Spilsbury. "I did it myself, over three days, and I am extremely thorough. Good condition to the end, which you may verify from the weights. Clean lungs. No bone lesions; he was too young to have been dived. Adrenals ordinary."

---

"Was he one of a pair?"

Tar says it.

It is the first thing he has said in twelve minutes and he says it quietly, and Spilsbury has known since the eleventh of May exactly how he intends to answer it.

"Yes."

---

"There had been two in that pen since the March of the previous year, which is to say since your paper," he says. "I knew it when I went down the jetty. I took him anyway."

"Alec —"

"I want the next part said properly." He puts both hands on the back of the chair. "I knew what an animal on its own does."

"How?" says Weald.

"Since May 1954, when I stood at the rail of Gamma's pen and your anatomist told me, at some volume, in order to be rid of me."

Leslie's head comes round. Weald has stopped writing.

"Then why —"

"Because I believed it was husbandry." He does not soften it. "Every animal I had seen go down alone had been badly kept. Cut fish, bad water, handlers who could not read a face."

"And in Nassau —"

"In Nassau I spent four months telling them they were losing animals to something they were not measuring. I was quite certain it was their own incompetence."

"So you took him to prove it," says Leslie.

"I took him because he was the best material in the building. I kept him alone because I was certain I could, and because I wished to show that a competent man could." He looks at her. "Those are both true. The second is the one you want."

---

"The weights," says Tar.

"Hold for fifty-one days, near enough. He was better kept than any animal in Nassau, and on the twentieth day he stopped regardless."

"And you went on believing it was the feed."

"For thirty-one days. On the morning of the second of April I wrote to you to ask whether your pair would take cut fish."

Tar does not move at all.

"*Mine will not,*" Spilsbury says. "Yes. He died that afternoon. It was not the feed."

---

"What I did not have was the mechanism," he says. "I had the observation — animals in company that stopped, and one on his own that stopped — and I could not join them."

"The fourth of July," says Tar.

"The fourth of July, at about half past eleven in the morning, on a verandah, when you said it."

"And before that —"

"Before that there was one animal in the world that would have given it to me." He looks at Tar and at nobody else. "It was written up in this building as a uraemia."

The office is entirely quiet.

Mrs Bevan's pencil has stopped. Weald is looking at the floor between his shoes. Leslie is looking at the side of Tar's head, and Tar is looking at the case on the desk with no expression at all, which on that face is a great deal of expression.

"Which I have never entirely believed," says Spilsbury. "I say it because it is the other half of the account. I was not the only person in the matter who arranged for something not to be known."

---

"So you ran it," says Leslie.

"I ran it. The experiment this station has spent five months refusing to run, on an animal I had no business having, certain the result would be no."

"And recorded it."

"Daily, to two decimal places." He does not move. "So I was wrong for fifty-one days in my own hand."

"Alec."

"That is a failure of the kind I have spent thirty years being insufferable to other people about, and I should be obliged if nobody in this room made it easy for me."

Nobody does.

---

Mrs Bevan has the case open by half past nine and is going through it with her pencil, and she does not say one word for nine minutes.

Then she says: "Fifty-one days, daily, weights, in your hand, dated."

"Yes."

"Written by a man who expected the animal to live."

"Yes."

"And the provenance is an accession number in their own ledger."

"One hundred and ninety-seven. Which they cannot deny without producing the ledger."

"And you are the man who took him, knowing."

"I am the man who took him, knowing."

"Then you understand," says Mrs Bevan, "that the moment I put this in front of that office you are finished as a witness, and probably finished."

"I have understood that since the eleventh of May."

"And you are giving it to me in November."

"Yes."

"Why in November?"

---

Spilsbury has an answer ready and it is a true one.

"Because in May it was worth keeping and in November it is not," he says. "A witness with a hole in him is worth something for exactly as long as nobody has looked in the hole."

"Go on."

"The case fails on the thirtieth without a second series. Therefore the hole is worth less than the series. I did it on Wednesday night and you may see the page."

Mrs Bevan looks at him for rather a long time.

"No," she says. "But it is all of it that you intend to say in front of Miss Leslie, and it will do."

"Mrs Bevan —"

"I have been reading men's arithmetic for twenty-eight years, Dr Spilsbury, and the line they leave off the page is always the operative one." She goes back to the case. "I do not require it. I merely dislike being handled by somebody who is better at it than I am."

Spilsbury considers, and discards, four replies.

"Noted," he says.

---

Tar finds him at the slipway at four in the afternoon.

He has been expecting it since the thirtieth of May, and has prepared a number of versions of it, and none of them is this one. Spilsbury is on the bollard with his hat on his knee, and Tar comes down the concrete and sits on it at his feet, with his back against the iron like a man of nineteen, and says nothing at all for four minutes.

"You could've given it to me in June."

"Yes."

"In June it'd have been a paper and an animal and no transfer application, and Hester could've taken six months over it."

"Yes."

"And you sat in a chair on the thirty-first of May and told six people our interests aligned."

"They did. They do." He looks at the water. "They have never included your convenience and I have not said they did."

---

"I'm not angry with you," says Tar.

"You should be."

"I know what I should be. I've known it for six months and it hasn't been of the slightest assistance." He has his arms on his knees. "Alec. Why did you come?"

"Because you had a body and I had an account."

"You had an account in April, and you wrote to me about a fixative."

"Yes."

"Why did you come?"

---

There is a version of this conversation in which Alec Spilsbury says that he does not know.

It has the advantage of being unanswerable, and he has always considered it a species of cowardice dressed as modesty. He does not intend to use it this afternoon. He is the best observer of his generation and the instrument works perfectly well on himself; it has always worked; the only thing he has ever done with it is decline to publish.

"The fourteenth of November 1946," he says.

Tar does not move at all.

---

"It was the fourteenth yesterday. I noticed at about ten last night, in your long room, when I dated a page." He is looking at the water and has no intention of stopping. "It was a Thursday then. Raining. The boiler had gone and the whole building was about four degrees."

"Alec —"

"You had got the first good series out of the immersion work and you came down the passage to tell me at about ten at night, and you were so pleased you had come out without your jacket."

"You remember that."

"I remember all of it, at the same resolution, and I have not had to work at it."

---

He gives it the way he gives everything: in order, at one speed, with the measurements in.

"You stopped about two feet away. I had my back to the wall by the fuse box. You put your hand on my arm, above the elbow, and left it there, and said something about the third subject which I did not hear and have not recovered."

Tar makes a sound.

"And I did not move for approximately nine seconds."

"Who stopped it."

"Nobody stopped it. A door opened two floors down and a man called Prentice came up the stairs to fetch his coat. By the time he reached the half-landing we were both talking about a centrifuge. He said good night. That was the whole of it."

---

"Prentice," says Tar, as though the name were an object somebody had handed him.

"He retired in 1951 and is, I believe, in Rhyl."

"Ten years."

"Ten years, in which you have believed that one of us stopped it, and have not known which, and have arranged a certain amount around not finding out."

"How do you —"

"Pobil told me. Not intentionally."

"Pascual wouldn't —"

"At a hole in the sand on the third of July he said I had been looking for something since 1946. Nobody names a year like that unless he has been handed it." Spilsbury turns the hat over once. "The rest I could do myself."

---

"Then tell me which of us it was."

"Neither. I have just told you. A man went to fetch his coat."

"That isn't the question and you know it isn't."

"No," says Spilsbury. "It is not."

He turns round, finally, on the bollard.

"You are asking me what I wanted."

"Yes."

"I shall tell you. I have known the answer since about the third second."

"Alec —"

"And I have not been confused about it at any point in ten years. If it would comfort you to think I was, you are not going to get it from me."

---

"I wanted you," he says.

The water goes over the reef.

"I had wanted you since about the spring of 1945, and I knew it the way one knows a temperature."

"Then in that passage —"

"There was no discovery in that passage. There was a man with his hand above my elbow, and a piece of arithmetic I had done a number of times before and did again, faster, in the dark."

"Arithmetic."

"I had a programme, a laboratory, a career, and the better part of a decade of work I was not going to get anywhere else." He does not look away.

"And?"

"And I had you already, Tar. Your whole working day, and every idea you had before anybody else got it. That was a very great deal and it cost me nothing."

"And the rest of it —"

"The additional quantity was not worth what it would have cost. I decided so, and I did not move." His hands are perfectly still on the hat.

"And since?"

"I have not revised it. I would rather you spent ten years knowing what I am than a further ten supposing I was bewildered in a corridor."

---

Tar is on his feet.

Spilsbury had expected that at about the fourth sentence and is interested to find it has taken until the ninth.

"*Nine seconds.*"

"Nine."

"You'd done that, and then you worked in the next room for four more years —"

"Yes."

"— and not once —"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I had what I wanted," says Spilsbury, "and it was costing me nothing, and a man who is being fed does not overturn the table."

"That's —"

"You would have given me everything, for as long as I let you, and been extremely unhappy without ever being able to say why."

"Alec."

"I knew that too. I did nothing about it. That is a good deal worse than the corridor and I should like it kept in that order."

---

Tar does not say anything for rather a long time. He has gone to the end of the slipway and stopped there with his back to the water.

"Nicholson," he says.

"Yes."

"You told that story at my dinner table in May 1954, in front of six people."

"I did."

"I've had four readings of it for two and a half years."

"You have had four readings of it," says Spilsbury, "and none of them is the right one, and I have known that since about the following morning, and I did not correct it."

"Then correct it now."

---

"In the year before I arrived you had got something you had not had in 1946. I saw it across a table in a day and a half, and I shall not pretend I did not for the sake of your nerves."

"Alec —"

"You had a man. The man was good, and you were being made better by him in a way that was visible from the other end of a verandah."

"And you told them about Nicholson."

"Yes."

"Why."

"Because it was the only way I could find of telling you what the fourteenth of November 1946 would have cost, and why I declined to pay it."

"Say it plainly."

"Nicholson was fined, lost his position, lost his wife, and is in Southport doing something with paint." He says it perfectly levelly. "That is the bill. I put it on your table, and I told you that I had read it in 1946 and paid attention to it, and that you, apparently, had not."

---

"It was also a threat."

"Yes. It was."

"You knew that when you said it."

"I knew it while I was choosing the word *afterwards*." Spilsbury does not soften it. "I knew what it would do to you. I knew what it would do to your anatomist. I said it anyway, in company, with the Colonel four feet away."

"Why tell me now?"

"Because that is the part of this you should keep. If you find in a year that you have mislaid it, ask Pobil. He has not mislaid it once."

"Then why'd you —"

"Because I wanted to know whether he could hold."

"And?"

"He answered a question of Mrs Bevan's about a calf's weight, and his voice did not alter by a semitone, and he did not look up the table once." He sets the hat down on the bollard beside him. "I have respected him since that evening. I do not propose to tell him so."

---

"And now," says Tar.

"Now the position is different. The first line is that I have no programme, no laboratory and no career, and therefore nothing whatever on that side of the page."

"Go on."

"The second is newer, and it is the one that decides it. I have now watched you work with him for five months."

---

Tar has gone very still at the end of the slipway.

"I know what you are with him," says Spilsbury. "I have measured it against what you were with me. The difference is not small and it is not in my favour."

"Alec —"

"I am not going to offer you something worse and call it a choice." He does not look away. "That is not decency. It is accuracy, and pride, which are what I have."

"So with all that," Tar says slowly. "What's the end of the arithmetic?"

Tar has always been able to cut through any nonsense whatever. Spilsbury smiles at him, and is interested to see what happens to Tar's face at that.

"That what you have with Pobil is better than what you had with me," he says, "precisely because both of you are willing to pay for it."

"It doesn't —"

"It cost you something to hold him off me on the thirtieth of May, when he would have gone straight through me. And to do it in front of me. I watched it cost you."

Tar does not answer, which is an answer.

Spilsbury nods. "So. Let us say I was inspired by you, and the doctor."

---

He stands up.

"I have not finished, and you will want the last part. If you offered it to me tomorrow I should take it."

Tar says nothing at all.

"It would be a worse thing for you than what you have, and I should take it anyway, and not lose a night over it." He walks down the slipway, not quickly, to where Tar is standing. "You are not going to offer it. I established that on the first of June."

Tar watches him come and does not move. Spilsbury understands that he has perhaps nine seconds, and that he could do anything at all in them. He could embrace him, or kiss him, or put a hand to the side of his face, and Tar would not stop him, because for that long Tar is not capable of any arithmetic at all.

Spilsbury does none of those things, because — and this is the whole trouble with him, and always has been — he has done the arithmetic on each of them already, and knows what each would cost, and which he can afford.

Instead he puts his fingers on Tar's arm, just above the elbow.

The shiver that goes through Tar at that is something Spilsbury will remember for the rest of his life, and that is both the gift and the cost. Tar's eyes close. His breath stops altogether. Spilsbury keeps his fingers where they are for nine seconds, and then takes them away, and the second shiver comes as the hand leaves, and Tar steps back and draws a long, uneven breath.

"Reggie," Spilsbury says softly. He does not disguise from himself the indulgence of it, knowing that he will not say it many more times, if at all.

Tar does not open his eyes.

"The case," he says. "One hundred and ninety-seven. Yesterday morning, in Hester's office."

"Yes."

"If you had still had the programme. The laboratory, and the career, and the four years." His voice is perfectly level. "Would you have opened it?"

"No," says Spilsbury, very gently.

He does not pretend to think the question is about a fibre case. It is about every item he has spent this afternoon — the nine seconds, the hand above the elbow, the name — because with a laboratory on the other side of the page not one of them would have been available, and Tar has worked that out in about two seconds with his eyes shut, which is why he asked it in that order.

Tar opens his eyes and nods, as if he had expected that, and Spilsbury nods back, and picks his hat up off the bollard, and goes up the path.

---

Behind him Tar is standing at the end of a concrete slipway at half past four in the afternoon with his hands at his sides, and Spilsbury does not look round, because there is nothing further to observe.

That is the reason he gives himself.

He is aware, at about the seventh step, that it is not the reason, and that he has known which of the two of them would be unable to look round since roughly ten o'clock this morning. He goes on up the path anyway. A man may have one preference at forty-four, and he has spent a good deal more than his allowance today.

---

He goes to the long room. He gets the case out and begins laying the wet tissue up in order for Mrs Bevan, jar by jar, with the dates, which will take four hours and which he intends to do properly.

At some point after seven Weald comes in with a lamp and does not say anything and starts labelling.

They work until half past one in the morning.

Neither of them mentions anything at all, and at about midnight Weald puts a plate of bread and cold fish down at his elbow, and Spilsbury eats the whole of it while reading a label, and knows perfectly well what has been done to him, and lets a draughtsman of twenty-six have it.
