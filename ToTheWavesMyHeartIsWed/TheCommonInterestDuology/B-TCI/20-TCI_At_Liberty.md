# At Liberty

Hester's fortnight has a rule in it, which she gives them on the twenty-second at breakfast: for three days nobody argues.

"Three days," Reggie says.

"Three days. You may think as much as you like and you may not do it out loud at anybody."

"And on the twenty-sixth?"

"On the twenty-sixth you may think out loud at each other until you are hoarse, and on the twenty-eighth we sit down." She butters her toast. "I have watched six men in this colony decide a thing in an afternoon because the room was hot and somebody had said something clever. We are not going to be those men at Christmas."

---

He keeps the rule, which is harder than it sounds and rather easier than he expected, because there is a place on this island where a man can think about nineteen animals without saying anything, and that is the rail of the north pen at ten o'clock at night.

Chumley is nine feet four. He was six feet two in May 1954, which is the same as the length of accession one hundred and ninety-seven at the time of its death, and Reggie has done that arithmetic so many times in the last week that it has stopped meaning anything and started simply being there, like the noise of the generator.

Gamma comes up at the near end and looks at him. She has begun, in the last year, to come over to whoever is on the rail if they stand still long enough, which is either a very great deal or nothing whatever, and which Reggie has written up twice and thrown away twice because he cannot get it into a form that would survive a referee.

Somewhere north of here there are nineteen of these in a cut with a sill across it. Every year about thirty more are put into it. He does not know their sexes, or their ages, or whether they are paired, and he is not going to, because the books that say so are in an office in Nassau on a shelf under a green cloth binding, and there is a letter on Hester's desk offering to keep them there.

He stands at the rail until the lamp goes out in the bungalow.

---

Christmas Day is the best day this station has had in two years and he is aware of it while it is happening, which is rare.

Juan's mother does a goat and there is far too much of it. The Colonel gets out four bottles that have been in the cupboard since the coronation and makes a speech which starts as a toast to the Institute and turns, halfway, into an account of a horse he owned in 1934, and which nobody stops.

John gives Reggie a drawing of Gamma's head that is better than any plate in the collection and does not want to talk about it. Jean gives John a box of tacks and a pencil sharpener and tells him she is tired of watching him borrow hers. Pascual gives Hester a bottle of something which she puts straight into her own cupboard and does not open, and gives the Colonel a tin of tobacco, and gives Reggie — in front of everybody, entirely deadpan — a jar of pickle.

"I am going to have that framed."

"It's for you to eat."

"I'm going to have it *framed*, Pascual."

And Spilsbury, who has been asked to Christmas dinner by Hester in a tone that did not admit of an answer, and who has spent the whole meal at the end of the table saying almost nothing, puts a flat parcel down beside John's plate on his way out.

It is a set of six drawing pens. German, pre-war, in a case, and John goes quite red and cannot say anything at all.

"Sir —"

"You have been working with a nib that is going at the shoulder," says Spilsbury, "since about July. I have watched you compensate for it in the hatching, which is a skill you should not have had to develop."

And he takes his hat and goes, and John sits looking at the case until Jean tells him to pass the potatoes.

---

On the twenty-sixth, on the path up from the pens, Reggie says the thing he has been carrying since the letter.

"I want the material."

"What?"

"Their material. *A standing arrangement for the supply of preserved material, of which it has a considerable quantity.*" He has his hands in his pockets. "I want it very badly. They'll have four years of gravid females in jars, and I have never seen one, and there is not another collection like it in the world. I've thought about it every day since the twenty-first."

Pascual walks about four paces without saying anything.

"Is that the whole of it?"

"No. The whole of it is that it's in the letter because somebody in that office thought about what I'd want. On purpose. And they were right, and I'd like that noted, because when I argue on Friday I want you to know the man arguing has something in his hand."

"*Ay.*" Pascual stops on the path. "All right. Noted."

"That's all?"

"What do you want, a row?" He is looking at Reggie with an expression that Reggie has not seen since about April. "You've told me. Out loud, before, and not in a drawer. You're allowed to want it. You'd be a poor scientist if you didn't want it."

"And on Friday?"

"On Friday I'll take you apart if you're wrong," says Pascual comfortably, "and you'll know it's about the argument."

---

They sit down on the twenty-eighth at nine and it takes three hours and it is the best-tempered serious argument Reggie has ever been in.

Jean goes first, because Hester makes her.

"Take it," says Jean.

"You wrote two lines in July."

"I did, and I'd write them again, and this isn't that." She has her figures in front of her and does not need them. "In July they wanted our two animals to dive. Now they'll leave them alone for good, in writing. We have two. We can reach two. I've counted the nineteen every night for a fortnight, Dr Tar, and I can't reach one of them, and I'm not trading a thing I can hold for a thing I can't."

"And there's the licence," she says. "March. A clerk's signature. No inspector making trouble over the pens."

John says, without looking up from his book: "The inspector's coming anyway."

"He is."

---

Spilsbury does the arithmetic, and it is the hardest twenty minutes of the morning.

"If you refuse, the application for production goes to a man in a department who has never ordered a party in a transfer matter to open its books, and who will not enjoy being the first. I put it at one in five, which is generous."

"And if he orders it?"

"Then you have the two books and you have the case, and it goes to a hearing in March which you may well win." He does not stop. "And in the four in five, the transfer application revives, the objection is decided on the papers by the same man in the same month, and it is fifty-fifty at best, and if it goes against you a lighter comes in April. You would have refused a certainty in order to buy a one-in-five chance of a very good outcome and a two-in-five chance of losing everything you have."

"You'd take the offer."

"I would have taken it on the twenty-first, by return, and thought less of anybody who did not." He puts his hands flat. "I am the wrong man to ask, Pobil, and you all know why, and I have said what the figures are because that is what I am for."

---

Hester gives her advice again, in the same words she used on the twenty-first, and the Colonel says he will put his name to whatever the room decides and would like the reasoning minuted either way.

Then Pascual says his piece, and he has plainly counted the sentences beforehand and intends to stay inside them.

"I'm for refusing and I'll tell you why, and then I'll shut up and vote with the room."

He puts one hand on the table.

"Those nineteen are in a cut with a sill across it. Thirty a year go in. Every time they put one on a line, the one left in the pen starts to die, and their book says *no cause found*, and it'll go on saying it. If we sign that letter, we're paid to leave it saying that. That's what the money's for. It's not for our two."

"It is also for our two, Dr Pobil."

"It's for our two *and* the book, ma'am, and the book's the part they're buying." He takes the hand off the table. "That's all. I'll vote with the room."

---

They come round to him at half past eleven and Reggie does not have an answer, which is not a position he has been in at this table before.

He can feel what everybody expects. He refused a transfer in a second and a half in May; he refused an experiment in four seconds in July; he did not refuse anything at all on the fourteenth of August and it cost them the licence.

"I want two days," he says.

"You have until Wednesday."

"I want two days, and I want everybody to understand that I'm not looking for a way to be right. I'm looking for a third thing, and if there isn't one, I'll say so on Wednesday and we'll take the offer."

And Pascual, across the table, says: "Then go and look," and it is the least surprising thing that has happened all morning and Reggie is obliged to look at the window for a second.

---

He finds it at eleven o'clock on the thirtieth of December, in the long room, and it is not clever at all.

It has been sitting in front of him since the fourth of July and he has not seen it because he has spent five months thinking about one department and one licence and one pair of animals in one pen.

The hypothesis does not need their books.

It needs a paper. A short one: the mechanism, and a series. Alpha, dead alone in 1952, adrenals on the plate. Gamma, five months alone, off food nine weeks, put with a calf on the twentieth of May 1954 and feeding in eleven hours, with thirty-one months of weights since. The male off the beach in April, eight years in a pound, seven foci of dead bone, and a belt with four settings of holes. And accession one hundred and ninety-seven: fifty-one days, daily, in the hand of the man who expected him to live.

Four animals. No station named, no applicant named, nothing anybody can sue over. A stated mechanism, a stated prediction, and an appendix any competent person can test in his own pens in a fortnight.

Once it is in print, every establishment in the world that keeps these animals in pairs and takes one out to dive it is doing a thing which is known to kill, and is doing it after publication, and can never again write *no cause found* in a book without somebody being able to ask him whether he has read the paper.

It will take a year. It will not get one animal out of that cut. And it does not need a single page from anybody's office.

He sits with it for about four minutes to see whether it survives, and then he goes and wakes Pascual up.

---

"It's a quarter past eleven."

"I know what time it is."

"You're very pleased with yourself."

"I'm extremely pleased with myself and you're going to try to break it," says Reggie, "and then we're going to Hester."

"Get the lamp, then."

He gives it to him sitting on the edge of the cot in a nightshirt with his hair standing up, and Pascual listens to the whole of it without once interrupting, which is how Reggie knows it is worth something, and then goes at it for forty minutes.

Where are the controls. Who will referee it. What happens when a man in Nassau reads it and recognises his own pound. Can they be sued over an animal that was stolen; can the journal; can Hester. What does it do to the licence in March if the Institute is in print about a dead specimen it should never have had. Is one fifty-one-day record enough to hang a mechanism on, and if the answer is *no*, does the paper say so on the first page.

"It says so on the first page. It says the mechanism is proposed and gives the test."

"And the boy's notebooks stay where they are."

"The boy's notebooks stay where they are."

"Say it properly, Reggie."

"They stay where they are," says Reggie. "I want them. I'm not having them. I'd like that said in front of Hester too, so it's on the record with everything else."

Pascual sits back.

"It's a good paper," he says.

"It's a good paper."

"It doesn't save a single one of them."

"No."

"Right," says Pascual, and gets his trousers.

---

And then, on the path down to the bungalow at midnight, with the lamp between them and Hester's light still on because Hester's light is always still on, Pascual stops dead.

"Give me the letter."

"It's on her desk."

"Then say it. The withdrawal part. Word for word, you'll have it."

"*Our client is prepared to withdraw its application of the eighteenth of October for the transfer of the Institute's two specimens, upon the Institute's withdrawal of its application of the fourteenth instant, the two withdrawals to be simultaneous and neither party to be at liberty to renew.*"

"Say the last bit again."

"*Neither party to be at liberty to renew.*"

Pascual stands in the middle of the path holding a lamp.

"Renew *what*," he says.

---

Reggie opens his mouth and shuts it again.

"I read it twice on the twenty-first and I read it again on Boxing Day, because I read everything twice and that one three times, and it's been sitting under my ribs ever since and I couldn't get at what it was." He has the lamp up now. "It's about the *applications*. Both of those sentences are about applications. There isn't one word in that letter about what anybody may publish."

"Pascual —"

"They've bought their books. They haven't bought the mechanism, because on the twenty-first of December it hadn't occurred to them that anybody could have it without their books." He is starting to grin and it is a bad grin. "And it hadn't occurred to us either, so we'd have signed it, and we'd have been right to sign it, and they'd have had the whole thing for nothing."

Reggie stands on a path at midnight and does the thing where he cannot speak for a second.

"Hester will know whether it holds."

"Hester's light's on."

"Hester's light is always on."

"Then we're not waking her, we're *expected*," says Pascual, and goes down the path at a pace that makes the lamp swing.

---

Hester reads the letter twice standing up, in her dressing gown, with her hair down, which Reggie has seen perhaps four times in four years.

Then she sits down and reads it a third time with a pencil.

"It holds," she says. "It holds because a man who is paid by the word chose *applications* twice, and because there is not one line in here about publication, or about the record, or about the account, and I will tell you exactly why."

"Because it hadn't occurred to them."

"Because on the twenty-first of December the only thing in the world that could hurt them was a department ordering their books open, and they bought that, and it was the correct purchase." She puts the pencil down. "Mr Lecky is a very good lawyer, and he was looking at the danger in front of him, which is what good lawyers are for."

"And when he reads the paper in a year?"

"He will be extremely annoyed and he will have no remedy whatever, because his client will have got exactly what his letter asked for and nothing it did not." She looks at the two of them over the top of her spectacles. "Which brings me to the part you are both going to dislike."

---

"We answer it in its own words," says Hester. "We accept the offer as made. We add nothing. We do not tell them what we intend to publish, we do not ask them whether they mind, and we do not put one syllable on paper that invites them to send us a fresh draft with a new clause in it."

"And if they ask?"

"They will not ask before we have signed, because they think they have thought of everything." She folds her hands. "If they ask afterwards, we shall tell them the truth, which is that the Institute publishes its results and always has, and that we accepted the terms they wrote."

John, who has appeared in the doorway in a jersey over his pyjamas because there is no such thing in this building as a private conversation after midnight, says: "Isn't that a trick, ma'am?"

And the room goes quiet, because it is the right question and it has been asked by the only person present who was going to ask it.

---

Hester does not brush it aside, which is one of the reasons the whole of this Institute would walk into the sea for her.

"It would be a trick if we were required to tell them," she says. "We are not. They asked us for two withdrawals and offered two things in exchange, and we are giving them both withdrawals and taking both things, and every word of it will be true."

"But they wouldn't have offered it if they'd known."

"No. They would have put in a clause about publication, and we should have refused it, and we should now be in a fight we are probably going to lose, with two animals in a lighter in April." She turns her head. "Dr Tar. Would you have signed a clause about publication?"

"No."

"Dr Pobil?"

"No."

"Then there is the whole of my conscience, Mr Weald: we are not concealing anything they asked about, and if they had asked we should have told them and lost. I have been at this for twenty-eight years and that is as clean as it comes." She picks up her pencil. "Write down that you asked. Write down what I said, and put your name on it, and if you are ever in a room in ten years' time where somebody is doing this to you, ask it again."

John writes it down.

---

They draft the answer on the thirty-first and the first and it is four sentences long.

Hester writes three of them. Reggie writes the fourth, which is the only one with anything in it at all: *The Institute's objection of the 30th November is not affected by this arrangement, and is not withdrawn.*

"They will not like that sentence."

"They'll have to have it. If the transfer application's withdrawn there's nothing for the objection to object to, and it lapses on its own. But it lapses on *our* paper, in our hand, and in ten years, when somebody goes and looks in that file, the last thing in it will be an objection that was never answered."

Hester looks at him for a moment.

"You have been listening to me," she says, "for four years."

"I have been listening to you since 1952, Hester."

---

The answer goes out on the mail boat on the second of January at twenty past ten, in the Colonel's hand, signed by him as licensee and countersigned by Hester as the holder of the bundle, with a copy to Bristol.

The whole station goes down to the jetty to watch it go, which is completely unnecessary, and John brings his new pens in his breast pocket, and Spilsbury stands at the back with his hat on.

Reggie watches the boat until it is round the head.

Then Pascual, beside him, says: "Nineteen."

"I know."

"They'll be dived in the spring and two or three of them will stop and their book will say *no cause found*."

"I know."

"And we'll be correcting proofs."

"In about a year, if the referees are quick, which they won't be." Reggie takes his glasses off and cleans them and puts them back on. "It isn't enough."

"No."

"It's what we've got."

"It's what we've got today," says Pascual. He is looking out at the head where the boat was. "In March we'll have a licence and a bench and a man who knows what's on the shelf above that ledger, and the year after that we'll have a paper with a test in it, and somebody in Plymouth or Woods Hole will run it and get the same answer, and *then* somebody with a department behind him can go and open that book."

"That's four years."

"It's four years," Pascual agrees. "I'm forty-three and I've got thirty of them promised to somebody, so I can spare four."

And he goes up the path towards the pens, leaving Reggie standing on a jetty at half past ten in the morning with his ears going red in front of the entire Institute.

---

The inspector comes on the twenty-first of January.

There are nineteen days until then, and a paper to write, and a green cloth binding on a shelf in an office in Nassau that Reggie intends, one way or another and not this year, to see the inside of.

He goes up the path.
