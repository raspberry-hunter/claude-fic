# The Inspection

The thing nobody tells you about an inspection, John finds, is that the two days before it are worse than the four days of it.

Mrs Bevan has them all in the office on the Saturday and gives them the rules, and there are only three, and she makes each of them say the third one back to her.

One: everything is where it says it is in the book, or the book is altered before Monday, in ink, dated, and initialled.

Two: nobody follows Mr Sturrock about. He is to be given a chair, a lamp, the run of the place and the keys to everything except the drug cupboard, which Dr Pobil opens for him himself.

Three: you answer the question he asks. Not the question you are afraid of.

"You answer the question he asks," says John.

"And then?"

"And then I stop, ma'am."

"And then you stop talking, Mr Weald, which I am aware is the hardest thing in this building for you and Miss Leslie both, and which is why I have said it twice."

---

On the Sunday, Dr Spilsbury comes to the office and offers to go over to the Bay for the four days.

John is there because he is always there, with the minute book, and so he gets the whole of it.

"I am a man who removed a specimen from the establishment that is in dispute with you," says Spilsbury. "My being on your premises is not an asset."

"No."

"Then I shall take a room at the Bay and come back on Friday. You may say, accurately, that I am not resident."

"I shall say, accurately, that you are here."

"Mrs Bevan —"

"You signed twelve sections in front of a magistrate and put your own motive in them," says Mrs Bevan, "and I filed them with the objection, and every word of that is in the Secretary's office already, and if I hide you in a boarding house for four days I shall have made it look like something. You will be at breakfast on Monday."

And then Dr Tar, who has been standing by the window the entire time and has not said anything at all, says: "You'll be in the long room on Monday morning with the wet tissue, as usual, with your name on the jars."

Dr Spilsbury looks at him for about two seconds.

"Very well," he says.

---

Mr Sturrock comes off the Monday boat at twenty past ten with one bag and a folder under his arm and a hat that has been rained on a great many times.

He is about fifty and very thin and he shakes hands with all six of them without appearing to look at any of them, and says he is obliged to them, and that he would like to start with the pens, and that he has no objection to anybody carrying on with their work.

John draws him, later, from memory: the hat, the folder, and the rubber band round his notebook, which he takes off and puts on his wrist while he writes and then puts back round the book afterwards, every single time.

He is at the north pen from eleven until one, and he does not ask a question for the first forty minutes.

---

On the first afternoon he does the store, and he is very quick and very good and he finds three things.

The first is a stock bottle of formalin on the third shelf with a label that has come away at the corner, which John has been meaning to redo since about September and which Dr Pobil takes off the shelf and relabels in front of him, and Mr Sturrock writes something down.

The second is that the drug book has two entries in December with no countersignature, and Dr Pobil says, "Those are mine, I was on my own, there was nobody to countersign," and Mr Sturrock says, "Who is your second signature normally?" and Dr Pobil says, "Dr Tar," and Mr Sturrock writes that down too.

The third is the one John feels in his stomach before the man has finished turning the page.

---

It is the feed book for the north pen.

It is a good book. It is John's own ruling and Dr Pobil's hand, and it goes back to May 1954 without a gap, which is the thing everybody in this building is proudest of and the thing Dr Tar's paper rests on.

There is a fortnight in September in a different hand.

Mr Sturrock does not say anything for a moment. He turns two pages back and two pages forward, which is what a man does who is checking whether it is one fortnight or a habit.

"Whose hand is this?"

"Mine, sir," says John, before he has decided to.

"And why?"

And John has one second to be an idiot in, and Mrs Bevan's third rule is sitting on his tongue like a stone, and while he is getting hold of it Dr Pobil says, from the doorway, in a perfectly level voice:

"Because I wasn't doing it."

---

John does not turn round. He looks at the book.

"You were unwell?"

"No."

"Away from the station?"

"No."

Mr Sturrock takes the rubber band off his notebook and puts it on his wrist.

"Then perhaps you would tell me what I am looking at, Dr Pobil."

"You're looking at a fortnight when I wasn't attending to the record properly," says Dr Pobil. "There was a licence matter and I made a bad decision in it and I was no use to anybody for about three weeks in September. Mr Weald kept the feed book and the weights because they needed keeping, and he told me he was doing it, and I told him to go on doing it until I was fit to have it back."

There is a silence of about four seconds, which is a very long time.

"And the animals?"

"Are in the book, sir. Fed twice daily, weighed on the Thursdays, no change in either of them. That's the point of a second hand. You can check every one of those figures against Miss Leslie's monthly return, and I'd be glad if you did."

Mr Sturrock looks at the page for rather a long time.

Then he says: "That is the right answer to that question," and writes four lines, and turns the page.

---

John gets out to the back step afterwards and finds that his hands are not entirely steady, and Miss Leslie comes and sits on the step beside him with two cups of tea and does not say anything about it.

"He told the truth," says John, eventually.

"Of course he told the truth. There's a fortnight in your handwriting; the alternative was to stand there and say *I don't know how that got there.*"

"He could have said he was ill."

"He could." Miss Leslie blows on her tea. "And then Sturrock asks Mrs Bevan whether Dr Pobil was ill in September, and Mrs Bevan doesn't lie to inspectors, and then we've a station where the vet and the secretary give different accounts of the same fortnight. That's how you lose a licence, John. Not by having a bad September."

---

He interviews them one at a time on the Tuesday, in the office, with the door shut, and each of them comes out and does not say what was asked, because Mrs Bevan has told them not to compare.

John is fourth.

Mr Sturrock is pleasant and entirely unhurried and asks him about the plate collection, and about the nib that is going at the shoulder, and about who rules the feed books, and how long he has been here, and whether he is apprenticed or salaried, and whether anybody has ever taught him.

"Dr Tar taught me the plates, sir. And Dr Pobil taught me the dissection. And Dr Spilsbury has been correcting my hatching since about June, though he does it by taking the pen out of my hand."

"And you are twenty-six."

"Twenty-six, sir."

"Whose hand is the herring drawing in the objection? Enclosure C, plate four hundred and sixty."

"Mine, sir."

Mr Sturrock takes the rubber band off the notebook.

"I have looked at a great many drawings of stomach contents," he says, "and that is the best I have seen from a government-supported station in this colony, and I intend to write that down, and you may go red about it after I have gone."

John goes red about it there and then and cannot help it.

---

And then, at the end, with the notebook already shut and the band already round it, Mr Sturrock says:

"One more. It is not about the animals."

John's whole chest goes tight.

"Do the scientific staff live on the premises?"

"Yes, sir. All of us. There's nowhere else to live."

"In what accommodation?"

"There's the long building at the end. Six rooms." He hears his own voice come out level and is astonished by it. "Mine's the first one. Miss Leslie has the second. Dr Pobil has the fourth and Dr Tar the fifth, and Dr Spilsbury's in the middle one at the end since Christmas. The Bevans are in the bungalow."

"Thank you."

And Mr Sturrock puts the notebook in his pocket, without opening it again, and says, "That is all, Mr Weald," and John gets out into the passage and stands there for a moment with his heart going like a bird.

He answered the question he was asked. He did not say one word that was not true, and he did not say one word more, and he has never in his life been so glad of a woman making him repeat a sentence back to her twice.

---

What John notices, over the four days, is how careful they are, and that he would not have noticed it if he had not known.

They work together the whole time; there is a compressor survey and a growth curve and thirty-two months of weights to put in front of the man, and the two of them do it the way they do everything, which is fast and loud and with a certain amount of *no, go back*. On the Wednesday afternoon they take Mr Sturrock out in the boat to look at the sill and the intake, and Dr Tar explains the tidal exchange and Dr Pobil explains the fouling, and it is the best hour of the inspection.

And not once in four days does either of them stand within three feet of the other in a doorway.

Dr Pobil does not put his hand on the back of anybody's neck. Dr Tar does not sit on the end of anybody's bench. At supper on the Tuesday, Dr Tar says something about a sample and Dr Pobil laughs and reaches out, and the hand stops in the air about six inches from Dr Tar's arm and goes back to the table, and John looks very hard at his plate, and Miss Leslie asks Mr Sturrock a question about the Kingston abattoir in a voice that could be heard at the Bay.

It is four days. They manage it perfectly. John has watched them work in this building for four years and it is the only time he has ever seen either of them do anything that looked like a lie, and neither of them said a word.

---

Dr Spilsbury is interviewed on the Wednesday and is in the office for fifty minutes.

Nobody knows what was said and Dr Spilsbury volunteers nothing, and at supper the Colonel finally asks him outright, which everybody has been waiting for somebody to do.

"He asked me whether I removed an animal from the applicant's premises," says Spilsbury. "I said I had. He asked whether I would do it again. I said I would not, for reasons that are in section nine, and that I should be obliged if he read section nine rather than accepting my assurance."

"And?"

"He read section nine. He then asked me a question about live-feeding that nobody in this colony has thought to ask me, and I answered it for twenty minutes." He picks up his fork. "He is a good inspector. You are fortunate in him and you should not tell him so, because he will discount everything after it."

---

Mr Sturrock leaves on the Thursday at ten past ten.

He shakes hands with all six of them again, and tells Mrs Bevan that she will have his report in about three weeks and that she will not enjoy paragraph five, which concerns the drug book, and Mrs Bevan says that she has never yet enjoyed a paragraph five.

And then, on the jetty, with the boat coming in and everybody standing about the way they do, he says something to nobody in particular that John writes down that evening, word for word, in the back of his own notebook and not in the minute book at all.

"I have inspected fourteen establishments in this colony," says Mr Sturrock. "Seven of them keep a book the way you keep yours. Two of them would have altered that fortnight in September before I got here, and I should probably not have caught it."

He puts his hat on.

"I am sent to look at the welfare of animals in captivity. There are a good many things in this world that I am not sent to look at, and I have found it a great convenience, in twenty years, to be extremely clear with myself about which is which."

Then he picks up his bag and gets into the boat.

---

Dr Tar walks up the path with Mrs Bevan and does not look at anybody.

Dr Pobil stands on the jetty until the boat is round the head, and then he sits down on the bollard, quite suddenly, the way a man does when his legs have been holding something up for four days.

Miss Leslie goes and stands next to him. After a while Dr Tar comes back down the path, unhurriedly, and stops about a foot away from him and puts his hand flat between Dr Pobil's shoulder blades and leaves it there, and nobody on that jetty says one single word about it.

---

John writes the minute that evening.

*24.i.57. Inspection concluded. Mr E. Sturrock, Dept. of Agriculture, departed 10.10 a.m. Report expected c. 14.ii.*

Then he sits with the pen for a while, and then he writes one more line, which nobody has asked him for and which he does not intend to take out:

*The feed book for the north pen was kept in the hand of J. Weald from 2nd to 16th September 1956, by arrangement with Dr Pobil, and this was stated to the inspector by Dr Pobil on 21st January without being asked twice.*

He blots it, and shuts the book, and goes to find his new pens.
