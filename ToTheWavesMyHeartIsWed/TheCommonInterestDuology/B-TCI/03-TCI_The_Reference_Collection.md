# The Reference Collection

The microtome is a disgrace, and for nine days it is the only thing on the island that will hold still.

He cuts at six microns because that is what the block wants and the machine does not care what the block wants; it wants oiling, and packing at the feed screw with a strip of tin that Pascual cut in 1954 and has replaced twice since, and it wants a man sitting at it in a particular attitude for a particular number of hours, and in exchange it gives him, on a good morning, nine ribbons out of ten without a chatter mark.

He has two hundred and six blocks. Liver, kidney, spleen, lung from four lobes, adrenal, thymus, the gut in nine places, the muscle at the belt site, the bone at the socket of a drawn tooth. He wants all of it and he wants it before the ice runs out of him, and the ice ran out on the fourth of May.

So he cuts. And while he cuts he is not thinking about Nassau, which is the other reason he is cutting.

---

On the Wednesday of the second week Reggie comes in at ten past nine at night, puts a plate down on the clear end of the bench, walks round behind him, and takes the handle off the microtome.

It comes off with a quarter turn. It is designed to.

Pascual sits with his hands in the air.

"Put that back."

"No."

"Reggie —"

"You have been on that stool since five." Reggie puts the handle in his trouser pocket, where it makes an entirely ridiculous shape. "Not this morning. Since the fifth. You are getting up at four and you are going to bed at midnight and on Monday you sharpened the knife twice because you had forgotten you had done it."

"I did not —"

"You did it in front of me and I said nothing, and I have been wrong about that for two days." He takes his glasses off and polishes them, which is what he does instead of raising his voice. "Four hours. Then you may have the handle back and I shall not say another word about it."

"You cannot *take a part off a man's instrument* —"

"I have taken it off."

"That is —" He gets up, and discovers on the way up that his back has opinions, and is extremely annoyed that the man is standing there watching him find it out. "That is the most high-handed, insufferable —"

"Yes."

"— thing anybody has ever done to me in a laboratory."

"Eat your supper," Reggie says.

---

The supper is wrong.

It is wrong in a way that is very difficult to convey. It is a plate of things that Reggie Tar eats, which is to say almost nothing, arranged carefully: bread, four slices of the good ham, a hard-boiled egg, two tomatoes cut in quarters, and a quantity of the wet green pickle that nobody on this island likes except Reggie, who has it sent, and which Pascual has been declining at that table for two years without ever saying so, because who says so.

It is about a third of what a man his size eats and half of it he does not want, and it has been laid out on the plate with the four ham slices squared to the edge.

He looks at it.

Then he sits down and eats every scrap of it, including the pickle, which is exactly as bad as he remembers.

"Was that all right?"

"It was excellent."

"It wasn't," Reggie says, "because in two years you have put that pickle back on the dish every time it has come near you. I noticed in about March of last year."

"And bought it anyway."

"And bought it anyway, which I'm aware is idiotic, and I couldn't work out what else to put on the plate." He is leaning against the door frame with his arms folded and he is not comfortable. "There's a dish of the rice with the fish in it under a cloth in the kitchen. Juan's mother sent it up on Sunday and Jean's been keeping it off John."

"Ah."

"I should have brought that."

Pascual puts the plate down.

He has been watched, then. For two years, at a table, at three meals a day, by a man who cannot tell you the colour of the Colonel's front door.

"Sit down," he says, "and don't be clever about it."

"I've got a —"

"*Siéntate.*"

Reggie sits down on the low chair for people who have fainted, and Pascual goes and gets the rice with the fish in it from under its cloth, and they eat it out of the dish between them with two forks at half past nine at night, and neither of them says anything of any consequence whatever, and it is the best hour he has had since April.

At one in the morning he gets the handle back. He does not use it until Thursday.

---

The stomach contents have been in a stoppered jar of spirit on the third shelf since the twenty-sixth of April and nobody has looked at them, because there is nothing in three pounds of half-digested fish that anybody wants.

He does it on the Thursday because he is a thorough man and because the microtome is in one of its moods.

It takes him five hours. Sieve, wash, sieve again, two grades, and then the residue in a white enamel dish under the lamp with a seeker and a pair of watchmaker's forceps, and it is the filthiest and most tedious work in his trade, and he does it with his sleeves rolled and his reading glasses on, which he almost never wears where anyone can see.

What is in three pounds of half-digested fish is bone. Vertebrae, mostly; ribs; a quantity of jaw. Nothing with a head on it, because the fish had no heads.

And, at twenty past two in the afternoon, under the seeker, two small white things shaped like nothing else in the world.

---

He knows what they are before his hand has stopped moving. Everybody who has ever opened a fish-eating animal knows what they are. They are the ear stones; there are two in every fish that ever swam; they sit in the skull and they grow in rings like a tree and they are the hardest and stupidest and most durable objects in a stomach, and they come out of a dead animal's gut in perfect condition when everything else has gone to soup.

He puts them on a slide with a drop of glycerine and gets the lamp down and looks at them for a long moment.

He is an anatomist and not a fisheries man. He knows perhaps thirty species by the ear stone and most of those are the ones he has been shown by other people.

He does not know this one.

What he knows is the shape of the thing — long, and deep at the front end, and with a notch cut into the edge of it that is not like the little Caribbean sardines he has looked at, and the little Caribbean sardines are the only fish on this island anybody would cut into portions.

He sits back.

Then he takes off the reading glasses and goes and finds Jean.

---

The old fisheries store is at the end of the long shed and has not been opened since the Navy came in 1954, and it smells of dust and camphor and, faintly, of the sea, which everything here does.

Charles Cholmondeley's reference collection is in four card-index drawers on the second rack.

Jean gets them out and puts them on the bench and stands with both hands flat on the top one for a second and a half before she opens it, and Pascual has the sense not to look at her while she does it.

Inside are packets. Small, folded, of squared paper, each one gummed down the long edge, each one numbered in a very small and very terrible hand: the station, the date, the boat, the total length of the fish, the sex where he could get it, and then the name, in Latin, and under the name, in three of the drawers out of four, a second line in pencil.

The second line is the correction. He wrote the name he thought it was, and then he checked it, and then when he was wrong he did not rub it out. He wrote the right one underneath and left both.

"Two hundred and forty-one species," Jean says. Her voice is entirely level. "He did the whole commercial catch, and then everything in the samples that wasn't commercial, which is most of it."

"And the trash fish."

"And the trash fish, because he said somebody ought to. I told him it was a waste of time."

"Did he stop?"

"Of course he didn't stop." She takes the first packet out. "Right. Give me your two and go away."

"I'm not going away."

"Then sit down and don't breathe on the drawer."

---

They get through it in four hours and it is not what he wanted.

Every clupeid in these waters is in that drawer, because the little herrings and the sardines and the thread herrings are the bulk of the trash catch and the boy weighed all of them. *Harengula. Opisthonema. Jenkinsia.* Jean lays them out down the bench in a row with the two from the stomach at the end of it, and by the third hour the thing is plain to both of them and neither of them says it until it is finished, because they are both people who finish.

At six o'clock Jean straightens up and pushes her hair off her forehead with the back of her wrist.

"It's a clupeid," she says.

"Yes."

"And it isn't any clupeid that has ever been landed on this island."

"No."

"Then it didn't swim here." She puts her hands on the bench. "Pascual, that fish came in a box."

---

He goes up the path at six-fifteen with the slide in his shirt pocket and finds Reggie in the office, and says it in about four sentences.

He gets as far as *and I'll write to Marchant tonight* before the man says, "No."

The room goes rather still.

"Say that again."

"No. Not tonight and not with two." Reggie puts the pen down. "You've got two ear stones and four hours of a woman's time and a conclusion you reached at the second hour."

"I reached it at the second hour because it is *correct* —"

"I expect it is. That isn't the point and you know it isn't."

"Then say what is, because I have had three pounds of rotten fish under my hands since nine this morning —"

"Sit down."

"I will not sit down."

"Then stand up and hear it." Reggie comes round the desk. "You've one letter to Marchant. One."

"I know how many letters I've got."

"Then you know that the moment it leaves this island there's a man in Plymouth who knows the Martin is asking about the diet of an animal it hasn't published." He does not look away. "If you're right, that costs us something and it's worth it."

"And if I'm wrong?"

"If you're wrong by one species, he tells three people at a meeting in September. Kindly, because he likes you. And then this is a station that got excited about a sardine."

"I am not wrong."

"Prove it and I'll put the stamp on myself."

---

There is a silence in the office of a quality Pascual does not enjoy at all, and in it he can feel the whole shape of the thing arriving, which is the worst part, because he has been here before and he was standing on the other side of it.

"Say the rest of it," he says.

"You'd rather I didn't."

"Say it anyway."

Reggie does not look away.

"In 1954 I came to you at seven in the morning and asked you to put another animal's kidney under a glass with Beta's name on the label," he says.

"I remember."

"And you would not. You told me that if a pathologist came we were finished before lunch, and you were right, and I dropped it inside four seconds." He does not look away. "This is the same rule. It merely happens to cost you this time instead of me."

Pascual stands in the middle of a small hot office and is furious for about ten seconds, and then is not.

"*Ay, Dios.*"

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be right somewhere else next time." He rubs a hand over his face. "What do you want."

"All of them," Reggie says, and there is something coming up in his voice now that was not there a minute ago. "Every ear stone in that dish. Not a sample — the lot, counted, so we can say what proportion of the identifiable remains are one species."

"And?"

"And Jean's hand on the elimination, and Jean's name under it and not yours, so it isn't your eye. And John draws six of them at twenty times, so Marchant has a plate before he has an opinion."

"That's four days."

"It's four days."

"You want a *count*."

"I want a count," Reggie says, "because if it comes out at eighty per cent then somebody threw a cheap fish in with the good, and if it comes out at a hundred then that animal ate one thing, out of a box, every day of his life."

And Pascual, who came up this path at a quarter past six intending to have a row and win it, stands there and watches Reggie Tar get the bit between his teeth about ear stones, and finds that he is not furious in the slightest and is having a certain amount of difficulty with his face.

"Four days," he says.

---

It takes five.

Ninety-one, in the end, out of the dish and off the sieves and out of the second wash that he would not have done if Reggie had not asked for a count. Ninety-one ear stones, and every single one of them the same fish, and Jean signs the elimination sheet at the bottom in her own hand — *J. M. Leslie*, against two hundred and forty-one species — and John does the six plates at twenty diameters with the notch on the edge drawn so cleanly that you could cut yourself on it.

It goes out on the Wednesday, the sixteenth. Reggie writes the letter and it is five lines long and asks Marchant a single question and does not tell him one other thing.

Then he pushes it across the desk.

"Sign it."

"It's your letter."

"It's your finding. I want your name on the bottom of it and I want Jean's sheet enclosed with hers on it."

"Marchant knows you. He doesn't know me from a —"

"Marchant knows you as the man in the fourth section of the pairing paper whose histology he has written to me about twice," Reggie says. "Sign the letter, Pascual."

He signs the letter.

He puts it in the bag himself and watches it go under the thwart, and then walks back up the jetty feeling obscurely as though somebody has put a coat over his shoulders, and is cross about it for most of the afternoon and gets over it.

---

Marchant does not write.

Marchant cables, which costs him money he has not got, and it comes up from the harbour office on the Thursday the twenty-fourth in a boy's hand at ten in the morning, and Pascual reads it standing in the middle of the path.

> BOTH SAGITTAE AND ALL SIX PLATES CLUPEA HARENGUS NO DOUBT WHATEVER
> AGE THREE PLUS FOUR PLUS NORTH SEA OR WEST OF SCOTLAND BY THE SCULPTURE
> WHERE IN GODS NAME DID YOU GET THEM
> MARCHANT

---

They have it out on the verandah that evening, the four of them, with the Colonel and Hester at the other end of the table and no pretence being made that this is anything but a meeting.

"Herring," Jean says. "Atlantic herring. In a mermaid, in the Caribbean, in April."

"Salted or frozen," says Pascual.

"Frozen," says Reggie. "You can't get a wild-caught animal onto salt fish; they won't take it. He'd have to be feeding it wet."

"Then he has a cold store."

"He has a cold store, which means he has power, which means he has a plant and a building and a man to run it." Reggie has not sat down.

"And?"

"And nobody imports Scotch herring across the Atlantic to feed one animal. You feed one animal out of the sea at the end of your own jetty."

"So."

"So you buy it by the ton, because it's cheap and it comes in a box of a known weight, and because you are feeding a great many things and you want the same thing every day so that you can keep a book."

Nobody says anything for a moment.

"How many is a great many," John says.

"I don't know."

"Guess."

"I am not going to guess," Reggie says, "in front of six people, on a verandah, in May," and John, who is twenty-six and not a fool, lets it go.

---

The Colonel's answer comes back the same week and Pascual is in the room for it because the Colonel sends for both of them.

It is two lines long and it is typed and it is signed with initials.

The gentleman regrets that he is not in a position to assist Colonel Bevan in this matter, and would be grateful if the Colonel did not raise it with him again.

"Which of the four was it?" Reggie says.

"One of the two I would have trusted." The Colonel puts it back in the file. "That is a great deal more than nothing, Tar."

"It's a refusal."

"It is a man I have known since 1938 declining, in writing, in a form I can show to somebody, to answer a question he could have answered in a sentence if the answer had been no."

"So it's service."

"So somebody wants it not discussed, which is not the same thing and may in time turn out to be worse." He closes the drawer. "The licence goes in for renewal on the first of November."

---

"You're doing it again."

"Mm."

"Up and down. Up and down." Pascual, on his back on the cot in the dark with one arm over his eyes, is not even required to open them. "Come to bed and do it lying down, you'll wear less of the floor."

"There's a thing I can't get round."

"There is always a thing you can't get round. Come here and I'll hold on to you while you don't get round it."

The floor stops. The cot goes down at the edge and takes on the weight, which is not a great deal of weight, and Pascual gets an arm round him and hauls, and Reggie comes where he is put, which he has been doing since the twentieth of May 1954 and has never once pretended he does not enjoy.

"Better?"

"Marginally."

"Then say it."

"If there is a cold store," Reggie says into his collarbone, "there is an invoice for the herring, and a shipping note, and a broker, and a customs entry, and every one of those is a piece of paper with a place on it."

"That's not a thing you can't get round. That's a good thing."

"It is a very good thing. It's the best thing we've had." A pause. "I can't get any of them, because a man at this Institute who starts writing to customs brokers about frozen fish is a man in a file by August."

"Mm."

"And I have been walking up and down for an hour," Reggie says, "arriving at a man arriving on Wednesday."

Pascual thinks about that for a while, with his hand moving on the man's back without his having told it to.

"I know," he says. "I got there on Thursday."

"You didn't say."

"No. I wanted another week of not saying it." He shifts, and gets the blanket up, and puts his mouth against the man's hair. "Go to sleep. It's Sunday tomorrow."

"And?"

"And I'm going to make you sit at the pen for two hours and look at animals that are perfectly well. You'll hate it, and then you'll stop hating it at about the forty-minute mark, like you did in March."

"That's an appalling thing to do to a man."

"It's the best hour of my week. *Duérmete.*"

---

Wednesday the thirtieth of May, and the mail boat comes round the head at twenty past ten, and there is a man standing up in the stern of it.

Pascual sees him before anybody else does because he is the only one at the rail; the rest of them are up at the huts, and he has come down to take the fish delivery, and he stands on the planks with a clipboard under his arm and watches the launch come in with a passenger nobody has been told about.

He knows the shape of him at a hundred yards. He has been carrying an opinion about that shape for two years.

The launch comes alongside. There is no tender behind it and there is no Navy in it, and the rating does not get up, and Alec Spilsbury hands his own two cases up onto the planks and then climbs up after them, in a linen jacket that has been pressed by somebody who was in a hurry, with his hat in his hand and no rating at his elbow and nobody to carry anything.

He has got thinner. Pascual, who reads bodies for a living and cannot stop doing it, has it in about four seconds: the jacket is the same jacket and it is not the same man inside it.

"Dr Pobil."

"Doctor."

"I got Tar's letter on the eleventh." He sets the cases down and straightens up and looks past Pascual, up the path, at the pens. "I have been travelling since the twelfth. I want you to know that before anybody at this station tells you otherwise, because there will be a great deal of nonsense talked in the next fortnight and I would rather we began accurately."

"You've come on your own account."

"I have come entirely on my own account," Spilsbury says. "I am not attached to anybody at all at present, which I imagine you will find amusing, and which you are welcome to."

Down at the pens, faintly, there is the sound of water moving.

Spilsbury hears it. Pascual watches him hear it — watches the head come round, and the whole of the man go towards it for about half a second, and then stop, and come back, exactly as if a hand had been put on his chest.

Neither of them says anything about that.

"Where have you put him?" Spilsbury says.

And Pascual Pobil, who swore in two languages on a concrete deck at half past five one morning in 1954 that he would not forgive this man if he lived to be ninety, and has not, and does not intend to, hears himself say:

"Behind the store. Above the high line. I'll take you."
