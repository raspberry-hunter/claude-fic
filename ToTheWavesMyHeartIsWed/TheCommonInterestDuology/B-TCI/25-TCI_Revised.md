# Revised

The book is in its fifth volume and it has not missed a day since the thirty-first of March 1957.

Pascual has it open on the rail board at half past six in the morning, in the ordinary way, with the pencil on the string, and there is nothing whatever ordinary about the figure he is writing down.

*9.v.62. G. — refused a.m. feed (9th consecutive). Est. wt. down 40 lb since 1.iv. Resp. unchanged. On bottom, seaward end, 04.10–06.20 obs. Tail tucked.*

He puts the pencil down on the string and stands there for a while.

He is forty-nine years old and he has been a veterinarian for twenty-seven years and he has known since about the second week of April, and he wrote it in this book on the fourteenth in six words, and every one of the six was correct.

*No treatable cause found. She is old.*

---

There are twelve people on the station now, which would have made anybody laugh in 1956.

John Weald is thirty-one and is Scientific Illustrator to the Institute, which is a post the Department invented in 1959 because it wanted his plates and could not have them otherwise, and he has two of his own people to train and has become, of all the unlikely things in this world, slightly bossy about it.

Miss Chin has been here since September. She is twenty-four, she came out of Mona with the best first they have given, she is the second postgraduate this Institute has ever had, and she is at the rail now with a watch in her hand and a board on her knee, writing down what an animal does and not what anybody thinks it means, for an hour, morning and evening, as she has done for two hundred and forty days.

She is very good. She is not Charles and Pascual has stopped needing her to be, which took most of the first month.

---

Jean came in on Tuesday, off the Kingston boat, with two students and a box of reprints.

She is Lecturer in Zoology at the University College of the West Indies and has been since 1959, and it happened the way Spilsbury said it would and nobody expected: the paper came out in the November number for 1957, and inside eighteen months three people had written to the Martin about the analysis in appendix two, and two of them had written to *J. E. Leslie* personally, and one of those was the professor who eventually gave her the post.

"I said ten years," she said on Tuesday, putting the reprints on the verandah table. "Look at the front of that one."

*J. E. LESLIE* on the 1957 paper.

*JEAN ELIZABETH LESLIE* on the 1961 one, in full, at the top, first name on the line, and her own two students underneath it.

"Four years."

"Four years and one of them still called me *Mrs* Leslie at the reading," she said, "and I let him get all the way to the end of his question."

---

What happened to the nineteen is this, and Pascual can say it in order because he has had to say it in order a number of times, to a number of committees.

The paper was read at the summer meeting of 1957 and printed in November. In 1958 the Plymouth people ran the test in the Appendix on two captive pairs and got the same result and published it, and in 1959 a Norwegian station did it a third time with better instrumentation and a more careful protocol than either.

By 1959 the thing was not a hypothesis any more. It was a line in a textbook.

In 1960 the naval contract came up for renewal, and the department that was buying the diving — which had never once been asked about any of it — was asked, in writing, by three separate societies, whether its contractor kept paired animals and separated them; and the contractor was required to produce its register; and the register showed, for the years 1955 to 1959, twenty-six deaths recorded as *no cause found*, of which twenty-one were animals whose pen-mate had been on the line inside the preceding six weeks.

The contract was not renewed. The operation was wound up in the spring of 1961.

Of the nineteen, six were already dead before the paper was printed. Four died in the winter of 1960, which was a bad winter there and would have been a bad winter anywhere. Nine were living when the place closed.

Two went to Bermuda, to a station that keeps them properly and writes every quarter. Four went to a commercial aquarium in Florida, which Pascual has written to twice and has had one reply from, and about which he has views that Hester will not permit at her table.

Two went into the sea off Andros, which was the best thing anybody did in the whole matter, and which was done by a handler on his own initiative at four o'clock in the morning and cost him his character with his employers, and about whom Pascual has written a letter of his own, which is on file at the Martin in case anybody ever asks.

And one came here, in February, in a lighter, on the Institute's application, with the Department paying the freight.

---

She is four years old and she is in the south pen and she is officially Delta.

Juan's mother calls her Perla and so, by now, does everybody except the minute book.

She has been across the gate from Chumley for three months, which is exactly how they did it in May 1954 and exactly what the protocol says, and they have four hundred pages of the two of them at that gate: who comes first, how long they stay, who goes away, who comes back.

It is not a bond. Nobody is pretending it is a bond. It is an acquaintance across a grating, which in this animal appears to be a thing that can exist, and which has never been documented before because nobody has ever had two pens and a reason.

---

Reggie is forty-three and has grey at the sides and needs his glasses for the plates now as well as the reading, and has not left this island in five years except twice, and is at the moment the most careful man Pascual has ever seen.

He does the weights with Miss Chin and does not tell her what he expects. He keeps his own record separately. He has not once, in a fortnight, said the word *hypothesis* at the rail.

On the evening of the ninth he comes down and stands beside Pascual with his hands in his pockets.

"How long?"

"Days. Maybe a week."

"Is she in pain?"

"No. She's tired, and she's old, and she's not eating. It's the way they go if nobody kills them first." Pascual keeps his eyes on the water. "I can't fix it and I'm not going to pretend for four more days that I might."

Reggie takes a hand out of his pocket and puts it flat between Pascual's shoulder blades and leaves it there, and by 1962 nobody on this station looks up when that happens, which has taken six years and one inspection and a great deal of bad temper.

"She was on the bottom in the corner when he brought me the calf," Reggie says. "Twentieth of May 1954. Nine weeks off her food."

"And she got eight more years."

"She got eight more years."

---

Gamma dies on the eleventh of May 1962 at about twenty past four in the afternoon, and it takes about forty minutes and there is nothing in it that anybody has to be ashamed of.

Chumley is with her for the whole of it.

He is thirteen feet two, and he weighs upward of nineteen hundredweight, and he has been the largest animal in captivity anywhere in the world since 1959, and what he does for those forty minutes is stay at her shoulder in four feet of water at the shallow end where she can hold her head up, and put the flat of one hand against the side of her face, and keep it there.

She answers it twice. The second time is at about ten past four.

Miss Chin writes down both times, and the interval, and the duration of each, in a steady hand, and does not stop writing, and afterwards she goes up to the long room and sits on the step outside it for twenty minutes, and John Weald goes and sits next to her and says nothing at all, which he learned from somebody.

---

Pascual does the post-mortem on the twelfth with Reggie, and it takes six hours, and it says what he wrote in the book on the fourteenth of April.

An old animal. The heart of an old animal. No adrenal enlargement whatever, which matters enormously and which he will still be explaining to people in 1970.

They write it up that night and Hester enters it in the file in her own hand, and she is sixty-seven and her writing has gone smaller but it has not changed in any other way.

*11.v.62. Specimen Gamma, adult female, N. aestuarius, died 16.20. Held at this Institute since 1951. The last of the animals taken in the first period. Post-mortem R. E. Tar and P. Pobil; no unexpected finding.*

Then she puts the pen down and says, "I have been writing that animal's name in this file since 1951," and goes to bed early, which she does not do.

---

And then there is Chumley, and the argument.

It lasts two days and it is the last serious one they have about an animal, and it is not the argument either of them would have had in 1956.

Reggie wants to wait. Not long: four days. The protocol says the onset of decline is observable from the second day, and nobody in the world has a record of the first week of it in an animal in good condition, and if they open the gate on the first afternoon they will never know what the first week looks like and nobody else will either, because nobody else will ever be standing here with two pens and a grating and this much warning.

Pascual wants the gate open on the first afternoon.

"He'll stop eating on the second day."

"He might."

"He'll stop eating on the second day, and you know it, because we wrote the paper that says so, and he's thirteen and he's never been on his own in his life."

"And if we open it at four o'clock on Saturday we'll have a pen with two animals in it and no idea whether we saved him or whether he was never going to go down at all." Reggie has his glasses in his hand. "And the next station that has this — and there'll be one, Pascual, there's one in Bermuda with two of them right now — won't know either."

"So he's the control."

"He's not the control. I'm asking for four days and a record."

"Four days is what it takes."

---

They stop. Both of them. In the middle of the long room, at about midnight, with John and Miss Chin very carefully not in the doorway.

And Reggie says, "All right. Then we do it the way we'd make anybody else do it. We write the rule down tonight, before we know anything, and we sign it, and then whatever he does we haven't chosen it afterwards to suit ourselves."

"And the rule?"

"You write it. You're the clinician. I'll sign whatever you write and I won't argue at the rail."

So Pascual writes it, and it is four lines:

*Gate to be opened on the first of: (a) refusal of two consecutive feeds; (b) 24 hr without leaving the bottom; (c) 96 hr from death, whichever falls first. Decision not to be revisited at the rail. P. Pobil, 12.v.62. R. E. Tar, 12.v.62.*

And then, because it is 1962 and not 1956, they cable Millport.

---

Alec Spilsbury is Keeper of the Collections at a small marine station in the west of Scotland, on two hundred and forty pounds a year, and has been since 1959, and got it on a reference from a veterinary officer in the West Indies which the director has kept in his desk because it is the only reference he has ever received that opens by saying the candidate is not worth liking.

He writes to this Institute twice a year, as instructed, and the letters are lists of facts with no salutation to speak of, and Hester answers every one of them.

In 1959 he sent, unasked, nine pages of protocol amendments for the bereavement observations, of which they adopted seven.

The cable goes at ten o'clock on the twelfth. It says: GAMMA DEAD ELEVENTH. MALE IN CONDITION. FEMALE IN SOUTH PEN THREE MONTHS AT GATE. PROPOSE FOUR DAYS OBSERVATION BEFORE OPENING. YOUR VIEW.

The answer comes back on the thirteenth, at twenty past nine in the morning, and it is five words, and Juan's brother brings it up the path at a run because the boy in the harbour office told him what was in it.

> REVISED. OPEN THE GATE.
> SPILSBURY

---

Pascual stands in the office with it in his hand for a moment.

"He's never revised anything in his life."

"He's revised one thing," says Reggie, and takes it from him, and reads it twice, and puts it down on the desk very carefully. "Get the winch."

---

They open the gate at twenty to eleven on the thirteenth of May 1962, fifty-one hours after Gamma died, and Pascual notices the number and does not say it out loud to anybody.

Delta comes through in about ninety seconds, which nobody expected; the protocol assumed the male would go to her.

Chumley is at the seaward end on the bottom with his tail under him and has been since the morning. He does not come up.

She goes down to him and stays about six feet off for eleven minutes, at the same depth, not moving much. Then she goes a little closer. At about the fourteenth minute she puts the flat of one hand on the stone next to him, which is not the vow and is not anything anybody has a name for, and leaves it there.

At forty minutes he moves.

---

It is not a rescue and Pascual is extremely clear about that in the file.

The animal is off his food for six days and he is on the bottom for four of them, and his weight goes down eighty-four pounds and does not come back for a month, and on the third day Pascual would not have given a great deal for him.

On the seventh day he takes eleven pounds of fish at the morning feed.

On the seventeenth of May, at six in the evening, with Miss Chin at the rail and a watch in her hand, the two of them come up together at the shallow end and put their hands to each other's faces, both of them, at the same time, for nine seconds; and it goes in the book in a steady hand with the time and the duration, and it is the fourth such record in the world and the second one this Institute has ever made.

---

Pascual takes the book up to the long room that night to copy the week fair.

Reggie is at his bench with a lamp, working on something that is going to keep him up until two, and does not look round, and says, "Well?"

"He ate eleven pounds this morning and they did it again at six."

"I know. I was at the rail." Reggie puts the pen down and turns on the stool. "I wanted to hear you say it."

And Pascual stands in the long room of the William Martin Institute for Marine Studies, at forty-nine years of age, with a book under his arm that has not missed a day in five years, and a woman asleep in the second room down who will run her own station in fifteen years, and nineteen animals accounted for on a sheet in a file, and a five-word cable in his breast pocket behind a nine-word one.

"He's going to be all right," he says.

"Say the rest of it."

"And we don't know that, and we're going to write down every day of it for the next four years so that the next man does."

"*That's* the rest of it," says Reggie, and holds his hand out for the book.
