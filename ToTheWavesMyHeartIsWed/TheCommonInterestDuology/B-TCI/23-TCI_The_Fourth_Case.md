# The Fourth Case

The paper is good.

Spilsbury has read four drafts of it and has spent ten months in a position to know, and it is the best thing Reginald Tar has written and about the fourth best thing anybody has written about this animal.

It is nineteen pages. It states a mechanism, gives four cases, and sets out a test that any competent person with two animals and a fortnight can run without killing anything, which is the whole of its value and which Tar has understood from the first morning.

Case one is Alpha: dead alone in a pen in 1952, adrenals on the plate, no other finding.

Case two is Gamma: five months alone, off food for nine weeks, put with a calf on the twentieth of May 1954 and feeding within a day, and thirty-four months of weights since, which is the best-documented recovery in the literature because there is no other.

Case three is the adult male off the beach: eight years in a pound, a belt let out four times, seven foci of dead bone, ninety-one otoliths from a single genus, dead within six weeks of being loose.

Case four is accession one hundred and ninety-seven.

---

He has written the methods for case four himself, because nobody else can, and it is eight sheets and it has taken him nine days and it is the last piece of science he expects to be asked for.

Water temperature at six-hourly intervals. The pound, its dimensions, its exchange, its sill. What was offered, in what state, at what hour, and what was taken. The weights to two decimal places and the balance they were taken on and the date it was last checked. The exact hour of cessation on the twentieth day, and what the animal did afterwards, described flatly and without one adjective in eight sheets.

He is aware, finishing it, that it is the best work of his life, and that it is a record of fifty-one days of being wrong, and that those two facts are in no way in tension.

---

The argument begins on the nineteenth, in the long room, at about four in the afternoon, when Tar puts the title sheet on the bench in front of him.

*R. E. TAR, P. POBIL, J. E. LESLIE and A. SPILSBURY*

"Take it off," says Spilsbury.

"No."

"Tar —"

"I've been waiting nine days for you to say that and the answer's no."

"Then you will want the reasons, and there are three."

"There'll be four, you always have four."

"There are three, and you have thought of all of them, and you are going to sit there and make me say them out loud, which I consider beneath you."

"It's entirely beneath me," says Tar. "Go on."

---

"One. By June every society in this field will have had a letter from Bay Street about me. Some of them will believe it and all of them will file it."

"Yes."

"Two. Your paper rests on a record made by a man who removed the animal without permission. That is a fact and it is going into the paper either way. But there is a difference between a paper that *reports* such a record and a paper that is *written by* the man who took it, and the difference is entirely in the mind of the referee, and the mind of the referee is what you have to get past."

"And three."

"Three. If it is rejected in July you will have it back with two reports and a suggestion to resubmit, and it will go in again in the autumn, and it will come out in 1958 instead of this year, and in the spring of 1958 the applicant will dive another twenty animals." He puts his hands flat. "That is the whole of it. There is no sentiment in any of the three and I should be obliged if you did not go looking for some."

Tar takes his glasses off.

"You've been at that bench nine days writing eight sheets nobody else on earth could write."

"Yes."

"And you want your name off it."

"I want the paper *in print in July*," says Spilsbury. "What I want is entirely irrelevant and always has been."

---

Tar does not answer that for about four seconds, which is longer than he normally requires, and Spilsbury observes with interest that the man has gone slightly white about the mouth.

"On the fourteenth of August you sat in the Colonel's chair and put terms on the table. Your name on whatever's published, second to mine. You asked for that."

"I did."

"And now you'd like me to take it off, quietly, in March, when there's nobody in the room."

"Yes."

"No," says Tar.

"On what ground?"

"On the ground that it's yours," says Tar, "and that I'm not having the record of what a man did get cleaned up because it's inconvenient to the men who benefit from it. We've had a year of that. There's a woman in that bungalow who wouldn't strike out a *nil* in her own hand because you don't rub things out."

---

And there it is, and Spilsbury had not seen it coming and ought to have.

He sits for a moment with the fact that Reginald Tar has just hit him with Hester Bevan, accurately, and that there is no reply to it on the merits, because the principle is correct and he agrees with it and has agreed with it since roughly the fifteenth of November.

"That is a good argument," he says.

"It's your argument. You made it to a magistrate in December."

"It is still a good argument, and it is going to get your paper rejected in July, and you know that too."

"I know it might."

"You know it will, and you are proposing to pay for a principle with an animal you have never met."

Tar puts his glasses back on, which is what he does instead of saying something unforgivable.

---

They leave it there for two days, which Spilsbury has not seen him do about anything.

He works. He checks the weights again. He rules the tables for John Weald, who has begun, in the last month, to draw a table without being asked and to get it right.

On the second evening he watches, from the end of the long room, Pobil come in at half past seven with a plate of food and put it down at Tar's elbow without a word and go out again; and watches Tar eat it while reading, without once looking up; and observes that the cost of this arrangement, which he calculated to a fair degree of accuracy in 1946, has been paid in full by both of these men within the last four months, and that it has bought them something he has no instrument for measuring and has stopped pretending he has.

---

The answer comes from John Weald, on the twenty-first, at breakfast, and nobody has asked him.

"Sir," he says, to the table generally, "where does it say who took the animal?"

"Page six. Methods, case four, first paragraph."

"And that's Dr Spilsbury's paragraph?"

"It's mine. He hasn't seen it."

"Then that's the bit that stays," says John. "Isn't it? Title pages get left off reprints. Everybody's got a drawer of reprints with the cover torn." He has gone red and is carrying on regardless. "But the methods go in every time. If it's on page six it's there in fifty years, and the title page isn't."

Hester puts her cup down.

"Mr Weald is right," she says, "and I have been trying to say that for two days without appearing to take a side."

---

Spilsbury reads the paragraph at eleven o'clock in the long room with Tar standing on the other side of the bench.

*The record of case four was made between 11 February and 2 April 1956 by Dr A. Spilsbury, who removed the animal from the holding of a commercial establishment without the permission of its holders, in the belief that an animal kept singly could be maintained by good husbandry. The circumstances are set out in his attested account of 28 November 1956, a copy of which is deposited with the Institute and available to any reader on application. The observations are reproduced here in full, including those which are inconsistent with the hypothesis proposed.*

He reads it three times.

"This is worse for me than the title page."

"Yes."

"Every person who ever reads this paper will read that paragraph."

"Yes."

"For as long as it is read at all."

"Yes," says Tar, "and every one of them will also read that the man who did it wrote down the fifty-one days in full, including the days that don't suit him, and deposited an account with his own motive in it. You don't get the second half without the first."

Spilsbury puts the sheet down on the bench.

"Very well," he says. "Take my name off the title page."

"Alec —"

"You are getting the better of this bargain and so am I, which is the definition of a satisfactory one, and I should like to stop talking about it now."

---

That afternoon he writes his own version of page six and gives it to Tar at supper.

It is four lines longer. It says the same things in a colder order and it includes two facts that Tar's paragraph left out, which are that he was dismissed by the holders nine days before he took the animal, and that he had been told in May 1954, at this Institute, by this Institute's veterinary officer, what solitude does to these animals, and did not act on it.

"That's worse."

"It is more damaging and it is more accurate," says Spilsbury, "and if a man is going to be in a methods section for fifty years he had better be in it correctly. Use mine."

Tar uses his.

---

The rest of the front matter takes about nine minutes and is the pleasantest argument Spilsbury has sat through in a decade.

Leslie will be J. E. Leslie, which she decides herself and about which she is perfectly clear.

"It's initials, and I know exactly what it is," she says. "I'd like the first man who reads it to argue with the analysis for half an hour before he finds out, because he will do the arithmetic differently once he knows, and I want to see his workings first."

"And in ten years?"

"In ten years I'll put the whole thing on the front and let him choke," says Leslie, and goes back to her tables.

John Weald is not an author and does not want to be and says so twice, and is put under every plate in the paper as *J. Weald del.*, at Tar's insistence, in his own hand on the proof sheet, and spends the rest of the day being unable to look at anybody.

---

The parcel goes off on the twenty-seventh: nineteen pages, four appendices, twenty-two plates, and eight sheets of methods for a dead animal in a fish pound on a coast that is not named.

They all go down to the jetty again, which is now apparently what this station does, and the Colonel says something about the Zoological Society that is not repeatable, and Mrs Bevan has the receipt from the post agent inside four seconds.

Spilsbury stands at the back with his hat on and watches the boat go round the head.

Four days after that he has an appointment with a case under the bed in the spare room, which is now empty, and a sailing on the thirty-first.

He has read the Bay Street letter. He has done the arithmetic on what happens to a man of forty-four with that circulating and no post and no laboratory and about twelve hundred pounds, and it comes out the way it comes out.

What he has not got a method for is the fact that he is not, at present, particularly sorry; and he stands on a jetty on the twenty-seventh of March 1957 with his hat in his hand, watching a boat take away the best piece of work he will ever do with somebody else's name on the front of it, and finds that the figure on the other side of the page — which he has been unable to name since November, and has written down twice as *see 22.vi* — is this:

that for ten months, in a building of six people, he has been of use.
