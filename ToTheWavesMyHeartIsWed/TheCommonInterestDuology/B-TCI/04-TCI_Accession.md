# Accession

They are standing at the pit behind the store when he gets there, the pair of them, at eleven in the morning with the sun already flattening everything, and for about three strides Reggie cannot make the picture mean anything at all.

Pascual has his arms folded. Spilsbury has his hat in his hand and is looking down at the sand, and there is nothing to look at; it is a rectangle of raked sand above the high line with a length of galvanised pipe driven in at the head of it, and it has been that since the twenty-seventh of April.

"Tar."

"Spilsbury."

"Your man tells me he got ninety-one ear stones out of three pounds of gut and they are all the same fish."

"He got ninety-one out of three pounds after two washes, and the second wash was mine, and he'd have got seventy and been right anyway." Reggie stops at the edge of the sand. "And he's not my man."

"No," Spilsbury says pleasantly. "I don't suppose he is."

---

There is a thing Reggie does and has done since he was about thirteen and could not now stop doing under anaesthetic, and it is at his disposal in the second and a half between that sentence and the next one, and he can feel it arriving the way you feel weather.

He turns his head.

"Pascual."

"I know," Pascual says. "Go on."

So he does it in front of both of them, which is a thing he has never had to do, and he feels the cost of it go through him like cold water and puts it away to be looked at later.

"You've had a filthy journey," he says, warmly, to Spilsbury, "and you've been fed on whatever the Kingston boat calls lunch, and I've kept you standing over a hole in the ground for four minutes."

"You have."

"Come up to the house. There's cold beer and somebody has made far too much bread, and you can tell me at your own speed and in your own order and I shan't ask you a single question until you've finished."

He gets it out perfectly. It is a very good one. He has used most of it before, on a Colonial Secretary's man, and it worked.

Spilsbury looks at him with his head slightly on one side.

"You've put it on," he says.

"I —"

"You did that in the spring of 'forty-seven, in the corridor outside the dark room, when you wanted the second centrifuge."

"That's —"

"It took me eight months to work out I'd been done. I have known what it looks like from the outside since about 1948."

"Then why —"

"Because it works better on me than on anybody living." He puts his hat back on. "And because it is the worst investment you could make, since you would have had it for nothing, and now you have spent it."

"Right."

"Was that the arrangement, incidentally? Telling him first?"

Nobody answers him.

"How interesting," Spilsbury says. "I'd like the beer, though."

---

They get into the sick bay at half past one and they do not come out of it for nine hours.

Reggie is aware afterwards that he ought to be able to give an account of those nine hours and cannot. What he has is a sequence of objects and a feeling in the chest like going downhill on a bicycle.

The plates first, all four hundred and nineteen to four hundred and sixty-one of them, in order, and Spilsbury goes through them at a speed that would look like carelessness in anybody else and is not, because at the ninth plate he stops and puts his finger on the belt buckle and says *that's a Corbie lock, that's a dog harness,* and he is right, and Hester has had four letters out to the trade for three weeks and has not got that.

Then the counts. Then Jean's elimination sheet, which he reads twice and then hands back and says is the best piece of work in the building, in front of nobody, so it does not even count as manners.

Then the histology, and this is the part Reggie loses time in.

Because Spilsbury sits down at Pascual's second microscope with his jacket over the back of the stool and starts going through two hundred and six blocks, and Reggie stands behind him with the day book, and inside twenty minutes they have stopped finishing each other's sentences because they have stopped needing whole sentences.

*Adrenal.*

*Cortex.*

*Slightly. Not Alpha.*

*No. Nowhere near. So he wasn't frightened.*

*He wasn't frightened for the last eight months of his life, whatever he was before that.*

*Which means the pen was working.*

*Which means somebody read something.*

And so on, for hours, with the day book filling up in Reggie's handwriting getting worse down the page, and the lamp getting hotter, and at some point somebody puts a plate of something at his elbow and he does not look up, and at some point after that the plate is taken away again untouched.

It is the best nine hours he has had since the twenty-second of May 1954, and that is a sentence he is going to have to look at properly at some stage, and does not, that night.

---

At about ten o'clock John's plate four hundred and thirty-one comes up on the pile, which is the head, opened, with the sockets of the four upper front teeth drawn at four diameters and the remodelled bone shaded in.

Spilsbury looks at it for rather a long time.

"Ah," he says.

"What."

"I drew those."

The room goes entirely quiet. Somewhere down the path the generator is doing what it does.

"Say that again," says Pascual, from the door, and Reggie had not known he was in the room.

"I drew those teeth. In November of last year, with a Read elevator and a Coupland number two, in about forty minutes."

"And the lower."

"And the lower four in the January." Spilsbury has not turned round. He is still looking at the plate. "Your boy has drawn it beautifully. That is the palatal root of the second and it came away clean, and I was rather pleased with it at the time."

"You —"

"They were going to be drawn by a man who had done nine of them and lost two animals doing it," Spilsbury says.

"That is not —"

"I could see the run of the roots off a plate and he could not. It was not a moral act, Dr Pobil, and I would be obliged if you did not treat it as one. It was a competent one."

---

Pascual gets about four feet.

Reggie does not remember deciding to move and is, in the event, not quick enough by any standard that matters; what saves it is that Pascual is a man who has spent twenty-one years putting his hands on things that can be damaged, and Reggie gets a fistful of his shirt at the shoulder and says his name once, and it is enough.

"*Suéltame.*"

"No."

"Reggie. Take your hand off my shirt."

"No." He has to put his other hand flat on the man's chest, which is like putting your hand on the side of a shed, and Pascual could go through him at any moment in the next four seconds and both of them know it. "Look at me. Not him. Me."

"He put an elevator in that animal's —"

"I know what he did."

"You have got hold of the *wrong man* in this room —"

"I have got hold of the only one I can reach," Reggie says. "If you put him through that window we have a body and a drawing and nothing else on this earth."

"Good."

"And in six months Dewar and Lecky write a third time, and there is nothing in this building that can say no to them." He does not let go. "Four seconds. I'm asking."

Pascual takes them.

Then he takes four more, and Reggie feels the whole of it go out of the man's chest under his hand, not all at once, the way air goes out of a bag you are kneeling on.

"Four," Pascual says.

"Thank you."

"I'm going out. Don't follow me out." He looks past Reggie at the back of Spilsbury's head. "Doctor. Tomorrow I want you at that bench at seven and I want the whole of the feed regime written down in your hand, and if you are one minute late I will come and get you."

"Seven," Spilsbury says, to the plate. "Yes."

---

He comes back at midnight with the whole of it, and he gives it standing up, and he gives it well.

Reggie takes nine pages of it and has to sharpen a pencil twice.

A shore station. Sea pens, four of them, in a cut that has a sill across it, with gates. A cold store and a generator house and a jetty that will take a lighter. Feed bought by the ton, frozen, through a broker, because you cannot run a station of that size off the end of your own boat. Animals held in pairs since March of last year and singly before that. Tethers on the ones that go out through the sill, on a padded belt, because an animal that will come back to a line will go down to a hundred and forty feet and come up again on command and an animal that will not is no use to anybody.

And blood twice a month, and a chart for every animal, and a number.

"What are they *for*," says Reggie.

"Men."

"Say it plainly."

"Breath-hold, cold, and pressure," Spilsbury says. "How long, how deep, how cold, how often, and what it costs."

"For whom?"

"There is a submarine escape problem and a diving-medicine problem, and both are extremely expensive to work on with men."

"And the animal isn't."

"The animal dives to two hundred feet on a single breath, has a face you can read, and will do as it is told." He shrugs. "You put an instrument on it and ask it a question and it answers. Nothing else on the planet does that."

"I had got as far as its being worth money."

"Then you are a fortnight ahead of where they were in four years, which tells you everything you need about them."

"And you were consulting."

"I was consulting from October to February."

"Doing what?"

"Telling them their controls were rubbish," Spilsbury says, "and that they were losing animals to something they were not measuring, and that the whole programme was four years of expensive noise."

"Were you right?"

"About all three. I was also, I am told, impossible, and I wanted the running of it, and in February they gave me a cheque and a car to the aerodrome."

Over by the door Pascual takes his weight off the frame and stands up straight. He has been leaning on it with his arms folded since a quarter to twelve. He does not say anything, and after a moment he folds his arms again and goes back against the wood.

Reggie puts it down to the man's back, which has been at him since the Thursday, and writes *Feb — paid off* in the margin, and asks the next question.

---

"How many."

"Ah," says Spilsbury. "Yes."

"How many."

"Do you want it in the order I have it or in the order that will hurt?"

"In the order you have it."

"Very well." He sits down on the edge of the bench, which nobody does, and takes his glasses off. "There is a book. It is a ledger, it lives in the office, and every animal that comes in is entered in it with a number, a date, a length, and where it came from. The number runs from one. It started in the spring of 1948."

"Yes."

"The last entry I saw was on the ninth of February and the number on it was two hundred and six."

Reggie writes it down. His hand does it. He watches it do it.

"And alive?"

"Nineteen."

"Nineteen out of two hundred and six."

"Nineteen out of two hundred and six over eight years." Spilsbury puts his glasses back on. "Of which fourteen had come in since the June before last."

"Fourteen."

"There. Now you have both numbers, and you may have the arithmetic, because you will do it anyway at four in the morning and you will do it badly."

"Don't."

"They kept five alive in seven years, Tar. They keep nineteen now. That is your paper."

"I know what it is."

"Section four. The pen dimensions and the gate schedule, which arrived in that office in the spring of last year, typed out by somebody who had written to you and got four pages back."

"I am aware," Reggie says, "that I wrote those four pages."

"Good. Then be aware of the useful half of it, because the half you are going to sit with is the useless half." Spilsbury does not raise his voice at all; he never has. "You did not increase the taking. Look at the ledger. They took thirty a year when the animals died and they take thirty a year now."

"Then what did I do?"

"You made the enterprise possible. Before your paper the man with the chequebook was buying corpses. Now he is buying a delivery date."

"That is worse."

"It is different, and you will need the difference, because somebody hostile will ask you it in a room one day."

"Hm."

"It was going to be worked out by somebody. It was worked out by you because you are better than they are." He picks up the water. "I should be extremely careful, if I were you, about which of those facts you spend the night on."

---

The Colonel has them all in his office at nine the next morning: Bevan behind the desk, Hester at his right hand with a pad, Pascual standing because he will not sit at that table, Jean in the window, and Spilsbury in the good chair with his ankles crossed.

The Colonel begins by saying what the meeting is for, which is a thing he does and which Reggie has never seen another man in science do once.

"Three questions," he says. "Whether this Institute takes Dr Spilsbury in. On what terms, in writing. And what we do with what he brings us, supposing he brings it."

"And if we can't settle them?" says Jean.

"Then he catches the Wednesday boat and we are where we were on Monday, which was nowhere, with a body."

"Admirably put," says Spilsbury.

"I have not finished." The Colonel puts both hands flat on the desk. "The third question is the one that matters, and it is the one every person in this room will want to leave until last. We will leave it until last. But we will do it before anybody eats."

---

Spilsbury wants four things and he says them in order without being asked.

Access to every block, plate and page. His name on whatever is published, second to Reggie's, and first if Reggie is not on it. Bed, board and a bench for as long as it takes. And the whole of it done here, at the Martin, and not at any institution that might be leaned on, because the Martin is nobody and that is its single greatest asset.

"And in exchange," says the Colonel.

"The account. All of it, in writing, signed, and attested by whoever your clever woman thinks a court would believe."

Hester does not look up from the pad.

"Not the place," says Reggie.

"Not the place."

"That's the whole of it. That's the only thing in your account that's worth anything."

"Yes," Spilsbury says. "That is why I still have it."

---

It is Hester who does the work, in the end, and Reggie enjoys about four minutes of it very much indeed.

She takes him through it: what he saw himself as against what he was told; whether he ever handled the ledger; whether there is a document anywhere in the world with his name and the station's name on the same sheet of paper; what he was paid and by whom and whether the cheque was drawn on a bank he can name. She does it in the voice she uses for the quarterly fisheries return, and by the end of it Spilsbury has stopped being amused and has started answering properly, which Reggie has seen happen to him twice in his life.

"You are a witness with no documents, no corroboration and a considerable motive," Hester says. "You would be taken apart in an afternoon."

"I should think so."

"Whereas an eight-foot-seven male with a dog harness on him and ninety-one Scotch herring in his stomach is a fact that does not care what anybody's motive is."

"That," says Spilsbury, "is the entire reason I am sitting in this chair."

"Then let us have it said, so that nobody is surprised by it in November." Hester sets the pen down. "Your account is worth nothing without our animal. Our animal is a curiosity without your account. Neither of us goes anywhere alone, and neither of us has to like the other to go together."

"That is the position."

"And you will leave the moment it stops being the position."

"Of course," says Spilsbury. "So will you."

"Then we understand each other."

"I doubt it." He picks a thread off his cuff. "But I do not think we need to, and I should be careful, if I were you, about wanting it. Understanding is what people fall back on when the interests have gone."

"We had better find out." Hester turns a page in the pad and squares it to the edge of the table. "Suppose you are given the whole of your list. The account is signed, the blocks are cut, the plates are numbered, and all of it is sitting on this table in October."

"Yes."

"Then what? Who is it given to, and what do they do with it?"

"It stops," says Reggie.

"That is a wish, Reginald, not a mechanism." She still has not looked up. "I should like the mechanism from each of you, because I have a suspicion about this table and I would rather have it settled before lunch than in November."

---

"Paper," Reggie says. "Not a journal. Paper. Dewar and Lecky are moving live animals through British colonial ports."

"Which means?"

"An entry, a manifest, an export licence and a quarantine certificate for every consignment. And somebody is buying frozen herring by the ton from a broker who keeps books, because you cannot land forty tons of anything in this part of the world without four people writing it down."

"You want the ledgers," says Jean.

"I want the customs entries. You don't take a station like that apart from the front. You strangle what goes into it — feed, freight, and the licence to land a live animal."

"How long?"

"Months. If anybody will look."

"And this Institute is in a file by August," says the Colonel.

"Yes. I know."

---

"And you, Doctor?" says Jean.

"Both of those are charming," Spilsbury says, "and neither of them arrives in time."

"Go on."

"Nobody in this is buying mermaids. Nobody has ever paid a shilling for a mermaid." He takes his glasses off and puts them on the arm of the chair. "They are buying numbers about men. Escape depth, oxygen debt, cold, and how long a rating lasts in the water in March. That is the product. The animal is the apparatus."

"And?"

"And if the apparatus is chronically sick, the numbers do not transfer to a man. Four years of file becomes waste paper, and the chequebook shuts on a Tuesday afternoon, and nobody is ever told why."

"You would not stop it," Hester says. "You would discredit it."

"I would make it not worth doing." He picks the glasses up again. "In my experience that is the only thing that has ever stopped anything at all."

---

"And yours, ma'am."

"Mine is the slow one." Hester puts the pen down. "For twenty years I have asked one department after another to make this animal something. A bird. An otter. A seal. Anything that appears on a schedule with a close season and a penalty attached."

"And they won't."

"They will not, because nobody has ever put one in front of them."

"And now?"

"Now I have an eight-foot-seven male with a harness on him, drawn and dated and numbered in a run of four hundred." She lays her hand flat on the pad. "And a witness. And a customs trail, if Reginald can get one."

"We have had two of them in front of anybody who asked for four years," says Jean.

"We have two healthy animals in a licensed pen, Miss Leslie. If I put those in front of the Colonial Secretary I hand him the argument against me in the same breath."

"How?"

"They prove the animal does perfectly well in a box." Hester does not soften it at all. "That is what a man who does not wish to schedule it will take away, and he will be within his rights to."

"And a dead one with a harness on him?"

"Proves somebody is taking them in numbers and not saying so. That is a different exhibit altogether, and it is the only kind that has ever got anything onto a schedule in the history of this Empire."

"How long?"

"Five years. Possibly ten. I am sixty-two and I have made my peace with it."

---

The Colonel looks at the three of them for a while without saying anything.

"Then we have three plans," he says.

"We have three plans," says Hester, "two of which take years, and one of which takes six months and costs this Institute the only two animals it has."

"Four," says Pascual.

Everybody looks at him. He has not moved off the wall since nine o'clock.

"Four what?" says the Colonel.

"Four plans. There's the one where we do nothing at all, and those nineteen get bled twice a month until we're all dead."

"That is not a plan."

"It's what happens if nobody picks one of the other three." He folds his arms. "Put it on the paper. In about a month somebody at this table will start calling it the careful one, and I want it written down first that it isn't."

Nobody argues with him.

---

And then, at about the ninety-minute mark, with the thing all but settled, he says the other thing, in exactly the same voice, because it has been sitting in his head the whole time and there is no mechanism in him for leaving it there.

"You realise the body proves nothing on its own."

"It proves —"

"It proves that an animal was kept. It does not prove that being kept did him any harm, and a good man on the other side will say so in ten minutes and be right."

"Then what would?"

"A matched series." Spilsbury uncrosses his ankles. "You have a female past breeding and a male of six in a pen forty yards from this window, in known conditions, with four years of your own figures on them."

"Go on."

"Put the belt on the male. Run the tether series, the feed, the bleeding, all of it, to the same schedule, for six months, and take the same tissues at the end."

"And then."

"And then your dead animal is not an anecdote. He is an arm of an experiment."

Nobody says anything at all.

Hester's pen has stopped moving and has not been put down. The Colonel has not shifted in his chair, which with him is the loudest thing in the room. Pascual has come off the wall by about six inches and is standing with his weight forward on the balls of his feet, in the attitude of a man at a gate that is going to open, and he is looking not at Spilsbury but at Reggie.

They are all looking at Reggie.

"No," he says.

"You have not thought about it."

"I have thought about all of it," Reggie says, "in about a second and a half, including the part where it works, and the part where it is the only one of the four that arrives in time. You have not allowed for either. The answer is no."

"On what ground?"

"On the ground that I am not going to."

Over by the window Jean Leslie sits down on the sill rather suddenly, as though a chair had been moved, and looks at the side of Reggie's head with her mouth open about a quarter of an inch. She has worked for him for four years and she was here in May 1954.

Pascual goes back against the wall. Reggie does not see him do it and hears it.

Spilsbury considers the answer for a moment.

"That is not a reason," he says.

"It isn't. You may have it anyway."

"Hm." He takes a small black notebook out of his breast pocket, writes in it for about four seconds, and puts it back; and it is perfectly clear to everybody in the room that he has not the slightest idea what has just happened, and that he will ask again in August.

---

"One thing," says the Colonel, at the end, "and then we are finished. Why are you doing this?"

"Because they took it away from me."

"That's honest."

"It has the additional merit of being true." Spilsbury stands up and gets his hat off the floor. "I want to be quite clear, Colonel, because it will save everybody a great deal of misunderstanding in August."

"Be clear, then."

"I do not care about the animals. I have not cared about them at any point and I am not going to start at forty-four because the company is good."

"Then what is it?"

"A very interesting problem is being ruined by third-rate men who will have exhausted the material in a decade and learned nothing from it. They did it with my method. And they put me in a car."

"And that is all."

"That is all. Our interests align," Spilsbury says. "They align closely and they will go on aligning for some time, and you may rely on that more safely than you could rely on my being sorry, because a man's interests are a matter of record and his conscience is a matter of opinion." He puts the hat on. "Good morning."

---

Reggie comes to bed at two, and he has not stopped moving since half past eight yesterday morning, and there is something going on behind his sternum and in both hands that is plainly not going to let him sleep.

Pascual is awake. He is sitting on the edge of the cot in the dark with his elbows on his knees, which is not the attitude of a man lying waiting for anybody, and he does not put the lamp on.

"Come here."

"I'm filthy."

"I know exactly what you smell of, I've had four years of it. Come here."

So he goes.

---

The hand that comes up and takes him by the back of the neck is harder than it should be by about a third, and holds a second longer than it needs to, and is not entirely steady.

"Your hands are shaking."

"I know."

"Pascual —"

"Not with words." The grip does not slacken. "Not tonight. Anything else you like."

And Reggie, who has spent nine hours failing to notice a plate at his own elbow, notices that, and does the only useful thing on offer, which is to stop talking.

"Better."

"You're still in that room, though."

"I —"

"You've got another man's ledger going round behind your eyes and you've had it there since midnight." The hand shifts, thumb under the jaw, and tips his head where it wants it. "So we're both going somewhere else for an hour. I'm going to put you there and you don't get a say in it."

"Oh," Reggie says, rather faintly. "Well. Good."

---

It is not slow and it is not careful and it bears no relation whatever to the usual order of things.

There is almost no talking in it. The shirt goes, and the rest of it goes, and at one point Pascual gets him flat with a forearm laid across his chest and simply holds him there, breathing, for four or five seconds, with his head down; and Reggie lies under about two hundred and forty pounds of man in the dark and does not push back and does not say a word, because he has worked out what this is and what it is for and is entirely willing to be it.

Then the hand at his hip is too hard again, and they both feel it happen.

"*Perdón* —"

"Don't you dare."

"Reggie —"

"Don't you *dare*," Reggie says, and gets a hand up into the man's hair and pulls, and that is the end of the apologising.

After that it is very fast for a while and then it is not, because at some point Pascual deliberately stops himself, and the stopping is audible, and takes about a minute to do.

"You slowed down."

"Yes."

"On purpose."

"Yes." Into his ribs, low. "Give me the minute."

"Take two." A pause of some seconds. "Take one, actually. Take one and then for God's sake — *ah* — Pascual, please —"

"There it is." He sounds enormously pleased about it, which is the first thing all night that has sounded like him. "Say it again."

"Please."

"Again."

"You are a *horrible* man," Reggie says, with both hands shut in the sheet and his back off the cot, and then says please a third time anyway, and gets what he asked for, and is not required to think about anything whatever for some considerable while.

---

Afterwards he lies half across him with his ear on the winch scar and the heart going under it, slowing, and his own breath coming back from wherever it went, and the room smelling of salt and lamp oil and the two of them.

"Better?"

"I have no idea what my own name is."

"Good," Pascual says. "That was the whole of the exercise."

"Whose?"

There is a pause of about three seconds.

"Mine," Pascual says.

Then he is quiet for a while, with one hand moving on Reggie's back.

"You didn't eat yesterday."

"I —"

"There was a plate at your elbow at seven and it went away at nine and it hadn't been touched." No heat in it at all. "I know, because I put it there and I took it away."

"Ah."

"And you didn't see me go out at half past eight, and you didn't see me come in at ten. When I said your name at the door you looked up like a man hearing a bell in another building."

Reggie lies there with his face against a collarbone and finds that he has nothing whatever to say, because all of that is true and none of it had reached him.

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry, it's no use to me." A hand comes up and moves slowly in his hair, which undoes him rather. "I'm not asking you to stop."

"You could."

"I've seen what the two of you did to those blocks in nine hours, and I couldn't have done it with him in a year, and I'd be a liar if I said it wasn't worth having." He shifts. "I'm telling you what it costs. That's all."

"It won't —"

"Don't say it won't happen again." Pascual's chest moves under him, which is nearly a laugh. "It'll happen on Tuesday."

"Probably Monday."

"Monday, then. And on Monday I'll put a plate at your elbow and take it away again, and I'll say your name at the door, and you'll look up like a bell in another building."

"Pascual —"

"That's a thing I can carry." He puts his hand flat on Reggie's back. "Don't hide it from me. That's all. That's the arrangement, and it's the same arrangement as the other one."

"Yes."

"Say it properly."

"I won't hide it from you," Reggie says, into his neck, and means it entirely, and gets both arms round as much of the man as he can reach.

His shirt is on the floor by the door where it went, and the letter is in the breast pocket of it, where it has been since the second of May.

He has given Pascual the letter, and the date on it, and four readings of it, all four in order, twice, and a fifth that is not a reading and is not a fact and would not survive being said out loud in a room — and that one he has not given him, and took out once at four in the morning, and put back.

That is not hiding it. He is entirely clear about that for about four seconds.

---

"Pascual."

"Mm."

"You'd have gone through him."

"I'd have gone through him."

"I know." He puts his palm flat on the ribs where the heart is. "I stopped you for the arithmetic and for nothing else, and I want that said out loud, because you have been lying here since midnight working out whether I stopped you because I've gone over to him."

The chest under his hand does not move for a moment.

"That's not what I was thinking."

"It is."

"...It's some of what I was thinking," Pascual says.

"Yes. Well. It isn't true, and I'd rather have it in the room."

---

He is nearly asleep when Pascual says it, in the dark, to the ceiling, not to him.

"I know why you stayed now."

"Stayed where?"

There is quite a long pause.
