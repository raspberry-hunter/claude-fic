# Section Four

He has been sitting in the office since eight doing nothing whatever that could be described as work, and when the knock comes he says *come in* in a voice that he hears leave his own mouth and does not recognise, and then has about a second and a half to stand there being appalled by it before the door opens.

"Don't," says Pascual, from the doorway.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to apologise for how you said *come in*."

"I —"

"And then I'd have had to tell you it was all right, and it isn't all right, and we'd both have to stand here knowing that." He comes in and shuts the door with his heel. "So don't. I won't either. We'll do it properly at some point when we're not carrying something."

"Are you carrying something?"

"I'm carrying a great deal."

"Then sit down."

"No," Pascual says. "You're going to want to be standing up."

---

He gives it the way he gives a finding: the whole of it, in order, at one speed, with nothing held back and nothing decorated.

Two or three a year, since the pens changed. Fat animals in good pens in company, fed and watered and warm, not dived and not hurt. Four in four months that Spilsbury saw with his own eyes — March of last year, the September, two in the winter. All four opened. *No cause found* written four times in the same hand and filed. And a ledger with a column for *date in* and a column for *date out* and none at all for *why*.

Reggie does not sit down. He does not, for about four minutes, do anything at all except stand in the middle of his own office with his hands behind his back.

"Say the thing about husbandry again."

"He told him in January that it was the single most important observation in the building, and the man said it was a husbandry problem and that husbandry wasn't his department."

"Which it was."

"Which it was. And still is." Pascual has his arms folded and has not moved from the door. "Reggie. It's ours. That's not their animal on that beach, it's ours; it's the same animal; it's the fifth one anybody's ever described and the only one that's been opened by somebody who knew what he was looking at."

"Yes."

"So stop standing there and get a chair."

---

He gets two chairs and the four-year run of the Institute's own mortality book and the three tables he built in 1953 and has not looked at since the retraction, and they are at it until twenty past three in the morning.

It is the best night's work he has done since the twenty-second of May 1954 and it is nothing whatever like the thirtieth of May.

That is the part he notices at about midnight and takes out and looks at and puts back, because it will not stand being looked at while there is work on the table. With Spilsbury it is a downhill run with the brakes off. With this man it is somebody who says *no, go back, you've assumed something in the third line* and is right, four times in seven hours, and who twice makes him put a pencil down and say a thing out loud in ordinary English before he is allowed to write it in the book.

At half past one Pascual gets up without a word, goes out, comes back with bread and cold fish and the wet green pickle, puts the plate at Reggie's elbow, and sits down and eats his half of it.

Reggie eats the whole of his, including the pickle.

Neither of them mentions that either.

---

The thing arrives at ten past three and it arrives the way they always arrive, which is sideways.

He is not looking for it. He is doing something dull: going through the Institute's own book for every death and every removal in four years and setting them against Jean's water figures, because if the answer were temperature or salinity it would be sitting in that column and it is not.

Alpha. Died 1952, alone in a box, adrenals like a bad joke. Accounted for.

Beta. May 1954. Accounted for, God help them all, in eleven days of his own handwriting.

And then the column that is not a column, which is the one he has never had a reason to draw before: not *who died*, but *who else was in the pen at the time*.

He draws it. It takes about ninety seconds and it is four entries long and it says nothing at all, because the Martin has never had more than two animals in a pen and has never lost one out of a pair.

He sits looking at his own four-line table.

"Pascual."

"Mm."

"How long was Gamma alone."

"After Alpha? About five months." Pascual does not look up. "Then Beta came in July and they were in separate pens and she could hear him. Why?"

"And the thirty-one belts."

"What about them."

"Eighteen calves in 1953, and six calves and three grown animals in 1955." Reggie puts the pencil down very carefully, because his hand has started doing something he does not want witnessed. "They put them in pairs in March of last year."

"Section four."

"Section four. Pen dimensions, gate schedule, and the paragraph at the end where I told a man I'd never heard of that he should on no account keep an animal on its own."

"Yes."

"And then in March of last year they started taking animals *out* of pairs. To dive them. Two hundred feet on a line, one at a time, because you can't dive two animals on one line."

The room goes very quiet.

"*Ay*," says Pascual.

---

"Say it," Reggie says. "I want to hear it out of somebody else's mouth, because if I say it first I'll believe it too fast."

"You've got them dying of the other one being gone."

"I've got a hypothesis that they die of the other one being gone, and I've got no mechanism and no series and one animal, and that animal's consistent with about four things."

"It's consistent with him breaking a tether he'd worn for eight years and swimming until he stopped."

"It's consistent with that, yes."

"And with him being in good condition and clean lungs and ordinary adrenals and nothing whatever on the slab."

"Yes."

"And with two or three a year in good pens with nothing on the post-mortem."

"*Yes.*" He is up and moving now and cannot help it. "And with Gamma in the winter of 'fifty-two."

"Gamma —"

"It's in that book. I've read that book four hundred times. It sat in that column for four years and I went past it." He gets as far as the window and turns round. "Five months on her own. Off her food for nine weeks of it. And she came back when Beta arrived in the next pen, where she could *hear* him —"

"Sit down."

"I'm not going to sit down —"

"Then breathe, and say the other half of it."

---

He stops in the middle of the floor.

"Which other half."

"The one you got to at about ten past three and have been walking round ever since." Pascual has not raised his voice and has not got up. "You're a scientist at twenty past three in the morning with the best idea you've had in four years. Say what it wants."

And Reggie, standing in his own office in his shirtsleeves with his glasses in his hand, hears himself say it, flatly, the way you read out a result:

"It wants a pair, in known conditions, with four years of figures on them, and it wants one of them taken away."

Neither of them says anything.

"That isn't a proposal," he says.

"I know it isn't."

"I want that clear in this room at twenty past three in the morning, because I shan't be able to say it as cleanly at noon in front of six people."

"It's clear."

"It is what the hypothesis wants. It is not what I want. Those are different sentences."

"They are."

"Say them back to me."

"They're different sentences," Pascual says, and gets up, and takes the glasses out of his hand and puts them on the desk, and puts both hands on the sides of his face, which is a thing neither of them has done since the twenty-second of June, and holds on. "Go to bed. You've got it and it isn't going anywhere, and you'll write it worse at four than at nine."

---

They do not get to bed.

They get four feet inside the door of Pascual's room, in the dark, with the bolt not shot, and then Reggie — who has been careful for twelve days, who has knocked at that door four times, who this evening said *thank you* for a plate of fish like a man staying in somebody else's house — stops being careful.

"Shoot the bolt."

"In a minute."

"*Now*, Pascual."

He shoots the bolt.

Then Reggie gets a fistful of the front of his shirt and puts him against the door with it, which is futile as a piece of mechanics and has never once been resisted, and Pascual lets himself be put there and gets both hands into the back of his shirt and holds on.

Neither of them says anything.

---

That is the thing about it.

They talk. They have talked through every hour of this that either of them can remember, in two languages, with instructions and complaints and a running commentary and a great deal that would not go into a book; it is half of what it is; it is the half Reggie would keep if somebody made him choose.

Tonight there is the sound of the two of them breathing and the generator and nothing else at all.

It is not slow and it is not kind. He gets the shirt off the man and puts his mouth wherever he can reach and is not remotely particular about it, and Pascual, who is four inches taller and ninety pounds heavier and could put him on the boards with one forearm, goes where he is put and stays there and holds on harder than he needs to.

And then, at some point, the hands come up off his back and take hold of his face. Both of them. In the dark. The way they have since the twentieth of May 1954.

Reggie turns his head.

It is not a decision. It happens about a second before he could have stopped it and it is finished before he has understood it, and he puts his mouth against the inside of the man's wrist instead, which is affectionate, which is a thing he would have done anyway, which fools nobody whatever.

The hands come down.

Neither of them says one word about it, then or at any point afterwards, and it goes on, and it is good in the narrow sense in which a thing can be good while something is the matter with it.

---

Afterwards they are on the floor, because they never reached the cot.

The boards are not comfortable. Neither of them moves.

"Are you all right."

"Yes."

"Reggie."

"I'm all right. I'm tired and I've been up since five and I've got a hypothesis that wants something I'm not going to give it." His own voice sounds entirely ordinary to him, and he has no idea at all what it sounds like from the outside. "Are you?"

"Yes."

Two men on the floor of a hot room at four in the morning, each having asked the other the only question in the building that matters and been handed the only answer that is no use, and both of them perfectly aware of it, and neither of them willing to be the one who says so first tonight.

After a while Pascual gets an arm under him. That much is not a lie.

"It's going to take a while," he says, to the ceiling.

"Yes."

"I'm not going anywhere while it takes a while."

"No," says Reggie. "Nor am I."

Which is true, and is the only entirely true thing said in that room in an hour, and is nothing like enough to be going on with.

---

The mail boat comes in on the Wednesday at twenty past ten, and Jean brings the bag up, and there are nine letters, and one of them is on very good paper with an engraving of nothing at all on it.

> DEWAR & LECKY
> Consignment Agents
> Bay Street, Nassau
>
> Colonel J. R. Bevan, C.B.E.
> William Martin Institute for Marine Studies
>
> Dear Colonel Bevan,
>
> We write once more, and we hope not unwelcomely, in the matter of the Institute's collections.
>
> We understand that the Institute had the good fortune in April of this year to recover an adult specimen of *N. aestuarius* in condition permitting full examination, and that the material has been worked up with the thoroughness for which the Institute is justly known.
>
> Our client would be glad to acquire the skeleton, or the preserved material, or such portion of either as the Institute might care to release, upon terms which we are confident the Colonel would find generous. Our client has considerable resources in this field and would welcome the opportunity of discussing a wider and more permanent arrangement, including the placing of animals.
>
> We should be happy for a representative to call. Mr Lecky expects to be in Kingston during the second week of August and would put himself entirely at the Colonel's convenience.
>
> We remain, etc.

---

They are all in the Colonel's office by noon, and nobody has to be sent for.

"How do they know," says John.

"Four ways, and it does not matter which." Hester has the letter flat on the desk and her hands folded on top of the pad. "In April I wrote to four firms in the saddlery trade describing a lined band for a large aquatic subject, from an address in the West Indies."

"And one of them —"

"One of them has a customer who buys precisely that article by the dozen. A covering note from Walsall to Nassau saying *we have had an enquiry of a similar kind* is ordinary good business and would cost the writer nothing."

"Hester —"

"That is the first. The second is my husband's enquiry in June, which was answered by a refusal and will have been mentioned to somebody."

"And the third."

"Reginald wrote to Plymouth yesterday with a great deal more in it than he has ever put on paper. I approved that letter, and altered one word in it, and would approve it again."

"And the fourth?"

"The fourth is that on the twenty-sixth of April, in broad daylight, four men from the village carried a mermaid up a public beach on a tarpaulin, and fourteen more people saw the truck."

Nobody says anything.

"It matters to me which," says the Colonel.

"It matters to me a great deal which. I shall find out, and it will take me until October." Hester does not move her hands. "In the meantime the room may take it that I am the likeliest of the four."

"Hester."

"I am not going to be delicate about it. We have not the time."

---

"Good," says Spilsbury.

Six people look at him.

"You are all about to be frightened, and I would rather you were frightened accurately." He has not sat down and is standing at the end of the desk with his hat in his hands. "So take it in the order I give it. They have known the Martin has a body since May or June at the latest."

"And?"

"And they have done nothing about it for two months. And now a letter."

"Because we got close to something," says Jean.

"Because you got close to something. A dealer writes to buy a skeleton when he is collecting, and he writes to buy a skeleton when he wants it off a table."

"And this is —"

"This is the second kind. I have read a great deal of that firm's correspondence, and it does not usually offer to put a partner on a boat."

"Then they're worried."

"They are worried, which means they think you have something, which means they think it can hurt them, which means it can." Spilsbury turns the hat over once. "You did not know that before this morning. You know it now. It cost you a letter."

"It cost us rather more than a letter," says the Colonel.

"It cost you the licence in November and possibly a good deal else, and I did not say the price was low. I said what you had bought."

---

And then Reggie gives them the hypothesis, because it is the only other thing on the agenda and because he has spent four hours getting it into ordinary English, and he gives it well, and Jean asks two hard questions and John asks a better one and Hester writes down every word.

When he finishes, Spilsbury is looking at the window.

"Well," he says. "Yes. Obviously."

"You've had it all along."

"I had the observation. I did not have the mechanism." He turns round. "No mortality series. No water figures. No animal kept properly for four years by somebody who wrote it all down."

"And we've got one."

"You have. And you know what it wants, because you said so at about ten past three this morning with a pencil in your hand."

"How do you —"

"Because I should have. I am asking you to say it in this room instead of making me say it for the third time."

The office is extremely quiet.

Reggie is aware of Pascual at the wall. He is aware of Hester's pencil. He is aware of the Colonel, who has not moved, and of Jean at the window who has gone very still, and of John, who is twenty-six and has just understood something and has gone the colour of the wall.

"It wants a pair in known conditions," he says, "with four years of figures on them. And one of them taken away."

"Yes."

"I am not going to do it."

"You said that on the thirty-first of May in one and a half seconds," says Spilsbury, "and I wrote the time down. That one took you four."

And then he looks, unhurriedly, across the office at the man against the wall.

Pascual is already looking at him.

It lasts perhaps a second, and Reggie catches the end of it and not the beginning, and neither of the other two gives him anything to work with.

---

Nobody says anything about the four seconds.

They go on. There is a great deal to go on with: the second week of August, and Mr Lecky, and whether the Colonel replies at all and in what terms, and Jean's launch, and what goes in the bundle and what does not, and whether John's plates should be photographed and a second set sent somewhere else entirely, which is Hester's idea and is a very good one.

The photographs take about ninety seconds to become a plan.

"Two sets," says Hester. "One in the bundle. One somewhere that is neither this island nor Plymouth."

"Where?"

"I have a cousin in Bristol. A solicitor. Dull, honest, and in possession of a safe."

"Is he interested in mermaids?"

"He is not interested in anything whatever. That is the point of him."

"I can't photograph four hundred and sixty plates by August," says John.

"You can photograph forty," says Jean. "The forty that matter. Reggie — which forty."

"They all matter, Jean, that's the whole —"

"Which *forty*, Dr Tar."

And Reggie, who has just been told by a woman in a headscarf to pick forty, picks forty, out loud, in about twelve minutes, while John writes the numbers down; and it is a better forty than he would have arrived at in a week on his own, because three separate times Jean says *and what does the other side say about that one*, and he has to take one out and put a duller one in.

"And the belt," says John.

"The belt doesn't photograph."

"The belt photographs beautifully. I did it in May. It's plate four hundred and twenty-two."

"Then why hasn't anybody —"

"Because nobody reads plate numbers," says John, "which I have been saying since 1954, and which every person in this room has ignored, and I should like it minuted."

"Minuted," says Hester, writing.

They are at it until half past one and they are good at it, and that is the thing about this station that Reggie has never stopped being surprised by. Six people who cannot agree on the plan can take a problem apart on a verandah in ninety minutes and hand it back in pieces that fit. By the end of it there is a list in Hester's hand with nine items on it and a name against each one.

Nobody mentions the four seconds.

Not at lunch, where Pascual sits at the other end of the table from him for the fourth day running and John looks between them twice and says nothing.

Not in the long room in the afternoon, where they get through three hours of blocks with the door shut and pass each other four times at the bench without either of them touching the other once, which is a thing that has not been true in this building since 1954.

Not at supper.

And not at the rail at six o'clock, when Pascual comes and stands beside him and after about a minute puts a hand flat between his shoulder blades and leaves it there, and does not say anything at all, and takes it away again when Juan comes up the path.

They do not mention it because there is nothing to mention. He said no. He said it plainly, in front of six people, and he meant it, and it is going in the minutes as a refusal, and anybody reading those minutes in October will find a straight line.

It took four seconds.

He goes to bed at half past ten and lies there in the dark next to a man who is pretending, badly and kindly, to be asleep, and does the only piece of arithmetic he has been any good at all day, which is that on the thirty-first of May it took one and a half.
