# Made to Measure

By the second week the man has a chair at the table, a bench in the long room, three shelves in the plate cupboard that nobody gave him, and a habit of answering rhetorical questions.

"God, is it going to rain again," says John, at breakfast, to the window.

"Yes," says Spilsbury, without looking up from the offprint. "Until about four."

It rains until about four.

By the Wednesday John has stopped being frightened of him and has started doing it back, which Pascual watches with a certain amount of professional interest, because John Weald at twenty-six is nobody's junior any more and has worked out inside nine days that the way to handle Alec Spilsbury is to treat him exactly as literally as he treats everybody else.

"Do you want the plate at four times or twenty?"

"Twenty."

"Would you like it before or after I've had my lunch?"

"Before."

"Right," says John, "then you can have it after," and goes and has his lunch, and Spilsbury looks after him with an expression Pascual has not seen on that face before and cannot identify, and which on anybody else he would have to call pleased.

Juan's mother has meanwhile established that the man will eat literally anything put in front of him and comment on none of it, and has taken this as an insult and a challenge, and the food at the Martin has got noticeably better since the thirtieth of May. Nobody has told her why she is doing this. Nobody is going to.

He does not say thank you. Not once, in a fortnight, to anybody, for anything, including the time Jean pulled him out of the water at the slipway by the back of his collar. It is not rudeness. Pascual has been watching for the rudeness and it is not there; the apparatus is simply absent, the way the sense of smell is absent in some people, and after about a week the station stops waiting for it and gets on.

What he is, is useful. That is the part nobody on this island was ready for.

---

Hester comes down to the long room on the Friday the eighth with the pad and a new box file and stands in the middle of the floor until everybody stops working.

"I am building the bundle," she says. "From today. Not in November — today, while everybody still remembers what happened in what order."

"What do you want?" says Reggie.

"Everything. The day book, the plate run, Miss Leslie's elimination sheet, the cable from Plymouth, Dr Spilsbury's account as he signs each section of it, my own drawings, and the belt itself, which goes in a sealed bag and does not come out except in front of two people."

"That's the physical."

"That is the physical. I also want the paper." Hester puts the box file down on the end of the bench. "Every letter in and out of this Institute that touches the subject, from the twenty-sixth of April."

"Which letters."

"The three institutions. Both of my husband's Nassau letters. My four to the trade and whatever comes back, the Plymouth correspondence, and all of yours, Reginald."

"All of mine."

"All of yours. Including anything in a pocket." She says it without any particular weight, to the room, in the voice she uses about the fisheries return. "The twenty-second. I shall be systematic about it and I shall be a great nuisance."

Pascual is at the microtome with his back three-quarters to all of this and does not turn round.

He knows exactly which pocket it is in. He has known since the second of May, when Reggie came up the jetty with Jean's bag over his shoulder and a letter folded along its old folds, and read it out to him that evening at the rail with the whole of it in his hand, every word, twice, and four readings of it, and asked him what he thought.

And he has known since ten past midnight on the thirtieth of May, in the doorway of this room, with his arms folded, when a man in the good chair said *in February they gave me a cheque and a car to the aerodrome* and Pascual came off the frame and stood up straight and did not say anything at all.

The twenty-second.

That gives him a fortnight.

---

Cattell & Barrow of Walsall reply to Mrs H. Bevan on the twelfth, on paper with an engraving of a factory on it, and they are absolutely delighted.

> We are in receipt of your enquiry of the 2nd ult. and are happy to say that the work described presents no difficulty whatever, our house having supplied padded surcingles and body-bands to zoological gardens, circuses and private collections in this country and abroad for upwards of sixty years.
>
> In the matter of a lined band for a large aquatic subject we have some direct experience, having executed a run of twenty-two to a similar specification in the autumn of 1953, and a further nine to the same pattern in the spring of 1955, all to the order of a shipping agency acting on a client's behalf. We enclose the schedule of that order as a guide to what may be done, and would observe that the linings gave excellent service, no complaint having been received.
>
> We should be glad to know the number of subjects, the girths where these can be taken, and whether Madam requires the Corbie pattern of lock, which we recommend, being secure against an intelligent animal.

"Being secure against an intelligent animal," says Jean.

"Read the last line again," says Hester.

"*No complaint having been received.*"

"Yes."

---

The schedule is two sheets and it is a list of girths in inches, thirty-one of them, in two blocks with the dates at the head.

Pascual takes it to the bench and gets his own book out and does not say a word for forty minutes, and everybody leaves him alone.

Twenty-two, autumn 1953. He copies them out in order: 18. 18½. 19. 19. 20. 20. 21. 21. 21½. 22. 22. 22½. 23. 23. 24. 24½. 25. 26. 27. 31. 33. 38.

Nine, spring 1955: 19. 20½. 21. 22. 24. 27. 44. 45½. 47.

He puts the growth curve beside it. It is Chumley's curve and it is one animal and it is not good enough and it is the only one in the world, and he has said all three of those things out loud to Reggie on four separate occasions and will go on saying them.

Then he does the arithmetic, slowly, twice, the way he does everything.

"Right," he says.

They come over. All of them; even the Colonel, who has been reading the shipping paper at the other end of the room for an hour and a half and pretending not to listen.

"Twenty-two belts in 1953. Eighteen of them are under twenty-six inches." Pascual puts his finger on the column. "Twenty-one inches is a calf of five. Eighteen's a calf of about three and a half, if it's anything like mine, and I want it said that mine's one animal."

"Noted," says Hester.

"That's eighteen animals under seven years old, in one order, in one autumn."

Nobody says anything for a moment. John has gone the colour of the wall.

"And the second lot," says Reggie.

"Nine in 1955. Six of them are the same. Small." He moves his finger down. "And three of them are forty-four, forty-five and a half, and forty-seven."

"Forty-four and a half," says Reggie.

"Forty-four and a half was ours. Off the beach." Pascual squares the sheet with the edge of the bench and takes his hand off it. "So in 1953 they were buying belts for calves, and in 1955 they were still buying belts for calves, and they'd started buying them for grown animals as well, which they hadn't done before at all."

"Because before, the grown ones died."

"Because before, the grown ones died."

The Colonel folds his shipping paper in half and puts it down.

"Why calves," he says.

"Because you can take one." Pascual finds that his voice has gone somewhere flat and does not do anything about it. "A grown male's eight feet seven and four hundred pounds and he bites."

"And a calf."

"A calf of four you'll get over the side of a boat with three men and a net. He eats less. He'll take the belt without fighting it, and he'll grow into it." He puts the sheet down on the bench. "They let ours out three times. It's on the strap."

"God," says John.

"That's the whole of the policy and it fits on a postcard."

---

He finds the other thing on the fifteenth and he finds it by accident, which is the part that will bother him later.

He is cutting bone. There is no reason to cut bone. He has done the alveolar blocks from the drawn sockets because those are the point, and then, because he is a thorough man and because there is a fortnight of ice-work still in him and nothing to do with it, he takes the long bones as well and decalcifies them in acid and cuts them, and gets nothing at all out of the forearm and nothing out of the ribs.

And then the head of the left humerus.

There is a wedge of it dead. It is about the size of a thumbnail, sitting up under the joint surface, and the bone inside it has gone: the marrow spaces are empty and the lamellae have no cells in them, and around the edge of the wedge there is a rind of new bone laid down thick and hard where the living part has walled it off. It is old. It is years old. Under it, on the next section, there is a second one, smaller, healed flat.

He sits back.

He knows what he is looking at in the sense that any pathologist would: a piece of bone that has had its blood cut off and died, and been fenced in by the rest. He does not know why. There is no fracture. There is no infection anywhere near it — no pus, no sequestrum, no periosteal mess. The animal was not old. The other humerus, when he goes back and does it, has one as well, further down.

He works at it until half past nine at night and gets no further, and at half past nine he does the sum he has been not doing since about six, which goes:

There is one man on this island who will know what that is.

---

"Doctor."

"Pobil."

"I've got something I want you to look at."

Spilsbury is at the end of the long room with his jacket over the stool and four of John's plates weighted down with a bottle. He does not say *what* and he does not say *now?* He gets up, and comes, and sits down at Pascual's microscope, and Pascual stands there with his arms folded and watches the back of the man's head and thinks about the twenty-eighth of May 1954.

It takes about nine seconds.

"Ah," says Spilsbury. "Yes."

"Say it."

"Caisson disease. Bone necrosis."

"That's a tunnel man's complaint."

"It is in every tunnel man in London who worked under air for more than about four years, and in a good number of Navy divers." He moves the stage an eighth of an inch. "Humerus and femur more often than anywhere else, because of the way the vessels run."

"There's a second one lower down."

"There is. And a third, I should think, if you cut further."

"There is."

"Then he was taken to depth and brought up faster than he should have been, repeatedly, over a period of years, and his bone died in pieces and he walled the pieces off and carried on."

Pascual puts both hands flat on the bench.

"Did it hurt him?"

"I have no idea," Spilsbury says. "It hurts men. It hurts them a great deal when the joint goes, and the joint here has not gone, so probably not yet." He sits back. "It did not kill him, before you ask. Nobody dies of that."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were going to ask in about four seconds."

---

"You knew."

"I suspected."

"In February you told them they were losing animals to something they weren't measuring."

"I did."

"That's this."

"That is a piece of it and I should be surprised if it were all of it." Spilsbury takes his glasses off and holds them up to the lamp and puts them on again, which is a thing he does when he is deciding how much to say, and Pascual has had two weeks to learn it. "I could not prove it there. I had no histology, because I had no pathologist, because the man they had could not cut bone and would not learn."

"And you've had a pathologist for a fortnight."

"Yes."

"And you sat in that chair on the thirty-first and gave us the ledger and the tethers and the feed and the depth, and you didn't say the words *cut the long bones*."

"No."

"Why."

Spilsbury looks at him for a moment with what is, unmistakably, interest.

"Because I wanted to know whether you would get there," he says.

"And?"

"You did. Sixteen days. It would have taken the man at that station four years." He turns the glasses over once. "And it is now in your hand and your book and your handwriting, which is worth a great deal more than it would have been in mine."

"That's —"

"Yes."

"You let me —"

"I let you do your work," says Spilsbury, "and I shall not apologise for it, because it was not done to you. It was done about you."

"That's a distinction."

"It is the whole of the distinction. The result is the best single thing in that box file, and it belongs to this Institute and not to a man with a motive." He takes his jacket off the stool. "Be angry if you like. I should be, in your position. It would not make you right."

He goes as far as the door.

"Pobil."

"What."

"Cut the femoral heads tomorrow, and the bodies of the last four vertebrae." He does not turn round. "And do it before you tell Tar, because he will want to be in the room, and you will do it faster without him."

And he goes out, and Pascual stands there in an empty long room at ten o'clock at night, being extremely angry, with a piece of perfectly good advice in his hands.

---

The weather comes through on the night of the nineteenth.

It is not a hurricane and nobody at any time thinks it is a hurricane. It is a line of squalls off a wave that has been sitting to the south-east for three days, and Jean, who reads the glass twice a day and has done since 1953, gets them a clear four hours' warning, and by eight o'clock the launch is up the slip and the shutters are on and the plates are in the tin locker and everything on this island that could go anywhere has been tied to something.

At twenty past ten the sea starts coming over the low end of the wall.

At half past ten the old timber gate at the seaward end of Gamma's pen, which was put in by the United States Navy in 1943 and has been Item Four on Reggie's list since 1954, goes at the top hinge.

"*Ay, no.* Juan! Juan, the lamps —"

They have perhaps twenty minutes, and everybody knows it inside about four seconds, because everybody on this station has stood at that rail and had it explained to them what an animal in an open gate in a running sea will do.

What happens then is the thing Pascual will think about for a long time afterwards, for a number of reasons, of which the first and simplest is that it works.

Jean has the two big lamps rigged off the generator hut and pointing down into the water before anybody asks her to. John and Juan get the spare pen gate — the same one they took off its hinges for the body in April, which has been leaning against the store ever since precisely because nobody got round to putting it back — down the path in the rain in under four minutes. The Colonel, who is sixty-three and has no business on that deck, stands at the rail with a lamp in his fist and calls the water for them for forty minutes without once saying anything that is not a fact. Hester is up at the house with the doors open and every light on, which sounds like nothing and means that every man on that deck can see where the path is.

And Reggie takes the whole thing apart into pieces of work in about a minute and a half and hands them out, which is the thing he does, which is why he has this job.

"Gate first. Pascual, in the water, you've got Chumley — north pen and shut it. He'll follow you and he won't follow anybody else. John, Juan: spare gate across the gap the moment Pascual's clear, don't wait for me. Jean, lights *on the gap*, I don't care what's in the pen. Colonel, call the sets."

"And Gamma," says Spilsbury.

"Gamma's mine."

"Gamma is past breeding, she has been in that pen since 1952, she is going to sit down on the bottom in the corner and put her tail under her, and you weigh ten stone."

There is a silence of about a second and a half, in the rain, with the sea going over the wall.

"Go on, then," says Reggie.

---

He gets in with her at twenty to eleven and he is in the water with her for nineteen minutes.

Pascual has Chumley by then — six years old and frightened and entirely willing to come, up the shallow end and through into the north pen at a walking pace with Pascual's hand flat on his shoulder and Pascual talking to him in Spanish the whole way, which is what he has been doing every Tuesday for two years for precisely this reason — and the gate goes across behind them, and then there is nothing to do but stand at the rail in the rain with his heart going and watch.

Spilsbury does not hurry her. That is the first thing.

He gets down beside her on the bottom in six feet of water in the dark with a sea running over the wall every fifteen seconds, and he stays down until he has to come up, and goes down again, and he does not take hold of her at any point. He puts a hand where she can see it and leaves it there. He does it a dozen times. And somewhere in the fourteenth minute the old animal comes up off the bottom and goes with him, along the wall, at her own speed, the whole length of the pen and through the gap into the north pen with Chumley, and the gate goes across, and it is done.

He comes out up the ladder streaming, and sits down on the wet concrete for a moment with his arms on his knees, because he is forty-four and has been in cold water in the dark for nineteen minutes, and nobody says anything.

Pascual stands at the rail with the rain coming off the end of his nose.

On the twenty-eighth of May 1954, this man got into a pen with an animal that was not his, without asking, because he wanted to see it, and Pascual came round the corner and saw him in there and swore in two languages on a wet concrete deck and has not taken a word of it back in two years and does not intend to.

Both of those things are true. They are going to have to go on being true at the same time.

He goes and gets a blanket out of the store and drops it over the man's shoulders from behind and does not say anything at all, and Spilsbury does not say anything either, and that, in the event, is the whole of it.

---

"Item Four," says Reggie, at one in the morning, sitting on the floor of the long room with his back against the bench and a cup of tea he has not drunk.

"Item Four."

"Nineteen fifty-four. I wrote *replace seaward gate, Gamma's pen — timber, rotten at the head, Navy 1943* on a list, and I have carried that list forward every quarter for two years, and there has never been a quarter when it was the most urgent thing on it."

"There hasn't."

"No."

"And there won't be next quarter either," Pascual says. "Next quarter the thing at the top of the list is a station nobody will give us the position of, with nineteen animals in it."

"Yes."

"And you'll write *replace seaward gate* at the bottom of the new list in your own hand, and carry it forward again."

Reggie puts the cup down.

"Then say it to me in April."

"I'll say it to you in April."

"Say it to me in April in front of Hester, because I shall argue with you in April and I won't argue with you in front of Hester."

"*Bueno.*" He puts his hand out and takes the tea and drinks it, since nobody else is going to. "That's the first sensible arrangement anybody's made in this building since the one about the coat."

---

They are in bed by two and neither of them is any use to anybody, and it does not matter in the slightest, because it turns out that the effect of having spent three hours in the rain not losing two animals is not what either of them expected and is exactly what both of them want.

There is not much talking in it. There is some laughing, which they have not had for a fortnight, and which nearly ends the whole business twice. And then Reggie, who is still cold to the bone in the small of the back and has said so, gets put face down with about two hundred and forty pounds of warm man arranged deliberately along the whole length of him, and stops talking altogether, and Pascual takes his time from the nape of the neck downward with his mouth and both hands and a running commentary in two languages about exactly what is wrong with him, and by the time he gets to the base of the spine the man underneath has stopped being cold and has started making a noise that Pascual would cheerfully commit several crimes to hear again.

"*Ay*, listen to you."

"Don't —"

"Don't what."

"Don't stop, don't slow down, don't say anything clever —"

"I'm not saying anything clever. I'm saying you've got the whole of the Atlantic Ocean in you and I'm getting it out." He puts his mouth to the hollow at the base of the neck and bites, carefully. "Ask me for it."

"Pascual —"

"*Pídemelo.*"

And Reggie says it, in English and then, badly, in Spanish, which he has been working on for two years in secret and has never once got the ending right, and Pascual laughs into his shoulder and gives him what he asked for and does not correct the grammar until afterwards.

---

Afterwards, much later, in the dark, with the rain gone through and the roof dripping and Reggie lying across him going heavier by the minute:

"Hester wants the correspondence on Friday."

"Mm."

"All of it. She means all of it." A yawn. "I shall have to give her the April letter."

And there it is. Open, on the table, with a lamp over it.

*Look at the date on it before you hand it over.* Eight words. He has them in his mouth.

And the reasoning arrives at the same time, because it always does, and it is good reasoning, which is the trouble with it. It is not a fact; it is a shape, and the difference between those two is the whole of his trade. It is a shape about a man he was ready to put through a window three weeks ago, said by the one person in this building with the most obvious reason in the world to want that man discredited, eight days after that same man lay in this bed and told him, accurately, what he had been lying there thinking. He would be listened to. That is the problem. Reggie would take it, and go and check it, and if it turned out to be a man writing in April about a job he had lost in February because he had not finished losing it yet, then Pascual will have spent something, and it does not go back in the box.

And under all of that, arriving last and taking about a second, is the one he does look at properly:

Hester will find it on Friday. In ten seconds, with the pad and the letter side by side, because that is what she does and she has never missed a figure in her life. And then it will be hers. And it will be a thing the bundle turned up rather than a thing Pascual Pobil said about Alec Spilsbury in the dark.

He looks at that for slightly longer than he is comfortable with.

Then he says, "Give her the plate sequence at the same time. She'll want the letter and the plates in the same week or she'll have them in two boxes and be cross about it in October."

"Mm. Yes. Good."

"And go to sleep, you're freezing again."

"I'm not."

"You are. Come here."

He gets him arranged and gets the blanket over the pair of them and puts his hand flat between the man's shoulder blades, and Reggie is asleep in about ninety seconds, which he does perhaps once a fortnight.

He has not lied to him in two years. He has not lied to him tonight, either.

He lies awake for some considerable time working out why those are not the same sentence, and does not get to the end of it, and sleeps.
