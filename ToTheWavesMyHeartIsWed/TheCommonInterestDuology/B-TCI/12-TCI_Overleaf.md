# Overleaf

The submission goes in on the twenty-eighth of September and it is nineteen pages and it is the best thing Hester Bevan has ever made.

It does not mention the programme. It does not mention Nassau, or Dewar & Lecky, or a ledger with no column for *why*. It says, in the flat voice of a woman who has been writing to departments since 1928, that the Institute has held these two animals since 1952 and 1954 respectively; that both are in better condition now than on arrival; that the mortality, husbandry and growth records are complete, monthly, and in three hands; and that the Institute would be glad to receive an inspector at any time with four days' notice.

It encloses forty photographs.

Reggie reads it twice and cannot find a word to move.

"It's very good."

"It is adequate," says Hester. "It has one weakness and it is not mine. This Institute is four scientists on a sandbank and no submission on earth makes that untrue."

---

He puts the protocol away on the twenty-ninth.

It is fourteen sheets by then and it has taken him twenty-six evenings and it is, he is aware, the best single piece of work he has done in six years. Protocol, subjects, intervention, duration, recovery, the tissues and the timings, the statistical requirement, the water and growth series it would need and which he has not got and is not going to ask for.

And, on the last two pages, under the heading he ruled on the twenty-sixth of September at about one in the morning, the reason it cannot be done.

He does not show it to anybody. He has been meaning to since the twentieth and there has never been an hour when it would not have sounded like a man justifying himself, and it has sat on his desk in plain sight for four weeks instead, which he had thought was the same thing.

---

The reply comes on the fourth of October, a Thursday, by the government launch, and Jean brings it up, and Hester opens it at the verandah table at twenty past ten in the morning with all seven of them there.

She reads it standing. Then she sits down, which Reggie has seen her do twice.

"Read it out," says the Colonel.

"John. Take the minutes, please, and take them properly."

> Sir,
>
> I am directed to acknowledge the Institute's submission of the 28th ultimo.
>
> I am further directed to acknowledge a letter dated 12th September from Mr P. Pobil, described therein as veterinary officer of the Institute, received in this office on the 19th September.
>
> The Secretary has considered both documents. He observes that the Institute's submission rests upon the settled and satisfactory character of its management of the specimens, while an officer of the Institute has thought it necessary to write separately to this office to warn against a procedure which he apprehends may be contemplated, and to ask that no such procedure be undertaken by the Institute or by any establishment to which the specimens may hereafter be transferred.
>
> The Secretary does not impute bad faith to any person. He is, however, unable upon these materials to be satisfied that the management of the specimens is settled within the meaning of the licence.
>
> I am accordingly directed to inform you that the licence will not be renewed in its present form on the 1st November, and that the Secretary will entertain an application by any suitably equipped establishment for the transfer of the specimens, such application to be made not later than 30th November.
>
> I have the honour to be, Sir,
> Your obedient servant,

---

Reggie hears the whole of it.

He has an odd and very clear memory afterwards of the sound of John's pencil, which does not stop at any point, and of Jean's hand going flat on the table at *12th September*, and of the Colonel not moving at all.

He does not look round.

That is the thing he will come back to, at intervals, for a long time: that he had about nine seconds in which he could have turned his head and did not, because he did not need to, because there is exactly one veterinary officer at this Institute and he has been standing at the end of that verandah for fourteen weeks.

"I wrote it," says Pascual.

---

Nobody says anything at all.

"On the twelfth," Pascual says. "I drafted it on the second and I put it in the bag myself on the twelfth and I watched it go under the thwart. There isn't anything in it that isn't true and I'd write every line of it again."

"Dr Pobil —"

"Let me finish, because you'll all get it wrong if I don't." He has both hands on the rail behind him. "It's a veterinary opinion. It attaches to the animal and not to the address."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning that if it's on the file before the first of October, then wherever they go, they go with a paper saying the thing that place wants to do to them will kill them."

"And?"

"And no licensing officer in the Empire signs over that."

"That was not stupid," says Spilsbury.

"Don't."

"It was not stupid. It was an extremely good idea badly aimed, and I want it on that boy's minutes that I said so, because in about nine weeks somebody is going to write this down as a piece of foolishness and it was not."

---

It is Hester who says the thing that ends it.

She has not raised her voice and does not.

"On the seventh of September I asked you in the long room whether you had anything on paper. Correspondence. Anything gone out or come in since July."

"Yes."

"And you said no, and I wrote *nil* against your name in my own hand, and that entry is in the bundle, and it is dated, and it is now false."

Pascual does not say anything.

"I want you to understand what that is," says Hester. "It is not a lie you told me. I should get over a lie you told me in about a fortnight and I have had worse from better. It is a false entry in the only document this Institute has got."

---

They go on for two hours after that, because that is what this station does, and it is the worst two hours Reggie has ever spent in a chair.

Hester takes them through it. The licence is gone in its present form, which is not the same as gone; the application window runs to the thirtieth of November; there is an appeal and there is a personal representation and there is the fact that *not renewed in its present form* is a door left ajar by a man covering himself. She will fight all three. She puts their chances now at rather worse than even and says so without being asked.

"And the animals," says John.

"The animals are here until somebody applies and the application succeeds. That is the honest answer and it is a better one than we had at eleven o'clock."

Jean has not spoken since the letter.

At about half past twelve she gets up, and goes to the end of the verandah, and stands in front of Pascual with her arms at her sides.

"I told you," she says.

"Jean —"

"At the ponds. On the tenth. I stood there and told you that the worst thing about this place is that everybody's so considerate and that I'd rather be shouted at." Her voice does not hold particularly well. "And you looked at the water and you said *would you*, and you had it in a drawer."

She does not wait for an answer. There is not one.

---

John brings him the minutes to sign at half past one.

"Sign these."

"Now?"

"Now, because if I leave it till tomorrow you'll want to change a word and you can't." He holds the pen out. "Mrs Bevan said."

"Did she."

"She said you'd try."

"I wasn't going to try."

"You were going to try."

"...I was going to try," says Reggie, and signs them, and John takes them away and does not say anything else, and it is the only thing that happens on the fourth of October that is not entirely awful.

---

At about two he comes past the long room and the door is open and John Weald is in there at the far bench with the plate box, doing nothing whatever that needs doing at two o'clock on a Thursday, and Pascual is at the microtome with his back to the room.

Neither of them is talking. John is twenty-six and has taken four hours of minutes today and has worked out, entirely on his own, that the useful thing is to be in the room.

Reggie does not go in.

---

Spilsbury finds him at four at the slipway, which is Spilsbury's place and not his, and which he has come to precisely because of that.

"You will want to know what I think."

"I'd rather have my teeth out."

"Then I shall be quick." He sits down on the bollard, which Reggie has not seen him do in four months. "It was a good letter, aimed at the wrong office, by a man who had nobody to check it with."

"That's a generous —"

"It is not generous, it is accurate, and it is not the same thing as a stupid letter. If you let this station settle on calling it a stupid letter, you will lose him."

"That's not your business."

"It is not my business at all and I am going to say it once." He looks out at the water. "The second thing is that in July he told me what you are, and I told him what he is, and he did not care for either."

Reggie goes extremely still.

"In July."

"On the third. At the pit behind the store, at about nine in the evening."

"He didn't mention it."

"No," says Spilsbury. "Not once, I should think. You may add that to your account or leave it out, as you please."

---

"What did you ask him."

"I asked him how you are stopped." Spilsbury turns the hat over on his knee. "I had looked for that for seven years and concluded in 1948 that there was no such thing, and then I watched it happen twice in five weeks, and I wanted it."

"And he told you."

"He told me. It was the best answer anybody has given me about anything in a decade and I could not use one word of it."

"Which was?"

"That there is no lever." He is not looking round. "That you could always have stopped. In 1948, at the labs, on the ninth of May 1954 and on the tenth."

"That is —"

"That there was nothing the matter with you, and that nobody had ever stood in a room and told you what a thing was going to cost."

The water goes over the reef.

Reggie stands on a concrete slipway at four o'clock in the afternoon on the fourth of October and finds that he has to put a hand on the rail, and that Alec Spilsbury has the manners, for once in his life, not to look round.

"He said that in July."

"On the third of July. At a hole in the sand. To a man he detests." Spilsbury stands up and puts the hat on.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I have had three months of watching the pair of you, and on the whole of the evidence he is the better of the two of you at this, and he has just failed at it in front of six people." He straightens the brim. "You are the worse. You have not yet been tested."

And he goes up the path.

---

It is ten o'clock at night before the two of them are in a room with the door shut.

Reggie has spent the day being extremely competent at things that do not matter and he is now standing in the long room with his hands behind his back, and he discovers that he has no plan whatever.

"Say it," says Pascual.

"I don't know where to start and I'm not going to start badly."

"Start anywhere."

"Very well." He takes his glasses off. "On the second of September you were in my office at half past five in the morning."

"Yes."

"You saw four sheets on the desk under the order book."

"Yes."

"Did you turn it over?"

---

The generator changes note.

"Pascual. Did you turn the page."

"No."

"Did you turn any of them."

"No."

"There were four sheets on that desk on the second and fourteen by the end of the month and they sat in plain sight the whole time, and you have been in that office perhaps forty times since, and you did not turn one page."

"No."

"*Why —*"

"Because I'd have had to ask you about it afterwards," Pascual says, "and I couldn't."

---

Reggie stands there.

"The last two pages," he says, "are headed *Grounds upon which the study cannot be performed*."

Nothing at all happens on the other man's face for about two seconds, and then everything does.

"I wrote them on the twenty-sixth of September at one in the morning," Reggie says, and hears his own voice go somewhere flat and unpleasant and cannot stop it. "There are five grounds. The fifth is the only one that matters."

"Reggie —"

"The design requires the survivor to be observed to the end point. And there is no version of this experiment in which the end point is anything but an animal dying alone in a pen while four people write it down."

"Stop."

"I put that in my own hand and initialled it, and it has lain on that desk under a paperweight ever since, and you walked past it for a month."

"Reggie —"

"You had it. You had the whole of it four feet from your hand and it'd have cost you nine seconds and one page."

---

"And you had fifteen weeks," says Pascual.

"I beg your —"

"You had fifteen weeks and a mouth." He has not moved off the bench and his hands are flat on it. "You refused it on the thirty-first of May in a second and a half. You refused it on the fourth of July in four."

"And?"

"And on the fourteenth of August that man put it to a table and you said *nothing at all*. I was at the rail. I have been standing at that rail since."

"I did not say yes."

"You didn't say anything!"

"I'd have said it in November!" It is out of him before he knows it is coming. "I'd have said it to an inspector with my hand on that box and you know I would!"

"I didn't know that!"

"*You could have asked me —*"

It comes out of both of them now at a volume that has not been used in this building since 1954, and it takes the top off the whole evening.

---

"You've been in the room every time —"

"I've been in the room every time and I've watched you get slower, and you know you got slower, because you're the one who counts." Pascual is up now.

"That is not —"

"The day you stop counting is the day I stop being able to read you at all. And then you ask for it drafted. Properly. Protocol, animals, term, intervention." He puts a hand flat on the bench. "And who writes it? *I do*, you said. Because it's mine."

"Because I wasn't going to have anybody else in this building write a thing like that and then have it taken off them —"

"I know that *now*."

"You could've known it on the second of September by turning over a *sheet of paper* —"

"And you could have told me on the twentieth of August what it was for!"

"I didn't know what it was for on the twentieth of August!" Reggie has both hands in the air and cannot get them down. "I found that out at one o'clock in the morning on the twenty-sixth of September, which is the hour I wrote it down, which is why it's on the last page and not the first!"

---

They stand there in the long room with about twelve feet of bench between them, both of them breathing like men who have been running, and there is nothing in either of them that wants to stop.

"I asked you," Reggie says.

"When."

"On the eleventh. In the dark. I asked you if you were all right and I told you I was asking you properly and I'd got about halfway through a sentence about the two of us and I stopped, and you lay there and let me stop."

"Yes."

"Why."

And Pascual Pobil, who has never in four years failed to give him a straight answer to a straight question, says:

"Because I was frightened of you."

---

Reggie does not say anything for a long time.

"Say that again."

"No."

"Pascual —"

"I'm not going to say it twice, and I'm not going to take it back, so you can have it where it is." His voice has gone entirely level, which with him is the end of something.

"Frightened of —"

"Not of what you'd do to me. Don't be stupid." He does not move. "Of what you were going to do at that pen. Of the fact that I couldn't get you to tell me you wouldn't. And of what it turned out I'd rather do than ask you."

"And what you'd rather do," says Reggie, "was write to the Colonial Secretary."

"Yes."

"Behind me."

"Yes."

"And lie to Hester."

"Yes," says Pascual, and does not offer one word in mitigation of any of it, and that is somehow the worst part of the whole night.

---

There is nothing after that.

That is what Reggie discovers, standing in the long room at twenty past eleven: there is no next thing. They have both said the whole of it and neither of them has anything to hand the other, and the room is hot and the lamp is going and the generator is doing what it does.

He is aware of a sentence somewhere behind his sternum that he has been carrying since the second of May, which is not a reading and is not a fact and would not survive being said in a room, and which he has taken out twice at four in the morning and put back.

*Now,* he thinks. *Now, or it becomes a thing you kept through this.*

"Pascual —"

"Don't."

"You don't know what I'm —"

"No," says Pascual. "And I've got nothing left tonight to put under it."

And he is right, and Reggie knows he is right, and puts it back for the third time.

---

"Thirty years," Reggie says, at the door.

"Thirty years," says Pascual, straight back, with no half second in it at all.

That is the only thing in fourteen weeks that has never once needed working at, and both of them hear that it is still true, and it does not help either of them in the slightest tonight.

---

He goes to the office and takes the fourteen sheets out from under the paperweight and reads the last two pages through twice, standing up, and then takes them to the lamp.

He gets the glass up. He holds the corner about four inches from the flame for rather a long time.

Then he puts the glass down.

Because it is a record. Because it is dated, and initialled, and it is the only object in this building that says what he was actually doing between the twentieth of August and the twenty-sixth of September, and because a thing that is burned is a thing that somebody in 1958 gets to describe for him.

He walks up to the bungalow at ten past two in the morning and Hester is awake, because Hester is always awake, and he puts fourteen sheets on the table in front of her without a word.

She reads the heading on the last two pages.

Then she takes her pencil and writes a number in the top right-hand corner of the first sheet, and puts it in the box, and says, "Sit down, Reginald. I shall make some tea."

And he sits down in Hester Bevan's kitchen at two in the morning and puts his head in his hands, and she makes the tea, and neither of them says anything at all about anything for about half an hour.
