# Consideration

Since the twenty-second, Reggie has knocked on the door of the long room four times.

It is his own laboratory. It is in his own Institute. He has walked into that room without a word of warning perhaps nine thousand times since 1952, usually mid-sentence, frequently with a plate in his hand, once at a quarter past four in the morning carrying two cups of tea and wearing one shoe.

Now he knocks. Twice, with two knuckles, high up on the frame, and waits.

Pascual has not said anything about it, because there is nothing to say about it that would not make it worse, and because he has worked out that the knocking is not an accusation. It is not even a sulk. Reggie Tar is a man who has been given a piece of information about a door and is being scrupulous about the door, and if you told him he was doing it he would be appalled and would stop and it would cost him something to stop, and then there would be two of them being careful instead of one.

So he says "Come in" four times in ten days, and they work, and they are extremely good at working, and none of it is anything like as bad as it might be, and that is the part he cannot get his hands round.

---

On the Tuesday morning Reggie brings him the Marchant letter without knocking, which is the first time, and Pascual notices himself notice it and puts it away to look at later.

"He's got bone in seals."

"He's got what?"

"Bone in seals. He's read the abstract you sent him and he's gone and cut four of his own." Reggie has the letter in both hands and his glasses pushed up. "Three clean."

"And the fourth?"

"A bull that lived nineteen years in a tank at Plymouth and dived twelve feet. There's a focus in the humeral head the size of a pea."

"Twelve feet."

"Twelve feet, which is nothing, which is the point. He wants to know whether we think it's the same thing and he says — here — that he has spent a fortnight being unable to sleep about it."

Pascual reads it twice.

"It isn't the same thing," he says. "It can't be. Not at twelve feet."

"No."

"But he's cut four seals in three weeks because we asked him a question, and he's going to cut forty, and in a year there will be a series."

"Yes." Reggie takes the letter back and squares it, which he does now with everything that is going into the bundle. "I'm going to tell him what it's for."

"Reggie —"

"Not all of it. I'm going to tell him there is a programme, that we are trying to stop it, and that his seals may matter."

"Hester will want —"

"I'm going to tell Hester I've done it before I do it." He is already at the door. "Not after. Before."

"Reggie."

"I've thought about it for two days, and I'd rather you argued with me now than in October."

"I'm not going to argue with you."

"You might. You've got about four hours."

He does not argue with him. At two o'clock the letter goes out, and it is a good letter, and Hester reads it before it goes and alters one word.

---

Spilsbury comes in at five with a slide box and the two of them do bone until half past eight, and it is, God help him, the best afternoon's work Pascual has had in a year.

That is the thing nobody warned him about. The man is a superb pathologist. Not a good one — a superb one, with hands that are better than his own and an eye that arrives about two seconds before anybody else's, and no interest whatever in whose slide it is or who said it first.

They get the whole series out: seven foci across both femoral heads and both humeri, and a graded run from a wedge that is nothing but a shadow to the one on the right femur that has come up through the joint and broken the cartilage and been walled off underneath.

"That one's the oldest."

"That one is the oldest."

"So you can date them." Pascual has the ruler on the section. "Not to a year. But you can say which came first, and how many episodes, and roughly how far apart."

"You can say four at least and probably six." Spilsbury moves the stage. "And you can say the last of them is more than a year old and that there is nothing acute anywhere, which is what I should like written down in your hand and not in mine."

"Why."

"Because it means they stopped diving him." He straightens up. "Whatever else they were doing to that animal in the last year of his life, they were not taking him deep. They had him on a line in a pen, fat, fed, bled twice a month, and they had stopped."

Pascual looks at the section for a long moment.

"Then why keep him."

"Yes," says Spilsbury, with something that in another man would be satisfaction. "That is the question, isn't it."

---

Twice in three hours Pascual tells him he is wrong.

The first time is a decalcification artefact in the third section, and Spilsbury looks at it for four seconds and says *yes, acid, cut me another from the same block and I shall not argue with you*, and that is the whole of it: no face, no rearguard, no half-minute of a man getting himself level.

The second time is whether the wedge on the left humerus is one focus or two, and this time Pascual is wrong, and is told so in exactly the same voice, and finds that he does not mind in the least.

He has worked with nine pathologists in twenty-one years. Two of them were better than he is. Neither of them could do that.

And at about half past seven, with the lamp hot and the ruler in his hand and a section of a dead animal's thigh on the stage between them, Pascual Pobil catches himself being happy, and it arrives so suddenly and so physically that he puts the ruler down on the bench.

Because this is what it is. This is the thing. Nine hours on the thirtieth of May with the day book filling up and a plate going cold at somebody's elbow — this, and nothing sinister in it, and nothing hidden, and no charm being used on anybody. Two men who are very good at a thing, doing the thing at full speed with the brakes off. It is one of the four or five real pleasures available to a human being and he has just had three hours of it and would take three more, tonight, now, and be glad.

He picks the ruler up again and does not look at that any further this evening.

---

At half past eight the lamp starts to go and Pascual says he has to turn the head in the paraffin and look at the pit, and Spilsbury says he will come, and Pascual is too tired to invent a reason why not.

So they go down the path together in the last of the light, the pair of them, past the store, to a rectangle of raked sand above the high line with a length of galvanised pipe driven in at the head of it.

They stood here on the thirtieth of May. Neither of them mentions that.

Pascual squats down and puts his hand flat on the sand at the eastern corner, which is what he does, because sand over a pit settles unevenly and you can feel a hollow before you can see one, and there is no hollow. Two years. Maybe eighteen months.

"Pobil."

"Mm."

"How do you stop him?"

---

He does not straighten up immediately.

He finishes what he is doing, because he is not going to be moved off a thing by a question, and when he does stand up he takes his time about brushing the sand off his hands.

"Say that again."

"You know perfectly well what I asked." Spilsbury has his hat in his hand and is looking out at the water, which has gone the colour it goes. "On the thirty-first of May I put a proposal to a room and it was a good proposal. It was the correct proposal and every person in that office knew it, including you, and including him."

"It was not the —"

"It was correct. You may object to it on other grounds and you will find me unmoved."

"I do object to it."

"I know you do. You will not get me to agree that it was not correct, and you are not stupid enough to try." He turns his head. "And he refused it in under two seconds. Not reluctantly. Not after weighing it."

"He'd thought about it."

"He was at the end of *no* before I had finished the sentence. His ground was that he was not going to, which is not a ground at all, and he handed it to me anyway and knew exactly what he was handing me."

"So?"

"So in seven years I did not find the thing that would stop that man." Spilsbury says it perfectly levelly, the way he says everything. "I looked. It was one of the more interesting problems available to me, and I had a great deal of access."

"And?"

"And I concluded in about 1948 that there was not one, and I was extremely comfortable with that conclusion for eight years."

"And now."

"And now I have watched it happen twice in five weeks. The second time was in an office in front of six people."

"And the first."

"The first was you, with a fistful of his shirt, in a room at ten o'clock at night." He puts the hat on. "So I should like to know what it is."

"Just like that."

"I am asking you directly, because asking people directly is under-rated, and because you are not a man I could get it out of any other way."

---

The light has gone while they have been standing here. The pipe at the head of the pit is a black line against a sky the colour of a bruise, and there are mosquitoes off the scrub behind the store, and Pascual can smell the paraffin bath on his own hands.

He stands on a beach path at nine o'clock in the evening, being asked courteously by a man in a linen jacket to hand over the only thing he has.

And the thing that goes through him is not anger, which surprises him, and he will think about that later. It is something much flatter and more dangerous, which is that he wants to answer.

Not to help him. To *win*.

"There isn't one," he says.

"I have considered that and I do not believe it."

"I know you don't, and I can't do anything about that." He wipes the last of the sand off on his trousers. "You want a lever. You've been looking for one since 1946 and you want me to tell you which it is, so you can put your hand on it."

"If there is one."

"There isn't a lever. I don't stop him."

"Then what happened on the thirty-first of May?"

"He stopped."

"That is a distinction without —"

"It's the whole of the distinction and it's the only thing I've got, so you'll have it in my order or not at all." Pascual hears his own voice come up and lets it. "He could always have stopped."

"That is —"

"He could've stopped in 1948. He could've stopped in the labs. He could've stopped on the ninth of May 1954, and he could've stopped on the tenth."

"And did not."

"Nothing was wrong with him. He wasn't *broken*, Doctor." He is not shouting and is not far off it. "Nobody had ever stood in a room and told him what a thing was going to cost."

---

The water goes over the reef about four times before Spilsbury answers.

"No," he says.

"No?"

"No, he did not." He is not arguing. He sounds like a man agreeing with a figure. "That is accurate, and I want you to know that I shall not pretend otherwise."

"You were in the next room."

"I was in the next room for seven years, latterly with a door between us that neither of us ever shut. I did not tell him what anything cost, at any time, about anything."

"Why not."

"Because it did not cost me anything," Spilsbury says.

Pascual stands there.

"There it is," Spilsbury says. "That is the whole of the answer and you may have it. The bill was his. I could read it perfectly well from the next room, and it was not addressed to me."

"You knew."

"I knew to within a very little." He does not look round. "One does not stop a man doing a thing when he pays for it and one collects. It would be a species of foolishness."

"That's —"

"That is not remorse, Pobil." He moves the sand with the side of his shoe, once. "I should be obliged if you did not hear it as remorse, because you will be waiting a long time. It is an account, and I am giving it to you."

---

"He was very good, you know," Spilsbury says, after a while.

"I know he was good."

"You do not know what at."

That is true, and it goes into Pascual like a splinter going in sideways, because it is true and because the man has said it without the slightest edge on it, as a plain correction of a plain error.

"He has never told you," Spilsbury says. "I should have been astonished if he had."

"It's classified."

"It is not a secret and it has no classification on it any more. Half of it was in the *Lancet* in 1947, under three names, and his is the second." He shrugs. "I can give you the whole of it in about six minutes, including the part in 1945 that he —"

"Don't."

Spilsbury stops.

"Don't," Pascual says again.

"You want to know."

"I want to know so badly I can taste it and I am not going to have it from you."

---

There is a pause of about four seconds, which on that face is a long time.

"Now that," says Spilsbury, "is extremely interesting, and I do not think you have looked at it."

"Don't."

"You have just refused a thing you want, from a source you consider contaminated, in order to preserve the possibility of getting it later from a source you consider clean."

"That's not —"

"Which is a preference about yourself. Not about him." He says it in exactly the voice he used about the femoral heads. "He is not in it anywhere. He does not know this conversation is happening and will not be consulted about it, and the information would be identical out of either mouth."

"That's —"

"It is not an accusation. It is a description and it is accurate and you know it is accurate, because you went white about four words in."

Pascual does not say anything.

"I find it interesting," Spilsbury says, "because it is the same operation as the letter."

---

The sea goes over the reef.

"Say what you mean."

"I mean the twenty-second of June." He is perfectly calm about it. "Mrs Bevan did not find that date."

"She found it in front of me."

"She found it on the twenty-second of June because you had had it since the thirtieth of May and did not put it on a table."

"When did you —"

"In the office. At about half past ten." He shrugs. "I have not mentioned it to anybody and do not intend to, because it is not useful to me and because I do not gossip. You may believe that or not, as you please."

"Then why are you —"

"Because it is the same shape. You had a true thing. You wanted it to arrive from somewhere else." He puts his hands in his pockets. "You are not a liar, Pobil."

"Thank you."

"That was not a compliment. I have watched you for five weeks and you have not told me one untruth. You are something rather rarer."

"Which is."

"A man who arranges for the truth to be said by other people. I should think you have been doing it since you were about twelve, and I should think it has worked extremely well until the twenty-second of June."

"That's one letter."

"It is one letter."

"I've said worse things to that man's face than you've said to anybody alive." Pascual does not move. "Ask him about the ninth of May 1954 and what came after it. Ask him who was standing in the doorway."

"I have no doubt of it."

"Then you've got one instance and you've built a man out of it. Which is what you did in 1948, and you were wrong then."

Spilsbury considers that for a moment and does not give up an inch of it.

"Possibly," he says. "I observe that you have not offered me a second instance."

Pascual looks at the sand.

He has one. He knows he has one, and it does not come, and he stands there in the dark waiting for his own head to produce it while his own head declines to, because the only thing in it at nine o'clock at night on the third of July is a date on a letter and a fortnight and a woman with a ruler.

"I haven't got anything to say to that," he says.

"No. I did not expect you to have." Spilsbury looks out at the water again. "For what it is worth, which is very little, I would have done the same thing and thought nothing of it at all."

"That isn't a comfort."

"It was not offered as one."

---

They stand there for a while. Out beyond the store the generator changes note, and up at the house somebody puts a lamp in the window of the office, which is Reggie, because it is ten past nine and that is what happens at ten past nine.

"You have given me something," Spilsbury says, "and I do not like to owe. So."

"I don't want —"

"You are going to have it whether you want it or not, because I am not going to carry it about." He does not look round. "The nineteen. They lose two or three a year now and have done since the pens changed, and they cannot account for one of them."

Pascual turns his head.

"Say that properly."

"Fat animals. In good pens. In company. Fed, watered, warm, not dived, not hurt, nothing on the post-mortem." Spilsbury counts it off with no inflection whatever. "They stop."

"How many did you see?"

"One in March of last year. One in the September. Two last winter." He looks round at last. "The man they had opened all four and wrote *no cause found* four times and filed it."

"And you said?"

"I said in January that it was the single most important observation in the building. He told me it was a husbandry problem, and that husbandry was not my department."

"Four of them."

"Four that I saw in four months. Ask me how many in eight years and I cannot tell you, because the ledger has a column for *date in* and a column for *date out* and no column at all for *why*."

---

He walks back up the path on his own, because Spilsbury goes down to the slipway to do whatever it is he does at the slipway, and Pascual is glad of the two hundred yards.

The answer arrives at about forty yards, which is where answers arrive.

Eleven days in May 1954, standing in a doorway saying it to the man's face and getting louder every day of them, until the last one, when he said the thing that worked. Nobody else on that island said a word. There was nobody else who would. He would say it again tomorrow and he still cannot go over it to himself without his hands going cold, and it is the exact opposite of a man arranging for other people to do his talking, and he had it the whole time, and it would have taken nine seconds.

He has walked forty yards past the place where he could have said it and he is not going back.

Because there is also a thing in his chest that has been there since about a quarter to nine and there is no instrument for it, and there is, now, a fact.

A fact with a shape. Well-kept animals in good pens with nothing wrong with them, stopping, and nobody counting, and the one on the beach at the end of April was not an accident or a bad batch or a thing that happened to a particular animal on a particular day. It is the fifth one this arc has heard of and the only one anybody has ever opened properly. It is a *series*. And the man who is going to want that in his hands inside four seconds, and who is going to be able to see straight down the middle of it in a way that Pascual cannot, is sitting forty yards away with a lamp in the window.

He gets to the office door with the whole of it in his mouth.

And then he stands there, on his own doorstep at half past nine at night in his own Institute, with the best thing anybody has been given in a fortnight, and lifts his hand, and knocks twice on the frame with two knuckles, high up.

There is a pause of about a second inside.

"*Come in,*" says Reggie, in a voice he has never once used in this building, and Pascual shuts his eyes for a moment and then opens the door.
