# Systematic

The letter has been in the breast pocket of whichever shirt he is wearing since the second of May, and it has been in four shirts, and every Monday for seven weeks he has taken it out of the one going to the wash and put it into the one coming off the line without particularly thinking about it, the way a man moves a watch.

At ten past nine on the twenty-second he takes it out and puts it on Hester Bevan's table.

"That's the last of it."

"Thank you." She does not pick it up. She is working through the pile in front of her with a pencil in her left hand and a ruler in her right, laying each sheet down in a row along the table by its date, and she does not look up. "Sit down, I shall be an hour."

"I've got a —"

"You have got the whole morning, because I told you on the eighth that you had, and you will want to be here for the index. Sit down."

He sits down.

---

It takes her forty minutes to get from the twenty-sixth of April to the end of May, and it is the dullest and most frightening thing he has watched anybody do in his life.

She does not read for sense. That is the part he cannot stop looking at. She reads for date and for hand and for what a thing is, and she puts it in the row, and if two things go in the same place she puts one above the other and draws a line between them and numbers them. John comes in twice with the plate run. Pascual comes in at about ten, with the bone blocks in a tray, because the Walsall schedule and the belt are going in with the physical and he will not let anybody else carry the belt, and he puts the tray down at the end of the table and does not sit down and does not go away.

The row of paper grows along the table like a tide mark.

*26.iv — day book, opening entry. 27.iv — Weald, plates 419–431. 29.iv — Bevan to Cattell & Barrow. 2.v — Tar to A. Spilsbury (carbon).*

"Stop," says Hester.

"What?"

"You have given me a carbon of what you wrote to him and you have given me what he wrote to you." She squares the two sheets. "They are both the second of May."

"Mine's the second. His isn't."

"His is the second of April," Hester says. "Yes. I can read."

She takes the ruler away and puts the April letter down on the table on its own, six inches clear of the row, which is a thing she has not done with any other piece of paper this morning. Then she goes back three feet along the table, to the block of her own foolscap from the thirty-first of May, and finds the page she wants without looking for it, and brings it back, and lays it beside the letter.

Two sheets, side by side, in the sun off the window.

Reggie is looking at the back of Hester Bevan's hand and has no idea at all what is about to happen.

"Read me the third line," she says.

"*Two questions, neither of them urgent* —"

"The one after."

"*Does your pair take fish that has been cut? Mine will not, and I have reached the end of my patience with it.*"

"Mine will not," says Hester. "Present tense."

"Yes."

"Second of April." She puts her finger on the foolscap. "And on the thirty-first of May I asked that man in this office what he was paid, and by whom."

"And?"

"He said that in February they gave him a cheque and a car to the aerodrome. I wrote it down. He read it back and initialled it."

The room is extremely quiet.

"February," Reggie says.

"February. And on the second of April he sits down and writes to you in the present tense about an animal of his that will not eat."

"Go on."

"So either he was still there in April, and what he told this office is untrue." She takes her hand off the paper. "Or he was not still there, and had an animal anyway, which I should like explained."

"There's no third thing."

"There is no third thing."

---

He has read that letter, by his own count, upwards of forty times.

He read it on the jetty on the second of May with the mail bag at Jean's feet. He read it that evening at the rail and out loud to Pascual, every word of it twice, with four readings of *mine* and a fifth thing he did not say. He has read it in the office at four in the morning. He read it on the night of the fourteenth going through the Walsall schedule for something he could not name and did not find.

He did not once put it next to a date.

"I should've had that," he says.

"Yes. You should."

"Seven weeks."

"Seven weeks, and you are the cleverest man in this building, and you have read that letter until it has gone soft at the folds."

"Yes."

"It is not a failure of intelligence, Reginald. It is a failure of method." Hester picks the pencil up again. "Which is why the bundle exists, and why I am a great nuisance about it."

And then he looks up, because he has run out of anywhere else to look.

---

John is standing at the end of the table with the plate box against his chest and his mouth open.

Hester has the pencil in her hand and has already gone back to the row.

And Pascual is exactly where he was.

That is all. That is the whole of it. He has not moved, he has not made a sound, he has not put his hand on the back of a chair or straightened up off the wall or done any of the four or five things that a man does when a thing lands on a table in front of him; he is standing at the end with the tray of bone blocks at his hip and his arms folded, looking at Hester's hands, with precisely the face he had at ten o'clock.

Reggie has spent two years reading that face across a supper table. He knows what it does when the man is tired and what it does when the man is about to be extremely rude in Spanish and what it does at four in the morning with a dead animal's belt across his knees.

It is not doing anything, and that is not a thing it does.

"Pascual."

"I've had it since the thirtieth," Pascual says.

---

He does not say it quietly and he does not say it to the floor. He says it flatly, to the room, in the voice he uses for a finding, and then he stands there and takes what comes, which is nothing, because nobody in the room can think of a single word.

"The thirtieth of May," says Hester, after a moment.

"Ten past midnight, in the long room. He said *in February they gave me a cheque and a car to the aerodrome*, and I had the date on the letter, because I'd read it on the second of May." His arms are still folded. "It took about four seconds."

"And on the thirty-first you stood against the wall in this office for an hour and a half."

"I did."

"While I took that man through the cheque and the bank and the aerodrome."

"Yes."

"I see," says Hester.

She does not say anything else. She turns round and goes back to the row of paper and picks up the twelfth of June, and that is somehow the worst thing that happens in the whole morning.

---

They have Spilsbury in at half past eleven, and he comes in with a slide box under his arm because he was in the middle of something, and puts it down, and looks at the two sheets on the table for about three seconds.

"Ah," he says. "Yes. Who found it?"

"Mrs Bevan found it," says Reggie.

"Mm."

And then Alec Spilsbury turns his head and looks at Pascual Pobil for a second and a half, with no expression on his face whatever, and turns back, and says nothing at all about it, then or afterwards.

Reggie sees him do it. He does not know what he has seen.

"Is it your letter?"

"It is my letter, it is my hand, the date is the date I wrote it, and I knew what was in it when I posted it."

"Then explain it."

"No."

The word goes down on the table like a dropped weight.

"I beg your pardon?"

"You heard me perfectly well." Spilsbury puts his hands in his pockets. "It is a fair question. It is the right question and it is the one I would have asked at about ten past nine, and Mrs Bevan is entitled to ask it and so are you. I am not going to answer it."

"You're a witness in a —"

"I am aware of what I am."

"Then you are aware," says Hester, without turning round, "that what you have just said is the most damaging sentence anybody has uttered in this building since April."

"Yes."

"You said it anyway."

"I said it anyway, because it is true, and because you would have had it out of me in a fortnight." He takes his hands out of his pockets. "I should rather you had it now, with the price on it."

"The price."

"You may put me on the Wednesday boat. I want that said before anybody feels obliged to say it kindly."

"And your account?"

"It is signed as far as section six and it stays in that box whether I am on this island or not. You would be entirely within your rights."

"And if we don't?"

"Then we go on precisely as we were, and you write *witness declines to account for the period February to May* on a card and put it in the front of the file where the other side would put it."

"Why," says Reggie.

"Why what?"

"Why won't you answer it."

Spilsbury considers him for a moment, and it is the first time in three weeks that he has looked at Reggie Tar as though he were a problem rather than a colleague.

"Because the answer makes me a worse witness than I am," he says, "and I have not decided yet which of those two things I would rather have. When I have decided I shall tell you, and you will not like the delay, and I shall not apologise for it."

He picks up the slide box.

"The femoral heads are cut," he says, to Pascual. "Both. Four foci in the right, two in the left, and one on the right has come up through the joint surface." He gets the door open with his elbow. "That is the first of them that would have hurt him. You will want it before lunch."

And he goes out.

---

"Then the bundle has a hole in it," says the Colonel, at noon, in his own office, with the door shut and the four of them standing.

"The bundle has a hole in it."

"How bad."

"That depends entirely on whether he ever fills it." Hester has her hands folded. "As it stands, my best witness has declined, in front of three people, to account for six weeks of his own movements in the middle of the period he is testifying about."

"And a hostile man does what with that."

"Gets it out of him in five minutes, and then spends the afternoon asking what else he has not mentioned. Every word of the account goes with it."

"And the physical?"

"The physical is untouched. The animal, the belt, the herring, the schedule and the bone are facts, and they do not care what Dr Spilsbury has been doing."

"Small mercies."

"It is the whole reason I have been a great nuisance about the bundle." She looks at Reggie. "I should be obliged if somebody would remember that in October."

The Colonel puts both hands on the desk.

"Is he lying to us?"

"He has not lied to us once," says Reggie. "Not once, in three weeks. He has refused, twice, in plain English, on the record."

"That is a very fine distinction."

"It is not a fine one, and I should be grateful if we did not start blurring it, because the day we do, we stop being able to tell the difference."

Nobody in the room looks at anybody else.

"Right," says the Colonel.

---

They have it out at ten o'clock that night in the long room with the door shut, and it lasts about forty minutes, and it is the worst forty minutes either of them has had.

It does not start well because Reggie does not let it.

"Twenty-three days."

"Yes."

"You stood in that doorway on the thirtieth and you came off the frame and stood up straight, and I saw you do it, and I'd written *Feb — paid off* in the margin before you'd got back against the wood, and I thought your back was hurting."

"I know you did."

"You were in that office on the thirty-first for an hour and a half while Hester went through his cheque and his bank and his aerodrome, and you said *four plans*, and you didn't say *look at the date*."

"No."

"*Why.*"

"Because it wasn't a fact." Pascual has not moved from the end of the bench and has both hands flat on it. "It was a shape. You've heard me say the difference four hundred times and I meant it every time."

"That's —"

"Let me finish it, because you'll get it wrong if I don't." He does not raise his voice, which is somehow worse. "If I'd said it to you, it'd have been me saying *your friend is lying*. About a man I'd been ready to put through a window three weeks before."

"So?"

"Eight days after you'd told me, in bed, correctly, what I'd been lying there thinking about him." He stops for a second. "And you'd have taken it. That's the problem. You'd have gone straight to him that night, and if it had turned out to be nothing —"

"It didn't turn out to be nothing."

"No. It didn't."

---

"And if it had?"

"Then I'd have spent something I don't get back, and I'd have spent it on being the man who wanted him off the island." Pascual's hands have not moved off the bench. "That's the reasoning and I'm not dressing it up. It's good reasoning. That's what's wrong with it."

"Say the rest."

"There isn't any rest."

"There is one more and you know exactly what it is, because you're the most honest man I have ever met and you've been standing there for ten minutes giving me three reasons in descending order of how much you like them." Reggie is aware that his voice has got somewhere he does not want it. "Say the fourth."

The generator changes note.

"I knew she'd find it," Pascual says.

"Louder."

"I knew she'd find it. She said on the eighth that she'd be systematic, and she said *including anything in a pocket*."

"And?"

"And I worked out that afternoon that it gave me a fortnight, and I let the fortnight run." He looks up. "And then it'd be hers. It wouldn't be a thing I'd said about him, and nobody would ever have to know I'd had it."

"And you let Hester do it."

"Yes."

"You let her do it in front of John."

"*Yes.*" It comes out of him at last, and it is the only word he raises all evening. "Yes. And I've been standing at the end of that table since ten past ten this morning with it in my hands, and I haven't put it down."

"Pascual —"

"So you can stop looking for the place it hurts. You found it about six minutes ago."

---

Neither of them has sat down.

That is the shape of the whole forty minutes and Reggie does not notice it until much later: two men in a long room at ten o'clock at night with twelve feet of bench between them and four empty stools, and neither of them has put a hand on one. Pascual has both palms flat on the bench top and has not lifted either of them since he put them there. Reggie has been at the far end by the window with his arms folded and has, at some point he cannot identify, taken his glasses off and been holding them.

The lamp is on over the microtome because that is where the lamp lives. Neither of them is anywhere near it.

Reggie hears himself say the next thing while it is happening, which is a sensation he does not enjoy.

"Fine. Then let's have the rest of it now and get it over with, because I'd like to know what else there is. Is there anything else you haven't —"

And he stops.

He stops in the middle of it, with the word still in his mouth, because the word is *anything*, and *anything* has a shirt in it, and a breast pocket, and a fifth thing that is not a reading and is not a fact, and four seconds of being entirely clear about it in the dark on the thirty-first of May.

The silence goes on for some considerable time.

"Finish it," says Pascual.

"No."

"Reggie. Finish the sentence."

"No."

He watches it happen on the other man's face — watches him see that something has arrived and gone behind the eyes and been put away, which is a thing Pascual Pobil can read at forty yards on a bad day, and cannot read now, because there is nothing to read; there is only a man stopping.

"What was that."

"Nothing," Reggie says.

He puts his glasses back on, which gives his hands something to do, and it does not help, because the other man has been watching his hands for two years.

---

It is the first thing he has ever said to him that is not true, and it takes about a second to say, and neither of them mentions it.

Pascual looks at him for a long moment.

"All right," he says.

---

They go to bed at midnight, because it is midnight, and because in two years they have not once solved this by going to separate rooms and are not going to start.

And it is fine.

That is the thing. It is entirely fine; it is slow and it is careful and both of them are trying, visibly, which is not something either of them has ever had to do before, and it works the way it always works, and the man underneath him says his name twice in the register that does not get used at a table and holds on to him hard enough to leave marks.

And afterwards Reggie lies with his ear on the winch scar listening to the heart underneath it come back down, in the dark, in the heat, with a hand moving slowly on his back the way it has moved on his back for two years.

And nothing is any different.

He can feel that quite clearly and so, he is entirely certain, can Pascual, because they are lying against one another and there is nowhere for it to go. Neither of them says so. It is the first night since the twentieth of May 1954 that this has not put a thing back where it belongs, and they lie there in it, both awake, for about forty minutes, being extremely careful with each other.

"Pascual."

"Mm."

"Thirty years."

"Thirty years," Pascual says, straight back, without a half second in it, and tightens the arm. "That's not in it. That was never in it and it isn't in it tonight."

"No. I know."

"Say it back to me anyway."

"Thirty years."

"*Bueno.*"

---

The letter is in a box file in the office with a number written on the top right-hand corner in Hester Bevan's hand.

His shirt is over the back of the chair by the door with nothing in the pocket at all.

It makes no difference whatever, and he lies there in the dark for a long time working out why, and gets nowhere near the end of it, and does not sleep until half past three.
