# Two Questions

The letter book is bound in black cloth and lives on the third shelf of the office, where it has lived since 1952, and Reggie has walked past it every working day of his life on this island under the impression that it was the stores ledger.

It is not the stores ledger. It is a carbon copy of every letter that has left the William Martin Institute for Marine Studies in four years, entered by date, indexed at the back in a hand he would know on the moon.

"You keep this."

"I keep that." Hester is standing in the doorway of his own office at twenty to six in the morning in a dressing gown, having heard him moving about, and is not remotely embarrassed about either fact. "I have kept one everywhere I have ever worked. Sit down and stop holding it like that, it isn't a relic."

"It's four years of everything anybody here has ever —"

"Yes. Turn to March of last year."

---

He finds all three of them inside a quarter of an hour, because they are where she says they will be, because she is exactly as good at this as he has spent two years half-noticing.

The first is the Oceanographic at Miami, and he can place the man who signed it and has met him twice.

The second is a Danish station at Helsingør that wants to know about salinity tolerance and has a letterhead with a crest on it, and is plainly and boringly what it says it is.

The third is on paper of very good quality.

> THE CARIBBEAN MARINE BIOLOGICAL STATION
> P.O. Box 411, Nassau, Bahamas

No telephone. No street. No names in the letterhead, and the signature at the bottom is an initial and a surname he has never encountered in any journal in his life, and the request is three lines long and asks for the cohabitation protocol in full, including pen dimensions and the intervals at which the animals were disturbed.

*The intervals at which the animals were disturbed.*

That is not a question a man asks who has read a paper. That is a question a man asks who has two animals in a pen and a difficulty.

And under it, carboned in his own hand and dated the nineteenth of March 1955, are four pages in which Reggie Tar gives him the lot: the dimensions, the water, the gate schedule, the feed, Jean's salinity ranges, the plate numbers to write for, and — because he was pleased with himself that morning, because it was a good result and he had been waiting two years to be asked about it — a paragraph at the end recommending that the correspondent on no account keep an animal on its own.

He sits with that for some while.

Then he copies the three addresses onto a clean sheet, and puts the book back on the third shelf, and finds that he has to square it to the edge twice.

---

Hester receives him at seven properly dressed, with the belt in front of her on a sheet of newspaper, and she has already done a thing he would not have thought of.

She has drawn it. Badly, but to scale, with the stitch lines marked and the buckle in elevation, and the number of stitches to the inch written in the margin.

"Fourteen," she says. "That's a machine, and it's a heavy machine. No saddler does fourteen to the inch on three-inch webbing by hand for one job."

"Then it's a firm."

"It's a firm, and it's a firm that does this sort of thing more than once, because they have lined it for the animal's comfort and nobody thinks of that the first time." She turns the drawing round. "I shall write to four people in the trade and I shall be a woman furnishing a private zoological collection, which I have done before."

"When have you —"

"Nineteen thirty-one, and it is none of your business." She puts her pen down. "Now. My price."

Reggie waits.

"You are going to find out who has been doing this," Hester says. "It may be six weeks and it may be a year, but you will find out, because you do not stop. I want you to tell me now, in a chair, with tea in front of you, what you will do on the day you know."

"I don't —"

"Take your time over it."

He takes it. The clock in her sitting room does what clocks do.

"I don't know," he says.

"No." She does not look disappointed; she looks like a woman confirming a figure. "That is the honest answer and it is the one I expected, and you will notice I asked you at seven in the morning on the second day, while it is still cheap. Come back when you know."

"And if I don't know by the time you have it?"

"Then I shall be sitting on a name," Hester says, "and deciding by myself, which I have done before as well, and did not care for."

---

He stops at the slab on the way down because he can hear John's stool scraping.

The animal is under the ice in the long tray with a tarpaulin over him and the head is out in the paraffin bath, and John has the belt laid out on a board with a rule beside it and has been at it since five.

"Plate four hundred and nineteen," he says, without looking up. "I've done it flat, buckled, and from the back with the ring. And the parted end of the line at four times, because you can see the chafe better big."

"Date them."

"They are dated."

"Number them into the main sequence."

At that John does look up.

"The main sequence," he says.

"The main sequence. In the book, in order, with everything else." Reggie puts his hands behind his back. "Two years ago I had two of your plates taken out of that book and sent to a clerk in London to lose, and I would do it again on that day. This is the other thing. Whatever happens after this, I want a numbered run in your hand that runs from the twenty-sixth of April without a gap in it, and I want four hundred pages on either side of it so that it cannot be made to look like a thing somebody assembled."

John thinks about that for a moment.

"So it's evidence," he says.

"It's a record. If it ever has to be evidence it will be a great deal better for having been a record first."

"Right." John turns back to the board and picks the pencil up. "I'll want more paper by Thursday, then, and the good pencils, not the ones Jean sharpens with a knife."

---

The Colonel sees him at eleven, at his desk, with the door shut, and Reggie tells him all of it.

Not the 1954 part; the Colonel has ruled on the 1954 part and Reggie is not going to make him unrule it. Everything else: the condition of the animal, the teeth, the belt, the fluke, the portions of cut fish, the adrenals that are only slightly heavy, the three institutions and the one that is a post office box, and the fact that the strap has been let out three times.

It takes nine minutes. The Colonel does not interrupt, which runs in the family.

At the end of it he says, "Eight years."

"Eight at the least."

"Right." The Colonel gets up, goes to the grey cabinet by the window that nobody has ever seen open, unlocks it, and comes back with a thin buff file. "Then you had better have this, and I had better hear what you think of me afterwards."

---

There are two letters in it.

The first is dated the twelfth of October 1954 and the second the ninth of June 1955, and they are on the same paper, and the paper is very good.

> DEWAR & LECKY
> Consignment Agents
> Bay Street, Nassau

The first is an enquiry as to whether the Institute would consider the sale or exchange of live specimens of *N. aestuarius*, terms to be discussed, all carriage and insurance at the purchaser's expense. The second, eight months later, is a good deal warmer, congratulates the Institute on its recent publication in the most flattering terms, and offers — this is the phrase, and Reggie reads it three times — to *relieve the Institute of surplus animals as they arise.*

Underneath each of them is a carbon of the Colonel's reply. Both replies are four lines long. Both say that the Institute is a scientific body and not a dealer, that it holds its specimens under licence, and that no correspondence on the subject will be entered into.

"You refused them."

"Of course I refused them."

"In October 1954."

"In October 1954, and again the following June, and there was a third in December that came by way of a man in Kingston and never got as far as paper." The Colonel sits down. "Now say the rest of it, Tar, because you are going to."

"You didn't tell me."

"No."

"There was a firm in Nassau writing to this Institute offering to buy mermaids, twice in writing, and you put it in a cabinet."

"I did."

Reggie finds he is standing up and has no memory of having done it.

"Why."

"Because it came to nothing," the Colonel says, "and because a dealer writes to everybody, and because in October 1954 you had been in my office three times in five weeks explaining to me at considerable length why we should not take another animal, and I had formed the view that a letter offering to sell you one would not improve your temper." He looks up. "That was the reason at the time and it was a reasonable one. It is not a good one now and I am not going to pretend it is. There."

That is the whole of the apology and it is more than Reggie expected and he does not know what to do with it, so he sits down again.

"Bay Street," he says.

"Bay Street."

"And Box 411 is Nassau."

"Nassau is a small place," says the Colonel, "and there are a great many boxes in it, and if you go from one to the other in your head this afternoon you will be a fool this afternoon and it will be twice as hard to stop being one tomorrow."

"Yes, sir."

"But you are going to."

"Yes."

---

"What do you want me to do?"

"Find out whether it's service," Reggie says. "Ours or theirs. Because if it's a service programme then it has a number and an estimate and a man who signs for it, and there is nothing whatever we can do about any of it. And if it's commercial, then it's money, and money leaves a track."

The Colonel turns his pen over on the blotter.

"I have four men I could ask," he says. "Two of them I would trust and two of them I would not, and the two I would trust are the two who would understand the question." He sets the pen down. "I will ask one. One, Tar, and I will choose which and I will choose the week."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet, because you have not understood what you have asked for." He leans back. "An enquiry is an answer. Whoever I write to will know within a day that Bevan at the Martin has begun to wonder who is keeping merfolk, and he will mention it to somebody, and inside a month there will be men in two capitals who know that this station is interested."

"I had got that far."

"Get further." The Colonel puts his hand flat on the file. "The licence comes up in November. It is four lines and it is signed by the Colonial Secretary's office on my recommendation. I am sixty-three. The Institute holds the only two specimens of this animal anybody has ever kept alive for longer than a season, and it holds them because a clerk renews a piece of paper for a man he has been told is sound."

The fan is off. Out beyond the window somebody is scrubbing something.

"I am going to make the enquiry," the Colonel says, "because I think you are right and because I am not prepared to sit here and be handled. But you will kindly hold in your head, from today, that everything you do about this happens with Gamma and Chumley in a concrete box forty yards from where you are sitting, and that they are not mine and they are not yours. They are the Colonial Secretary's, and he has never seen them."

Reggie sits with that.

"Understood."

"Good." The Colonel picks up his pen again. "One more thing and then go away. Whatever comes of this — you do not do it and tell me afterwards. You have done that to me once in this building and I let it go by, for reasons which are my own."

"Sir —"

"I am not asking about it. I am telling you it was once."

---

He gets four drafts done by nine that evening and every one of them is worthless.

The trouble is not the writing. The trouble is upstream of the writing and he can see it perfectly well and cannot get round it, which is that there are two men who might open the envelope.

If Alec Spilsbury has no part in this, then he is the only person alive with the reach, the appetite and the sheer ill-bred persistence to find out who has been keeping merfolk in a sea pen since 1948, and he would do it for nothing, out of interest, inside a year.

And if Alec Spilsbury has a part in this, then a letter from the Martin telling him that there is a body on the slab is a signal flare over the one place in the world that has evidence.

Every draft he writes is a letter to one of them. The fourth is the worst, because it is genuinely clever, and he reads it back at nine o'clock and hears the voice come off the page — the easy one, the useful one, the one that has been getting him things since he was thirteen — and puts the pen down and goes out of the office because he can feel the shape of the next hour and does not care for it.

---

"You'll wear it out."

"What?"

"The floor." Pascual is at the bench with his back to him, doing something to a slide, and has not turned round. "Up and down, up and down. You've been at it since you came in. Sit down or go out."

"I can't write it."

"I know you can't write it. You told me about it for a quarter of an hour and then you started walking." He puts the slide in the rack. "Tell me the hard part in one sentence."

"I don't know which man it's going to."

"Right."

"If he isn't in it he's the only one who can find them, and if he is in it —"

"That's two sentences and I've got both of them." Pascual turns round and leans against the bench and folds his arms. "So don't ask him for anything."

Reggie stops.

"Say that again."

"You've been trying to write a man a question." Pascual says it the way he says a diagnosis, flat, as though it were the temperature of something. "Don't. Tell him you've got a thing and that you're not going to write to him about it. If he's in it, he learns you have a body and nothing else, which he'd have inside a month anyway. And if he isn't in it, then he'll come."

"That's —"

"I know what it is."

"Pascual, that is the coat. That is precisely and exactly the coat, in writing, with a stamp on it —"

"Yes," Pascual says.

The generator changes note. Reggie stands in the middle of the floor of a sick bay and looks at a man who has spent two years being told before, every time, and who has never once asked him not to.

"You hate it."

"I don't hate it." Pascual is not smiling and his face is doing about four things. "I hate it when I can't find you in it. I know exactly where you are tonight. You're standing in front of me looking appalled." He picks up the rack. "Put it on, and write the letter, and then come here."

---

There is something he has learned about himself in two years that he had not previously had occasion to find out, which is that on the nights when a thing has been taken away from him he wants to be held still, and on the nights when a thing has been handed to him he wants to do something with his hands.

Tonight he has been handed something. So he takes the rack out of Pascual's hands and puts it down on the bench, and gets a fistful of the front of the man's shirt, and the noise Pascual makes is not far off a laugh.

"Oh, we're doing that, are we."

"We are."

"Bolt's not across."

"Then shoot the bolt."

"Say please."

"Shoot the *bolt*, Pascual."

He shoots the bolt.

---

He is four inches shorter and about ninety pounds lighter and neither of these things has ever mattered as much as it ought to, because the man goes where he is put when he wants to go there, and the wanting is legible from the door and always has been.

Reggie gets him sitting on the edge of the cot, which puts them nearly level, and takes his face in both hands and holds it there for a second — the answered thing, the vow out of Andersen, made deliberately in a room with nobody else in it, for the fourth or fifth hundredth time — and Pascual shuts his eyes and lets out a breath he has plainly been holding since about six o'clock.

"There," Reggie says. "Now be quiet and let me."

"You'll have to make me."

"I intend to make you." He gets the shirt off him and puts his mouth on the winch scar, which is his, and then on the throat under the jaw, which is also his, and finds the place at the side of the neck that has been reliable since the first week and stays there until the hands come up and shut in the back of his shirt. "There it is."

"*Ay* —"

"Don't move."

"I'm not moving."

"You are moving, extensively."

"Then stop doing that and I'll —" and then he does not finish it, because Reggie does not stop doing that.

It goes on for a while. He takes his time about it on purpose, because he has been given the whole evening and because there is something in being the one who is unhurried that he has only lately discovered and intends to make a study of. The room is hot. The man under his hands is enormous and entirely pliant and absolutely not quiet, and says a great deal of it in Spanish, and some of it is instruction and some of it is not language at all, and Reggie understands the second category perfectly.

"Reggie. *Reggie* —"

"I'm here."

"I know where you are, that's the — *ah*, that's —"

"Say it in English."

"No."

"Say it in English and I'll do it again."

There is a pause of about a second and a half, during which he can hear the man deciding, and then Pascual tells him, in English, in some detail, in a voice like gravel going down a chute, and Reggie has to put his forehead against a shoulder for a moment and think about the mail boat schedule before he is fit to keep his side of the bargain.

He keeps it.

Afterwards Pascual lies with one arm over his eyes and his chest going like a man who has been up a ladder, and says, to the ceiling, "*Madre de Dios.*"

"Mm."

"Where do you get it."

"Eton," Reggie says, "and then Cambridge, which is the same place with punts," and gets hit with a pillow.

---

He gets up at half past two and goes out to the office in his shirtsleeves and writes it in one go, without a draft, in eleven minutes, and does not change a word of it in the morning.

> Dear Spilsbury,
>
> On the 26th of this month an adult male *N. aestuarius* came ashore on the seaward beach of this island, dead about thirty-six hours. He measured eight feet seven inches and he was in excellent condition. I have him: gross, blocks, and a numbered plate sequence in the hand of the man whose drawings you admired.
>
> He is not one of ours. He had been somebody's for a number of years and I can put a figure on the number.
>
> I am not going to tell you the rest of it in a letter and I would be obliged if you did not write asking me to. If you have anything to say to me on the subject you will know how to say it.
>
> Yours,
> R. Tar

He reads it once and seals it and does not read it again, and goes back to bed, and lies there listening to the rain that is not coming, and at some point stops doing the arithmetic on what he has just put in an envelope and goes to sleep.

---

"If he is in it," he says to Pascual on the Sunday, at the rail, with Chumley going round and round below them showing off for nobody, "then I have just told a man in a programme exactly where the body is."

"Yes."

"You're not going to argue with me about it."

"No." Pascual has the residue net over his shoulder and is watching the water. "You said it in the room on Friday before you wrote it, and I heard you say it, and we both looked at it and neither of us had anything better. That's the whole of the conversation and it hasn't changed in two days." He shifts the net. "Now come and look at his flank, because that raw place from the tank has gone entirely and you've never once looked at it."

"I looked at it in August."

"In *August* —"

"It was doing well in August."

"It was doing well in August. It is doing extremely well now, and it is the only piece of good news on this island, and you will come and look at it for two minutes."

He goes and looks at it for two minutes. It is doing extremely well.

---

The mail boat comes in on the Wednesday at twenty past ten.

He takes the outgoing bag down himself, which he has never done, and he stands on the planks and watches a rating take it and toss it in under the thwart with four sacks of returned empties, and the launch goes astern and swings and heads for the head, and that is that; there is no arrangement in the world by which a man gets a letter back out of a Post Office bag once it has gone under a thwart.

He knows this. He has known it since he was about nine.

He stands there anyway until the engine note drops away round the point, and then he turns round, and Jean is coming up the jetty with the incoming bag over her shoulder and her hair in the scarf and the tide tables under her arm.

"Four for you," she says. "One's the *Journal*, one's Marchant — he's written on the back of it, look, the man's incapable of putting a thing inside an envelope — and one's some sort of circular, and one's been all over the Caribbean by the look of it."

---

It has been readdressed twice.

It went first to a London address that has been struck through with a single line, and then to something at Kingston in a different hand, and then to here, and there is a Miami postmark on it that is nearly a month old, and the corner of it has been wet at some stage and dried again.

*A. Spilsbury* is on the back of it, in pencil, as though he could not find a pen.

Reggie opens it standing on the jetty in the sun with the mail bag at Jean's feet and the water going under the planks.

> Tar —
>
> This will find you or it won't. I have given up on the Kingston people, who appear to regard the redirection of mail as a species of blood sport.
>
> Two questions, neither of them urgent, and don't waste ink on the first.
>
> One: what is Pobil using to fix a fluke section? Whatever it is I want it, because everything I try turns the elastic tissue to string and I am told by a man in Baltimore that this is unavoidable, and I have never yet been told a thing was unavoidable by anybody who had troubled to look properly.
>
> Two: does your pair take fish that has been cut? Mine will not, and I have reached the end of my patience with it.
>
> I shall be in your part of the world at the end of May, on my own account, which will amuse you.
>
> A.S.

Jean says something about the *Journal*.

Reggie says something back. He is not able afterwards to remember what either of them was, and Jean, who misses nothing, does not ask him a single question about it then or at supper or at any time in the following week, which is the third time in two years she has done him that particular kindness and the first time he has been in any condition to notice.

A mile out, going round the head with its wake spreading white behind it, is a launch with his letter in it.

*Mine will not.*

He reads the line again, and then once more, and then he folds the letter along its old folds and puts it in his breast pocket, and picks up Jean's bag for her because his hands want something to do, and walks up the jetty.
