# Disposal

The box file is now four box files and they live on the shelf behind Hester's chair in an order she could reconstruct in the dark.

She has been doing this sort of thing since 1928, on and off, for a variety of men who did not know she was doing it, and she is better at it than at anything else she has ever attempted, including marriage, at which she is merely good.

What she has, on the fifth of August, is this. A body, buried and drawn and blocked and described. A belt with four settings of holes in it and a maker in Walsall who will confirm the order. Ninety-one ear stones and a cable from Plymouth. Seven foci of dead bone in four long bones. Two letters from Bay Street and a third, and a refusal in writing from a man her husband has known since 1938. A witness with a hole in him. And, since the twenty-seventh of July, nine ports with six of them wrong.

What she has not got is a single piece of paper with the word *Nassau* and the word *Nereopithecus* on the same sheet, and until she has one, the whole of it is a very good story.

She has been asking somebody to make this animal something for twenty years. She is sixty-two. She has had it in her head since April and has not said it to a living soul, and will not, that this is the last of these she is going to be well enough to do properly.

---

She also has the two of them, and they are in her building, and they are her business whether they care for it or not.

It is not that they are unkind to each other. She has watched a great many people be unkind to each other and would recognise it at forty yards. It is that they have become *courteous*, which in those two is a wasting disease, and that something has gone out of the noise level of the entire station, and that John Weald has started manufacturing errands.

On the sixth she watches Reginald come along the verandah with a tray of blocks and turn sideways to get past Pascual at the door.

Sideways. In a doorway four feet wide.

Hester goes back into the bungalow and stands in her own hall for a moment with her hand on the newel post, being extremely angry with two men who have not done anything to her.

---

Her husband goes to Kingston on the seventh and comes back on the ninth.

He tells her he is going, of course; he tells her it is Institute business and that he will be back Thursday; and she says *very well* and does not ask, because they have been married since 1921 and the whole of it runs on a small number of things not being asked.

He comes up the path at half past four on the Thursday with his jacket over his arm, and she looks at his face and knows within about a second and a half that he has done something.

"John Bevan."

"Yes."

"You have seen him."

"I have seen him," says the Colonel. "Sit down, Hester, and let me get it out in one piece, because I have been arranging it in my head since Wednesday morning and it will come out wrong if you stop me."

So she sits down, in her own sitting room, and lets her husband tell her what he did without telling her, and does not say one word for nineteen minutes.

---

He went to the Myrtle Bank at noon on the eighth and sent up his card and Mr Lecky came down within four minutes, which the Colonel remarks on, twice.

Lecky is about fifty and dresses better than anybody in the room and has a Belfast accent under about thirty years of something else. He was delighted. He ordered coffee. He asked after Mrs Bevan by name and after the Institute's rainfall, and he did not mention the April specimen for forty minutes.

When he did mention it he mentioned it as a kindness.

"He said his client would be very glad to have the material," says the Colonel, "and that the terms would be generous."

"And if we said no."

"That he understood entirely, and hoped we would at least allow his client to be of assistance in other ways."

"Which ways."

"Placing animals. He used that phrase four times and I have written down where in the conversation each of them fell." He takes a folded sheet out of his breast pocket and puts it on the table.

"And?"

"And the Institute is very small. He said that twice as well." He smooths the sheet with his thumb. "Very small, doing remarkable work, holding the only two surviving specimens anybody has kept — on a sandbank, on a four-line licence, with one generator."

"He said *four-line*?"

"No. That is mine and I ought not to have put it in." He is visibly annoyed with himself, which she has seen perhaps six times. "He said *on the present licence*. But he knew there was a licence, Hester, and he knew there was one generator."

---

"And the threat," says Hester.

"There was no threat."

"John."

"I am telling you exactly what happened, which is what you would want. What happened is that there was no threat."

"Go on."

"There was not one sentence in ninety minutes that I could put in front of anybody." He looks at the window. "He was perfectly civil."

"And?"

"I have been threatened by better men than Mr Lecky and it has not frightened me. I came out of that hotel and stood on the pavement in the sun and had to put my hand on a railing."

Hester does not say anything.

"There is one more thing and it is the thing I have been carrying since Wednesday."

"Go on."

"He asked after Dr Spilsbury."

The clock does what clocks do.

"By name."

"By name. In the middle of something else, the way you ask after a man's brother. *And I believe you have Alec Spilsbury with you at present. How is he finding the climate.*"

---

They put it to the others on the Friday, and the Colonel gives it again, in the same order, from the folded sheet, and does not soften it and does not defend himself.

Reginald says nothing for a long moment and then says, "Thank you for going."

"You may be angry about it."

"I shall be angry about it on Monday. Today I should like to know what he wants."

And Hester, watching, sees Pascual Pobil turn his head at that and look at Reginald for about two seconds, with an expression she cannot read and would give a good deal to have a photograph of, and then look away.

Spilsbury is not in the room. He is at the pens, where he goes at four, and has been going at four since the middle of June, and stands at the rail for half an hour with his hands behind his back and does not get in.

---

The letter comes on the Tuesday, the fourteenth, by the government launch, which calls when the Secretariat pleases and not on any day a person could plan for, from the office of the Colonial Secretary.

It is not long. Hester reads it standing at the end of the verandah table with the launch still at the jetty, and then reads it again sitting down, which she has not done with a document since 1946.

> Sir,
>
> I am directed to inform you that the specimen-holding licence issued to the William Martin Institute for Marine Studies falls due for renewal on the 1st November next, and that the renewal will be subject to review in the ordinary way.
>
> In connection with that review I am directed to draw your attention to the following considerations. The Institute is at present the holder of two specimens of a species of which very few are held anywhere. The holding is remote, is dependent upon a single generating plant, and is in the charge of an establishment whose permanent scientific staff numbers four.
>
> It has been represented to this office that a well-found establishment exists within the region possessing facilities of a superior order, and that the interests of the specimens and of science would be better served by their transfer thereto. I am directed to invite the Institute to submit, by 1st October, any observations it may wish to make upon the disposal of its specimens.
>
> I have the honour to be, Sir,
> Your obedient servant,

---

Hester has been reading this kind of letter for twenty-eight years and there is only one word in it that matters and it is in the last line.

*Disposal.*

Not transfer. Not custody. Not review. *Disposal*, which is the word a department uses about a thing it has already decided belongs to it, and which is also, and this is the part that will keep her awake, the ordinary word for what you do with material at the end of an experiment.

"Say it out loud," says the Colonel.

"They have six weeks and we have seven."

"Meaning."

"Meaning that whoever got to that office got to it in July, and that a submission by the first of October will be read by a clerk who has already been told what he thinks."

"And the word."

"And the word they have used about two living animals is *disposal*." She puts the letter down. "I can fight this. I want that understood before anybody says anything else in this room."

"You can fight it."

"I can fight it, I am going to, and I should put our chances at rather better than even."

"And how long?"

"Two years," says Hester. "Possibly three. And they will be in a lorry by Christmas."

"Then they don't go," says Pascual.

Nobody answers him. It is not the sort of thing anybody answers.

He says it once more, at the same volume, to the table. "They don't go."

"Dr Pobil —"

"I'm not arguing with the letter, ma'am. I've read the letter." He has both hands flat on the wood. "I'm telling you what's going to happen."

Hester writes *Dr Pobil dissented* in the minute book, in her own hand, at about twenty past three in the afternoon of the fourteenth of August, and does not think about it again until October.

---

There is a silence on that verandah that she will remember.

It is John Weald, of all people, who breaks it.

"Then we publish."

"No," says Hester.

"We publish the whole thing. The animal, the belt, the herring, the bone, all of it, in something with a name on it, and then they can't —"

"They can, and they will, and it will cost you the case." She does not enjoy this. "Publish in October and you publish a body, a belt and a hypothesis. No station. No ledger. No name, and nothing whatever that touches Nassau."

"They'd have to answer it."

"They answer it in a page. And the instant it is in a journal it stops being evidence and becomes a scientific controversy, and a controversy is a thing a department is entitled to wait out."

"Then what's it *for* —"

"It is for the day we can put a place and a name into it," says Hester, "and that day is not the first of October, and I am as sorry about that as you are."

John sits down.

---

They go round it for two hours and there are four ways and all four of them are bad.

Submit and fight, which is Hester's, and which takes two years the animals have not got. Publish, which is John's, and which she has just taken apart. Refuse the review outright, which is the Colonel's and which he does not press, because refusing a review is how you lose a licence in six weeks instead of twelve. And give them up, which nobody says, and which is on that verandah the whole afternoon like a fifth chair.

At ten past five Spilsbury comes up from the pens and stands at the end of the table and reads the letter, which nobody offers him and nobody withholds.

"Ah," he says.

"Don't."

"Dr Pobil, I have not said anything yet."

"You're about to, and I know what it is, and I would rather have half an hour first."

"You may have half an hour," says Spilsbury, "and it will not change what it is," and he puts the letter down and goes and sits on the rail and waits.

---

He waits the half hour. She notices that, and files it, and has no idea at all what to do with it.

Then he says it in four sentences.

"A specimen under review can be moved."

"Yes."

"A specimen which is the subject of a licensed experiment in progress cannot be moved without wrecking it, and no department on earth signs the order that wrecks an experiment." He has his hands on the rail behind him.

"Go on."

"Put those two animals into a programme with a start date before the first of October and a stated term, and that office has nothing whatever it can do until it ends."

"That is —" says Reginald.

"It is also the only thing on this verandah that works in seven weeks." Spilsbury does not raise his voice at all. "I am not going to say what the programme ought to be. You have all known what it ought to be since the thirty-first of May. I shall be at the slipway."

And he goes.

---

And Reginald Tar does not say anything at all.

Hester has been in this Institute since 1952 and she has watched that man refuse things, and he refuses fast, and it is one of the more agreeable qualities he has. He refused on the thirty-first of May. He refused on the fourth of July. She wrote both of them down, because writing things down is what she is for.

She sits with the pencil on the page and the whole verandah waits, and he stands at the end of the table with his hands behind his back looking at the letter, and does not say it.

Nobody makes him.

Pascual Pobil gets up at twelve minutes past five and goes down the path to the pens without a word to anybody, and Hester notes the time, because she notes times. At half past eleven that night she puts her head out of the bungalow door to look at the weather and there is a figure at the north pen rail with no lamp.

---

She does not write down that Reginald did not repeat his refusal.

She sits over the minute book for some while with the pencil in her hand, considering it, and decides that she is not going to be the person who puts *Dr Tar declined to say no* into a book that a clerk in an office will read in 1958 with his mind already made up.

Then she sits for rather longer, because she has just done, on her own verandah, in her own handwriting, the precise thing she has been shouting at every person in this building about since June.

She does it anyway. She is sixty-two and has been keeping records for twenty-eight years and has never yet found a rule that does not have an afternoon like this one in it somewhere.

---

Hester sits at her own table on her own verandah and understands that she is about to do the worst thing she has done in four years, and does it, because it is her job and because somebody must and because a thing that is going to be found out in October is better found out in August.

"There is a document," she says.

"Hester —"

"Miss Leslie wrote to Reginald on the twenty-seventh of July and copied it to me. It is two lines long."

"Saying?"

"Declining to release the water and growth series on either animal for any experimental purpose whatsoever." She gets it out of the file, because she brought the file, because she knew. "It is dated, it is in her hand, and it is in the bundle."

Jean has gone white.

"I did that in July —"

"You did it in July, it was correct in July, and I put it in that file with my own hands and was glad of it." Hester puts the sheet on the table.

"Then why are you —"

"Because it is now the fourteenth of August. And what it means is that the one course open to this Institute that keeps those two animals on this island past Christmas cannot be run."

"Because of two lines."

"Because it requires four years of figures which are the property of a member of staff who has refused them in writing."

Nobody says anything.

"I want it minuted that I said that," says Hester, "and I want it minuted that Miss Leslie did nothing wrong, and I want both of those in the same paragraph, because in two years somebody is going to read this book."

---

She sits up until half past one with the four box files on the table.

At about midnight Reginald comes and stands in the doorway with a lamp and does not come in.

"Hester."

"Reginald."

"You could've told Jean that privately."

"I could."

"Why didn't you."

"Because in this building for the last eight weeks," says Hester, without turning round, "every single thing that ought to have been said to a person's face has been arranged to arrive from somewhere else."

"Hester —"

"I am the one who taught half of you to keep records, and I do not intend to be the last in this house to work out what that habit costs." She squares the file. "She had it from me, in front of the room. She will hate me for a fortnight and she will know where she stands."

The lamp does not move for a while.

"Yes," says Reginald.

"Go to bed."

"Yes."

"And Reginald."

"Mm."

"Turn sideways in a doorway in my building again," says Hester Bevan, "and I shall have something to say to you that neither of us will enjoy."

The lamp goes away up the path. She hears it stop, about forty feet along, for rather a long time; and then go on.
