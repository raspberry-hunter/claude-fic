# The Fourth Line

The door shuts behind John Weald and neither of them moves.

It is a room about eight feet by six with a bench down one side and a red lamp on a bracket, and there is a perfectly good plate on the bench, and there is a smell of hypo that Pascual has associated with that boy for four years and will now associate with this for the rest of his life.

Reggie is on the other side of the bench. He has his glasses in his hand.

"Well," he says.

That is all. It goes on for some while.

---

"I'd write the letter again," Pascual says.

"I know you would."

"Every line of it. I'd have shown it to you first."

Reggie does not say anything at all for about four seconds, and then he puts the glasses down on the bench, which in that light is a small red event.

"Yes."

"That's not an apology. I haven't got one for the content and I'm not going to invent one at nine o'clock at night in a cupboard."

"I don't want one for the content."

"No. I know what you want one for." Pascual has both hands on the bench edge and can feel the grain of it. "The page. And the seventh of September, and *nil* in her hand. Those three, and you'll have them properly and not here."

"When."

"When I can do it without you having to help me."

---

Reggie makes a noise that in a better month would have been a laugh.

"That is going to be a long time."

"Yes."

"Pascual —"

"And I'm not going to ask you what you've been carrying since May, either," Pascual says, "because you've had it in your mouth twice that I've watched and both times you swallowed it, and I'm not going to be the reason it comes out badly."

---

The red lamp hums. Somewhere under the floor of this building the sea is doing what it does.

And then Reggie comes round the end of the bench.

He does not say anything. He comes round the end of the bench and stands in front of him with the whole of the room's distance gone in three steps, and takes hold of Pascual's wrists, both of them, and lifts them, and puts them where they belong.

Both hands. On the sides of his face. In the dark.

Pascual's chest does something he would not be able to describe to a colleague.

"I turned my head," Reggie says.

"I know you did."

"On the fourth of July. You put your hands up and I turned my head and I have thought about it every day since and I have not been able to say it."

"Reggie —"

"I'm not asking you to do anything about it. I'm telling you I know what it was." His face is quite still under Pascual's palms and he is not looking away and he is trembling very slightly, which Pascual can feel and which he is fairly sure the man does not know. "There. That's mine. That's the first one back."

---

They stay like that for a long time, in a room the size of a cupboard, with a perfectly good plate on the bench.

Nothing else happens. Nothing else is going to happen tonight and both of them know it, and that is, in a way that Pascual will think about on and off for years, the best thing about it.

At about ten past ten he takes his hands down.

"Go up," he says. "I'll come after."

"How long?"

"Twenty seconds."

"Why twenty?"

"Because I've got to stand in here for twenty seconds," Pascual says, "and you'd rather not watch me do it."

---

He counts them.

He stands in a red room with his hands flat on a bench and counts to twenty, out loud, in Spanish, because that is what his mother counted in, and gets to *veinte* and finds that his face is wet and that this is an entirely new development at forty-three years of age.

Then he puts the plate away properly, because it is a good plate, and turns the lamp off, and goes up the path.

---

It does not fix anything.

That is the part nobody tells you. He wakes on the twenty-fifth expecting some portion of the weight to be gone and it is all exactly where he left it, and the second stool is still under the far bench, and at breakfast Reggie says *Dr Pobil* to him in front of Jean before he can stop himself and then goes scarlet to the hairline, which is the most cheerful thing that happens all week.

What changes is small and it is measurable, which is how Pascual likes things.

The evening pen round goes back to six on the twenty-eighth.

On the thirtieth Reggie stops at the long room door on his way past, and does not knock, and does not come in either, and says *the salinity's up at the north gate again* and goes on, which is nothing at all and is also four days of work.

On the second of November they have a short, ugly, entirely ordinary argument about the fixative in front of two people, and John Weald goes out of the room at speed with his hand over his mouth.

---

Hester has been at him since the twenty-fifth.

"Three weeks," she says. "You will hate every hour of it and I am not going to apologise. Sit in that chair. Not the good one. That one, because it is the one they will give you."

"Ma'am —"

"Dr Pobil. You wrote to His Majesty's — the Queen's, I beg your pardon, I am sixty-two — you wrote to that office in September, over your own name, warning against a procedure. Was that procedure proposed by anybody at the William Martin Institute?"

Pascual opens his mouth.

Then he shuts it.

"There," says Hester. "That is nine seconds and you have lost the room. Again."

---

They do it for three hours a day for nineteen days.

She is the worst thing he has ever sat opposite and she is magnificent. She does not raise her voice once. She asks him the same question nine different ways and takes it apart every time, and twice she makes him so angry he has to go and stand outside, and on both occasions she lets him go and is waiting when he comes back with the question in exactly the same words.

"Was the procedure proposed by anybody at the Institute?"

"It was raised."

"By whom."

"By a visiting scientist, in a meeting, on the thirty-first of May."

"And adopted?"

"It was refused."

"By whom?"

"By the head of the station. Twice, on the record, in the minutes, and a third time in writing."

Hester puts her pencil down for the first time in four days.

"Say the last one again."

---

"In writing," says Pascual.

"Where."

"In the bundle." He can hear his own voice doing something and does not stop it. "Fourteen sheets in his hand, dated between the twentieth of August and the twenty-sixth of September, headed *Protocol for the determination of the mortality consequent upon the separation of a bonded pair*, of which the last two pages are headed *Grounds upon which the study cannot be performed* and are initialled."

"And when did that document enter the bundle?"

"The fourth of October. At ten past two in the morning."

"Who brought it to me?"

"He did."

"Yes," says Hester. "He did. He could have put it in the lamp, Dr Pobil, and I should have been none the wiser, and it is the single best document in four boxes, and it is the reason you are going to survive that question."

---

He sits in a hard chair in Hester Bevan's sitting room and does not say anything for some while.

"Did he know?"

"Did he know what?"

"That it would do this. When he brought it to you."

"He was standing in my kitchen at two in the morning with his head in his hands," says Hester, "and I should be astonished if he knew what day of the week it was."

"Then why —"

"He brought it because it was a record." She picks the pencil up again. "Which is what I have been telling every person in this building since April, and which nobody believed until it saved somebody."

---

She works him over the whole thing.

The animal on the beach, which he can do in his sleep. The herring, which he must attribute to Miss Leslie by name, every time, because a man who takes credit is a man who might take other things. The bone, which he must not enjoy. Spilsbury, who must be described as *a scientist who has given the Institute an account and who is at the Institute now*, in nine words, without one adjective.

And the letter.

"They will come to it in the last ten minutes and they will be very pleasant about it."

"Yes."

"You will not apologise for it."

"No."

"You will not defend it at length either. Length is guilt."

"Then what do I say?"

"That you wrote it. Why you wrote it. That you did not show it to the head of your station first, and that you were wrong about that." Hester looks up. "And then you will stop talking. Can you stop talking?"

"I can stop talking."

"You are Spanish, Dr Pobil."

"Ma'am, I have been stopping talking since June," says Pascual, "and it is the only thing I have got really good at this year," and Hester Bevan laughs out loud, once, and then is appalled with herself.

---

Gamma goes off her feed on the ninth and they have thirty-one hours of it.

Nobody says the word. Nobody has to; there is not a person on this station who has not read the hypothesis, and Chumley is in the same pen and perfectly well and that is precisely the arrangement the whole of the last five months has been about.

What happens is that at twenty past six on the ninth Pascual comes up the path and finds Reggie already in the long room getting the water kit out, and neither of them says good evening, and they work.

It is the first time since June that they do the thing they have always been able to do.

Salinity at both gates and at the sill. Temperature at four depths, hourly, all night, turn and turn about. The full feed record back to August against Jean's water figures. Faeces. The old animal's respiration counted from the rail in the dark with a watch, twice an hour, by two men who take it in turns to hold the lamp and who do not speak except to say the number.

At half past four in the morning Reggie says, "Her rate's up four."

"It's up four because she's cold and it's November and she's older than either of us knows." Pascual writes it down. "Say the other thing."

"It's also up four if she's off her food because of something in the next pen."

"Yes. That's the one. Now we've both said it and we can stop doing it separately."

---

She eats at eleven o'clock on the morning of the tenth: three pounds, then four, then the whole bucket, with Chumley getting in the way of everything.

Pascual sits down on the concrete rather suddenly.

"There's a chill in the north gate," he says, to the water. "That's the whole of it. There's been a chill in that gate since the seventh and I'd have had it on the sixth if I'd been doing the round at six."

"It's back at six."

"It's back at six since the twenty-eighth."

"Yes," says Reggie. "I noticed."

---

On the eleventh he goes down to the pens at five and Reggie is at the rail.

They stand there. Chumley is doing his lengths. Gamma is out in the middle where she goes, and has been off her feed for two days and is not, Pascual is nearly certain, off her feed for any reason that matters.

"Hester says you're ready."

"Hester says I'm adequate."

"That's what she says about the submission and the submission is the best thing she has ever made." Reggie has his arms on the rail. "You'll be all right in the room. In four years I have not seen you care what a department thinks of you."

"That's not true."

Reggie turns his head.

"I care what this one thinks," says Pascual. "I've cared since the twenty-fourth of October."

"Why the twenty-fourth?"

"Because you stood up in front of six people and said out loud that you'd cared every day of your life and I hadn't, and that it wasn't to your credit."

"It was true."

"It was true in 1954." He puts both hands on the rail. "And I sat against that wall and let you say it, and I've thought about it every day for three weeks. Ask me again in the boat on Tuesday."

---

On the night of the twelfth he is repacking a trunk he has already packed twice when Reggie comes in without knocking, which he has not done since the twenty-second of June.

He stops with his hand still on the frame, having heard himself not knock.

"Come in and shut it," says Pascual, "before you think about that for ten minutes."

---

They sit on the edge of the cot. Reggie leans against his shoulder, which he does, which he has done since about the third week of 1954, and which is the single most ordinary thing that has happened in this room since the summer.

"Reggie."

"Mm."

"I'd like to." He says it looking at the trunk. "And I'd like you to say no if it isn't right, and I want you to know before you answer that I'll believe you."

Reggie does not say anything for about four seconds.

"It isn't right," he says. "I want it a great deal and it isn't right and I can't tell you why, and I'd rather not invent a reason."

"Good."

"*Good?*"

"You said no to me. Out loud, in a room, in four seconds." Pascual gets an arm round him and hauls, and the man comes where he is put. "That's the first no anybody in this building has managed since June without a letter and a department in it."

"That is a very low standard."

"It's the standard we've got. Lie down."

---

They lie down.

It is a hot night and neither of them sleeps for a long time, and Pascual lies there with a considerable quantity of Reginald Tar arranged along him and does the arithmetic he cannot help doing, which comes out at a hundred and thirty-one days, and does not say it, because the man will be counting too and there is a boat in the morning.

At about two o'clock Reggie says, "Pascual."

And Pascual hears the whole of it coming — hears the particular quality of the breath before it, which he has now heard three times since May — and puts his hand flat on the back of the man's neck.

"Not before a boat."

"I —"

"No. Listen to me, because it's the last useful thing I'm going to say before the thirteenth." He gets the hand up into his hair. "When you say it, I want three days, and nowhere either of us has to be."

"Pascual —"

"I want to be able to sit in a chair afterwards and not be halfway to a jetty. That's not me stopping you. That's me asking for it properly."

There is a long pause.

"That is the best offer I have had since April," Reggie says, into his collarbone.

"Then take it."

"I am taking it."

"*Bueno.* Go to sleep."

---

They go on the thirteenth, he and the Colonel, on the government launch, which Hester has got them passage on because the mail boat is not until Wednesday and the Colonel will not travel to anything at the last minute, with the four box files in a locked trunk and the forty photographs in a separate case because Hester will not have them in the same piece of luggage.

Everybody comes down to the jetty, which nobody arranged.

Jean shakes his hand and says *don't let them call it a husbandry problem* and does not let go for a second longer than she means to. John gives him an envelope with a list in it of every plate number he might conceivably be asked about, cross-indexed, in a hand that has got a great deal better in two years. Spilsbury says, "They will offer you tea. Take it. A man who takes the tea is a man who intends to stay in the chair," and goes back up the path before anybody can say anything to him at all.

And Reggie comes last, and puts a hand out, and they shake hands on a jetty in front of six people because that is what two colleagues do.

"Ask me what," he says.

"What?"

"On Tuesday. You said *ask me in the boat on Tuesday* and it is Tuesday and you are getting into a boat."

Pascual looks at him.

"Ask me whether I care what that office thinks of me," he says, "and I'll tell you that I don't, and that I care very much indeed what one man on this jetty thinks of me, and that it's the only opinion in the Empire that has ever once made me stop and do a thing differently."

Reggie does not let go of his hand.

"That is not what I was going to ask," he says.

"I know it isn't. Ask me the other one when I get back."

---

The launch goes astern and swings and heads for the head, and Pascual sits on the thwart with a locked trunk between his knees, watching seven people get smaller on a jetty, and one of them does not go up the path with the others.

He is still there when they go round the point.
