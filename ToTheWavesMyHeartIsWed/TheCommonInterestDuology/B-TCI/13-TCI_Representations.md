# Representations

John Weald's job, as of the eighth of October, consists of four things.

He draws. He photographs what he draws. He keeps the plate sequence, which now runs to five hundred and fourteen and has not had a gap in it since the twenty-sixth of April. And, since the fourth of this month, he takes the minutes of every meeting held in this building, properly, in a bound book, because Mrs Bevan asked him to and because when Mrs Bevan asks you something you find yourself saying yes about four words before you have decided anything.

He is twenty-six. He came here at twenty-four to draw fish for a fisheries return.

He now knows what a Corbie lock is, what a sagitta looks like at twenty diameters, what caisson disease does to the head of a femur, and what the inside of a government department sounds like, and he would give a very great deal to un-know at least two of those.

---

What he mostly does, though, is watch them.

Not on purpose. It is simply that a man who draws for a living looks at people the way other people look at print, and John has been looking at these two for four years, and the thing he cannot stop seeing is the *distance*.

It has a measurement. That is the horrible part. In 1955 it was about eighteen inches: Reggie would come down the long room and stop at eighteen inches from Dr Pobil's shoulder and stay there, and lean, and argue. Since June it has been four feet. Since the fourth of October it has been the length of a bench.

They are perfectly civil. They do the work and the work is good. On the tenth they get through the whole bone series with Dr Spilsbury in five hours and produce something that John, who cannot follow a third of it, can tell is very fine.

And at the end of it Dr Pobil says *thank you, that's the lot*, and Reggie says *thank you*, and they go out by different doors.

John writes *bone series completed 10.x.56* in the plate book and puts his pencil down and sits in an empty room for a minute.

---

Things John Weald has noticed in October and has not drawn.

That Dr Pobil has moved the evening pen round from six to five, which is when Reggie does the water samples; that the two rounds used to overlap by about twenty minutes; and that they now do not overlap at all.

That there is a second stool in the long room which has been pushed under the far bench since the fourth and has not come out.

That Reggie has stopped saying *Pascual* in front of people and says *Dr Pobil*, and that Dr Pobil has not adjusted by so much as a syllable and still says *Reggie*, and that this is somehow the worst of the lot.

That Miss Leslie has begun sitting between them at supper without appearing to have decided anything.

That Chumley, who is six and idiotic and enormously fond of everybody, has twice come up to the shelf while Reggie is at the rail and put his chin on the concrete and waited; and that Reggie has put a hand flat on the top of his head both times and stood there for a good while, and that this is the only thing in five weeks that has made him look like a man rather than a filing system.

And that on the fourteenth Dr Pobil came into the dark room, asked John whether the fixative plate was dry, was told that it was, said thank you, and went out.

The fixative plate had been dry for nine days.

He is the one who put it there.

---

Mrs Bevan's plan for November is in the minutes of the eleventh and it is the best thing anybody has come up with since August.

A personal representation. Not a letter — a man, in a room, in Kingston, in front of the Assistant Secretary, with the four box files on the table and forty photographs and an hour.

"They cannot read four boxes," she says. "They can be shown them. There is a difference and it is the difference between a file and a fright."

"Who goes?"

"My husband, because he is the licence holder. And Reginald, because he is the only one of us who can answer a scientific question in a sentence and because he has the particular quality that is wanted."

"Which is?"

"He is extremely difficult to patronise," says Mrs Bevan, and everybody laughs, which they have not done in the minutes since September, and John writes down *agreed* and underlines it.

---

The notice of application comes on the eighteenth.

It is a form. That is what nobody is prepared for: after five months of letters on very good paper, the thing itself is a printed form with boxes on it and a departmental number in the corner, and the office has sent a copy as a courtesy because the Institute is the present holder.

Miss Leslie reads out the boxes.

"Applicant. *The Caribbean Marine Biological Station.*"

"Address of applicant."

"Post Office Box four hundred and eleven, Nassau, Bahamas."

Nobody says anything.

John, who has spent two months in the letter book with Mrs Bevan and who can find anything in it in under a minute, does not need to look it up and looks it up anyway, because he wants to be wrong.

*19 March 1955. R. Tar to the Caribbean Marine Biological Station, P.O. Box 411, Nassau. Four pp. Cohabitation protocol in full: pen dimensions, gate schedule, feed, salinity ranges, plate numbers, disturbance intervals. Recommends animals be kept in company.*

He carries the book over and puts it on the table open at the page and does not say anything, because there is nothing to say and because he is twenty-six and not stupid.

Reggie looks at four pages of his own handwriting for about nine seconds.

"Yes," he says. "Well. There it is."

And then he asks Mrs Bevan a perfectly sensible question about the thirty-day objection period, and gets on, and John thinks that is the most frightening thing he has watched a human being do.

---

The second letter comes six days later, on the twenty-fourth, in the same post as a seed catalogue and a bill for paraffin.

Mrs Bevan reads it standing up at the verandah table. Then she puts it down and says, "John. You will read this one out, because I want it in the minutes in the exact words, and I am not going to trust myself to get the exact words."

So John Weald picks it up.

There are three paragraphs and the first two are about the objection period and the inspector's visit and he gets through them without any difficulty at all.

Then there is the third.

> I am further directed to say that representations have been made to this office touching the domestic arrangements of certain of the Institute's senior staff. The Secretary does not propose to act upon them, and does not require the Institute to answer them. He is, however, bound to record that they have been received.

---

John reads it out in a perfectly steady voice and writes it into the minute book in his own hand, *verbatim*, and underlines nothing, and then sits with the pen in his fingers and looks at the words *bound to record*.

He has known since 1954.

He has known since a morning at the end of May 1954, when he came up the path at half past five with a board under his arm to catch the light, and Reggie Tar came out of Dr Pobil's door in yesterday's shirt and shut it behind him with the flat of his hand, the way you shut a door you do not want to wake anybody by shutting.

John turned round before either of them saw anything, and went and drew the seaward beach instead, and has never mentioned it to one living soul: not to Jean, not to his mother, not to the two of them.

Four years. He is rather proud of it, in the way you are proud of a thing you have never once been thanked for because nobody knows you did it.

And now it is in his handwriting in a bound book.

---

"It is not a threat," says Mrs Bevan.

"It reads like —"

"It reads like nothing at all, which is the point, and it is why I made John read it. There is no accusation in it. There is nothing to answer."

"Then we answer it anyway."

"If we answer it, we have made it a thing. If we complain of it, we have made it a thing. A department that is bound to record something has recorded it and gone home." She puts both hands flat on the table.

"Then what is it?"

"It is a receipt. It says: this is on the file, we can pick it up whenever we care to, and we should like you to know we have got it."

"Who?"

"I do not know. I shall find out, and it will take me until March." She does not blink. "It may be Bay Street. It may be somebody in Kingston who was told to make himself useful."

"Or?"

"Or a person on this island who was asked a question in a shop and answered it without the least idea what he was doing."

And nobody in that room says the name that everybody in that room is thinking of, and John, who is looking at Dr Spilsbury because he looks at everybody, sees him register that nobody has said it.

He does not look offended. He looks, for about a second and a half, extremely interested.

---

Reggie has not said one word.

He says one now, and it is not what John expects at all, because what John expects — what he has been braced for since the third paragraph — is the quiet voice, the one that comes out when the man is at the end of himself.

What he gets is: "Right. Then I'm not going to Kingston."

"Reginald —"

"Don't. I've had nine minutes to think about it and it isn't difficult." He is up and moving with his glasses in his hand and is entirely, recognisably himself. "The whole of your plan is a man in a room who can't be patronised."

"Yes."

"Put me in that room now and I'm not a man who can't be patronised. I'm a man they've got a paragraph about, and the Assistant Secretary will be thinking about that paragraph for the whole hour, and so will I."

"That is exactly why they sent it," says the Colonel.

"Of course it is. It cost them nothing and it takes your best card out of your hand in three sentences and there is no way to put it back." He stops at the end of the table. "So don't put it back. Send somebody else."

"Who?"

"Pobil," says Reggie.

---

John's pencil stops.

It is the first time either of them has said the other's name in a meeting since the fourth of October. He has been counting, because he keeps the minutes, and because there is a part of him that has been counting things at this station since he was twenty-four and will not stop.

"That paragraph does not name anybody," says Mrs Bevan. "It says *certain of the senior staff*. It touches him exactly as much as it touches you and you know it does."

"I know it does."

"Then how is he any safer in that chair than you are?"

"He isn't," says Reggie. "He is better in it."

Nobody says anything.

"He will sit in front of an Assistant Secretary with that paragraph in a folder on the desk, and it will not move him one inch."

"Why not?"

"Because in four years I have not once seen him care what a department thinks of him." Reggie is looking at the table. "And I have cared every day of my life. That is the whole of the difference, it is not to my credit, and there it is."

---

"He can answer a scientific question in a sentence," Reggie says. "He's the veterinary officer and his name's in the fourth line of the licence."

"They have had a letter from him."

"They've had a letter from him, so they know who he is, so he isn't a surprise, so they'll have to deal with him instead of wondering about him."

"They have had a letter from him," says Mrs Bevan, "which went rather badly."

"Yes. And the man who wrote it is the only person in this building who has told that office a true thing about those animals at his own expense."

"That is one way of putting it."

"Put him in a chair in front of an Assistant Secretary and let him say it out loud, and I think he'll be extremely hard to get round."

---

Dr Pobil has not moved from the wall.

"Reggie —"

"I am not doing you a kindness," Reggie says, and he is not looking at him, and his voice has gone somewhere John does not like at all. "I have thought about it for nine minutes and it is the correct answer and I should have got to it in August."

That is all he says.

He says it and he sits down and asks the Colonel a question about the inspector's dates, and the meeting goes on for another forty minutes, and John writes down every word, and at the end of it Mrs Bevan says *agreed* and John underlines it, and Dr Pobil has not said one word in favour of it or against it.

---

Dr Spilsbury finds him at six, in the dark room, with the door open because there is nothing in the dishes.

"You were watching me."

"I watch everybody. It's my job."

"You were watching me particularly, and so was Mrs Bevan, and neither of you asked me anything." He is in the doorway with his hat in his hand. "It was not me."

John puts the tongs down.

"Nobody said it was."

"Nobody said anything, which is the same thing done more slowly." Spilsbury does not come in. "I am telling you rather than Tar, because you will believe me this evening and he will spend a week deciding whether to, and because it is true, and because I have no use whatever for any of you being wrong about it."

"Why should I believe you?"

"Because I have not told you an untruth since the thirtieth of May." He puts the hat on. "And because if I had wanted to do that to him, I should have done it in 1948, when it would have been worth something."

And he goes, and John stands in a room the size of a cupboard for rather a long time.

---

Dr Pobil finds him at seven, at the plate cupboard, and does not come further in than the frame.

"John."

"Sir."

"That thing in the meeting. The Kingston thing." He has his arms folded and is looking at the floor. "Was that for me or was that for the animals?"

John thinks about it properly, because he has been asked properly.

"Both," he says.

"Yes." Pascual nods slowly, twice. "That's what I thought."

He stands there a moment longer.

"It was the right answer, too," he says. "That's the part I can't get round," and goes.

---

What John does at nine o'clock that evening is the only genuinely brave thing he has done in his life, and he is sick about it for two hours beforehand.

He goes to the long room, where Dr Pobil is, and says there is something wrong with plate five hundred and six and would he come and look.

Then he goes to the office, where Reggie is, and says there is something wrong with plate five hundred and six and would he come and look.

Then he stands in the dark room between the two of them with the door shut and a perfectly good plate on the bench and his hands in his pockets so that nobody can see what they are doing.

"John," says Reggie.

"There's nothing wrong with the plate."

"I had worked that out."

---

"I'd like to say one thing," says John, "and then I'm going to go away and I am never going to mention any of it again, and if either of you wants to sack me for it you can do it in the morning."

Nobody says anything. Dr Pobil has gone very still in the red light.

"I've known since May 1954."

The room does something.

"I came up the path at half past five one morning at the end of May to catch the light on the seaward beach," John says, "and you came out of Dr Pobil's door, sir, and shut it quietly, and that is the entire sum of what I have ever seen."

"John —"

"It took about a second. I turned round before either of you knew I was on the path, and I went and drew the beach."

"John —"

"And I've not said it to anybody. Not to Jean. Not to my mother, not in a letter. And I'm not going to."

"John —"

"Let me get to the end, sir, because I've been putting it together since four o'clock and it'll fall apart."

He gets his hands out of his pockets and puts them on the bench.

"It's never made the slightest difference to me. Not in 1954 and not now."

He gets his hands out of his pockets and puts them flat on the bench.

"And this morning I wrote a sentence about the two of you into a bound book, in my own handwriting, because I was asked to. I'll have to look at it every time I open that book for the rest of my life."

"John."

"And I'd much rather have written down that you'd had a row about a microtome."

---

He stops.

Neither of them says anything, and the red lamp hums, and somewhere outside the generator changes note.

"That's it," says John. "That's the whole of it. There's nothing wrong with the plate and I'm going to bed."

And he goes out and shuts the door behind him, and does not stay to hear anything, because he has thought about that part very hard and decided that the one thing he must not do is stand there and make them say something to him.

---

He is halfway up the path before he realises he has left them in there.

He stops.

There is one small square window in the dark room, high up, and it is red, and from the path you can see whether there is anybody in front of the bench because the light goes.

He stands in the dark on a sand path at ten past nine at night with his heart going like a rat in a box and does not look round.

*Go to bed, John Weald,* he thinks. *You are twenty-six years old and you have just told two of the best men you will ever work for that you have been keeping their secret for four years, and you did it without asking either of them for anything, and that is the most you have got and it is more than anybody else in this building has managed since June.*

He goes to bed.

At about half past two he wakes up and finds he cannot get back to sleep, and lies there, and at some point after three he hears two sets of footsteps go up the path past the hut, not together, perhaps twenty seconds apart.

He counts the twenty seconds.

Then he puts the pillow over his head, because it is not his business, and because twenty seconds is a very great deal better than the length of a bench.
