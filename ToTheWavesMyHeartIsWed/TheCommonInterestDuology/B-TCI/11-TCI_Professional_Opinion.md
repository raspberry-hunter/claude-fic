# Professional Opinion

He has not touched him in forty-seven days.

Pascual knows the figure because he knows every figure in this building; it is what he is; he could tell you Chumley's tail root for every month since May 1954 and the hours of the generator and the date of the last time he put his hands on Reginald Tar, which was the fourth of July, on the floor, in the dark, with a man turning his head away from him.

They still share a room four nights out of seven. They lie in it and talk, and they are extremely good at it, and they talk about the fixative and about Juan's brother's boat and about a paper in the *Journal* that neither of them thinks much of.

"He's put the engine in the wrong way round."

"He hasn't."

"Pascual, I've seen it. The shaft comes out at about nine degrees and he's shimmed it with a bit of a crate."

"That's how his father did it."

"That's how his father sank."

And Pascual laughs in the dark before he can stop himself, and there is about a second and a half afterwards in which neither of them says anything whatever, because they have both heard it.

Then Reggie turns over and sleeps with his back against him, because he is cold at four in the morning in August in the tropics, which is a thing about him that has never made any sense at all.

Forty-seven days.

He has not said the figure to anybody and does not intend to and would rather not have it.

---

Reggie puts his idea to the verandah on the twentieth.

It is a good idea and it takes him about four minutes and Pascual can see the shape of it arriving and cannot help admiring the construction, the way you admire a wall you are about to be shut behind.

They register a programme. Not a separation. A paired-holding observational study: four years of existing data, a stated three-year term, a start date of the first of September, intensive daily observation of the bond in an established pair under known conditions. Every word of it true. Every word of it, in fact, a description of what this Institute already does on a wet Tuesday.

"And it blocks the transfer," says Reggie.

"It blocks it on paper," says the Colonel.

"It blocks it on paper, and paper is what we've got seven weeks to make, and it costs nobody anything, and it separates no animal from any other animal."

Nobody says anything for a moment. John is looking at Reggie with an expression Pascual has never seen on him, which is hope.

Then Spilsbury puts his cup down.

---

"No," he says.

"On what ground?"

"On the ground that it is not an experiment." He does not even sound unkind about it, which is worse. "There is no intervention. You do not do anything to anything. You watch two animals and write down what they do."

"That's a study."

"It is a study and it is a perfectly respectable one and I would read it," says Spilsbury, "and it is not an experiment in progress within the meaning of the thing you are trying to do, and the first competent person that office consults will say so in one sentence."

"They mightn't consult anybody."

"They have consulted somebody already. That is why the letter is on that table."

Hester puts her pencil down.

"He is right, Reginald."

"Hester —"

"He is right, and it is worse than he says." She does not look pleased about any of it.

"How worse."

"If they read it as an attempt to hold the animals by a device, then this Institute has been caught trying it on, in writing, over your signature, six weeks before a review."

"And?"

"And every document in those four boxes is then the work of a station that tried it on."

---

Reggie stands at the end of the table for a long moment with his hands behind his back.

Then he says it.

"Very well. Then I want the real one drafted."

Pascual's hands go still on the rail.

"Drafted," says Hester.

"On paper. Properly. Protocol, animals, term, intervention, recovery period, the tissues that would be taken and when." Reggie's voice is entirely level and entirely reasonable, and Pascual has known him four years and would rather be listening to almost anything else. "We have spent twelve weeks talking about a thing nobody has written down, and a thing nobody has written down gets easier every time it is mentioned."

"That is true," says Hester.

"So I should like it on a sheet of paper with a date on it, and I should like to be able to read it and know exactly what it is that I am not going to do."

"Who drafts it?"

"I do," says Reggie. "It is my hypothesis. It would be my experiment. I am not going to have anybody else in this building write it and then take it off them."

---

There is nothing wrong with any of that.

Pascual stands at the verandah rail with his hands on the wood and goes through it four times while the meeting moves on to Jean's ports, and there is nothing wrong with it. It is exactly what he would want a man in that position to do. It is the thing he himself does with every dangerous idea: write it down, look at it, measure it, put it where you can see it.

He says nothing at all. Nobody notices, because Pascual Pobil has said very little at any meeting since the fourteenth.

---

Spilsbury finds him at the pens at six, which he does now, two or three evenings a week, and which Pascual has stopped objecting to because objecting was costing him more than the company.

They stand at the rail. Gamma is out in the middle. Chumley is doing his lengths.

"He will write a good one," says Spilsbury.

"I know he will."

"It will be the best protocol either of us has read on this subject, because he has thought of nothing else since ten past three on the fourth of July, and because he does not know how to write a bad one." He has his hands behind his back. "You are aware of what that means."

"Say it and stop enjoying it."

"I am not enjoying it." He sounds, for once, as though that might be true. "A thing you have written down properly is a thing you can do. That is the whole of the difference between an idea and an experiment and it is why I have spent thirty years telling people to write things down."

Pascual looks at the water.

"And you're going to sit there and watch."

"I am going to sit here and be extremely useful," says Spilsbury, "which is what I came for, and you may hold whichever of those two you prefer against me."

---

He finds the draft on the second of September.

He is not looking for it. He is in the office at half past five in the morning because the fixative order has to go out and the order book lives on Reggie's desk, and it is under the order book, four sheets, held down with the paperweight, in a hand he could read upside down from the door.

He does not touch it. That matters to him afterwards; he wants it on the record, at least to himself, that he did not pick it up or turn it over.

He reads what is on top.

> *Protocol for the determination of the mortality consequent upon the separation of a bonded pair of N. aestuarius.*
> *Subjects: Gamma (f., est. 30+), Chumley (m., 6). Institute pens, north and south.*
> *Commencement: 1 September 1956. Term: 24 weeks.*

*Commencement: 1 September 1956.*

It is the second.

---

He goes out and stands on the step in the dark and does the only thing he is any use at, which is to take it in order.

One: the date has gone by. A protocol with a commencement date that has already passed is a protocol that somebody intends to date back, or a protocol that has started.

Two: it is written out fair. Not notes. Not working. Four sheets in a clean hand with the headings ruled.

Three: it is under the order book, which is where you put a thing you do not want somebody to see when he comes into the office at half past five in the morning to look up the fixative order.

Four — and this is the one that takes the longest, and that he arrives at standing in the dark with the sea going under the jetty — is that ten weeks ago he would have walked back into that office, put the four sheets in front of the man at breakfast, and said *what is this*, and there would have been an argument, and it would have been loud, and it would have taken about nine minutes.

And he does not do it.

He stands on the step and finds out, at twenty to six in the morning on the second of September, that he cannot make himself walk back through the door and ask, and that he has not the first idea when that happened.

---

He drafts the letter that night and does not send it.

It takes him two hours and it is three paragraphs long and there is nothing in it that is not true.

He is the Institute's veterinary officer. He has held that appointment since November 1952 and it is in the licence, in the fourth line, which Hester Bevan wrote. He is professionally of the opinion — and he can support it, and he sets out how — that the removal of one animal from an established pair of this species carries a substantial risk of the death of the other, and that no such procedure should be undertaken upon either of the animals held by this Institute.

He addresses it to the Colonial Secretary's office, because that is the office which will decide.

> Sir,
>
> I am the veterinary officer of the William Martin Institute for Marine Studies, and have held that appointment since November 1952. My name stands in the fourth line of the Institute's specimen-holding licence.
>
> I understand that the disposal of the Institute's two specimens of *N. aestuarius* is under review. I write upon one matter only, in my professional capacity, and I ask that this letter be read together with the Institute's submission and not in place of it.
>
> It is my opinion, which I can support and am prepared to support before any tribunal, that the removal of one animal from an established pair of this species carries a substantial and probably a high risk of the death of the animal which remains. My grounds are set out overleaf, with the Institute's own mortality record since 1952 and such observations as I have been able to obtain elsewhere.
>
> I say further that no procedure involving such a separation ought to be undertaken upon either animal now held by this Institute: not by this Institute, and not by any establishment to which they may hereafter be transferred.
>
> I have the honour to be, Sir,
> Your obedient servant,
>
> P. Pobil, M.R.C.V.S.

---

He takes it in order, twice, the way he takes everything, and it holds.

A veterinary opinion on a file attaches to the animal and not to the address. It goes where they go. If it is on the Colonial Secretary's file before the first of October, then any establishment that receives those two animals receives them with a written professional opinion saying that the one thing that establishment wants to do to them will kill them, and no licensing officer in the Empire signs over that.

He goes through it looking for the hole in it, because he has spent twenty-one years going through things looking for the hole in them, and the hole he finds is the one he can live with: that it tells the office there is somebody at the Martin who is contemplating the thing. He turns that over for about half an hour on the step in the dark and decides that the office has been told worse by Mr Lecky already, and that a warning from a vet is not a scandal, and that the animals are worth it.

He is wrong about the last part and he will not find that out for four weeks.

Then he puts it in the second drawer of the bench in the long room, under the spare microtome knives, and goes to bed.

---

He does not send it on the fifth, or the sixth.

On the seventh Hester comes down to the long room with the pad.

"Anything for the bundle?"

"The bone series is finished. It's in the book, and John's got four more plates coming."

"Good." She writes. "Anything of yours on paper? Correspondence. Anything gone out or come in that touches the subject since July."

There is a drawer twelve feet away with a box of microtome knives in it.

"No," says Pascual.

"Right," says Hester, and writes *nil* against his name, and goes, and it is the first time in his life that he has lied to that woman and he has done it in two letters without moving his hands.

---

He does not send it on the eighth, or the ninth, or the tenth.

What he does instead is watch, which is the thing he is best at and which is at this point the only thing he is still doing well. Reggie works on the draft in the evenings. He does not mention it. He does not hide it either, after the second — it lives on the desk in plain sight, growing, and on the seventh it is nine sheets and on the eleventh it is fourteen, and once Pascual comes in and finds him at it with his head in his hands.

On the eighth Hester asks at supper how the draft is coming and Reggie says *it is coming*, and Hester says *good*, and everybody eats.

On the ninth Reggie gives John a raise, out of a budget that has not got one, by cutting his own book allowance, and does it without telling anybody, and is found out by Jean.

"You cut your own book allowance."

"I did nothing of the kind."

"Jean's seen the ledger."

"Then Jean can put the ledger back where she found it and mind her own —"

"Reggie."

"It's forty pounds a year, he's worth three hundred, and I'm not discussing it," says Reggie, and goes out, and slams a door for the first time since June, which cheers three people up considerably.

Jean catches him at the salt ponds on the tenth. She has been under a cloud since the fourteenth of August and is carrying it better than anybody expected her to.

"I'm glad she did it in front of the room."

"Are you."

"I hated her for nine days." Jean has a bucket in one hand and does not put it down. "Then I worked out that if she'd told me privately I'd still be walking round this station thinking I'd done something clever, and everybody else would have known, and nobody would have said."

Pascual looks at the water.

"That's the worst thing about this place," Jean says. "Everybody's so considerate. I'd rather be shouted at."

"Would you."

"I was shouted at for three years and it was the happiest I've ever been." She picks up the second bucket. "Tell Reggie that if you like. He won't believe you."

He stands at the ponds for some while after she has gone.

Then he goes back up to the long room and opens the second drawer of the bench and looks at what is in it and shuts it again.

---

Later on the tenth Reggie comes into the long room and stands behind Pascual at the microscope for a while and puts one hand flat between his shoulder blades and leaves it there and says nothing at all and goes away again.

Pascual sits at that bench for four minutes afterwards without moving the stage.

---

On the eleventh, at night, in the room, in the dark:

"Pascual."

"Mm."

"Are you all right."

"Yes."

"No. I'm asking you properly and I'd like it properly, because I have asked you that eight or nine times since June and had *yes* every time and I have said *yes* back to you just as often, and I think we have both been —"

He stops.

Pascual lies there in the dark with his arm over his eyes and waits, and the waiting goes on for about four seconds, and in those four seconds there is a thing about twelve feet away in the second drawer of a bench under a box of microtome knives.

*Ask me again.*

"— I think we have both been tired," says Reggie.

"Yes," says Pascual. "We have."

---

Neither of them sleeps for some while.

At about one o'clock Reggie turns over and puts his forehead against the back of Pascual's shoulder and stays there, which is not a request for anything and which both of them know is not a request for anything, and Pascual reaches back and gets a hand round the back of his neck and holds on to it.

They stay like that for a long time. It is the most either of them has touched the other since the fourth of July and neither of them says one word about that either.

"Sixty-nine days," says Reggie, into his shoulder blade, very quietly, after about ten minutes.

And Pascual's whole chest goes to water, because the man has been counting too; because of course he has been counting too; because he counts everything and always has; and because there is a sentence sitting in Pascual's mouth that he could say right now, in the dark, with a hand round the back of a neck, and it would cost him nine seconds and the whole of the rest of this would be different.

"Go to sleep," he says.

---

On the twelfth of September he takes the letter out of the drawer at eight in the morning, seals it, and puts it in the outgoing bag himself.

The mail boat comes round the head at twenty past ten.

He carries the bag down and hands it to the rating and watches him toss it in under the thwart with three sacks of returned empties, exactly where a man standing on these planks on the second of May watched his own go, and the launch goes astern and swings and heads for the head.

There is no arrangement in the world by which you get a letter back out of a Post Office bag once it has gone under a thwart.

He knows that. He has known it since he was about nine and living four thousand miles from here, and he stands there anyway until the engine note drops away round the point, and then he turns round and walks up the jetty, past John, who says something about the plates and gets an answer, and up the path, and into the long room, and puts his apron on, and cuts sections until four o'clock.
