# Insufficient

## The eleventh of May, 1967

Hester Bevan brings the post up at ten and puts it on the long table and does not go away.

He reads it twice.

"Rørstad's dead."

"I am very sorry to hear it."

"February." He puts it down. "He's not coming."

"No," says Hester.

---

Reggie Tar is fifty-two years old, he is the Director of Research of the William Martin Institute, and he has been reading this man across four thousand miles of post for twenty-one years.

There is one sentence in the letter that is wrong, and he has it at about ten past ten.

*I am not able in conscience to be four thousand miles off in the middle of it.*

In July 1964 there was a chartering dispute over the survey vessel, and Alec Spilsbury spent nine days being appalling to a man in Trondheim about it, and came anyway. Reggie knows this because he was told the whole of it on his own verandah in the July of 1964, at length, with enjoyment on both sides, including an imitation of the man in Trondheim that he has thought about perhaps a hundred times since.

A man who does that does not stay away because there is no second man.

---

He writes the condolence first and it takes him two hours and it is the best letter of its kind he has in him. Rørstad was a good man and Alec liked him, which is rare enough in that station to be a fact about both of them, and there is nothing in those two pages that is about anything else.

Then he puts a fresh sheet in and sits and looks at it for a long time.

The trouble is what to sign it as.

There is no paper anywhere in the world with his name and the other man's name on the same line. There is a file in a cabinet with nine applications in it from a qualified person not being a member of the staff. There is twenty-one years of correspondence about fish. Whatever he puts on this sheet, he is going to have to put it there with no standing at all, in his own hand, and post it, and then wait five weeks.

At four o'clock he writes nine lines.

*I am going to be troublesome and I am sorry for it, and you may tell me to go to the devil.*

*In July 1964 the charter went wrong and you spent nine days on a man in Trondheim and came anyway. I had the whole account of it on my verandah and I enjoyed it very much. A man who does that finds the way round a survey. You have found the way round everything since 1958, including a septic hand.*

*So the reason you have given me is a true reason and it is not the reason, and I have known that since about ten past ten this morning, and if I write back and say "of course you can't come" I shall have taken it, and once I have taken it I do not see what stops me taking it again next year.*

*I am not asking you to account to me. I have no standing to ask you to account to me and we both know exactly why not. I am asking you as a man who has read your handwriting for twenty-one years: tell me the actual reason.*

Then he sits for another quarter of an hour, and then writes:

*R.*

Because there is nothing else to put, and because there never has been, and because that is very nearly the point.

---

He gives it to Hester at half past four with the condolence.

"Two."

"Two," says Reggie.

Hester Bevan looks at the second envelope, which is thin, and then at him.

"Is he all right?"

"I don't know," says Reggie Tar. "That's what's in it."

"Ah," says Hester, and takes them both down to the boat.

---

## Ålesund, the twenty-sixth of May

Solveig brings it up at eleven with the rest and he opens the thick one first because it is obviously the condolence and he wants to have read it.

He reads it standing at the window and it is very good and he has to put it down twice.

Then he opens the thin one.

---

Alec Spilsbury reads nine lines twice, standing up, and then sits down in the chair at the desk and does not move for some time.

The first thing he feels, and he is scrupulous enough to record it to himself in those terms, is relief so acute that it is physically identifiable somewhere under the sternum.

The second thing, arriving about four seconds behind it, is the reason the first thing is not to be acted on.

---

Because he has a sum, and he has had it since the July of 1966, and it goes like this.

He cannot do twelve days a year for twenty years. He has done six and the sixth was a thing he decided in advance was going to be good. He is fifty-seven. On any reasonable view he has fifteen working years left and rather more than that of the other kind.

If he writes back and tells Reggie Tar the truth, Reggie Tar will resign the directorship of the William Martin Institute inside four seconds of reading it, and will do it well and cheerfully and will never once say a word about it afterwards, and the thing on that island — which did not exist in 1946 and exists now, and has a schedule and an Order and a release programme and two hundred animals a year on the returns — will be in other hands by Michaelmas. It will not stop. It will simply be somebody else's, and it will be about a tenth less, for forty years.

And Alec Spilsbury, who has been struck off the staff of one institution for putting one animal in front of the column, is not going to be the cause of that twice.

Therefore he must not tell him.

But he has been asked directly by a man he has never lied to, and a false reason given in writing to that man, in his own hand, over his own signature, is a thing he is not going to do.

So there is exactly one move on the board.

---

He does not write for nine days.

He does the station's work in that time and does it properly. He drafts a reply on the twenty-eighth which is four pages and which he reads over on the twenty-ninth and puts in the stove, because it is an account, and an account is a confession with the numbering left in.

He drafts one on the thirty-first which is two lines and is unforgivable.

He drafts one on the second of June which is correct and which he cannot make himself sign, and he sits with it for an hour and then understands why, and the reason is that it leaves a door open, and a door left open will be a door he stands in for fifteen years.

He posts the fourth one on the fourth of June.

---

*My dear Reggie,*

*You are quite right and I am not going to insult you. The reason I gave you is true and it is insufficient, and you knew that before you had finished the page, and I should have known you would.*

*I am not going to give you the sufficient one. I have thought about this for nine days and that is my position and I do not intend to move off it, and I would ask you to believe that it is not stubbornness and is not shame.*

*What I will tell you is the conclusion, because you are entitled to that and because you would arrive at it yourself by about August.*

*The arrangement has become unsound. It was not unsound in 1958 and it was not unsound in 1962. It is now. I have had it under observation for rather better than a year and I have struck the observation out a number of times and it keeps coming back, and a man who goes on striking out the same finding is not doing science.*

*I am therefore not going to apply next year, or the year after. I think we should discontinue it.*

*I want to say the next part plainly because I shall not be able to say it twice. This is not a quarrel and it is not a withdrawal of anything and there is nothing whatever you have done. It is a piece of arithmetic. An arrangement that is going to fail is better ended in one piece, by the man who can see it, than allowed to come apart in instalments over ten years with both parties being impeccable about it and neither able to say when it happened. I have watched that second thing done to other men and it is worse than anything I am proposing here.*

*I shall go on writing to you about the work for as long as you will have me, and I hope that will be a long time, and I mean that in every sense that you will let it carry.*

*Send the growth sections back with your queries. I want Pobil's view on the weights and I want his raw counts, not his tidied ones, and he is to be told that I have asked for them in those words.*

*Yours, and I am sorry,*

*A.S.*

---

## The sixteenth of June

It comes in the ordinary bag on a Friday morning with a returns query from the Bay and a circular about lamp oil.

Hester puts the post on the long table at ten, and goes down, and comes back up at twenty to eleven with the estimates because there is a thing in section three she wants settled, and Reggie Tar is standing at the window with the letter in his hand and has not moved.

She does not ask. She goes back down and shuts the door and sits at her own desk with her hands flat on the blotter for about a minute and a half.

Then she gets the sailings out.

---

He tries to answer it by post. He tries for three days.

Pascual finds him in the office at ten past three on the Monday morning with the lamp going and nine sheets on the floor, and stands in the doorway for a moment, and then comes in and picks one up and reads it, which he would not normally do.

"That's no good."

"I know it's no good."

"No, hombre, listen." Pascual puts it down. "It's beautiful. It's the best thing you've written since the Order. I read four lines of it and I wanted to lie down." He picks up a second one. "And it's still no good, and you know why."

"Tell me why."

"Because he's had nine days with his and you'll have had five and he's better on paper than you are." Pascual sits on the corner of the desk. "He's not going to be argued out of a position in writing. Nobody's ever argued that man out of anything in writing in his life. He does it to himself, in his own time, with a pencil, and then he tells you the answer."

Reggie puts his hands over his face for about four seconds, which Pascual has seen him do twice.

"He's not wrong," he says.

"About what?"

"About the arithmetic." He takes his hands down. "That's the thing. He's put a finding in front of me and it's *correct*. Twelve days a year for twenty years is not an arrangement, it's a — " He stops. "He's been carrying that on his own since the July of last year and he's carried it well enough that I did not see it, and he has now done the only decent thing available to a man in his position, and the only thing wrong with it is that I would rather be dead."

Pascual Pobil sits on the corner of the desk in the middle of the night.

"Then go and say that," he says.

---

## The competent part

It takes them from Monday morning to Tuesday afternoon and it is, everybody agrees afterwards, an extremely fine piece of work.

Hester has the sailings and has had them since Friday. There is a seat out of Kingston on the Tuesday night which connects at Gander and London, and a Norwegian internal service to Vigra three times a week which he will make by four hours if nothing at all goes wrong, and she has the telephone number of a man at the shipping office at the Bay who owes the Institute a favour from 1961 and who is applied to at half past seven on Monday morning and delivers by nine.

The difficulty is not the travel. The difficulty is that the survey ship is at the Bay taking stores, the annual inspection is on the twenty-seventh, and the Director of Research has not been off this island for a consecutive fortnight since 1958.

Pascual takes the inspection.

"You'll be appalling at them."

"I'll be *charming* at them, and they'll write it down as fatigue, and we'll get the boat money." Pascual is already writing a list. "Sinclair does the release data, Weald does the plates, Hendy runs the seven o'clock and I'll countersign, and Marguerite will come over for the week because she likes watching me suffer."

"The inspection wants the deposited-material figures—"

"The inspection," says Hester Bevan, from the door, "wants section four, which I made up on the thirtieth of June last year and will make up again on the thirtieth of this month, and which is my department and has been since 1951, and I would be very glad if the pair of you would stop discussing it."

---

There is one other thing.

At noon on the Tuesday she comes up with a foolscap sheet and puts it on the desk in front of him and stands there.

"Sign this."

"What is it?"

"It is an application for leave of absence," says Hester Bevan. "You have not made one since 1958. I have kept the record because keeping the record is what I am for." She puts a finger on the second column. "As of the thirtieth of April you have accrued and not taken one year, seven months and eleven days."

Reggie looks at the column.

"That can't be right."

"It is right. I have checked it twice and Mrs Bevan does not make errors of that kind." She takes the pen out of the stand and holds it out. "You are going to sign for fourteen days, and it is going to be entered, and the reason you are going to let me enter it is that in nine days' time you are going to be standing in a cold room in Norway telling a man that you want something, and it will be very much easier to do that if you are not also, at that moment, absent from your post without leave."

He has the pen in his hand and does not put it to the paper.

"The purpose column."

"I have written *personal*," says Hester Bevan.

"Hester—"

"Once," she says. "It will stand once, from a man who has taken no leave in nine years, and nobody living will look at it twice. I would not write it twice and I shall tell you so if you ask me to, and I imagine you will come back from Norway with something better, because you generally do."

He signs it.

"How long have you had that column?"

"Since 1958," says Hester Bevan, and takes the form downstairs.

---

## Ålesund, the twenty-third of June

He comes into it at twenty past nine in the evening and it is not dark and is not going to be.

That is the first thing, and it takes the legs out from under him rather, because he has been imagining this for nine days in the colours of the place he is standing in, and Ålesund on Midsummer Eve is full of light at nine in the evening and there are fires along the water on both sides of the sound and on the islands and people out on the rocks with bottles, and the whole town smells of woodsmoke and salt and somebody's herring.

He has one case and a coat he does not need.

The station is a long low building at the end of the yard and there is nobody in it, because it is Sankthansaften and the junior staff are on the rocks with everybody else. The office window is dark. The window above it is not.

---

Solveig is on the step with her coat over her arm, going out, and stops.

She looks at him. Then at the case. Then she does something with her face that he will think about for a long time afterwards, because it is the face of a woman who has been writing a man's messages down twice for nine years.

"He is upstairs," she says, in English. "Go up. He is not going out; he does not go out on this night, he says it is noisy."

"Is he—"

"He is very tidy," says Solveig, "and he has been very tidy since the beginning of June," and puts her coat on and goes down the yard.

---

## The rooms

Two rooms. A stove not lit. A good lamp not needed. A desk under the window, a shelf of offprints with the spines squared, a bed made the way a man makes a bed when nobody is ever going to see it.

Alec Spilsbury is at the desk in his shirtsleeves with a pen in his hand and the whole of the north light on him, and he turns round, and he goes absolutely white, and stands up so fast the chair goes over behind him.

"What has happened."

"Nothing's happened."

"Pobil. Is it—"

"Nothing has happened, Alec. Nobody's dead and nobody's ill and the animal was at the sill on the eighteenth." Reggie has his hands up. "I'm sorry. I should have wired. I could not think what to put on it that you wouldn't have answered."

Alec stands in the middle of his own room with the chair on its back.

"Then why are you here."

"Because you're better on paper than I am," says Reggie Tar, "and you've had nine days and I had five, and Pascual pointed out that nobody has ever argued you out of anything in writing in your life. May I sit down? I've been travelling for fifty hours and I have not eaten since Bergen."

---

He picks the chair up himself, because Alec does not move for about ten seconds.

Then Alec says, "There is bread and there is some of a fish," and goes and does that, and is entirely competent about it, and neither of them says one word for six or seven minutes while it happens.

Reggie Tar sits in a wooden chair in Norway at half past nine at night in full daylight and looks at a room.

At the deed box on the shelf with twenty-one years of his own handwriting in it. At the boots by the unlit stove. At the four miles of dark-green shore out of the window with a fire going on the point.

At a desk with nothing personal on it at all.

---

"Eat that."

"Thank you."

"You have crossed the Atlantic in June with a winter coat over your arm, which is the most eloquent thing about you I have observed in twenty-one years." Alec sits down on the edge of the bed, which puts him lower than the chair. "Eat it, and then we shall have the conversation, and I should like to say before we start that my position has not changed and that I am very glad to see you, and that those two statements are both true and are going to be uncomfortable for both of us for about three hours."

"Noted."

So he eats it. It is very good fish.

---

"Right," says Alec. "Go on, then."

"Your letter is correct."

Alec's head comes up.

"Say that again."

"Your letter is correct, and I've had a week with it, and it's the best piece of reasoning anybody's sent me in ten years, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise because you'd know inside a line." Reggie puts the plate down. "Twelve days a year for twenty years is not an arrangement. You've carried that on your own since last July and you carried it well enough that I did not see it, which I shall be thinking about for some time. And ending a thing in one piece is better than letting it come apart in instalments. All of that is right."

"Then—"

"And I am not going to accept it, and I'm going to tell you why, and the why is going to take about four minutes and it isn't an argument."

---

Alec Spilsbury sits on the edge of his own bed with his hands on his knees.

"Go on."

"You have made a decision about an arrangement that has two people in it," says Reggie, "having consulted one of them."

"I have consulted the only one of them who has the relevant facts."

"You have consulted the only one of them who has *your* facts." He leans forward. "You have not got mine. You have never had mine, because I have never given them to you, because there has never been a piece of paper in the world that entitled me to and I am apparently the sort of man who waits to be issued with one." He hears his own voice do something and lets it. "So I am going to give you mine now, out loud, in a room, and then you may do your arithmetic again with the whole of the data in it, and if it still comes out the same way I shall go home on Monday and not say another word about it as long as I live."

"That is not fair."

"No," says Reggie Tar. "It isn't. I've had five days to get it not fair and that's the best I could do."

---

He does not do it well.

He has had the whole of a Tuesday night over the Atlantic and the whole of a Wednesday in London and a Thursday on two aeroplanes to compose it and he has composed about four versions of it and he abandons all of them in the first ten seconds, and what comes out instead is not composed at all.

That the arrangement has twelve days in it because twelve days is what Alec Spilsbury's leave is, and that nobody has ever once asked what Reggie Tar's leave is, and that he has not taken any since 1958 and did not know the figure until Tuesday at noon.

That this is not the reason, and he is going to give the reason now, and it is going to take him a moment to get it out in the right order because he has never said it out loud to anybody, including himself, in nine years.

That in 1958 Hester Bevan invented a form. A qualified person not being a member of the staff, applying to consult deposited material. That she did it in one afternoon and never explained why and has kept the file ever since, and that what that form is *for* is to make it possible for Alec Spilsbury to be on that island for twelve days a year and for there to be a document at the Bay saying, in the Institute's own hand, exactly why he is there.

That the form runs one way, because the material is at the Bay, and that this has been perfectly convenient for nine years and nobody has had to look at it.

And that for Reggie Tar to go the other way, somebody has to write the first piece of paper in twenty-one years with both their names on it as the reason for a journey. Application for leave: purpose, Norway. Fourteen days in February, and then fourteen more the February after that, and the February after that, in a cabinet, in order, in a file that the Secretary keeps and the Colonel signs and an inspector may call for.

That there is no scandal in it and there does not need to be. That a pattern in a file is not a scandal, it is simply a pattern in a file, and that there are men whose whole professional function is to notice patterns in files, and that he sat in a magistrate's room in 1956 and watched what a filed document does to a man when somebody decides to pick it up.

That he has known this since 1958.

That he has let it decide every single year since, without once putting it to the other party, and has told himself the whole time that the thing stopping him was that he had no standing.

That he is fifty-two, and has been alone in a hut at the top of a row for fifty weeks of the year for nine years, and has called it being busy.

And then — and this is the part he will not be able to account for afterwards, because he did not decide to say it — that he does not want to be understanding about it. That he has been understanding about everything for twenty-one years and he has been good at it and it has been a kind of cowardice with excellent manners, and that he is not going to be understanding about this, and that if Alec Spilsbury wishes to discontinue the arrangement he will have to do it to a man who is standing in the room objecting, and not to a man four thousand miles away writing back *of course, and next year then*.

---

The light out of the window does not change at all, and that is the part that stays: that there is no dusk to mark any of it, and that at one point a boat goes past the end of the yard with somebody singing in it.

Alec Spilsbury has not moved.

"Say the part about the form."

"Which part."

"All of it," says Alec. "Say it again from Mrs Bevan."

So he says it again, more slowly, and it comes out better the second time, and at the end of it Alec Spilsbury puts one hand flat on the bed beside him, the way a man does when the floor has moved.

"I did not have that."

"No."

"I have run this sum nine hundred times. I have run it every Sunday on four miles of that shore since the July of last year." His voice is perfectly level and is not right at all. "I have run it with twelve days on one side and twenty years on the other and it comes out the same way every time, and I have been quite certain of it, and what I have had on your side of it for nine years is *a man who is busy.*"

"I know."

"You have let me hold that."

"Yes."

"You have let me hold it since 1958 and you have been four thousand miles away being reticent about it, and I have spent nine days composing a letter on the strength of it—"

"Yes," says Reggie Tar. "That's why I'm in your room."

---

Alec gets up. He walks to the window and back, once, which is not a thing he does.

"Very well. The objection is sound. I am not going to pretend to you that it is not, because I sat in that magistrate's room as well and I know precisely what a document does when somebody picks it up, and I have the distinction of being the document." He stops in the middle of the floor. "And it is also the most extraordinarily stupid thing I have ever heard you say."

"Steady."

"I shall not be steady. You have described the difficulty exactly and then drawn the wrong conclusion from it for nine years." Alec has his hands behind his back. "The difficulty is not that there would be a document. There is always going to be a document; that is what an institution is. The difficulty is that the document would be *false*."

Reggie opens his mouth and shuts it again.

"In 1956 I was a man with no employment and no standing and one reason to be on that island, and the reason was not a professional one, and I was aware that if I came on it I should be exactly what everybody at that table already said I was." Alec is speaking rather fast now. "So I wrote four hundred and eleven pages, over six years, on a subject nobody had asked me for, in order that there should exist a true professional reason for me to be permitted up your jetty. I did not do that out of modesty. I did it because a true reason in a file is a wall and a false one is a door."

"Alec—"

"And it did not occur to you," says Alec Spilsbury, "in nine years, that the same instrument runs in both directions."

---

Reggie Tar sits in a wooden chair in Norway at eleven o'clock at night in full daylight.

"No," he says. "It did not."

"No."

"I looked at the file and I saw a trap and I never once looked at it as a thing that could be built." He has both hands flat on his knees. "That is the single most humiliating sentence I have ever said out loud and I should like it entered."

"It is entered." Alec sits down on the edge of the bed. "Now. What is your Institute about to spend money on?"

"What?"

"You heard me. You are the Director of Research and it is June and the estimates go to the board in the autumn. What is the largest item?"

"The second vessel."

"How much of the endowment?"

"About two thirds of the new money. Sixty-one thousand."

"Hull and gear?"

"Not settled. That's the January business, we've had three quotations and they're all—"

And then Reggie Tar stops talking.

---

"Say it," says Alec Spilsbury.

"You've got four of them in that sound."

"There are nine yards between here and Kristiansund and four of them have built exactly the vessel you are describing in the last six years, and the state station at Ålesund has been writing gear specifications for those yards since 1951, and in February the yards are not busy and neither am I." He is perfectly calm again. "A director who commits sixty-one thousand pounds of endowment to a survey hull without going to look at the four best examples of it in the world is not a careful man. He is a man who will be asked a question about it by the board, in writing, and will not have an answer."

"They'd minute it approvingly."

"They will minute it approvingly, and Mrs Bevan will enter it, and it will be entirely true."

---

Alec gets up and goes to the window and stands with his back to the room, and there is a fire on the point and a boat going past and it is twenty to eleven at night and full daylight, and he stands there for rather a long time.

"I am going to say a thing and I should be obliged if you would not be kind to me for about two minutes."

"I'm not going to be kind to you at all. I've come four thousand miles specifically not to be."

"Very well." Alec does not turn round. "I have not been able to do the twelve days since the July of 1966. That is the sufficient reason and you have had it now and I would rather have had my hand off. And the whole of my objection to telling you was that you would resign inside four seconds and the island would be somebody else's by Michaelmas, and I was struck off a staff in 1956 for putting one animal in front of the column and I am not going to be the cause of that a second time in one life."

"I know."

"You knew that?"

"Alec, I've read your handwriting for twenty-one years," says Reggie. "I have known the shape of it since about the third line of your letter. I did not know the front of it, which is that you have been alone with it since last July. That's the part I've come about."

---

"You are not going to resign."

"No."

"You say that very quickly."

"I say it quickly because I worked it out at Gander at four in the morning and I've had two days with it." Reggie is still in the chair. "If I resign, you will have me and no work, and you told me in 1956 what that produces and I watched it produce it. I'm not doing that to you to make myself feel better in a fortnight."

Alec turns round at last.

"That is the first entirely sound thing either of us has said tonight."

"Don't sound so surprised."

"I am extremely surprised. I have been the only competent person in this discussion for nine days and it has been exhausting," says Alec Spilsbury, and it is not quite a joke and it very nearly is, and Reggie Tar laughs once, badly, with his hands on his knees, and then cannot stop for about ten seconds, and Alec stands at the window and watches him do it with an expression that has not been on his face since the July of 1966.

---

## What there is, and what there is not

They do it properly, because they are what they are, and it takes until about two in the morning, and at no point does it get dark.

*What there is not.* There is no post. Sinclair is two years into the release directorship, she is good, she is not going anywhere, and Alec would not take it if she were: it is a field post wanting a person of forty, and he will be fifty-eight in the new year, and if Reggie Tar appoints his own man to a field directorship at fifty-eight then every single thing said about the Martin in 1956 becomes true again inside a fortnight.

Reggie argues for about twenty minutes and then stops, because he is wrong.

There is no monograph either, not yet. There is the 1956 material and there are the Vigo returns and there is not enough of it, and there is a prosecution pending against a firm in Bristol which will, if it goes the right way, put twenty years of somebody else's records into a cabinet at the Bay, and that is a thing that may happen in 1969 and may not.

There is Ålesund, which cannot be left. Not this year and not next. There is no second man, there will be a Bergen graduate in the spring if the Ministry keeps its word, and that man will want two seasons before he can be given a survey.

"So there is nothing," says Alec, "for about four years."

"There is nothing *permanent* for about four years."

"That is the same sentence."

"It is not remotely the same sentence and you know it isn't," says Reggie, "because you're the man who spent six years writing an annex nobody asked for so that there would be a reason for you to be permitted up my jetty, and you did not call that nothing."

---

*What there is.*

Twelve days in July, which is Alec's leave and has been since 1958.

And the vessel, which is this year, and which will do for February 1968 on its own and cannot do for February 1969, because a man does not go and look at four hulls twice.

So they build the second one, at half past midnight, on the back of a sheet of station paper, and it takes them about forty minutes because between them they have been drafting instruments for institutions since before the war.

*Scientific liaison between the William Martin Institute for Marine Studies and Statens Havforskningsstasjon, Ålesund.* Reciprocal. Annual. Gear and hull specification, survey method, cold-water release and holding practice. A fortnight in each direction in alternate years, or in the same year if either party finds it convenient, the receiving station to bear subsistence and the sending station the fare. Minuted by both boards. Renewable annually on application.

"They will sign that," says Reggie.

"They will sign it with enthusiasm and they will be right to. It is a good instrument and I should have proposed it in 1961 if I had had any idea it was wanted, and that sentence is going to keep me awake." Alec turns it round. "Every line of it is true. That is the whole of the point and it is the only thing that makes it safe, and if any part of it ever stops being true we shall stop doing it, because a false line in a file is worth nothing to either of us and would be worth a great deal to somebody else."

"Agreed."

"Say it properly."

"If it ever stops being true we stop," says Reggie Tar. "Agreed."

---

Twelve and fourteen. Two blocks, one at each end of the year, five months apart, on both sides.

Alec does the arithmetic out loud, twice, and then a third time very slowly, and then says: "Twenty-six."

"Twenty-six."

"It is not enough."

"It is not remotely enough," says Reggie Tar. "It is a bridge, and it is four years long, and at the end of it there is a thing I am going to build and I do not yet know what it is, and I want you to hold on to the rail."

---

"I should like a date."

"February the fifth. I'll be here on the fifth and I go back on the nineteenth, and Pascual has the station and Hester has the return and the inspection is in June so it doesn't touch it." He puts a hand out flat. "The hulls are real and the estimates are real and the board will minute it in September, and Hester will have that liaison drafted inside a fortnight of my getting off the boat."

"Wire her."

"Alec—"

"*Wire her in the morning,*" says Alec Spilsbury, "because I have had nine days of being the only man in Europe who could see the whole of this and I would like something with a number on it before I go to bed," and then he sits down on the edge of the bed rather suddenly and puts his hand over his eyes.

---

"Take a minute."

"I do not require—"

"Take the minute. It's two in the morning, it's broad daylight, I've been up for three days and I have no idea what time it is anywhere." Reggie gets up out of the chair and crosses the room and sits down beside him on the bed, which he has not done since the twentieth of July 1966. "Take the minute."

It is rather more than a minute.

At the end of it Alec says, into his hand, "I burned a four-page account of it on the twenty-ninth of May."

"Why?"

"Because it was true and because you would have been on an aeroplane within a week of reading it."

"Yes," says Reggie. "I would."

"I am aware of that now," says Alec Spilsbury. "It is quite a lot to take in."

---

## The twenty-fourth of June

There is no lamp, because there is no use for one, and the whole business happens at one and two and three in the morning in the flat white light off the sound with the window open and somebody's fire still going on the point.

"I keep waiting for it to go dark."

"It will not. Not until August."

"How do you sleep?"

"Badly, and with a cloth over my face, and I have not once minded it until this precise moment," says Alec, "when it occurs to me that you can see all of it, and I am fifty-seven."

Reggie props himself up on one elbow and takes rather a long look, on purpose, for about eight seconds.

"Yes," he says. "I can."

"That is not a comfort."

"It wasn't meant as one."

---

"You flinched."

"I did not flinch. You were cold."

"I'm not cold, it's June." Reggie has one hand flat on his chest and has not moved it. "Eleven months, Alec. It's been eleven months and you flinched when I put a hand on you and I'm not going to pretend I didn't see it."

Alec looks at the ceiling for a moment.

"Very well. Since the twentieth of July there has been a dentist in Bergen and a man at a conference who wished to shake hands." He says it perfectly evenly. "It is not a complaint and I should be glad if you would not go careful on the strength of it, because the careful is what has done all of the damage so far this year, on both sides, and I have had enough of it to last me."

Reggie takes his hand off.

"Then tell me what you want."

"That is not—"

"It's exactly how we do it. We've done it that way since 1947, I've asked you every single time, and you have never once let me off it." He puts the hand back, flat, in the middle of him. "Tell me what you want."

---

There is a pause of perhaps four seconds.

"I want my wrists held," says Alec Spilsbury. "One in each hand, up, the way you do it. I want you to be extremely slow about the rest of it, because I have spent nine days getting used to the idea that this was finished and I should like some evidence."

"Right."

"And I should like you to keep talking. It does not matter about what."

"That one's new."

"It is not new. It has been the case since about 1959 and I have never asked for it, and I find that I have stopped being able to see the point of not asking for things." He turns his head. "That appears to be what this week has done to me."

"Ask, then."

"I have asked."

"Ask again," says Reggie, "and look at me."

"Keep talking," says Alec Spilsbury, and looks at him, and his voice goes on the second word, and he says it anyway.

---

So he takes the wrists, one in each hand, up, against the iron of a bed in Norway, and feels the whole of him go — not brace, not resist, simply *go*, in about a second and a half, the way it has since the March of 1947 — and holds them there, and says his name once, and then goes on talking, quietly, for a long time.

He talks about the fifth of February. He says the date perhaps nine times over the course of the next hour, in among everything else, without making anything of it, and each time he says it he can feel it land somewhere under his hands. He talks about the column in Hester's book and what her face did when he signed it, and about Pascual on the corner of the desk at three in the morning saying *then go and say that*, and about Solveig on the step saying *he is very tidy*, and Alec says "oh" very quietly at that one and does not say anything else for a while.

He gets his mouth on the place under the jaw where the pulse is and stays there and feels it going like something running, and says the true thing into the skin at four inches, because four inches is the only distance at which either of them has ever been able to manage it.

"I should have written it in 1963."

"You did not have it in 1963."

"I should have written the leave column in 1963. It was there. It was in the book."

"So was I," says Alec, with his wrists up and his eyes shut, "and I would have said no, and I would have been wrong, and I should like it noted that I would have been wrong for four years."

"Noted."

"Now stop talking about 1963."

---

He is very slow about it, as instructed, and does not hurry any part of it, and twice stops and asks, because that is the arrangement and has been for twenty years, and both times is told to go on in a voice that is losing its grip by degrees. He takes the shoulder with the old strain in it and works it under his thumb until it gives. He takes the collarbone and the hollow beneath it and the ribs on the left where a man of fifty-seven is thinner than he was in 1946, and the inside of one wrist, on the veins, for about four seconds, and that is when Alec Spilsbury says his name twice in a row, which he does not do.

And at some point, in the white light, with the window open, he says:

"I should like to be in the same place as you."

Reggie stops.

"Say that once more."

"I withdraw it. It is not a reasonable thing to—"

"*Alec.*"

"I should like to be in the same place as you," says Alec Spilsbury. "I have wanted it since the November of 1946. I have not said it once in twenty-one years and I did not intend to say it tonight and I find I cannot take it back, and I have no proposal to attach to it and no post and no plan, and I am fifty-seven years of age, and it is a quarter to three in the morning and broad daylight."

Reggie puts his forehead against his.

"Four years," he says.

"That is not an answer."

"It's the only honest one in the room, and it's got a number on it, and you asked for one of those about six hours ago." He does not move. "Four years, and twenty-six days a year in the meantime, and I am going to spend the whole of the four years building the thing, and I shall tell you every single step of it by post whether you want it or not."

"I shall want it."

"I know you will," says Reggie Tar. "Now hold still, because I haven't finished and it isn't dark yet."

---

## Coda: the fifth of February, 1968

The Norwegian internal service from Oslo gets into Vigra at ten past two in the afternoon and it is dark by half past three, and there is snow on the flat parts of the town and none on the steep ones, and the coat is the right one this time because it was bought in Ålesund in June by a man who does not make the same mistake twice.

Alec Spilsbury is on the step of the station with no hat on, in about minus four, having come down at twenty past one.

"You are fourteen minutes late."

"The aeroplane was fourteen minutes late."

"I am aware whose fault it is. I am telling you the figure." He takes the case, because it is the heavier of the two things Reggie is carrying and because he has been standing on a step for fifty minutes and requires something to do with his hands. "Solveig has left a pot of something and a note, and the note is four lines long and two of them are about you, and I am not going to tell you what they say."

"Hester sends her regards and says the liaison went through the board on the fourteenth of September without one question being asked, and that the minute runs to four lines, and that she has had it typed twice so that there is a copy in Ålesund as well."

"There is. It came in November and it is on the wall of my office," says Alec Spilsbury. "In a frame. The junior staff think I am very proud of the gear specification."

"Are you?"

"I am extremely proud of the gear specification," he says, and takes the other bag too, and goes up the yard ahead of him in the dark at some speed and does not look round, because it is the fifth of February, which has been written in two books in two countries since the autumn, and which is a date, and which he has had on the other end of it for two hundred and twenty-four days.
