# No Application Received

## The fifth of September, 1964

Gamma goes off her feed on the twenty-fourth of August and comes back on for six days and then does not, and dies in the early part of the night of the fourth of September, in the shaded half, with Chumley alongside her and Pascual sitting on the sill in the dark since half past nine because he had looked at her at six and known.

She is nineteen years on the Institute's books and God knows what before that.

It goes, from the Institute's point of view, about as well as such a thing can go. They have the whole of the autumn. The protocol is Gamma's — it says so at the top of the first page in Pascual's hand, *On the death of Gamma* — and it was written in 1958 for precisely this, and when they take it out of the cabinet on the sixth of September it turns out to want four small corrections and nothing else.

September. October if the water holds. A good tide in the right month with the fish running, and if it goes wrong they put the boards back in and wait a year.

Which is what the protocol always said, and which is now, because nobody has yet written a paragraph nine, simply true.

---

On the morning of the fifth, Reggie Tar sits in the office at twenty past six and drafts a cable to Ålesund.

It takes him about four minutes, which for him is a long time over nineteen words, and when it is done he reads it and it is a perfectly good cable and it says, in substance, *she is dead and we are going to put him out in October and I should like you here.*

And there is no reason for it.

The protocol works. The four corrections are Pascual's and they are right. Jean's soundings are five years better than they were. There is no release problem that needs a man who has read the Norwegian literature, because the Norwegian literature is in the cabinet in Alec Spilsbury's own hand, in four hundred and eleven pages of it, indexed, with the relevant sections marked in the margin in 1958 by the author, against exactly this week.

He has made himself unnecessary. He did it deliberately over six years and it was the best professional thing he ever did for this Institute.

Reggie reads the cable once more.

Then he folds it in half and puts it in the left-hand drawer under the tide tables, because a cable that says *there is no reason for you to come and I should like you to come* is not a cable, it is something else, and he has no form for it and no standing to send it and Hester would have to enter it in the book.

He writes a letter instead, that evening, four pages, and it is a very good letter.

---

## The eleventh of October, 1964

They take the boards out at twenty past six on a falling tide with the fish running and the water at its best temperature of the year, in the month the protocol specifies, having had five weeks to pick the day.

Chumley goes out at four minutes past seven and is back at the sill by half past eight and out again by nine.

John Weald exposes nine plates and is not asked to stop.

It is, from first to last, the best week of Reggie Tar's professional life. Nobody is in any doubt about it at the time, which is unusual. Hendy — who is twenty-four now and has a certificate and a small salary and a fiancée at the Bay — stands on that sill with the stopwatch he was given in 1962 and cries for the second time in his career, and Codrington, who is a graduate student at Cambridge and came out on his own money for a fortnight to see it, puts an arm round him.

Pascual gets in the water on the fourteenth and stays in for two hours and comes out with nothing to report except the time.

Reggie writes nine pages to Ålesund on the sixteenth.

The reply is four pages of the best notes anybody has ever sent him, with three questions in the margin that change the following year's schedule, and at the bottom, below the signature:

*I am very glad. I should have liked to see it. A.S.*

Reggie puts it in the file and does not take the cable out of the drawer, because it is October, and the thing is done, and there is no reason now at all.

---

## July 1965; July 1966

The fortnight in 1965 is the best of the six. He is here for the first full season of the release data and there is more work in it than either of them can get through, and they get through most of it, and there is one afternoon at the north end with the tide out and nothing to do for two hours which Alec Spilsbury will still have, intact, in every particular, in 1971.

The fortnight in 1966 is also good.

It is good in the way a thing is good when a man has decided in advance that it is going to be.

He is fifty-six. He is here from the ninth of July to the twentieth. The light in the window of the hut at the top of the row goes out at about half past ten every night, and Pascual Pobil, who has a wife at the Bay and two small children and a practice and no reason on earth to be looking, looks at it from the path on his way down at eleven on the eleventh of July 1966 and thinks *good*, and goes home.

On the eighteenth, which is day ten, Alec sits on the end of the bed at six in the morning with his boots in his hand and does an arithmetic he has done before.

Six fortnights. Seventy-two days.

Against six years.

He does not write it down, because he has struck that observation out nine times since the fourteenth of July 1962 and a man who keeps striking out the same observation is not doing science, he is keeping house; and because the correct response to an unsound arrangement is to say so to the other party; and because he knows exactly what the other party would do inside four seconds of being told, and that the other party has built something on this island that there is nothing else like in the world, and that it would be gone by Michaelmas.

So he puts his boots on and goes down to the seven o'clock, and the day is a good one, and so are the two after it.

He goes on the twentieth. On the jetty Reggie says, "Next July, then," and Alec says, "Next July," and that is the last time either of them says it.

---

## The ninth of February, 1967

Ole Rørstad, who has been the second man at the Ålesund station for nine years and who takes the winter surveys and who is fifty-one, has a coronary on the eighth of February in the yard and dies on the ninth in hospital in the town.

He was a good man and Alec Spilsbury knew him by name and by nine years of survey returns and liked him.

There is nobody else. The station has a director, a second man, four juniors and a woman in the office called Solveig who writes his messages down twice. The summer programme is the survey; the survey is the station's whole justification to the Ministry; and the Ministry is not going to find him a second man before September, and probably not before the spring after.

He works it out on the tenth and it is not difficult.

He cannot be out of that station for a fortnight in July 1967.

---

## The nineteenth of April, 1967

The form is a single foolscap sheet. Application to consult deposited material of the Institute by a qualified person not being a member of the staff. He has filled one in every year since 1958 and he could do it in the dark.

He fills it in on the nineteenth of April at the desk in his rooms with the good light on it, and it takes eleven minutes, and he signs it and dates it and puts it in the envelope and writes the address.

And then he does not post it.

---

The thing worth saying about this, because Alec Spilsbury will spend four years saying it to himself, is that the reason is not false.

He is the director of a state fisheries station which has lost its second man and is carrying a survey obligation to a ministry that has already asked one question about it in writing. If he took a fortnight's leave in July 1967 and anything at all went wrong with the survey, he would deserve everything that followed and so would the station, and there are nine men whose employment is not a matter of his private arrangements.

That is an excellent reason and it is true and it would be true in front of any panel in Europe.

What is also true, and what he does not look at directly for about four years, is this: in July 1963 the ministry inspection fell in the same fortnight and he moved it. In July 1964 there was a chartering dispute over the survey vessel and he settled it in nine days by being extremely unpleasant to a man in Trondheim, and went. In July 1965 he had a septic hand and went.

In April 1967 he has an excellent reason and does not go looking for the way round it.

That is the whole difference and it is not visible from outside and it will not be visible from outside for four years.

---

He writes the letter instead. It takes him longer than the form.

*I am very sorry to have to tell you that I shall not be able to come this year. Rørstad died in February and there is no second man, and the survey cannot be left, and I am not able in conscience to be four thousand miles off in the middle of it. I hope to be able to come in 1968.*

*I have the fourth section of the growth material ready and I propose to send it by post in the autumn, which will answer the question you put in January, and I should like Pobil's view on the weights before I commit to a figure.*

*Please give my regards to Mrs Bevan, and to Pobil, and tell the boy Hendy that he was right about the tide and I was wrong.*

*A.S.*

He reads it over and there is nothing in it that is not accurate.

He posts that one.

The form stays on the desk for two days and then goes into the drawer on the left, under the offprints, because he is not a man who throws away a completed document.

---

## The eleventh of May, 1967

Hester Bevan brings the post up at ten and puts it on the long table and does not go away, which she does perhaps four times a year and which is how Reggie knows before he has the envelope open.

He reads it twice.

"Rørstad's dead."

"I am very sorry to hear it."

"February." He puts it down. "He's not coming."

"No," says Hester.

---

Now. This is the part.

Reggie Tar is fifty-two years old, he is the Director of Research of the William Martin Institute, he has been reading this man across four thousand miles of post for twenty-one years, and he has in his hand a letter which does not contain one word that is not correct.

And there is one sentence in it which is wrong, and he sees it at ten past ten on the eleventh of May, and he sees it clearly, and he can say afterwards to the day when he saw it.

*I am not able in conscience to be four thousand miles off in the middle of it.*

In July 1964 there was a chartering dispute and Alec Spilsbury was extremely unpleasant to a man in Trondheim for nine days and came anyway.

He knows that. He knows it because he was told about the man in Trondheim at some length on a verandah in the July of 1964 with considerable enjoyment on both sides.

So he sees it.

And what he does with it is this.

---

He writes back the same afternoon. It is four pages. It is the best letter of condolence he has ever written and the man it is addressed to will keep it for the rest of his life, and it is entirely about Rørstad for the first two pages, and then it says:

*Of course you cannot come. Nobody could, and I should think much less of you if you tried. Don't give it another thought — we have had six of them and there will be others, and the material will be here, and so shall I.*

*Send the growth sections whenever you like. Pascual says he will do the weights but that he wants the raw counts and not your tidied ones, and I am to tell you that he means it.*

And then two more pages of work, which are very good, and then:

*Next year, then.*

*R.*

---

He is understanding about it.

He is understanding about it in writing, at length, warmly, accurately, on the eleventh of May 1967, and he means every word, and it is the single most destructive thing he does in his life.

Because he had the sentence. He had it at ten past ten. And a man who has the sentence has two things he can do with it, and one of them is to put it in a letter — *you went in 1964 with a chartering dispute on and you were foul to a man in Trondheim about it, so tell me the actual reason* — and that is a thing you may write to a man who is your equal and who you are entitled to be troublesome to.

And Reggie Tar is not sure, on the eleventh of May 1967, that he is entitled.

There is no paper anywhere that says he is. There is a file in a cabinet with nine applications in it from a qualified person not being a member of the staff. There is twenty-one years of correspondence. There is a window on a path. There is nothing whatever with his name and the other man's name on the same line.

So he is understanding, because being understanding is what you are permitted to be about a colleague's leave, and because the alternative is to ask a question he has no standing to ask, and because — and this is the part he will get to in about four years, in a cold room in Norway, and not before — if he asks it and the answer is *the actual reason is that I have had enough*, he will have to hear it.

He seals the letter and gives it to Hester at four.

"Is he all right?" says Hester Bevan.

"He's lost his second man in February and he's carrying a survey on his own."

"That was not my question."

"I know it wasn't," says Reggie, and picks up the estimates, and Hester Bevan looks at the back of his head for rather a long moment and then takes the letter down to the boat.

---

## The thirtieth of June, 1967

The annual return of the Institute is nine pages and Hester Bevan has made it up every year since 1951.

Section four is *Access to Deposited Material*. It has four lines. Applications received. Applications granted. Applications refused. Days of access.

She has entered a figure in the first line every year since 1958 and the figure has been 1 every time, and the figure in the fourth line has been 12 every time, and she has never once had to think about either of them.

She does the return on the thirtieth of June in the office with the shutters half down.

*Applications received: nil.*

*Applications granted: nil.*

*Applications refused: nil.*

*Days of access: nil.*

She takes it up at four for signature. Reggie reads it through the way he reads everything, which is fast and completely, and signs it on page nine, and passes it back.

Neither of them says anything about section four.

Hester Bevan takes it downstairs and puts it in the envelope for the Colonel and then sits at her own desk for a while with her hands flat on the blotter, and thinks about a room over a magistrate's court in 1956 and a thing she worked out in it and has never said to anybody since 1956, and decides — correctly, as it turns out, and she will be sorry about it for four years — that it is not hers.

---

## 1968

The reason in 1968 is better than the reason in 1967.

The Ministry gives Ålesund a second man in the February, a young Bergen graduate called Haugland who is able and who is going to be good and who cannot be left with the summer survey in his first season. Any director in Europe would say so. Alec Spilsbury says so, in April, in a letter, and it is true.

He fills the form in on the nineteenth of April and does not post it.

The drawer on the left has two in it now.

---

Reggie's reply in 1968 is three pages, and is warm, and is understanding, and contains nine lines about Haugland which are shrewd and useful and which Alec acts on.

*Next year, then.*

*R.*

---

## 1969, 1970

The letters do not stop.

This is the thing that anybody looking at the file afterwards will find hardest to believe, and it is on the record, in order, in two hands, in a cabinet: they write to each other every month for four years without a single gap.

Forty-eight letters each way. Work, mostly. The growth sections. The release data, which is now the best long series on the species in existence and is Spilsbury's design and Pobil's hands and Tar's money. The Bristol prosecution in 1969, which they win. Jean Leslie's chair at Southampton. The death of Colonel Bevan in March 1970, which produces the only letter in the whole four years that either of them writes twice.

There is nothing wrong with any of them.

That is precisely what is wrong with them.

---

At the Martin, Reggie Tar is extremely good.

He is better in 1969 than he was in 1964, and he is better in 1970 than he was in 1969. He gets the Institute a second vessel, he gets the release programme on a permanent footing with a post at the head of it — which he advertises, and fills in 1966 with a hard-working woman from Aberystwyth named Dr Sinclair who is still in it — and he doubles the endowment in eighteen months after the Colonel dies, by being, in Hester's phrase, unignorable.

He is also, by about 1970, not very easy to be near.

He is not unkind. He does not shout, he does not sulk, he has no vices anybody can point to, and the day book says he has not missed a seven o'clock in three years. He is simply, quietly, about nine per cent less present than he used to be, and the effect of that on a small institution is not nine per cent.

A first-year student called Amery resigns in the November of 1970 and goes home, and gives as her reason, in writing, that she does not think the Director likes her. She is wrong. He thinks extremely well of her and has said so twice in the estimates. He has not said it to her.

Pascual reads the resignation and goes up the path and does not go in.

---

In Ålesund, Alec Spilsbury is also extremely good.

The station's survey returns for 1968, 1969 and 1970 are the best in the country, and are held up as such at a conference in Copenhagen which he attends and at which he is pleasant to a great many people and remembers none of it.

He is sixty. He lives in two rooms above the office, which are very tidy. Solveig writes his messages down twice. On Sundays he walks out past the yard along the north shore for about four miles and back, and in the summer he does it in the evening and in the winter he does it in the dark with a lamp, and he has done it every Sunday for thirteen years.

He keeps the Martin letters in a deed box, in order, in their envelopes.

He fills the form in on the nineteenth of April, 1969, and on the nineteenth of April, 1970.

By the spring of 1970 the drawer on the left has four in it, and he has stopped pretending to himself that any of them is going to be posted, and he goes on filling them in, because the alternative is to be a man who does not fill it in, and he is not going to be that yet.

---

## The fourth of January, 1971

Pascual Pobil comes up the path at a quarter to seven in the evening with a dead lamp in his hand because he has come straight from the north pen and has not thought about it, and goes into the office without knocking, which he does about twice a year.

"Sinclair's leaving."

"I know. She's told me. Aberdeen have offered her a readership and she should take it." Reggie does not look up. "I'll have the post advertised by the end of the month. Have you got a view on the specification?"

"My view on the specification," says Pascual.

"You're the veterinary officer and the animal's yours. Of course I want your view on the specification."

"*Ay, Dios.*"

Reggie puts the pen down.

"Say it, then."

---

"Four years," says Pascual.

"Four years of what?"

"Don't." He puts the lamp on the corner of the desk, hard. "Don't do that to me, hombre, I've known you eighteen years. Four years since he was on this island. The ninth of July 1966 to the twentieth. I can give you the tides."

"He's got a station to run and no second man, and then a second man who needed bringing on, and then—"

"I know what he's got. I've read the letters, you read them out at breakfast, they're *beautiful*." Pascual is not shouting yet, and that is worse. "I want to tell you what I've got."

"Pascual—"

"The window."

Reggie does not say anything.

"I've been looking at that window since 1958. I didn't ask to, I didn't want to, I've got a wife and two children at the Bay and I don't need the extra work." He has both hands on the desk now. "Nine months of the year there's a light in it at one in the morning. The twelve days he was here it went out at half past ten. Every night. Nine years I had that, twice a night, and I didn't write it down and I didn't say it, because it isn't mine."

"It isn't yours."

"It's been on every night since the twentieth of July 1966," says Pascual Pobil, "and that's four and a half years, and I'm saying it."

---

Reggie Tar sits at his own desk and looks at a dead lamp.

"He wrote in April and said he couldn't come," he says, "and he was right, and I wrote back and said so."

"I know you did."

"There wasn't anything else to write."

"There was one other thing you could have written and you know exactly what it is, because you're the cleverest man in this hemisphere and you worked it out inside about ten minutes." Pascual straightens up. "You've had four years to write it."

"And what would I have put, Pascual?" It comes out louder than he intends, which does not happen. "There is no — I have no *standing* in it. He is a qualified person and there is a form and he did not send it. What do I put at the top? What do I sign it as?"

"Sign it *Reggie*."

The office is entirely quiet.

"That's it," says Pascual. "That's all of it. That's the whole of what I came up here to say and I've said it badly and I'm not sorry."

---

"You had a girl resign in November," he says, from the door, "because she thought you didn't like her. You think extremely well of her. It's in the estimates, I've seen it. You never told her."

"That's a separate—"

"It's not a separate matter and you've been telling yourself it is since about 1969." He picks the lamp up. "You're not unhappy. I'd have come up here three years ago if you were unhappy. You're *fine*. You're the best director in the Caribbean and you'll be the best one for another fifteen years and everybody on this island respects you and about four of us used to like you."

He opens the door.

"Get on a boat," says Pascual Pobil.

---

## The ninth of February, 1971

Ålesund in February is dark by half past three and there is snow on the flat parts of the town and none on the steep ones. Reggie Tar comes into it at twenty past four on a bus from the aerodrome at Vigra, with one case, in a coat bought in London two days ago that is not adequate, having been travelling for fifty-three hours.

He has told nobody he is coming. He has told Hester where he is going, which is a different thing, and Hester said, "Yes," and then, after a moment, "It is about time," and turned round and got the sailing times out of the drawer where she has kept them, up to date, since 1967.

The station is a long low building at the end of the yard with a light in the office window.

Solveig is putting her coat on. She looks at him for a moment, and at the case, and then does something with her face that he will think about for a long time afterwards, because it is the face of a woman who has been writing a man's messages down twice for thirteen years.

"He is upstairs," she says, in English. "Go up."

---

## The rooms

Two rooms. Very tidy. A stove, a good lamp, a desk under the window, a shelf of offprints with the spines squared, a bed made the way a man makes a bed when nobody is ever going to see it.

Alec Spilsbury is at the desk in his shirtsleeves with a pen in his hand, and he turns round, and he goes absolutely white, and he stands up so fast the chair goes over.

"What has happened."

"Nothing's happened."

"Pobil. Is it Pobil—"

"Nothing has happened, Alec. Nobody's dead. Nobody's ill. The animal's fine, he was at the sill on the second." Reggie has his hands up. "I'm sorry. I should have wired. I didn't wire because I couldn't think what to put."

Alec stands in the middle of his own room with the chair on its back behind him.

"Then why are you here?"

"Because Pascual told me to get on a boat," says Reggie, "and he was right, and it took me five weeks to do it, and I have been on three aeroplanes and a bus and I have not eaten since Oslo. May I sit down?"

---

He picks the chair up himself and sits on it, because Alec does not move for about ten seconds.

Then Alec says, "There is soup," and goes and does that, and is extremely competent about it, and neither of them says one word for six or seven minutes while it happens; and Reggie Tar sits in a wooden chair in Norway and looks at a room.

At the deed box on the shelf with the letters in it. At the boots by the stove. At the four miles of dark shore outside the window that a man has walked every Sunday for thirteen years.

At a desk with nothing personal on it at all.

---

"Eat that," says Alec.

"Thank you."

"You are fifty-six years old and you have crossed the Atlantic in February in a coat that is an insult to the concept. Eat it and then we shall discuss whatever it is."

So he eats it. It is very good soup.

"Now," says Alec Spilsbury.

---

"In April of 1967 you wrote and told me you could not come, and you gave Rørstad, and it was true and it was sufficient." Reggie has the bowl in both hands for the warmth. "And there was one sentence in it that was wrong, and I had it by about ten past ten on the morning it came."

Alec does not move.

"In July of 1964 there was a chartering dispute over the survey vessel and you spent nine days being appalling to a man in Trondheim and you came anyway. You told me about it on my own verandah and you enjoyed it and so did I." He puts the bowl down. "A man who does that does not stay away because there is no second man. He finds the way round it, because he has found the way round it every year since 1958."

"That is correct."

"I know it's correct. I knew it on the eleventh of May 1967." Reggie looks at him. "And I wrote back four pages and said *of course you can't come* and *don't give it another thought* and *next year, then*."

"You did."

"Why do you think I did that?"

---

Alec Spilsbury sits down on the edge of his own bed, which puts him lower than the chair, which he has not done in twenty-five years.

"Because it was the correct thing to write."

"No."

"It was the correct thing to write. I have had it on the shelf for four years and I have read it a very great number of times and there is nothing in it that a decent man would not have—"

"*Because I did not think I was entitled to ask.*"

---

The stove does what a stove does.

"That is the whole of it," says Reggie, and his voice has gone somewhere he cannot get it back from and he does not attempt to. "There is no paper anywhere in this world with your name and mine on the same line. There is a file with nine applications in it and twenty-four years of letters about fish. I have had no standing to ask you a single question about your own life since 1946 and I have been extremely careful about it, and I was careful about it on the eleventh of May 1967 at ten past ten in the morning, and I have been careful for four years, and at some point being careful stopped being decency and started being the thing I did instead."

"Reggie."

"I would have gone on being careful. That is what I came here to tell you." He is holding the arms of the chair. "Not indefinitely. About four more years, I should think, and then it would have been ten, and then we would have been two old men writing to each other about growth curves, and both of us would have been able to account for every single year of it."

---

Alec Spilsbury gets up.

He goes to the desk and opens the drawer on the left and takes out the offprints and puts them on the top, and then takes out four envelopes and puts them on the desk in a row, squared, in order, the way he squares everything.

Then he stands back so that the lamp is on them.

1967. 1968. 1969. 1970. Each one filled in, signed, dated the nineteenth of April, addressed in his own hand to Mrs H. Bevan, Secretary, the William Martin Institute for Marine Studies.

Unposted. Unstamped.

"There is a fifth," says Alec. "It is not April."

---

Reggie Tar looks at four envelopes on a desk in Norway for a considerable time.

"Every year."

"Every year on the nineteenth, at that desk, in about eleven minutes." Alec has his hands behind his back. "I should like to give you an account of it and I am not certain I can make it come out in order, so I shall give you the end first. I stopped being able to do the twelve days some time in 1966 and I did not tell you, because I knew what you would do inside four seconds of being told and I was not going to be the reason that island stopped. And then Rørstad died and handed me a reason that would stand up anywhere, and I did not look for the way round it, and that is the only dishonest thing I have done in my adult life."

"Alec—"

"I am not finished and if you are kind to me now I shall not get through it."

"All right."

---

"I filled the form in because a man who has stopped filling it in has decided, and I had not decided. I posted the letter and not the form because the letter was true. And in the year after that the reason was better and in the year after that it was better still, and at no point — this is the part I should like you to have — at no point was there a morning on which I could have pointed to the thing and said *that is where it went*, because it did not go anywhere. It was extremely gradual and every individual step in it was correct."

He stops.

"And I have been walking four miles up that shore every Sunday for thirteen years and I could not have told you what I was thinking about for one of them."

---

Reggie gets up out of the chair.

"I'm going to say something you won't like."

"Very probably."

"We did it together." He is standing about three feet away. "That's the bit I've had five weeks with, and I don't think you've had it yet. You didn't do this to me and I didn't do it to you. We did it at each other, four thousand miles apart, in forty-eight letters a year, both of us being absolutely impeccable, and there was not one moment in four years when either of us could have been accused of anything."

"That is accurate."

"It's more than accurate. Alec, look at me. It's what we *built*. We built a thing with no line in it for either of us to stand on and then we both stood correctly off it for four years, and the mechanism performed exactly as designed."

---

Alec Spilsbury looks at the four envelopes on his own desk.

"Yes," he says. "Yes, that is right, and I have not had it, and I should have had it in about 1968."

And then he puts a hand out and holds the edge of the desk, and sits down on the bed again, rather suddenly, and says, in an entirely level voice, "I am afraid I am going to need a minute."

"Take it."

"It is not—"

"Alec." Reggie crosses the three feet and gets down on his heels in front of him and takes both his hands, which he has not been able to do since the twentieth of July 1966. "Take the minute. I've come four thousand miles. I'm not going anywhere at half past six."

---

It is rather more than a minute.

Reggie Tar kneels on the floorboards of a cold room in Ålesund with his hands full of another man's hands and his knees complaining and does not move and does not say anything, and thinks, with complete clarity, that he could have had this in the May of 1967 for the price of one sentence in a letter, and that he did not write it because there was no form for it, and that he is fifty-six and he has just this minute stopped waiting for somebody to issue him one.

"Right," says Alec, eventually, in a voice that is nearly his own.

"Right."

"I should like the record to show that I was better about this in 1963."

"You weren't in 1963."

"I was not in anything in 1963," says Alec Spilsbury. "That is rather the point," and Reggie makes a noise that is half of a laugh and puts his forehead down on their four hands and stays there.

---

## That night

The lamp is on, because the lamp has been on since October 1958, and neither of them mentions it, and neither of them has been at all certain, at any point in the last hour, that it was going to be on tonight.

"You are being careful again."

"I'm being fifty-six. It's different." Reggie has one hand flat on his chest and has not moved it in about two minutes. "And it's been four and a half years and you flinched when I took my coat off, and I'm not going to pretend I didn't see it."

"I did not flinch. I was startled."

"You were startled."

"I have not been touched by another human being since the twentieth of July 1966," says Alec Spilsbury, conversationally, to the ceiling, "except by a dentist in Bergen in 1969 and by a man at a conference who wished to shake hands, and I was startled. It is not a complaint and I should be glad if you would not go careful on the strength of it, because the careful is what did the damage."

Reggie takes his hand off.

"Then tell me what you want."

"That is not—"

"It's exactly how we do it. It's how we've done it since 1947 and I have asked you every single time and you have never once let me off it." He puts the hand back. "Tell me what you want, Alec."

---

There is a pause of perhaps four seconds.

"I want my wrists held," says Alec Spilsbury, "one in each hand, up, the way you do it, and I want you to be quite slow about the rest of it because I have had four and a half years and I am not going to get this evening twice. And I should like you to keep talking, and it does not matter about what."

"That last one's new."

"It is not new. It has been true since about 1959 and I have never asked for it."

"Then ask."

"I have just asked."

"Say it again," says Reggie, "and look at me while you do it."

"Keep talking," says Alec, and looks at him, and his voice does something on the second word and he says it anyway.

---

So he takes the wrists, one in each hand, up, against the iron of a bed in Norway, and he feels the whole of the man go — not fight it, not brace, just *go*, in about a second and a half, the way it has since the March of 1947 — and he holds them there and says his name once, and then goes on talking, quietly, for a long time.

He talks about the eleventh of October 1964 and the boards coming out and Hendy with the stopwatch. He talks about the light in the window and what Pascual has been doing with it for thirteen years, and Alec says *no* once, very quietly, in the way a man says it when he has been handed something he cannot carry, and Reggie says *yes*, and goes on. He gets his mouth on the place under the jaw where the pulse is and stays there and feels it going like something running, and says the true thing into the skin at four inches, because it is the only distance at which either of them has ever been able to manage it.

"I should have written it in the May of 1967."

"Yes."

"Say what I should have written."

"*You went in 1964 with a chartering dispute on and you were foul to a man in Trondheim about it,*" says Alec Spilsbury, with his wrists up and his eyes shut, "*so tell me the actual reason.*"

"That's it."

"Write it in the morning."

"I'll write it in the morning."

---

He takes the shoulder with the old strain in it and works it with his thumb until it gives, which takes longer than it used to and which gets him a sound that was not approved first. He takes the collarbone and the hollow under it and the ribs on the left where a man of sixty-one is thinner than he was in 1946. He is very slow. He keeps the wrists the whole time. Twice he stops and asks, because that is the arrangement and has been since 1947, and both times he is told, in a voice that is losing its grip on itself by degrees, to go on.

And at some point — Reggie could not put a time on it, and Alec certainly could not — Alec Spilsbury says his name twice in a row, which he does not do, and then says, "I should like to be in the same place as you," and it is not part of the evening at all, it has come out of the wrong drawer entirely, and he hears himself say it.

Reggie stops.

"Say that once more."

"I withdraw it. It is not a reasonable—"

"*Alec.*"

"I should like to be in the same place as you," says Alec Spilsbury. "I have wanted it since the November of 1946 and I have not said it once in twenty-four years and I did not intend to say it now and I am not able to take it back, and I have no proposal to attach to it and no plan and no post and I am sixty-one years of age."

Reggie puts his forehead against his.

"Right," he says. "Thank you. Now we can start."

---

## The tenth of February

They start at about eight in the morning, at the desk, with the four envelopes moved to the shelf and a sheet of the station's paper in front of them, because they are what they are.

It takes all day and most of the next and it is not a nice document.

*What there is not.* There is no post. Sinclair's readership at Aberdeen means the release programme has a vacancy, and Alec Spilsbury will not take it, and says so in the first ten minutes: it is a field post, it wants a person of forty with ten years in front of them, he would be sixty-two on appointment, and if Reggie Tar appoints his own man to a field directorship at sixty-two, every single thing that has been said about the Martin since 1956 becomes true again in a fortnight.

Reggie argues for about forty minutes and then stops, because he is wrong.

*What there is.* Ålesund can be given six months' notice and will manage; Haugland is ready and has been for a year and is owed it. There is a pension, small. There is twenty-four years of unworked material in a cabinet at the Bay: the 1956 blocks, the Vigo returns, the whole D & L archive that came in after the 1969 prosecution, and not one line of the monograph that ought to have been written out of it in 1960. It is six years' work. It is the most important thing nobody at the Institute has time to do. It is not a post, it does not pay, and it would make Alec Spilsbury an old man in a room with somebody else's boxes.

They look at that for quite a long time.

"It is also," says Alec, "the best piece of work available to me on this earth, and I should like to be honest about the fact that I would take it if it came with a cupboard to sleep in."

"I know you would. That's why I'm not going to offer it to you like that."

---

*What is wanted.* Reggie writes it out, in the end, in five lines, and they are five lines he could not have written in 1967 because he did not then believe he was allowed to want anything in writing.

An instrument. Something with a name on it and a term on it and a date on the far end. Something that has to be applied for, because the man will not take a thing that is given, and because the form is the only structure either of them has ever been able to stand up in. Something the Secretary can enter in section four of the annual return and the Colonel's successor can sign without asking a single question he does not want the answer to.

"You have described a Research Associateship," says Alec.

"We haven't got one."

"Then you have described a thing that Mrs Bevan will have drafted inside a fortnight," says Alec Spilsbury, "and you know that perfectly well, and you have known it since about Oslo."

---

## The fourth of March, 1971

Hester Bevan drafts it in nine days.

*Research Associateship of the Institute. Unsalaried. Tenable for one year from the first of October. Renewable annually on application. Open to any person of standing in the field who undertakes a defined programme of work upon material deposited with the Institute. Associates shall be entitled to the use of the library, the long room, a working space, the four o'clock on Thursdays, and accommodation on the cay at the discretion of the Secretary.*

She puts *renewable annually on application* in herself, and does not explain it, and Reggie Tar reads that line four times and then signs the minute and hands it back without saying anything, and Hester Bevan says, "Quite," and takes it downstairs.

It is advertised in three journals in April. There are two applications. One is not serious.

---

## The nineteenth of April, 1972

The post comes up at ten and Hester puts it on the long table.

"Dr Pobil. You are a member of the panel and you will kindly stop pretending to read the returns."

"I'm reading the returns."

"You are holding them upside down." She has the letter opener. "Dr Tar. You are the Director of Research and you are an interested party."

"Declared," says Reggie.

"It is going in the minute in those words and you will initial it."

"Read it out, Hester."

---

"*Application for a Research Associateship of the Institute,*" says Hester Bevan. "*Applicant: A. Spilsbury, Ph.D. Present appointment: none.*"

She turns the page.

"*Programme of work proposed: the monograph. Nine pages, with the whole of it costed against nothing at all, and a schedule of the boxes in the order he means to take them.*"

"Qualified?" says Pascual Pobil.

Hester Bevan looks at the last page for slightly longer than she needs to. The form is dated the nineteenth of April and it has come by post, with a stamp on it, from Ålesund, which is the fifth time that form has been filled in at that desk and the first time it has been out of that drawer.

"He appears to be qualified," she says.

"Then he may have it," says Pascual, "in the ordinary way," and Codrington, who is a lecturer at Cambridge and is out for a fortnight on his own money as he is every year, says, "Sir — *again?* Doesn't he have to do this every—"

"Every year," says Hester Bevan, and puts the form in the cabinet, and does not lock it.

---

He comes on the twenty-ninth of September, 1972, with two cases and a deed box, and Pascual Pobil is on the jetty, which nobody asked him to be, with his hands in his pockets and an expression on his face like a man with a stone in his boot.

"You're late. Boat was due at three."

"The boat was due at half past."

"*Ay.* Well." Pascual picks up the deed box without being asked, because it is the heaviest and the other man is sixty-two. "Hut at the top of the row. Four o'clock on Thursdays, and you take your turn with the tide gauge like everybody else."

"Understood."

"And Spilsbury—"

Alec stops on the boards.

"I'm not going to say anything about it," says Pascual Pobil, "not this week and not ever, so don't wait for it." He shifts the box onto his shoulder. "But the light in that window goes off at half past ten now, and I sleep like a Christian, and you're welcome."

He goes up the path.

Alec Spilsbury stands on the jetty for a moment with a case in each hand, in the ordinary way, on a Friday, with nothing whatever on the other end of it.
