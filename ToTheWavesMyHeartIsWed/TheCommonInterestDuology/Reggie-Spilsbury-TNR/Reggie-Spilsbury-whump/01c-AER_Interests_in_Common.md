# Interests in Common

## Tuesday, the fifteenth

The secretary of the meeting is a capable young man called Feather who has been on his feet since half past eight and is doing a genuinely excellent job, and at twenty past eleven on the Tuesday morning he takes Reggie Tar by the elbow in the corridor outside the lecture room and says:

"Dr Tar — may I introduce Dr Spilsbury, of Ålesund. I think you'll find you have interests in common."

---

"We have corresponded," says Alec Spilsbury.

"For some years," says Reggie.

"Oh, splendid," says Feather, delighted, and goes off to find somebody else.

---

Twenty-four years, in nine words, to a young man in a good suit who will not think about it again.

---

He looks extremely well. That is the first thing and it is a relief that goes all the way down: sixty years old, greyer, thinner in the face, entirely upright, in the suit he has had since 1958 and a tie Reggie has not seen before and which is therefore new since the twentieth of July 1966.

"That is a very loud tie."

"It was given to me by the office at Christmas," says Alec Spilsbury, "and I have been made to understand that I am to wear it in England, and I have not had the nerve to ask what is being implied."

"Oh, I should ask."

"I am sixty and I have worked out that there are some things a man is better off not having on the record," says Alec, and then, in exactly the same voice: "The Thursday counts have come down since the spring and I do not believe it is the observer."

"Nor do I. Come and have some coffee and I'll show you why not."

---

And that is the Tuesday.

They have the better part of two hours over the coffee and a further hour and a half in the afternoon, and it is, without any question at all, the best professional conversation Reggie Tar has had since 1966. Nobody separates them and nobody would think of it: their names have been on the same papers since 1959, they are a known pair, and two men from marine stations sitting at a table for an afternoon with a sounding chart between them is the least remarkable sight in the building.

He gets four things out of it that change the following year's schedule, one of which he would not have got out of six months of post.

They part at half past five and he goes back to the hotel and has a bath and lies on the bed and thinks, with something that is not far off contentment, that it has gone extraordinarily well, and that Alec was right in 1967, and that this is what the thing was always for.

---

## Wednesday, a quarter to seven

The Society gives the delegates a reception on the Wednesday evening, in a room too warm for the time of year, with a table of drinks at one end and a great many men in dark suits saying things they have said before.

Reggie is at the table with a glass in his hand, waiting for Jean Leslie to finish disembowelling a young man from Plymouth about his standard errors, when a Norwegian of about forty comes up and introduces himself as Berge, from the institute at Bergen, and says some very civil things about the release series.

And then, pleasantly, making conversation, on the assumption that he is talking to a man who knows:

"And Spilsbury — he is better now, after the spring? He looks well. It was a bad business, that."

---

Reggie Tar says, "Much better. Thank you."

"Good. Good. Gallstones are a stupid thing to nearly die of." Berge shakes his head comfortably. "The best part of a fortnight he was in, and they would not operate for four days because of the jaundice, and Solveig telephoned our director twice, and you know he told nobody at the station until he was already in the hospital. He walked down there. In March."

"That would be like him."

"It is very like him," says Berge, and laughs, and says something else about the release series, and moves off.

---

He is holding a glass.

That is the thing he is aware of, principally, for about ninety seconds: that he is holding a glass, in a warm room, in September, and that he has just lied about a man's health to a stranger because he could not think of anything else to do with his face.

March 1970.

In March 1970 Colonel Bevan died on the eighth and Reggie wrote to Ålesund on the tenth, four pages, and did not get an answer for three weeks, and then got one, and then got a second one a fortnight after that which went over the same ground again in different words because the first had not been sufficient and its writer had known it was not.

He has that second letter. It is a very fine letter. It is about the Colonel, and about 1956, and about what the man did with the magistrate's room, and there is one line in it about the Bristol business and nothing whatever else, and it was written by a man who had come out of a hospital ward inside the month.

---

He gets away from the table by ten past seven, and stands in the corridor, and does the arithmetic.

The best part of a fortnight in a hospital in Ålesund. Jaundiced. Not operated on for four days. A woman in an office telephoning Bergen twice because she did not know what else to do, and there being — this is the part — there being nobody at all in that arrangement whose business it was to be telephoned.

He knew that in 1962. He worked it out on a corridor floor at four in the morning with Pascual at thirty-nine and a half, in the other life, the one where he was still permitted to notice such things, and he had a form in his hand for about a week and did nothing with it.

And this year, in March, a man walked himself to a hospital in Norway and did not tell his own station until he was inside it, and wrote twice about the Colonel, and did not mention it, and has not mentioned it since, and will not.

Reggie Tar goes to the desk at half past eleven and asks for the room number.

---

## The number

It takes him two hours to do it, which is to say he goes to the desk at half past eleven and does not ask, and goes up, and comes down at midnight and does not ask, and walks to the Marylebone Road and back, and asks at twenty past one.

The night porter looks it up without the smallest interest and tells him, and says goodnight, and goes back to his paper.

That is the whole of the danger and the whole of the safety of it. One man in a uniform, who has done this four hundred times this month, and a number in a ledger with two names near it, and nobody on earth who will ever read that page.

He stands outside the door for some time.

---

## The room

Alec opens it in his shirtsleeves with his reading spectacles pushed up and a pen in his hand, and his face does one thing and then stops doing it, and what he says is:

"You cannot be here."

"I know."

"There are two dozen men in this building who know both our faces by sight. Meacher has the room at the end of this corridor and he was at the Labs in 1956 and he has never once been civil to me in fourteen years—"

"Alec, I know. Let me in or send me away, but do it in about four seconds, because the corridor is worse than the room."

Alec Spilsbury looks at him, and then along the corridor, and then steps back.

---

The door shuts.

Papers on the bed, in three piles, squared. The reading spectacles. A glass of water. The loud tie over the back of the chair.

"Is somebody dead?"

"Nobody's dead."

"Then I am going to sit down," says Alec, "because you have taken about six years off me in the last fifteen seconds, and I would be obliged if you would say the next thing quickly."

"You were in hospital in March."

---

Nothing happens to his face at all. That is how Reggie knows he has it right.

"Who told you?"

"A man called Berge, from Bergen, at the drinks table at a quarter to seven this evening, making pleasant conversation with a colleague of yours of twenty-four years' standing on the entirely reasonable assumption that he knew." He is still standing. "I said *much better, thank you.* I lied about your health to a stranger in a warm room because I could not think what else to do with my face, and I have been in the corridor downstairs for two hours since."

Alec sits on the edge of the bed with the papers beside him.

"Sit down, Reggie."

"Don't manage me."

"I am not managing you. You are fifty-six and you have been standing in a corridor since seven o'clock."

He sits down.

---

"It was gallstones. It was not interesting."

"You were jaundiced and they would not operate for four days."

"They would not operate for four days because my bilirubin was absurd, which is correct practice, and on the fifth day they did operate and it went perfectly well, and I was out on the twenty-ninth and back at the station on the sixth of April." Alec has his hands on his knees. "There is no part of that which is not entirely ordinary for a man of sixty."

"You walked there."

"It was eight hundred yards and I was not in extremis, I was in discomfort—"

"You walked to a hospital in Norway in March in a state that had a woman in your office ringing Bergen twice, and you did not tell your own staff until you were inside the building." Reggie is looking at the carpet. "And you wrote to me about Colonel Bevan on the — when was it."

"The twenty-fourth of March."

"From where?"

Alec Spilsbury does not answer that.

"From where, Alec."

"From the ward," says Alec. "They are extremely good about letting you have a pen."

---

Reggie puts his face in his hands for about four seconds.

"And the second one? The one that came in April, that went over the whole of it again because you thought the first hadn't done it properly?"

"From the station. On the eighth."

"Two days after you went back."

"Yes."

"And there is not one line in either of them."

"No," says Alec Spilsbury.

"*Why.*"

---

And here is the thing he has come four thousand miles and two corridors for, and it arrives in about fourteen words and is not what he expected at all.

"Because a letter saying *I have been in hospital and it was nothing* is a letter asking to be worried about," says Alec, "and I have no standing to ask that of you."

---

The room is very quiet.

"Say that again."

"I would rather not, it was not well put—"

"Say it again."

"I have no standing to ask you to worry about me," says Alec Spilsbury, slowly, "and I discontinued the arrangement myself, in writing, in 1967, on my own reasoning, and a man who does that does not afterwards write to the other party from a ward in order to be asked how he is. That is not a rule anybody gave me. It is a thing I worked out in about March of 1968 and I have kept to it since and I should like it understood that I do not think it is noble, I think it is simply correct."

---

Reggie Tar sits on the edge of a hotel bed in Marylebone at twenty-five to two in the morning and laughs, once, with absolutely no humour in it whatsoever.

"That's mine."

"I beg your pardon?"

"That's my reasoning. That is word for word my reasoning." He turns round. "In May of 1967 I had your letter in my hand and I knew inside ten minutes that the reason you gave me was not the reason, and I did not write and ask you, and the thing that stopped me was that I did not think I was *entitled* to ask. Not entitled. The word I used to myself was *standing.*"

Alec does not move.

"You had it."

"I had it at ten past ten in the morning on the eleventh of May and I wrote back four pages saying *of course you can't come* and *next year, then*."

"I have that letter."

"I know you have. It's a very good letter." Reggie puts both hands flat on his knees. "We each built the same wall, Alec. Independently. Out of the same brick. And the whole of the arrangement is constructed so that neither of us could ever possibly find out that the other one had."

---

"That is correct," says Alec Spilsbury, after a while, "and it is the single worst piece of design I have ever been party to, and I have been party to some remarkable ones."

"Don't."

"I am not being funny. I am attempting to be accurate and it is coming out sideways because it is two in the morning and you are in my room."

---

## Two o'clock to about four

They do not go to bed.

They both want to, and they both know the other wants to, and it is on the table inside twenty minutes because Alec Spilsbury puts it there himself, in the flattest voice in the building, the way he would put a figure on a chart.

"I want to. You are going to have worked that out, so I would rather say it."

"I'd worked it out."

"And I am not going to, and I do not believe you are either, and I should like to give the reasons so that neither of us spends the next four hours wondering whether the other one is being noble about it."

"Go on, then."

---

"One. This building." He counts it off. "Meacher. Loveday. Twenty-odd men who know both faces and a night porter who has already written a number in a ledger. It is the single most exposed hour of the last fifteen years and we are already in it."

"Agreed."

"Two, and this is the real one." Alec stops counting. "If we do that, I shall have to put you out of this room before six, and I shall spend the whole of it knowing that. And I have had four years and two months, Reggie, and what I have wanted for the last of them is not — "

He stops.

"Say it."

"I want the talking more," says Alec Spilsbury. "I should like that entered and I should like it noted that it is the most revealing sentence I have produced in my adult life."

---

"It's entered," says Reggie.

"You are not going to argue."

"I'm not going to argue because I want the talking more as well, and I have been sitting here for twenty minutes trying to work out how to say so without it sounding like I don't — " He stops too. "Alec, I have four hours and I don't know when I get four hours again. I'm not spending them on the only thing in the world I could get somewhere else."

"That is an appalling sentence."

"It's true, though."

"It is entirely true and it is still appalling," says Alec Spilsbury, and moves the three piles of paper off the bed on to the floor without squaring them, which is the most extraordinary thing that happens all night.

---

They get themselves arranged, which takes a moment and is undignified, and there is a silence of about a minute and a half.

Then Reggie says, "That thing I said."

"You have said a number of things."

"About getting it somewhere else."

"I had assumed that was rhetorical," says Alec, carefully.

"It was rhetorical. It was also not true and I'd rather not leave it lying about in a room with you for four hours." He is looking at the ceiling. "There hasn't been anybody. Since 1946. Not once, not on a trip, not in London, not in twenty-four years."

---

Alec Spilsbury does not say anything at all for some seconds.

"I am not telling you that to be admired for it," says Reggie, "because there's nothing in it. It has not been an act of discipline. There has been nothing whatever to be disciplined about, which is a different thing, and I should hate to be given credit for a virtue I have not had to exercise."

"That is the most Reggie Tar sentence I have ever heard."

"Probably."

"Say the first part again without the disclaimer on it."

"There hasn't been anybody since 1946."

---

"I had assumed," says Alec Spilsbury. "I want to be accurate about this because you have just been accurate with me. I had assumed, and I had deliberately declined to check, and declining to check is not knowing, and I have this moment discovered that I have wanted to know for about twenty years and had not admitted to wanting it."

"And you?"

"Oh — no. Nobody." He says it exactly as he would say a figure. "That one is rather less to my credit, because I have never been in any danger of it. It has not been a temptation resisted, it has been a thing that has never once been available to me, and I have arranged my life since 1957 in such a way that it could not become available, and I did not examine that arrangement at any point between 1958 and last March."

"What happened last March?"

"I was lying on my back in a ward with a tube in me," says Alec Spilsbury, "at three o'clock in the morning, and it occurred to me to ask myself who I should want in the corridor, and the answer took no time at all to arrive and there was nothing whatever I could do with it. I have never been so angry in my life."

Reggie reaches over and takes his hand.

Not a gesture. He simply takes it, the way a man takes a thing off a table, and keeps it.

Neither of them remarks on it.

It is the first deliberate touch of any kind since the twentieth of July 1966, which is four years and two months, and Alec Spilsbury's hand is not perfectly steady for about the next quarter of an hour, and neither of them remarks on that either.

---

So they talk.

They talk from about two until about four and it is not orderly and it is not, for the most part, about the work at all.

The station. Haugland, who is good now and will have Ålesund in five years and ought to. Solveig, who telephoned Bergen twice in March on her own initiative and has never mentioned it to him and whom he has therefore never been able to thank. The two rooms above the office. The four miles up the north shore on Sundays, every Sunday since 1957, in the dark in winter with a lamp, and the fact that he could not tell you what he thinks about on them.

Chumley, who is six years out and is in the third year of the best long series on the species anywhere, and who came to the sill on the second of September and took nothing, because since the middle of June he has mostly not needed to.

Pascual, who has two children and a practice and is fifty-six and got in the water in July.

Hester, who has run that Institute since 1951 and who put *nil* in section four of the annual return on the thirtieth of June 1967 and carried it upstairs for signature and said nothing about it, and has said nothing about it every June since, four times.

And the Amery girl, who is a first-year, and who Reggie thinks extremely well of and has said so in the estimates twice and has not said to her, and who has started coming to the four o'clock with her questions written down in advance because she is frightened of him.

---

"That one I mind about," says Reggie.

"You should."

"Thank you, that's very consoling."

"I am not here to console you, and if you wanted consoling you should have gone to Meacher." Alec has his back against the headboard. "You have become a formidable man, Reggie. I have watched it happen at four thousand miles and I have read it in the letters and it has been going on since about 1968. You are better at that job than you were in 1964 and you are a great deal harder to stand next to, and I should have said so in a letter in 1969 and I did not, because — "

"Because you had no standing."

"Because I had no standing," says Alec Spilsbury. "Yes. Quite."

---

At ten to four, Reggie says:

"I should have written it in 1967."

"You have said that."

"No, I've said I had the sentence. I haven't said the other thing." He is looking at their hands. "The other thing is that in May of 1967 I did not write and ask you the question because I was afraid of the answer. Not of asking. Of the answer. I thought there was a chance that the actual reason was *I have had enough of this*, and if you had written that down I should have had to have it, in your handwriting, in a file, for the rest of my life."

Alec says nothing for a moment.

"That is worth the whole of the rest of tonight."

"Is it."

"It is the only thing you have said since seven o'clock that I did not have." He turns his hand over. "And I am going to give you a hard answer to it, because you have had three years to say that sentence, and you have chosen to say it at ten minutes to four in the morning in a hotel containing Meacher, Loveday and the greater part of the council of the Society."

Reggie laughs.

"That's fair."

"It is entirely fair and it is also a deflection and we will both pretend it was not," says Alec Spilsbury, "because it is ten to four and I have not got another one."

---

## Six o'clock

At two minutes to six he has his jacket on and his hand on the door handle, and then he takes his hand off the door handle and turns round and puts both arms round him.

---

"You cannot do that."

"Yes, I can."

"Reggie—" But his arms have come up anyway, which is the whole of the argument and neither of them says so. "I have to sit in a room at ten o'clock and listen to you give a paper. Four rows back. With Meacher in the third row. I have to do that with a face on, for fifty minutes, and if you do this now I am not going to be able to."

"You'll manage it. You managed forty minutes of a flow meter."

"That was before."

"It's a *hug*, Alec." He does not let go. "Men do this. I have done it to Pascual Pobil twice."

"That does not count."

"Why does that not count?"

"Because one of them was at his wedding," says Alec Spilsbury, into his shoulder, in the flattest voice available, "and the other was when he had nearly died."

"You nearly died in March."

"I did not nearly die in March, I had an inflamed gall bladder—"

"You were jaundiced in a ward in Norway for the best part of a fortnight, and the only person who telephoned anybody about it was a woman in your office who has never mentioned it to you since." Reggie has his hand flat between the man's shoulder blades. "So there are two of them, and I am counting it, and you may take it up with me by post."

"I shall."

"I know you will."

---

It goes on for rather a long time by the standards of the thing — long enough that the light in the window changes, and a van goes past in the street below, and somebody four doors down runs a bath — and it is nothing at all like long enough, and at no point during any of it does either of them remark on the fact that Alec Spilsbury has both hands full of the back of Reggie Tar's jacket and is not letting go of it.

"I have to go."

"Yes."

"Alec."

"Yes. In a moment," says Alec Spilsbury, and it is a moment and a half.

---

He leaves at eight minutes past six, which is exactly as bad an hour as any other and rather better than most, and he gets to the lift and the lift is not there and so he takes the stairs.

Jean Leslie is on the second-floor landing in a dressing gown with an empty cup, going down to find somebody who can produce tea at six in the morning, and she has held a chair at Southampton since 1968 and was at the Labs from 1948 to 1955 and has known Reggie Tar for twenty-two years.

"Good morning, Reggie."

"Morning, Jean."

"They have nothing at all before half past," says Jean Leslie, "and the man on the desk will tell you otherwise and he is lying," and goes past him up the stairs.

She says nothing about it to anybody for a year.

---

## Thursday morning

He gives the release series at ten o'clock to a full room, and it is the best thing he has ever done on a platform, and he has had two hours' sleep.

The credit is in the fourth sentence, where it belongs, because that is where the design belongs and he has never put it anywhere else:

"The release protocol is Spilsbury's. The site criterion is his, the provisioning schedule is his, the twenty-one-day figure is his against my own advice, and I was wrong and he was right and the curve behind me is the reason we know it."

Alec Spilsbury is four rows back and does not move a muscle.

Meacher is in the third row. Loveday is at the side. There are at least four men in that room who were in a corridor at the Labs in 1956 and who have spent fourteen years knowing what everybody knew.

It takes about nine seconds and it is the largest thing anybody has ever done for that man, and Reggie Tar does it as a matter of ordinary scholarly hygiene, and they both understand both of those facts at the same time and neither ever refers to it.

---

## The lobby

He has a train at four and there are people everywhere.

"Well," says Alec.

"Well."

They shake hands, for about three seconds, in front of forty people, which is permitted; and Alec holds on for perhaps half a second longer than the form requires, and lets go, and straightens his cuff, which does not need it.

"You got through the paper."

"I got through the paper," says Alec Spilsbury. "I have no idea what was in the first four minutes of it and I shall read it when it is printed."

"It was in the fourth sentence."

"I am aware of what was in the fourth sentence. Loveday turned round." He picks up his case and puts it down again. "Write to me about the Amery girl," he says, in the ordinary conversational voice he uses for gear specifications. "I should like to know what you do about it. That is not a professional enquiry and I am making it anyway."

"I will."

"Good," says Alec Spilsbury, and picks up his case, and goes, and does not look round, and Reggie Tar stands in the middle of a hotel lobby off the Marylebone Road at half past three in the afternoon and watches him all the way to the door, which is the most reckless thing he does all week.

---

## The letters

The October letter is five pages and is about the Thursday counts and is signed *A.S.*, and Reggie reads the bottom of it before he reads the top, which he has been doing since 1946 and has never told anybody.

There is a page and a half in the middle of it which is not about the Thursday counts.

*A separate matter, which I undertook to take up with you by post and am now taking up.*

*The proposition that I nearly died in March is not established and I should like to set out why. (i) The operation was elective by the time it was done and went without incident. (ii) The four days' delay was correct practice and not neglect; to cut a man with a bilirubin of that order is to lose him on the table, and I would have declined it myself if anybody had asked me, which nobody did. (iii) Solveig telephoned Bergen because she had been given no instruction as to what one does when the director is in a ward, and not because anybody thought I was dying; I have since given her the instruction and she was extremely short with me about the delay and was entitled to be.*

*I am not going to pretend to you that the figure for a man of sixty with an obstructed duct is one in a thousand, because it is not, and you would find out and be insufferable. It is a good deal better than the thing you were describing in that room, and the thing you were describing in that room is what I am objecting to.*

*What I will give you, and I have been thinking about it since the sixteenth of September, is this. If the figure had gone the other way, you would have learned of it in about seven weeks, from a letter that was not answered. That is what you were actually objecting to. It is not an exaggeration and it is not a figure of speech, it is the plain arrangement as it stands, and I have no answer to it at all.*

*I should be obliged if you would not reply to that paragraph. I am aware that it wants an answer. I have not got one yet.*

---

He does not reply to that paragraph. He replies to the one before it.

It takes him a week, because he writes to the Bay and asks Marguerite Sealy, who has the hospital library and a friend at the Kingston medical school and who sends back, by return, three references, a paragraph of her own, and a figure.

His answer goes on the twenty-sixth of October. Four pages about the counts, and then:

*You asked me not to answer the paragraph about the seven weeks and I haven't. This is about the paragraph before it, in which you said I would find out and be insufferable. I have found out.*

*I asked Marguerite. She went through the literature for me — acute gall bladder, over fifty, stones in the duct, jaundiced, duct opened at operation, which is the only way anybody on earth was getting that stone out of you in March of 1970 — and the figure she gives me for a man of sixty in that condition, in that year, is about one in twenty. One in twenty, Alec. She says a pessimistic surgeon would have put it nearer one in ten and that she would not argue with him.*

*I have had animals in that pen at better odds than one in twenty and sat up all night with them.*

*You told me it was a good deal better than the thing I was describing in that room. It is a good deal better. It is also one in twenty. I should like to know which of those two sentences I am meant to find the more comforting, because I have been looking at both of them since Thursday and neither of them has done its job.*

*R.*

---

The November letter comes on the nineteenth. It is three pages. Two of them are about Amery, whom Reggie has taken on to the release returns and told, in words, at the four o'clock, in front of people, that her transect work is the best thing that has come into that office in three years; and the letter is extremely pleased about it in the driest available manner and contains one sentence of advice which is correct.

The third page is not about Amery.

*I told you in October that you would find out and be insufferable, and you have found out, and you are. I had anticipated the first and allowed for the second and I find I was insufficiently pessimistic about both.*

*The figure is right. Dr Sealy is a very good physician and I should be obliged if you would thank her from me, and also if you would tell her that there is a precedent at your Institute for corresponding with her about other men's anatomy without their consent, and that I understand from Pobil the remedy is litigation.*

*But one in twenty is not "nearly died." I should like that established before this goes any further. Nineteen in twenty is not the near bank of anything; it is a very comfortable majority, and I have based entire papers on less. A man who nearly dies has a figure on the other side of one in two, not one in twenty, and you know that perfectly well, because you have spent thirty years telling students not to let a number mean what it does not mean. I have been through every one of my own letters since March and I did not once put that proposition forward except to refute it. You did not have it from me.*

*Where did you get it?*

It is signed:

*Yours, A.*

---

He reads the bottom of that page for about a minute and a half.

Then he thinks, for approximately four minutes and no longer, about the fact that a letter goes into a deed box in Ålesund and stays there, and about what that means; and then he decides that a man who stood in a hotel room at six in the morning with both arms round him and would not let go has already answered that question and is not entitled to raise it again eight weeks later over a comma.

He answers it the same evening.

It is six pages. It is mostly about Amery and about the north gate, which is Pascual's plan and is a bad plan, and he says so at length.

And on the fifth page:

*I had it from Berge.*

*At the drinks table on the sixteenth, holding a glass. His words were "a stupid thing to nearly die of", and he had had two of whatever it was the Society was pouring, and I carried it home and have had it about me since as though it were a clinical finding. I withdraw "nearly." You are right, and I should have caught it myself; it is precisely the thing I tell students.*

*I do not withdraw "appalled."*

*And I have passed your message to Marguerite, and she says that she will be delighted to be sued alongside me, that she has been waiting nine years for somebody to file, and that you are to take care in March, because she has read the literature now and does not intend to un-read it.*

And at the bottom of the sixth page, in a hand that is not quite his usual hand:

*Yours, R.*

## The January letter

It comes on the eleventh, and it is seven pages, and it is the best thing Alec Spilsbury has written him since 1965.

There is a paragraph in the middle of it that has nothing to do with the soundings:

*I had intended to put that at the bottom of two letters before I drew anything from it, on the grounds that a single observation is an anecdote, and I had the second one planned for January. You answered by return, which has made the whole of my method unnecessary, and I do not believe I have ever been so pleased to have wasted a piece of it.*

*I should also tell you, because you will not ask, that I read the bottom of your letters before I read the top and have done since 1947.*

*One other thing, and then I shall go back to the soundings. In November I had the personnel file out downstairs and put a name on the line which may be left blank and frequently is, and which has said "ingen" since 1957. It took about a minute. I am not going to make anything of it and you are not to either, and if you write me four pages about it I shall know exactly what you are doing.*

*Yours, A.*

## Coda: the nineteenth of April, 1971

There is no covering letter.

The envelope comes up from the Bay in the ordinary bag on a Tuesday with the returns and a circular about lamp oil, and Hester Bevan opens it at the long table with the letter opener in her hand and looks at it for slightly longer than she needs to.

One foolscap sheet. Application to consult deposited material of the Institute by a qualified person not being a member of the staff. Completed, signed, dated the nineteenth of April 1971, addressed in his own hand to Mrs H. Bevan, Secretary.

Nothing else in the envelope at all.

"Dr Tar," says Hester Bevan, "you are an interested party."

"Declared," says Reggie, and puts his cup down before anything can happen to it.

"It is going in the minute." She turns the sheet over and there is nothing on the back of it either. "Twelve days. The ninth of July to the twentieth."

"Qualified?" says Pascual Pobil, from the end of the table, who has not looked up and who has been waiting four years for this and has never once said so.

Hester Bevan puts the form down.

"He appears to be qualified," she says.
