# In Case of Accident

## The eighth of November, 1962

The strop is nine months old, it was inspected in August, it is entered in the book as inspected in August, and Reggie Tar will look at that entry a great many times over the following year and it will go on saying the same thing.

They are swinging the new counterweight for the seaward gate, which is a concrete block of about four hundredweight, and it is a five-minute job that they have done nine times that autumn with a different block.

It parts at ten minutes to four in the afternoon.

The block goes down the outside of the coping and takes Pascual Pobil with it off the sill at the seaward corner, and he goes in under it, and he does not come up.

---

Hendy is in the water in about two seconds, which is the wrong thing to do and is also the reason Pascual Pobil is alive, and Codrington goes in four seconds later, and between them they have him on the stone by twelve minutes to four with his face the colour of the stone.

Reggie is at the top of the path with the day book in his hand and does not remember any part of the next ninety seconds and is told afterwards, by three separate people, that he was down that ladder before Codrington surfaced and that he was the one who turned him over.

He comes up at some point in the first minute. The water comes out of him and he breathes and he does not wake.

---

Left collarbone. Three ribs on the same side. A laceration behind the ear that Reggie holds closed with his hand for nine minutes because there is nothing else to hold it closed with and because Miss Pryce has gone for the flag.

And the head, which is the thing nobody says out loud on that sill.

They have him off the cay by half past five on Alleyne's boat with the sea getting up, and into the hospital at the Bay at twenty past seven in the evening, and Marguerite Sealy is standing in the doorway of it when the lorry comes up the hill, because Hendy went ahead on the first boat with the flag and did the best piece of work of his life.

---

## The ninth

He comes round at some time in the small hours and is sick and is awake, and by nine in the morning he is talking.

Reggie is brought in at twenty past nine.

Pascual Pobil looks at him with both eyes open and says, in Spanish, quite politely, that he is very sorry but the light is very bad and would somebody kindly tell him where his glasses have got to; and then asks the sister a question about the boat; and then looks at Reggie again and says, still in Spanish, and still politely, "I am sorry, I don't think I know you."

---

Reggie says, "Pascual."

"Yes," says Pascual Pobil, agreeably.

"Pascual. It's Reggie."

"Yes," says Pascual Pobil.

---

Marguerite has him by the arm in the corridor about forty seconds later and puts him in a chair and crouches down in front of it.

"Listen to me. That is not what you think it is."

"He didn't—"

"It is extremely common after a head injury of that sort and it is very frightening to watch and it very frequently resolves inside a day or two." She has both his hands. "I will not lie to you and I am not going to start today, so I am going to tell you the rest of it as well. It may not resolve in a day or two. It may take weeks. And the thing I am actually worried about is not that and is not the ribs."

"Then what is it."

"He had water in him for a minute and a half," says Marguerite Sealy, "and he will have a chest by Sunday, and he is forty-nine, and there is no oxygen at this hospital past two cylinders."

---

## The eleventh to the fifteenth

She is right about Sunday.

He goes hot on the evening of the eleventh and is thirty-nine and a half by the small hours of the twelfth and the left base is wet and everybody in that building knows what it is. The penicillin goes in four-hourly from the twelfth and Marguerite Sealy does not leave the hospital at the Bay for six days except to change, and does the district's work out of the sister's room, and sleeps in a chair three times.

The bad night is the thirteenth.

Reggie is in the corridor for the whole of it, because there is one chair by the bed and it is not his, and at about four in the morning Marguerite comes out and sits down on the floor next to his chair with her back against the wall, which is not a thing a district medical officer does, and neither of them says anything for about twenty minutes.

Then she says, "I have sent for his brother in Havana and I do not expect him to get here."

"Marguerite—"

"I am telling you because you are the only person in this building I can tell," she says, "and because if it goes the other way tomorrow you are going to be very glad that you knew on the thirteenth and not on the fourteenth."

---

It does not go the other way.

He is thirty-eight and one on the morning of the fifteenth, and by the seventeenth he is complaining, and by the nineteenth he has asked four separate people what has happened to the counterweight and has been unhelpfully answered by all of them, and Marguerite tells Reggie on the nineteenth, in the corridor, with her hair not done, that her husband is out of danger and is going to be insufferable and that she intends to go home and sleep for twelve hours.

He says the right things. He is very good about it. He goes back out to the cay on the four o'clock boat because there is a station to run.

---

## The twentieth and the twenty-first

He runs it extremely well.

That is a thing everybody on the island agrees about afterwards and it happens to be true. From the eighth to the nineteenth of November 1962, Reggie Tar does the seven o'clock and the four o'clock and the veterinary officer's own rounds by the book with Pascual's notes open beside him, holds the release schedule together, signs off Miss Pryce's last month of soundings, gets a full report to the Colonel on the eleventh which does not spare the Institute one word about the strop, and stands up in front of three shaken students on the ninth and tells them, correctly, that Hendy is not to be praised for going into that water because it was extremely dangerous, and then praises him.

Nobody sees him do anything wrong at all.

On the twentieth he adds the salaries column of the December estimates and gets it wrong by four pounds, and does it again and gets a different wrong answer, and Hester Bevan takes the sheet off him without a word and does it herself.

On the twenty-first he signs a returns certificate *1961*.

---

Hester puts that sheet on his desk at half past eleven with the date ringed.

"You will want to initial the correction."

"Yes. Thank you."

"Dr Tar."

"Yes."

"When did you last sleep?"

"I slept last night."

"You did not," says Hester Bevan, "because the lamp in that hut was on at twenty past three and I know it was on at twenty past three because I was awake myself, and I am going to go downstairs now and I would be obliged if you would look at the second sheet I have put under that one."

He looks at it after she has gone.

It is the leave record. He has not taken any since 1958. The figure in the accrued column is one year, one month and nine days.

---

## The twenty-second, the hospital at the Bay

Marguerite Sealy takes him out into the yard at the back of the hospital where the bins are, which is the only place in that building where two people can have a conversation, and stands in front of him with her arms folded.

"Sit on the wall."

"I'm perfectly—"

"Sit on the wall, Reggie."

He sits on the wall.

"You have lost about nine pounds in a fortnight, you have made two errors of arithmetic in two days, and my husband asked me this morning why you looked like that and I told him it was the strop, which was a lie, and I do not like lying to a man with a head injury because it is unsporting." She sits down beside him. "Now. Who knows?"

"Knows what?"

"Don't," says Marguerite. "I have been married to that man for four months and I have known about the other business for about two years and I have written to you every month since the October and you have written back every month since the October, so let us not sit in a yard full of bins and pretend."

---

Reggie Tar looks at his own hands.

"Nobody," he says.

"Nobody at all."

"Hester. Not in words." He turns the hands over. "Pascual. In words, once, in about 1958, and he has never raised it since and he never will."

"And in Norway?"

"I posted him a letter on the eleventh."

"What did it say?"

"That Pascual had had an accident with a derrick and was very ill and that I would write again when there was news," says Reggie. "It was four pages. It was a good letter. It had the release figures in it."

Marguerite Sealy says something in French that she does not translate.

---

"On the thirteenth," says Reggie, "at about four in the morning, sitting in your corridor, after you told me about his brother in Havana—"

He stops.

"Go on."

"It occurred to me to wonder who would send for me."

Marguerite does not say anything.

"There's a station in Ålesund with nine staff and an office and a woman called Solveig who writes his messages down twice, and there is not one piece of paper in that building with my name on it." He is speaking quite steadily and very slowly, which is how he does this. "If a strop parted in that yard on a Thursday afternoon, the Institute would find out when a letter went unanswered. In about seven weeks. And I should be sitting in my office in February opening the post and wondering why he had not written since November."

"Yes."

"I have been aware of that since 1946 and I have never once sat down and looked at it," says Reggie Tar, "and I looked at it on the thirteenth of November at four o'clock in the morning in your corridor, for about two hours, while your husband was at thirty-nine and a half."

---

Marguerite Sealy stands up off the wall.

"Right," she says. "Get on an aeroplane."

"I can't leave the station—"

"Miss Pryce is competent and has finished her soundings, Hendy has a certificate, John Weald is on the island until March, and my husband will be out of that bed in nine days and back on the cay in three weeks and will be perfectly capable of sitting on a stool and shouting at people, which is ninety per cent of what he does anyway." She is counting on her fingers, which she does when she is being a doctor at somebody. "The Colonel is at the Bay. Mrs Bevan will do the rest and will enjoy it. You have accrued a year and a month of leave and I have seen the column because she showed me, which was a deliberate act and you may take it as one."

"Marguerite—"

"You held the whole of this for eleven days and you were magnificent and everybody says so, and that is precisely why you are sitting on a wall by the bins not able to add up four pounds." She puts her hand flat on the top of his head for a second, which she has never done. "The danger is never in those days. It is always in the week afterwards. Go and be in the same room as him for a fortnight, and tell him the thing about the piece of paper, because he will not have thought of it either and he ought to have."

---

## The twenty-third

Hester Bevan has it done by three in the afternoon on the Friday.

"Kingston on Sunday night, New York on the Monday, London Tuesday. There is a Norwegian service to Ålesund from Oslo that will not take you on the Wednesday and will take you on the Thursday, and I have wired a hotel in Oslo which the shipping agent says is respectable." She puts the sheet down. "You will be there on the twenty-ninth. You will come back on the twelfth of December. Sign that one."

He looks at the form.

"The purpose column."

"I have written *personal*," says Hester Bevan.

"Hester."

"It will stand once. It will stand once from a man who has taken no leave in nine years and whose veterinary officer has just been pulled out from under four hundredweight of concrete, and there is not a soul at the Bay who will look at it twice this side of the Judgement." She holds the pen out. "Once, Dr Tar. I would not write it twice and I shall tell you so if you ever ask me to."

He signs it.

"Thank you."

"Yes," says Hester Bevan, and takes it downstairs, and does not say the other thing, which she has not said since a room over a magistrate's court in 1956 and does not intend to say now.

---

## Ålesund, the twenty-ninth of November

It is dark at half past three in the afternoon and it is snowing in a thin unserious way off the sound, and he has no gloves, because it did not occur to him at the Bay in November that there was anywhere on earth where a man required gloves.

The station is a long low building at the end of a yard with all its lights on.

Solveig is at the desk inside the door with the post book open in front of her, and looks up, and he says, "My name is Tar. I'm from the Martin Institute, in the West Indies. I should like to see Dr Spilsbury, if he's free."

She looks at him for a moment.

"Tar," she says. "T-A-R."

"That's right."

"I have written that on envelopes for five years," says Solveig, and closes the post book, and something happens to her face that he will think about for a long time afterwards. "He is in the wet laboratory. He is with the two young men from Bergen and they will be there until five. Come."

---

## Forty minutes

Alec Spilsbury is at the third bench with his sleeves up and his hands in a tray, saying something in Norwegian to a young man of about twenty-four, and he turns round at the sound of the door in the ordinary way, and sees him.

And that is the whole of it.

There is no noise and nothing falls over. Reggie, who has read this man across benches for sixteen years, watches the entire event go through him and out the other side in about a second and three quarters, behind a face that does not do one single thing, in front of two junior staff and a woman from the office, and thinks, with a completely irrelevant and enormous pride: *nobody in this room has the first idea.*

"Dr Tar," says Alec Spilsbury. "Good God. Nobody told me."

"Nobody knew. I'm sorry — I've come on the chance."

"You have come four thousand miles on the chance."

"Rather more like six," says Reggie, "and I have made a serious error about gloves."

---

"Haugland. Nordbø. This is Dr Reginald Tar, who is the Director of Research of the William Martin Institute for Marine Studies in the West Indies, whose release programme you have both read about because I made you read about it in September." Alec is drying his hands on a cloth and his voice is doing nothing at all. "Dr Tar, these are two very promising young men who are about to ruin that tray if they go on looking at you instead of at it."

The tray is attended to.

And then there are forty minutes.

Reggie Tar stands in a cold wet laboratory in Norway in a coat he has not taken off and is shown the holding tanks, the new circulation, the winter sampling, and the gear bench, by a man whose hands are not perfectly steady and who is explaining a flow meter with enormous competence to the only person in the building who can see it; and one of the juniors asks him a good question about the sill design and he answers it well; and Solveig comes in twice.

At twenty past four Alec says, in English, "I am going to take Dr Tar up out of this cold before he loses a finger. Haugland, the six o'clock is yours. Nordbø, do not touch the pump."

At the foot of the stairs he says, quietly and quite pleasantly, "Is anybody dead."

"No."

"Thank you," says Alec Spilsbury, and goes up first.

---

## The rooms

Two rooms above the office. The stove lit. A good lamp. A desk under the window with the shutter up because there is nothing to see. A shelf of offprints with the spines squared.

He shuts the door.

"Now."

"Pascual went into the north pen under four hundredweight of concrete on the eighth of November."

Alec's hand is still on the door.

"Go on."

---

And Reggie Tar gives the account, because giving the account is what he does, and he does it properly, standing in the middle of a room in his coat.

The strop, and the book entry, and the inspection in August. Ten minutes to four. Hendy in the water in two seconds and Codrington in six. A minute and a half under. The collarbone and the three ribs on the same side and the laceration behind the ear. Off the cay at half past five with the sea getting up. The Bay at twenty past seven. Round in the small hours.

The ninth, at twenty past nine in the morning, when Pascual Pobil looked at him with both eyes open and said, in Spanish, quite politely, that he was sorry but he did not think he knew him.

The eleventh, when he went hot. The twelfth, thirty-nine and a half, and the left base. The penicillin four-hourly. Marguerite at the hospital for six days and sleeping in a chair three times.

The thirteenth—

And that is where it stops.

---

Reggie Tar stands in the middle of a room in Norway with his coat on and his mouth open and nothing whatever comes out of it, and goes on standing there for about four seconds, and then says, "I'm sorry. Give me a moment," in an absolutely level voice, and does not get one.

---

Alec Spilsbury crosses the room.

He does not say anything at all. He takes the coat off him, which takes a moment because Reggie is no help with it, and puts it over the chair, and then puts both arms round him and holds on, which he has done perhaps four times in sixteen years and never first.

And Reggie Tar, who is forty-eight years old and who has run a scientific station for the better part of a fortnight on nothing, comes apart in a fisheries station above an office in Ålesund, with his face in the shoulder of a man's shirt, at five o'clock in the afternoon on the twenty-ninth of November 1962.

It goes on for some time.

Alec does not say *there there* and does not say *he is going to be all right*, because he has not been told the end of it yet and is not going to say a thing he does not know; he says nothing whatever for about six minutes, and then says, into the top of his head, "I have got you," once, and nothing else.

---

Later, sitting on the end of the bed with a cup of something he has not touched:

"He's out of danger. I should have led with that and I'm sorry. He was insufferable by the nineteenth and Marguerite says he'll be back on the cay in three weeks."

"I had worked that out at about the fourth sentence."

"How?"

"Because you are here," says Alec Spilsbury, "and if he were dying you would be there, and if he were dead you would be there as well, and you would not be taking your coat off in Norway."

---

## The letter

The post comes up at eleven in the morning on the thirtieth and Solveig brings it with the tea, and there is a foolscap envelope on the top of it with a Bay postmark and the Institute's stamp and Reggie Tar's handwriting on it.

Alec looks at it, and at him, and opens it.

Four pages. Posted the eleventh of November.

*My dear Alec,*

*There has been an accident here. A strop parted on the derrick at the north pen on Thursday afternoon and Pascual went into the water under the counterweight. He has a broken collarbone and three ribs and a head injury and he took in a good deal of water, and he is at the hospital at the Bay and Marguerite is with him. He is very ill. I will write again as soon as there is anything to tell you.*

*The strop had been inspected in August and is so entered. I have reported it to the Colonel without qualification and I shall be amending the lifting procedure in any case.*

*The release figures for October are enclosed. You will see that the Thursday counts are still running high against the Sunday ones and I do not believe it is observer bias, because we changed the observer in September and it did not move.*

*Yours ever,*

*R.*

---

Alec Spilsbury reads it twice and puts it down on the desk, squared to the edge, and looks at it.

"The release figures for October," he says.

"I know."

"Four pages, and the man is under a derrick, and there are nine lines about the Thursday counts."

"I *know*," says Reggie. "I wrote it at three o'clock in the morning on the eleventh and it was the best I had, and it was the best I have had for sixteen years, and I posted it and then I sat in Marguerite's corridor on the thirteenth and worked out the other thing."

Alec turns round.

"What other thing."

---

"If it had been you," says Reggie Tar, "nobody would have sent for me."

The stove does what a stove does.

"There are nine people in this building and a post book and a personnel file, and there is not one piece of paper anywhere in this town with my name on it, and there is no reason why there should be. If a strop parted in that yard on a Thursday I should find out in about seven weeks, when a letter went unanswered. I should sit in my office in February and open the post and wonder why you had not written since November." He has his hands flat on his knees. "I have been aware of that since 1946 in the way a man is aware of a thing he has not looked at. I looked at it on the thirteenth at four in the morning for about two hours and it is the reason I am in this room."

---

Alec Spilsbury sits down at his own desk, slowly, and is quiet for rather a long time.

"I have a form downstairs," he says.

"What?"

"Personnel. It is a state station; we have a form for everything and I have filled in about nine hundred of them." He is looking at the window shutter. "*Ved ulykke, varsle.* In case of accident, notify. There is a line for a name and a line for an address and a line for the relation of that person to the employee, and the third line may be left blank and frequently is, and mine has said *ingen* since 1957, which means none."

"None."

"There has been nobody to put in it," says Alec Spilsbury, "and I have looked at that line once a year for five years when the file is reviewed, and I have written *ingen* five times, and I did not — " he stops, and starts again — "it did not occur to me that it was a line I was permitted to fill in."

---

"Fill it in."

"It goes in a personnel file in a state office in Norway and it stays there for thirty years."

"Fill it in," says Reggie Tar.

"You have spent sixteen years extremely properly declining to have your name on the same sheet of paper as mine, and you were right to, and you have been right about it in three separate decades—"

"*Alec.* It is a next-of-kin line on an employment form. It is the single most boring document in this building. Nobody in the history of the world has ever picked one of those up for the fun of it." He leans forward. "And if you go under a derrick in that yard I would like to be told inside a day instead of finding out in February, and there is no other mechanism on this earth by which that happens."

---

Alec Spilsbury looks at him.

"Mrs Bevan will have to do the same at your end."

"Mrs Bevan," says Reggie, "has had a form in her second drawer for that since about 1959 and has been waiting for me to ask her for it, and when I do ask her she will say *yes* and nothing else, and I shall have to live with that for the rest of my life."

"Then you had better ask her."

"I'm going to."

---

He goes down at eight the next morning and does it before breakfast, because he is that sort of man and because if he leaves it until the afternoon he will spend the whole morning on it.

Solveig finds the file. Alec strikes out *ingen*, which he does with a ruler, and initials the striking-out, and dates it, and writes on the line beneath:

*Dr R. Tar, William Martin Institute for Marine Studies, the Bay.*

And on the third line, the one that may be left blank and frequently is, he writes *kollega*, and then looks at it for a moment and does not change it, and says, "That is true, and it is the only word on that form that anybody will ever read, and I find I mind rather less than I expected to."

Solveig takes the file and puts it back in the cabinet and locks the cabinet and says something in Norwegian, and Alec says something back, and neither of them translates it.

---

## The first of December

"You have not asked me for anything since you arrived."

"I've been asleep for two days."

"You have been asleep for two days because you had not slept since the eighth of November, and you have eaten four meals, and this morning you did the six o'clock with Haugland because you cannot help yourself." Alec is sitting up against the iron with the lamp on. "And you have not asked me for one thing, and I have been lying here working out why, and I believe it is because you have spent sixteen years constructing an arrangement in which you are the one who asks and I am the one who is asked, and it has been extremely convenient for you."

Reggie says, "That's not—"

And then stops, because it is.

"Since 1947," says Alec Spilsbury. "Every single time. You have asked me what I want and I have been made to say it out loud, which I have not liked and have never refused, and in sixteen years I have not once asked you." He puts a hand out and flattens it in the middle of Reggie's chest, which is a gesture he has taken from somebody. "I am going to now, and you are going to answer it properly, and if you say *whatever you like* I shall put you out in the snow."

---

"That's fighting talk from a man with one bad shoulder."

"Answer the question."

"You haven't asked one yet."

"What do you want," says Alec Spilsbury.

---

Reggie Tar lies in a bed in Norway and finds, rather to his astonishment, that his throat has closed.

"That's harder than I expected."

"Yes. It is. I have been telling you so since 1947 and you have been perfectly unsympathetic about it, and I have waited sixteen years for this and I am going to enjoy every second of it. What do you want."

"I want you to hold on to me and not let go for about an hour."

"That is not an answer to the question I asked."

"It's the true one," says Reggie. "You can have the other one afterwards."

---

So Alec Spilsbury does that.

He is not good at it, because he has not had the practice and because one shoulder is not what it was and because he has spent the whole of his adult life being the one who is held; and he is extremely thorough about it, the way he is thorough about everything, and at some point he gets his hand into the back of Reggie's hair and holds there, hard, and says into his ear, "The thirteenth of November. Say the rest of it."

And Reggie says the rest of it.

It takes a while, and it is not orderly, and none of it is news except one sentence, which is that at some point on that corridor floor he had got as far as composing the letter he would have written to Ålesund if it had gone the other way, and had got four lines into it, and the four lines were about the station.

"Four lines about the station."

"I couldn't think how to start it. There isn't a form for it." His voice goes. "There's a form for everything else in my life and there isn't one for that, and I sat on the floor of a hospital corridor at four in the morning drafting a *notification.*"

"There is now," says Alec Spilsbury, "in a cabinet, downstairs, since Thursday," and holds on.

---

And afterwards, and rather later, in the ordinary way, with the lamp still on because the lamp has been on since October 1958:

"The other answer."

"Which other answer."

"You said I could have it afterwards. It is afterwards." Alec has not moved his hand. "What do you want."

Reggie tells him.

He does it badly and with considerable difficulty and it takes him three goes, and he is told, at the end of it, in a voice that has gone entirely unsteady, that it was worth waiting sixteen years for and that he is to say the middle part again; and he says the middle part again; and the lamp stays on for another hour and a half.

---

## Coda: the nineteenth of January, 1963

Pascual Pobil comes back on to the four o'clock on the seventh of January with his arm out of the strapping and a great deal to say about lifting procedure, and by the middle of the month he is doing the seven o'clock as well and has stopped going grey in the face by three in the afternoon.

On the nineteenth, Hester Bevan puts the December file on the long table, and the leave record is in it, and Pascual Pobil, who is signing the veterinary returns and reading everything else at the same time because that is how he has always worked, stops.

"'Twenty-third of November to the twelfth of December. Purpose: personal.'"

"That is not your file," says Hester.

"It's on the table." He looks up. "Reggie. Where did you go?"

The long room becomes noticeably quiet.

"Norway," says Reggie Tar.

---

Pascual Pobil puts the pen down.

"*Norway.*"

"Yes."

"You went to *Norway.*"

"Yes."

"In *November.*" He is going red. "Because I fell off a derrick."

"You did not fall off a derrick, the strop parted, and I have amended the procedure and you have read the amendment four times and made two entirely reasonable objections to it—"

"You got on an *aeroplane*," says Pascual Pobil, and puts his head down on the long table, and laughs until he has to hold his ribs, which are still not right, and which makes it worse, and Hester Bevan looks at the ceiling and Miss Pryce leaves the room.

---

Later, at the rail, with the four o'clock done:

"Four years."

"Four years what?"

"Four years I've been looking at that light in that window on my way down the path," says Pascual, "and it took four hundredweight of concrete and my chest." He is still enjoying it enormously. "I'd have jumped in the pen in about 1958 if I'd known that was all it wanted."

"Pascual."

"No, I'm having this. I'm going to have this for twenty years, hombre, you may as well sit down under it." He wipes his eyes with the back of his wrist. "Did he know you were coming?"

"No."

"*Ay.*" That stops him. "What did he do?"

"He introduced me to two juniors and showed me a flow meter for forty minutes," says Reggie, "and then we went upstairs."

Pascual Pobil looks out at the water for a while.

"Yes," he says. "That's about what I'd have expected of him," and does not make a joke about that part, then or ever.

---

"There's one other thing and you're going to hear it from me and not from the file." Reggie has both hands on the rail. "There's a line on my personnel record at the Bay, in case of accident, and Hester put a second name on it in December."

"I know. She showed me."

"She—"

"I'm the veterinary officer, I'm on the sheet above yours, she showed me the whole page because she wanted me to see it." Pascual straightens up off the rail.
