# Save on Certificate

## The eighth

Hendy finds her at the seven o'clock and does the right thing, which nobody on this island would have bet on.

He does not go in. He does not shout. He comes up the path at a fast walk with the bucket still in his hand and knocks on Pascual's shutter and says, through it, in a voice that is nearly steady: "Sir. Gamma's on the bottom at the north end and she hasn't moved since I came down, and Chumley's lying over her."

Pascual is out of the hut in about nine seconds with his shirt not fastened.

"Good lad. Get Dr Tar. Don't run past the pen."

---

She has been dead some hours. Reggie can see that from the path before he can see anything else, from the way the light is going through the water and not being interrupted.

Chumley will not let them near her.

He is not aggressive about it. That is somehow a great deal worse. He lies along her in the shaded half with his head across her and he does not come for the fish, and when Pascual goes down the ladder at nine o'clock he comes off the bottom once, slowly, and puts himself between, and Pascual comes straight back up.

"Right," he says. "He gets as long as he wants."

"Pascual, we've got a post-mortem —"

"We've got an animal who's been lying on her since about four this morning, and who hasn't been out of arm's reach of her since the day they put him in with her, and I'm not taking her off him at nine because it suits my afternoon." He is drying his forearms on his shirt and not looking at anybody. "He gets as long as he wants. Hendy, go and get your breakfast, you've done very well and I'll tell you so again later when I'm fit to."

He gets four hours. At ten past one he comes off her and goes up to the seaward end and faces the wall, and Pascual has her out of the water in seven minutes flat with Codrington and two of Alleyne's boys and does not say one word during any of it.

Codrington cries on the path afterwards and is furious with himself about it.

"You're allowed," says Pascual, going past with the stretcher poles. "Once. Then you come and hold a lamp for me, because I'm going to be four hours over this and I want somebody who liked her."

---

## The ninth to the eleventh

Nothing on the gross findings that anybody could argue with. She was old. She was old in 1953 when Pascual first put a hand on her and said so out loud to a room that did not want to hear it; the heart is the heart of an old animal and the kidneys are worse, and there is a healed fracture in the fourth rib on the left that nobody living put there.

He does three days on her. He writes nine pages and he does not once come up to the long room to be agreed with.

Chumley takes nothing on the ninth and nothing on the tenth. On the eleventh he takes two fish at the seven o'clock, off the gaff, from Hendy, and puts his head back down.

That is the whole clinical record of those three days and it is the only thing any of them care about.

---

## The trap

It is Hester who lays it out on the long table on the ninth, in her own hand, on a half-sheet, because she has been expecting it since the first of January and has had the half-sheet in the cabinet since the fourth.

*Paragraph nine. No animal to be held otherwise than in the company of at least one other of its own kind — save on the written certificate of a veterinary surgeon, and then for not more than fourteen days.*

*Schedule, paragraph two. Taking from the wild prohibited except under permit.*

"Certificate signed yesterday," says Pascual. "That's the eighth. So it runs to the twenty-second."

"It runs to the twenty-second," Hester agrees.

"And the permit," says the Colonel.

"There will be no application for a permit," says Reggie.

"I only asked —"

"I know you did, sir, and the answer's no, and it's no on the ninth and it'll be no on the twenty-first." He has his spectacles off and is holding them. "We spent twenty-two years getting that paragraph written. I'm not going to be the first man to ask for an exemption from it, and I'm certainly not going to ask for one so that I can go and take somebody else's animal out of the sea to keep mine company."

"*Bueno*," says Pascual, from the end of the table, with some feeling.

"So we wrote both halves of it," says the Colonel, "and we appear to have got ourselves inside."

"We did, sir. It's a good box." Reggie puts the spectacles back on. "It's the best piece of drafting in this hemisphere and there is exactly one door in it."

Everybody looks at the half-sheet.

"The seaward wall," says Pascual.

---

## The cable

He drafts it in the office on the Wednesday morning and it takes him forty minutes and comes out at nineteen words, and he hands it to Hester and stands there while she reads it, which he has not done since 1958.

She reads it twice.

"That is a correct cable," she says, "and I am going to send a different one."

"Hester —"

"A cable from the Director of Research of this Institute to a man in Norway, in April, is one kind of document." She is already writing. "A cable from the Secretary, requesting the attendance of a consultant on a matter arising under the Order, is another kind of document."

"And it goes on the file."

"It goes on the file. And in fourteen years, when somebody in London is being difficult about something quite else, that is the one they will find."

She blots it and turns it round.

*INSTITUTE REQUESTS YOUR ATTENDANCE CONSULTANT RELEASE 197 LINE FEMALE DECEASED EIGHTH FOURTEEN DAYS RUNNING FARE AND FEE AUTHORISED REPLY SOONEST BEVAN SECRETARY.*

"Fee," says Reggie.

"He will refuse it," says Hester Bevan, "and I shall enter the refusal, and that will go on the file as well."

---

The reply comes on Thursday afternoon and is three words long and is the three words he would have bet the Institute on.

*LEAVING TONIGHT. SPILSBURY.*

---

## Marguerite, on the verandah, the eleventh

She comes over on the Thursday boat with her bag, which she does not open, because there is nothing wrong with anybody on this island; she comes because her husband has spent three days up to the elbows in an animal he has known for ten years, and she intends to look at him over dinner and then take him home.

She gets Reggie on the verandah at about six with the lamp not yet lit.

"He'll be here Sunday or Monday," says Reggie. "He's flying to Oslo tonight."

"Good." She takes the glass he is holding out. "And July?"

Reggie does not say anything.

"He runs a station," says Marguerite. "Stations have leave. He has been taking a fortnight in July since 1958 and it is the same fortnight every year, which means it is a fixed arrangement with somebody above him, and a man does not get a fixed arrangement twice in one calendar year out of the same office." She drinks. "So he is spending it now."

"Yes."

"You had got there."

"I'd got there on Wednesday morning," says Reggie, "about four minutes after I put the cable in her hand. I've been not doing the rest of the sum since."

"Do it now. I would rather you did it in front of me than at two o'clock in the morning on your own."

So he does it out loud, on his own verandah, to a district medical officer in a straw hat, because there is nobody else on earth he could do it in front of and because she has arranged, over two and a half years, to be somebody he can.

Twelve days in April. Nothing in July. The Norwegian station shuts in August and the boats are impossible from October, and the next thing after that is the spring, and the spring is not a date, it is a season.

"Eleven months," says Reggie. "Give or take."

"That is the figure," says Marguerite Sealy, "and he will have spent every one of them knowing that he chose it, and being quite sure he chose correctly, which he did." She puts the glass down. "I am not going to tell you what to do. I am going to tell you one thing about people, because I have been doing this for twelve years and you have not."

"Go on."

"A man who has made a large decision correctly and on his own is in more danger in the fortnight after it than he was in the year before it." She picks her hat up off the boards. "Now come in to dinner, and be very ordinary with him when he arrives, because he will have had three aeroplanes and no sleep and the last thing he will want is to be met by a committee."

---

## The fourteenth

He comes up the jetty at four in the afternoon on a Sunday in April, which is wrong in every particular, and the three students stand on the boards not knowing what the form is, because there is no form.

He has one case. He has no hat. He has been travelling for sixty-one hours.

"Dr Spilsbury," says Codrington, "sir — Chumley took four fish this morning."

Alec puts the case down.

"Off the gaff or off the surface?"

"Off the gaff."

"Then write it as off the gaff and do not let anybody write it as feeding," says Alec Spilsbury, and picks the case up again, and that is the whole of the greeting, and it is precisely the right one, and Codrington is visibly steadied by it in a way that a kind word would not have managed in a fortnight.

Reggie, standing at the top of the jetty with his hands in his pockets, has time to register two things before the man reaches him.

The first is that he is grey in a way that is not the aeroplanes.

The second is that in sixteen years he has never once come up that jetty on a date that was not on a form, and that the form is what has always made it bearable, and that there is no form in the world for the fourteenth of April.

---

## The week

And then, for six days, they are extremely good.

That is the part Reggie will keep: not the twenty-second and not the twenty-third, but the Tuesday, when the three of them get the fourteen-page protocol out of the cabinet and take it apart at the long table from nine in the morning until a quarter past two the following morning.

The protocol is Gamma's. It says so at the top of the first page in Pascual's hand, in five words: *On the death of Gamma.* They wrote it in the February and March of 1958, the pair of them, in the fortnight after she went off her feed for nine days and came back on; and they initialled every page and put it in the cabinet, and neither of them has taken it out since, and nobody on this island has ever wanted them to.

It is a good document. Six things in it were correct in 1958 and are not correct now, and five of the six are the same thing.

"We had the whole of it," says Pascual. "September. October if the water held. A good tide in the right month with the fish running, and if it went wrong we'd have put the boards back in and waited a year."

"And you now have fourteen days," says Alec.

"Now we've got fourteen days."

"Then every line about choosing the week is waste paper and section four is the only part that matters." Alec squares the sheets. "Say what is in section four."

"Section four provisions the sill for nine days. That figure came out of the Lisbon lobster work and it is the wrong animal, the wrong latitude and the wrong century." Alec has both hands flat on the paper. "I should like it at twenty-one, falling by a third at day fourteen, and I should like the fish live from day three."

"Twenty-one days of a man at that gate at seven in the morning," says Reggie.

"Yes."

"We've got three students and a tide gauge and Miss Pryce goes back to Bangor in June —"

"Then you have twenty-one days of a man at that gate at seven in the morning," says Alec, "or you have a curve that looks exactly like the one in appendix two, and I have read that curve four hundred times and I would rather not read it again with a name on it."

---

The row is on the Wednesday and it is Pascual's and it is entirely serious.

He wants to hold Chumley past the twenty-second.

He says so at the long table in front of Hester and the Colonel and both men: keep him, take the summons, he will sign the certificate and he will sign a second one and he will stand up in a magistrate's room at the Bay and say why, and he is not — he says it twice — he is not putting a hand-reared animal into the sea in April on the strength of a Norwegian lobster paper.

And Reggie brakes him.

He does it badly, because he is tired, and he does it in front of the Colonel, which is the part he minds.

"You'll get the certificate. You'll get a fine and a paragraph in the *Gleaner*." He has his spectacles in his hand. "And in five years Dewar and Lecky will be in front of a magistrate about four animals in a shed in Bristol."

"They will."

"And their counsel is going to stand up and say *the first prosecution under paragraph nine was of the institute that drafted it, and they held their animal nine months and paid four pounds*." He puts the spectacles down. "That's the end of it, Pascual. Not the end of the case. The end of the paragraph."

"So the paper matters more than the animal."

"The paper *is* the animals. It's two hundred of them a year for forty years and you know the figure better than I do, you wrote the returns." He puts them back on. "You told me in 1956 that I'd got one animal in front of me and no column for the rest of them. This is the column. You made me make it."

Pascual stands at the end of the table with his jaw going.

"*Ay*," he says. "All right. All right, that's fair, and I hate it, and I'm going to say one more thing and then I'll do the work properly."

"Say it."

"If that boy's in the water on the twenty-second and he's on the bottom on the twenty-ninth, I'm the one who signed the paper that put him there, and I'll carry it, and I'm not going to pretend to any of you that I won't." He picks up section four. "Now. Twenty-one days. Where's the fish coming from, because it isn't coming from Alleyne, he can't get live fish past the channel in a bucket and neither can you."

They have the fish problem solved by Thursday lunchtime. It is Miss Pryce who solves it, with a holding crate and the wrong end of the north pen, and she is insufferable about it for the rest of the week and has earned every minute of it.

---

## What he has not published

It comes out on the Wednesday night, in the long room, at about eleven, over Jean's sounding chart and a cold pot of tea, because Pascual has finally said the thing all three of them have been walking round since Monday and has said it flatly.

"He won't go."

"He may not go."

"He won't go. He's been in that pen since he was a calf." Pascual has both hands on the table. "He's lying at twelve feet against the stone at the seaward end and he hasn't come off it since Tuesday, and you can take every board out of that wall on the twenty-second and he'll lie there and look at the gap."

"That is possible."

"It's what's going to happen and you know it is, and then he's lawfully at liberty in the open sea and he's on the bottom of my pen, and I'll have signed the paper that—"

"I am going to tell you something that is not in any paper," says Alec Spilsbury.

---

The room goes very quiet. Reggie, who is at the far end with the tide tables and has been listening to that voice on good lines and bad ones since 1946, puts the tables down.

"In 1956 I put a live fish at the seaward end every morning for fifty-one days. I gave that account at this table on the thirtieth of May and you were both in the room."

"I remember it," says Pascual, in a voice with nothing whatever in it.

"What I did not give at this table is that on the twentieth day I put my hand flat on the wall and tapped it twice, and he answered me."

Nobody says anything.

"He answered you."

"Twice, on the stone, from the same place on the wall, at the same hour, for thirty-one days." Alec has not moved. "It is in my daybook for 1956. It is in nothing I have published, because it is not a measurement and I could not have defended it in a room, and I am aware to the shilling what that reasoning is worth."

"Why are you telling me now."

"Because I am about to ask you to get into that water," says Alec, "and I am not going to ask it on a better footing than I have got."

---

He puts it in four sentences and does not decorate any of them. In the water, not the boat and not the rail. At the seaward end, every day, for as long as a man can stand it. Talking the whole time, and it does not matter about what.

"And you are not to stop. That is the part."

"Why is that the part?"

"Because I stopped on the fifty-first day," says Alec Spilsbury. "The animal was taken out of my hands and I stopped, and I have had seven years of finding out what that is worth."

Pascual looks at the chart for a while.

"What's the evidence?"

"One animal. One observer. The observer was the man who took him." He puts his hands flat. "It is the worst evidence I have ever asked anybody to act on. It is what I have got."

---

Pascual Pobil does not say anything at all for about twenty seconds.

Then he takes his watch off and puts it on the table.

"Boots," he says.

"It's eleven at night."

"Seven, then." He picks the watch back up. "Both of us."

Alec does not move.

"Both —"

"You heard me. You've got a fortnight's worth of hours and you'll do half of them, and you'll talk to him the whole time same as I will, and I don't care what language it's in." Pascual is already going for the door. "And eat something and get to bed. You're fifty-three and that end's cold at seven and I'm not hauling you out as well."

Reggie says, from the far end of the table, "As Director of Research I should probably minute an objection."

"Then minute it," says Pascual, and goes.

---

## The eighteenth to the twenty-first

Four days, in stages, because the water at the seaward end is not warm at that hour and one of them is fifty in October and the other is fifty-three.

Hendy rigs a line and a float so that there is something to hold, and is told he is a useful boy, and goes about for two days looking as though he has been knighted. Codrington, who is not asked and not invited, is on the sill from half past five every morning with a flask and hands things down and does not speak.

Chumley does not come near either of them for two days.

On the third day he comes up off the bottom and holds off about fifteen feet and stays there, and Pascual talks to him in Spanish for forty minutes and comes out unable to do his own buttons.

On the Sunday afternoon, which is the last shift before the boards come out, Alec Spilsbury goes down the ladder at four o'clock and works along to the seaward corner and puts his hand flat on the stone.

He taps twice.

Reggie is on the sill with Codrington and the second stopwatch and a sheet, and afterwards he will not be able to say how long it was. Codrington has it at four minutes and ten seconds, because Codrington had the watch and is exactly the sort of young man who starts one.

Then twice, on the stone, from about four feet away.

---

Alec puts his forehead against the wall of the north pen and stays there.

Nobody on that sill says one word, then or at any time afterwards. Codrington writes down 16.07 and the interval and does not write anything else, and that sheet goes into the file, and in about forty years somebody reading the primary record of the release will find four minutes and ten seconds of nothing at all in the middle of a Sunday afternoon and no explanation whatever.

---

## The twenty-second

They take the sill out at six in the morning on a falling tide, with Jean's 1961 sounding chart pinned to the gatepost under a stone, because it is the sounding chart that decided everything: animals through that channel in April, May and June, four separate sets of soundings, three of them hers and one of them the Colonel's boy's.

John Weald is on the far side of the gate with a plate camera he set up at a quarter to seven and has not exposed since, and nobody tells him to.

It takes nine minutes to get the boards out. It takes the animal fifty-five minutes to go and look at the gap, and another hour to put his head through it, and he comes back twice.

He goes out at twelve minutes past eight.

Nobody says anything at all. Codrington writes the time. Hendy is holding the second stopwatch and crying again and has stopped minding. Pascual has both hands on the rail and his shoulders up around his ears.

And Alec Spilsbury stands six feet back from the gate with his notebook shut in his hand, because he has worked out — Reggie watches him work it out, at about a quarter past seven, and watches him move — that the place to stand is where he cannot possibly be in the way, and that the man who should be at that gate is the veterinary surgeon who has had that animal for nine years — and that Sunday afternoon does not alter that by one inch, and is not a claim, and is not going to be made into one.

They provision the sill at nine. Nothing comes.

They provision it at four. Nothing comes.

---

## The twenty-third, four in the morning

Reggie wakes at half past three and the lamp is not on.

He finds him at the seaward end of the north pen, sitting on the coping stone in the dark with the notebook open on his knee and the pencil in his hand, not writing.

Reggie sits down on the stone about a foot away.

"How long have you been here?"

"Since about one."

"Have you written anything?"

"No."

"Give me the book."

"No," says Alec.

So they sit. The water in the empty pen does the thing it does, which is to sound exactly like the water outside the pen and to be twelve feet across.

"Alec."

"I cannot write the entry."

"Because of the animal?"

"The animal is a separate matter and the animal is going very much as I expected and I have written that four times this week." He is looking at the gap in the wall. "I cannot write the entry because I do not know the number."

---

Reggie sits with that for a moment, and then gets it, and wishes he had not.

"You count the days."

"I have counted the days of every fortnight since 1958. Elapsed, for the first four years; and from the eleventh of July last year, downward, which I noticed and recorded and did not interpret." The pencil goes round once in his fingers. "This is day ten. I know it is day ten. Day ten of what I do not know, because there is no form, and there was no application, and there is no date on the other end of it."

"There's the fifth of May. That's your boat."

"That is the date I leave. I am asking for the other one."

---

And Reggie Tar, who has been reading this man across tables and benches and four feet of water since the November of 1946, and who does it the way other men breathe and has never had to be told to, sits on a coping stone in the dark and says the thing he has had in his mouth since Hester put the half-sheet on the table on the ninth.

"Paragraph nine."

"No."

"I haven't said it yet."

"You have said quite enough of it. It is a false analogy and it is the cheapest one available." Alec says it fast, and flat, and entirely without heat, which is how Reggie knows. "The provision concerns a wild animal in a licensed pen. The figure is derived from a mortality curve and from nothing else in the world. To set the two numbers beside one another is to let a number mean a thing it does not mean, and I spent four hundred and eleven pages refusing to do that, and I am not going to do it at four in the morning on a wall."

"All right."

"It is a bad analogy."

"All right."

"I have been striking it out," says Alec Spilsbury, "since the fourteenth of July."

---

The pencil stops going round.

"Say that again."

"I struck it out in the schoolroom at the Bay, at about half past five in the afternoon, with a glass in my hand, four seconds after Codrington shouted. It was the only observation I struck out that fortnight and I was correct to strike it." He has not moved. "I struck it out again in Ålesund in September, and in the October, and twice in February, and I did not write any of those down, and a man who is striking out the same observation six times in nine months and not entering the fact that he keeps having to is not doing science, he is keeping house."

"Alec —"

"I cannot do another twenty years of this at twelve days a year."

---

There it is, on a wall, in the dark, on the twenty-third of April.

"I am going to say the rest of it in order," says Alec. "I have had since about the October to get it into order and I should like to do it properly, because I am not going to be able to do it twice."

"All right."

"One. The annex is finished." He puts the notebook down on the stone and squares it, which takes him longer than squaring a notebook requires. "It went into a schedule on the first of January and it will not want doing again, and it was the only thing since 1957 that was mine, and I have nothing after it except a station in a town where I know nine people by name and like three of them. That is not a complaint. It is a good station and I am well paid."

"I know."

"Two. The fortnight is the whole of my year. I did not intend that and I did not watch it happen, and it is an unsound way for a man of fifty-three to —"

He stops.

---

It is not a pause. Reggie has sat across benches and tables and four feet of dirty water from this man for seventeen years and he knows every one of the pauses, and this is not one of them.

"Alec."

"Three is —"

"Alec."

"I have got three," says Alec Spilsbury. "I have had three since the October. It is perfectly straightforward and it will take about forty seconds."

And then he does not say it.

---

What he does instead, after about a quarter of a minute, is put both hands over his face, which Reggie has seen him do twice: once in a corridor at the Labs in 1949, and once in the hut at the top of the row in the February of 1957, and not since.

"I cannot do twenty years of it."

It does not come out in order. It comes out fast and in the wrong places and with the back of it first — the twelve days done six times and done well, and not unhappy, not in any way he could have entered in a column; and a woman at Ålesund who writes his messages down twice and is kind to him and whose name he cannot at this moment produce; and the counting downward, which he noticed in July and recorded and did not interpret, and has interpreted every night since the fourteenth; and *I could do another six if somebody would write down when, because eleven months is a quantity and a man can hold a quantity* —

— and then nothing for a while, on a wall, at four in the morning, with his hands over his face and his shoulders going.

---

Reggie puts a hand flat between them.

"Don't," says Alec.

"No."

"I mean it. Do not be kind to me for about two minutes, because I shall not get through the other side of it if you are."

"I heard you," says Reggie, and does not take the hand away, and does not say anything else either.

So they sit like that for some considerable time, on the coping stone of an empty pen, with the water going in and out through a gap in a wall they took the boards out of yesterday morning.

---

"I am sorry about that," says Alec, eventually.

"No."

"It was not —"

"*No.*" Reggie has got his voice down about half an octave and it is not steady. "You are fifty-three years old and you have been in that water four days and you have just told me the truth about the last six years, and you are not going to apologise to me for the state of your face. Not tonight, and not on a form in the spring either."

Alec takes his hands down.

"That is the most English sentence you have ever produced."

"I know it is."

"It was a very good sentence."

"It was a terrible sentence," says Reggie, "and I would have done better on paper," and Alec Spilsbury makes a noise that is not quite a laugh and puts his head back against the stone.

---

"Say the third one."

"I have said it."

"You've said it in about nine pieces with your hands over your face. Say it once, in order, the way you wanted to, and I'll sit here till you do."

The water goes in and out.

"I did six years of the twelve days and I could do another six if somebody would write down when," says Alec Spilsbury. "I cannot do twenty. I have been trying to since about the October and I am not able to."

"And if I hadn't sent the cable."

"Then I should have gone on not saying it."

"For how long."

Alec looks at the gap in the wall.

"I have thought about that and I have an answer and I do not like it," he says. "I should not have said it at all. One spring the application would not have gone in. There would have been a reason — a survey, or the boats, or a man ill at the station — and it would have been a perfectly good reason, and I should have written you a civil letter about it in the April and another one in the June, and the year after that it would have been easier."

The water goes in and out.

"That's how it ends."

"That is how it ends," says Alec Spilsbury. "Not in twenty years. In about four, on a form, with an excellent reason."

---

Reggie does not answer that, because what he is doing is running it forward himself and arriving: April 1967, a civil letter regretting that the station cannot spare him this year; a second one in the June; Hester entering both in the book without comment and not asking him about either.

"Say it again."

"I would rather not."

"Say it again, Alec."

"One spring the form would not have gone in."

"And I'd have let you."

Alec turns his head.

"That's the part you've left out." Reggie can hear his own voice doing something and cannot get hold of it. "I would have written back. I would have been *understanding about it.* And I would not have got on a boat, because there has never been a piece of paper anywhere that entitled me to get on one, and I would have sat in that office being reasonable at you for the rest of my life."

"Yes."

"Don't say *yes* like that."

"It is a finding," says Alec Spilsbury, "and I did not have it until about ninety seconds ago. I have been at this arithmetic since the October and that is the one piece I did not do." He turns the notebook over on the stone. "And I have now done it out loud, in front of a witness, and I should like that noted, because it means I cannot strike it out in the morning."

---

Reggie says, "Then I'll resign."

He says it inside four seconds. He hears himself say it and he means every syllable of it and he is already, in the back of his head, drafting the letter.

"No."

"Alec, I can be out of this by —"

"*No.*" It is the loudest thing that has happened on this island since the wedding. "That is exactly what I have spent six years not saying it in order to avoid, and you have done it in four seconds, and if you do it I shall have taken the only thing you have built out of your hands and I shall have done it by being tired on a wall at four in the morning."

"That isn't —"

"It is precisely that." He gets his hands round the notebook. "I have watched you run a station for eight years and I have watched you put a species on a schedule, and I have been the most useful thing I have been in my life, by post, because of it, and I am not going to be the reason it stops. I should be no good to you afterwards and you know it. You would have me and no work, and I have seen what that produces. It produced the man on this wall."

Reggie sits with his elbows on his knees.

"All right," he says.

"Say it properly."

"I'm not going to resign."

"Thank you."

---

And then Reggie Tar does the thing he does, which is the best thing about him and which has cost more people more than any other single fact in his life.

He solves it.

He sits on that wall for another forty minutes and he is, by about ten past five, no longer entirely in the conversation; and at half past five he takes the man inside and gets him to sleep, which takes four minutes and is the easiest thing he has done all week, because sixty-one hours of aeroplanes and six days of section four have to go somewhere.

And then he goes up to the office and lights the lamp and has it drafted by seven.

---

## The twenty-third, breakfast

The male is at the sill at eight minutes past seven and takes six fish off the gaff, and Hendy comes up the path at a dead run, which he has been told not to do, and nobody cares.

So it is a very good breakfast.

And Reggie Tar stands up at the long table with four sheets in his hand, in front of Hester and the Colonel and Pascual and three students and the man himself, and reads out the Martin Institute Release and Monitoring Programme 1963–65: two years, costed, with the boat time in it and the fish in it and Jean's soundings in it, and a post at the head of it — Field Director, resident, two years certain, from the first of October — and the name of the only qualified person in the world already typed into the line.

Hester says, "The money is available and I can have the second half of it by Michaelmas."

The Colonel says, "Well. Yes. Obviously."

Codrington says something that gets him sat on by Hendy.

And Alec Spilsbury sits at the end of the long table with his hands flat either side of his plate and says, "That is an extremely good programme and section six is wrong about the boat time," and then answers four questions about the fish, accurately, and eats his breakfast, and goes down to the pen at twenty past eight to do the morning's work.

Reggie, watching him go, thinks it has gone rather well.

---

## The twenty-third, the path

Pascual catches him at the top of the path at nine and does not bother with any of the preliminaries.

"What did he say when you asked him?"

"He said section six was wrong about the boat time."

"No." Pascual has a bucket in each hand and puts them both down. "When you *asked* him. Before. Last night, this morning, whenever you wrote the thing. What did he say?"

Reggie opens his mouth.

"*Ay, Dios*," says Pascual Pobil.

"Pascual —"

"You didn't ask him."

"I solved it."

"You solved it. Four sheets. In front of the Colonel and three students and a boy who came out last year." He has gone entirely quiet, which is much worse than the other thing. "That man sat on a wall at four in the morning and gave you the worst of it, whatever it was, and I don't want to know what it was — and you had it turned into a memorandum with a heading by seven and read it out over the eggs."

"It's a good programme."

"It's the best thing you've written in three years and that's not the point and you know it isn't." Pascual picks the buckets up. "You did this in 1954. Same thing exactly. You went straight past the man at the problem, and it was a good method then as well, and we put a boy in the water over it."

Reggie stands on the path.

"That's not fair."

"It's not fair and it's true and you can have both." He is already going. "Go and ask him what he wants. Not what he needs, hombre. What he *wants*. And then shut up for about a minute and a half, which will nearly kill you."

---

## The twenty-third, the long room

He goes down at six with the four sheets and puts them on the bench and does not sit.

"I want to take this back."

Alec looks up.

"It is a good programme."

"It's a good programme and I wrote it at somebody." He has his spectacles in his hand and does not know it. "You told me a thing on that wall that took you six years to get to."

"It did."

"And I had it costed inside two hours and read it out over the eggs, and I did not ask you one question." He puts the spectacles down on the bench. "I am not going to do that to you again."

"You have gone formal."

"I have," says Reggie. "I do that. Ask Charles Cholmondeley."

---

"Sit down," says Alec, after a moment, "because this is going to be embarrassing and I would rather you were not standing up for it."

He sits.

"Three things, and they are not objections to the programme."

"Go on."

"One. I will not be appointed to a post that has my name typed into it already." He holds a hand up. "Do not tell me it is a formality. I have been a qualified person on a form five times, and the form is the reason I was permitted up that jetty, and it is the only thing in seventeen years that I have had off you that I got by being fit for it. Advertise it. Three journals, eight weeks, a panel with the Colonel in the chair, and if somebody better applies you will appoint them and I will hear about it in a letter."

"Nobody better is going to apply."

"Then that will be established by a panel and not asserted by you," says Alec Spilsbury, "and I shall have it for the rest of my life."

"Two?"

"Two is the date. Two years certain was correct and I should like a review in the twenty-second month, in writing, with the Secretary in the room, and an end on the paper." He turns the sheets round. "I know what you are about to say and I know why you would rather leave it open. Do not. I have spent nine months finding out what I am like without a date on the other end, and so have you, and neither of us cared for it."

"No," says Reggie. "I did not."

"Then put the date on."

"And three."

---

Alec is quiet for slightly too long.

"Three is that you will ask Pascual Pobil, in private, with nobody else in the building and no programme in your hand, whether he wants me here for two years; and you will ask him in such a way that he is able to say no."

"Alec —"

"He was at that table on the thirtieth of May 1956 and he heard all of it in order, and he has been decent to me for twelve days a year since 1958, which is an easy span to be decent across." Alec puts the pencil down. "Two years is not twelve days. It is his station, he has a wife at the Bay and a practice to be married into, and he is entitled to be asked before a man he has good reason to think badly of moves into the hut at the top of the row until 1965."

"He'll say yes."

"Then he will have said it," says Alec, "which is a different thing from not having been asked."

---

## The twenty-fourth

Reggie gets him on his own at the north pen at half past six in the morning, before anybody is up, which is the only hour on this island when nobody is.

He asks him properly. It takes him about ninety seconds and he does not do it well, and at the end of it he says, "And you can say no, and if you say no that's the end of it and I'll not raise it again and I'll not be odd with you about it."

Pascual Pobil looks at the water for a while.

"He put you up to this."

"He made it a condition."

"Of course he did." He rubs a hand over his face. "*Ay.* Yes. Bring him."

"Pascual, I want you to actually —"

"I've actually thought about it, I thought about it in about 1958, and I've had it ready ever since in case anybody ever had the sense to ask me." He picks up the gaff. "Yes. Bring him. Two years, the hut at the top, and he does the four o'clock on Thursdays like everybody else and he can take his turn with Hendy and the tide gauge."

"That's it?"

"That's it. And one more thing, and then we'll do the fish."

"Go on."

"I don't forgive him," says Pascual. "He knows I don't, he agrees I shouldn't, and we've had a perfectly good arrangement about it for seven years. That doesn't change because he's got a desk." He puts the gaff over his shoulder. "And I'm not having him miserable in my north pen for two years either, so you'd better sort out the other business."

"What other business?"

"The hut at the top of the row has one window on the path," says Pascual Pobil, "and I've been looking at it since 1958."

Reggie does not say anything.

"Nine months of the year there's a light in that window at one in the morning. The twelve days he's here it goes out at half past ten, like a Christian's." He picks the gaff up off the boards. "I've been reading that window twice a night for five years, hombre, and I'd like to stop. Bring him."

"Pascual —"

"Don't. It's twenty to seven and I haven't had my coffee." He starts down the ladder. "And you can tell him that if he goes near my drug cupboard I'll put him in the water."

---

## The night of the twenty-fourth

The lamp is on, because the lamp has been on since October 1958.

"You are looking at me."

"I'm allowed. You've just given me two years of it."

"A panel has given you nothing yet, and the advertisement has not been drafted —"

"Alec."

"— and there are four men in this field who could do the post and two of them are younger than I am."

Reggie gets a hand flat in the middle of his chest and holds him there, on his back, on a bed in a hut at the top of the row, and looks at him for rather a long time, and does not say anything at all, which works.

"Stop," says Alec. "Please stop doing that."

"No."

"You have been doing it since 1946 and I have not got a defence for it and I am not going to develop one now."

"Good," says Reggie.

---

He takes the wrists first. One in each hand, up, above the man's head, against the iron, which they have done since the March of 1947 and which neither of them has ever once discussed in daylight; and he holds them there and feels the whole of him stop resisting it in about a second and a half, which is the thing, which has always been the thing.

"Ask me," says Alec.

"I know."

"Once. You know how I —"

"I know how you want it. I've known since November of 1950." Reggie gets his mouth on the place under the jaw where the pulse is and stays there while he says it, so that it goes in at four inches. "Do you want this."

"Yes."

"Say it properly. You made me say it properly on a wall."

"I want it," says Alec Spilsbury. "I have wanted it since about Thursday afternoon and I have been extremely well behaved about it, and I should like you to be quick and I should like you not to be, and I am aware those are incompatible."

"They're not incompatible. They're sequential."

"That is —" And then he laughs, once, helplessly, with his wrists up, and Reggie feels it go all the way through the man's chest and under his own hands and thinks, with complete clarity: *that. That is what a year of nothing costs. Nobody in Ålesund has heard that noise.*

---

He is not quick.

He takes the shoulder that has the old strain in it and does it slowly with his thumb until it gives, which takes a while and which gets him a noise that was not approved first; he gets the collarbone, and the hollow under it, and the flat place over the ribs on the left where a man of fifty-three is thinner than he was in 1946 and where Reggie can feel the whole heart going like something running. He keeps the wrists the entire time. He puts his mouth on the inside of one of them, on the veins, for about four seconds, and Alec makes a sound and says his name once, and that is the only word either of them gets out for some minutes.

"You have got to stop counting," says Reggie, into his neck, later.

"I am not —"

"You're counting. I can feel you doing it." He puts his forehead down against the man's jaw. "The first of October. Three hundred and forty-two days into two years certain. That's the number. Put it down somewhere and stop carrying it."

"It is not on a form yet."

"It'll be on a form in eight weeks and you'll have applied for it like a qualified person, and Hester will read it out at that table and ask me if you're qualified, and I shall say you appear to be." He gets a hand into the hair at the back of the man's head and holds on. "And then you can have the hut and the four o'clock on Thursdays."

"What did he actually say. This morning."

"That he's been looking at the window of this hut since 1958. That nine months of the year there's a light in it at one in the morning, and the twelve days you're here it goes out at half past ten." Reggie does not move his hand. "He says he'd like to stop reading it."

Alec does not say anything for a while.

"That is an observation," he says, eventually, "kept twice a night for five years by a man who was not asked to keep it and had nothing whatever to gain by it, and I find I have nothing to say about it at all."

"No."

"I should like the lamp out."

"No," says Reggie. "Lamp stays on."

And Alec Spilsbury puts his face into Reggie Tar's shoulder, on a Wednesday night in April, and does something for about a minute and a half that Reggie does not look at and does not mention, and then says, into the shirt, in an entirely level voice: "I should like it noted that the objection to section six still stands."

"Noted."

"He is wrong about the boat time."

"He is *not* wrong about the boat time, and you are the worst man I have ever —"

"Lamp," says Alec.

"No."

"It was worth another attempt," says Alec Spilsbury, and puts his head down.

---

## Coda: the second of August

The advertisement runs in three journals in the third week of May, eight weeks, panel of three, Colonel Bevan in the chair, Mrs H. Bevan as secretary to the panel and taking the minute.

Four applications. One from a young man at Plymouth who is not ready, and who is told so in writing by the panel, at Spilsbury's insistence and in Spilsbury's words, with the reasons set out and a list of what to do about it. One entirely unqualified. One from a man in Aberdeen with excellent field release experience and no interest whatever in the species, who is interviewed for fifty minutes by post and found wanting on exactly the ground Pascual predicted in June, which Pascual has not stopped mentioning.

And one that comes up from the Bay in the ordinary bag on the second of August, which Hester puts on the long table at breakfast with the letter opener still in her hand.

"Dr Pobil, you are a member of the panel. Dr Tar, you are the Director of Research and you are an interested party, and I intend to watch you declare it."

"Declared," says Reggie, and goes on with his egg.

"It is not a joke and it is going in the minute."

"Read it out, Hester."

"*Application for the post of Field Director, Release and Monitoring Programme,*" says Hester Bevan. "*Applicant: A. Spilsbury, Ph.D., Statens Havforskningsstasjon, Ålesund. Sixteen pages, with a list of publications and three referees, one of whom is a Norwegian fisheries inspector and none of whom is anybody in this room.*"

"Qualified?" says the Colonel.

Hester turns the last page over and looks at it for slightly longer than she needs to.

"He appears to be qualified," she says.

"Then he may have it," says Pascual Pobil, "in the ordinary way," and Reggie puts his cup down before anything can happen to it, and Codrington, who has been on this island two years and has learned precisely nothing about when to keep quiet, says: "Sir — does that mean he's *staying*?"

"Two years certain," says Hester, and puts the form in the cabinet, and locks the cabinet.

---

Chumley comes to the sill at seven on the second of August, as he has on ninety-one mornings out of a hundred and two, and takes nothing, because since the middle of June he has mostly not needed to.

Miss Pryce, who went back to Bangor and came out again in July for the season on Jean Leslie's money, with an expression that dares anybody to mention it, writes down the time and the tide and the six minutes he stays.

And on the eleventh day of that month, at four in the afternoon, at a distance of about nine hundred yards off the north point, there are two of them.
