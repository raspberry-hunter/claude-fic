# Change of Address

## The chances

It is worth saying, because both of them will say it to themselves for years, that the chances came and were correctly declined.

In May 1967 a man had a sentence at ten past ten in the morning and did not write it. In September 1970 two men had two hours at a table in London with a sounding chart between them, and it was the best professional conversation either had had in three years, and they shook hands in a lobby in front of forty people and one of them went home steadier. In January 1971 a veterinary officer came up a path with a dead lamp in his hand and said every word of it, and the Director of Research agreed with every word, and thanked him, and meant it, and then there was a vacancy in the release post to be filled and the spring was heavy, and he wrote a warmer letter to Norway instead of getting on a boat.

Pascual Pobil did not raise it again. He had said he would not.

The letters went on. Monthly, without a gap, for eight more years. They were very good letters.

---

## Ålesund, the thirty-first of January, 1975

The station gives him a dinner.

It is held upstairs at the Scandic because that is where the station has always held its dinners, and there are thirty-one people, including a man from the Ministry who makes a speech that is almost entirely accurate, and Haugland, who has had the station since the first of the month and is going to be very good at it, and who makes a speech that is entirely accurate and rather short, and then sits down and looks at his plate for some time.

They give him a barometer. It is a good barometer and he will keep it.

Solveig does not make a speech. At the end of the evening, when he is getting his coat, she hands him an envelope in which there is a single sheet of the station's paper with a list on it in her hand: every date on which a letter came to the station from the William Martin Institute, the Bay, addressed in one particular hand, from the first of March 1957 to the twenty-second of January 1975.

Four hundred and nine.

"I kept the post book," says Solveig, in English. "It seemed to me somebody should have the figure."

He puts it in his inside pocket and cannot say anything at all, and she nods, as though he had, and goes down the stairs.

---

## The first week of February

He is sixty-five and he has no employment and he has two rooms above an office that is no longer his office, and for about four days he does the thing he always said he would do, which is to be no good to anybody.

He walks the north shore on the Sunday, four miles in the dark with the lamp, and back.

On the Tuesday he sits at the desk under the window and takes the drawer on the left out entirely and puts it on the desk and looks at what is in it.

Eight envelopes. 1967 to 1974. Each one completed, signed, dated the nineteenth of April, addressed in his own hand to Mrs H. Bevan, Secretary. None stamped.

He has not filled in a ninth. He did not sit down at this desk last April and he does not know why; nothing happened; it was a Friday and he went for a walk. He has been careful not to examine it.

He examines it now.

---

It takes him about a week, and it arrives, when it arrives, not as a feeling but as a structural observation, which is the only way anything has ever arrived for him.

The form was the problem.

Not the distance. Not the twelve days. The form. Every mechanism that has governed the whole of this since 1958 has run through an institution: a qualified person not being a member of the staff; a Secretary who enters things; a section of an annual return; a cabinet; a board that might ask. The form made it possible for him to go, and the same form made it impossible for the other man to follow, and made the whole of it a pattern in a file, and made both of them careful for seventeen years.

And he is retired.

There is no institution at this end of the correspondence any longer. There is a pension from the Norwegian state, a barometer, and two rooms he is entitled to for another ninety days.

A retired man may live anywhere he likes.

He requires nobody's leave to choose where. He fills in no form to do it. Nobody minutes it and nobody enters it and nobody will ever call for the file, because there is no file. There is a lease.

---

He gets up from the desk and goes to the window and stands there for some time, and then he says, aloud, in an empty room in Ålesund, in English, "Oh, *God*," which he has not said since 1956.

Then he goes and gets the atlas.

---

## The letter

It takes him four days and it is the most carefully constructed document of his life, including the annex.

He posts it on the fourteenth of February.

---

*Reggie,*

*I have retired. Haugland has the station and is going to be very good at it. The Ministry gave a dinner and I was given a barometer.*

*I propose to take a house at the Bay from the first of April.*

*I set out the particulars below, because you will want them and because I have them.*

*There is a house on the Pelican road, the third above the Customs, which the agent at the Bay describes as having two bedrooms, a verandah on the harbour side, a water tank recently relined, and "a quantity of books left by the previous tenant, a clergyman." The rent is nine pounds a month. I have the agent's letter and I have written back and taken it for a year from the first of April, and I have paid a quarter in advance, and I have the receipt. None of this is conditional on anything you say in reply.*

*The Norwegian state pension is payable abroad. The mail boat to the cay leaves the Bay at seven on Tuesdays and Thursdays and returns at half past four, and the Saturday boat leaves at eight and comes back at noon, as I imagine you know better than I do.*

*I anticipate your objection and I should like to answer it before you make it, because otherwise you will spend a fortnight composing it and I would rather you did not.*

*Your objection is that I cannot be at the Bay because the Bay is ten miles from the Institute and it will be seen. It will not. A retired Englishman who takes a house in the West Indies is the least remarkable object in the Caribbean; there are, by the agent's account, nine of them between the Customs and the church, two of whom are admirals. I have no employment at the Martin, no post, no associateship, no liaison, and I am not going to have any. I have no connection with your Institute on paper of any kind. I have a lease.*

*As for your crossing to the Bay on a Saturday: you have done that since 1954. The bank is there, and the post, and the Pobils' table. Nobody has ever looked at it twice and nobody is going to begin now.*

*You will next say that I shall have no work, and that I have told you myself what that produces. That is correct, and I have thought about it. There is a quantity of material deposited at your Institute that nobody has had time to put in order since 1956, and a qualified person resident at the Bay may apply to consult it in the ordinary way, as anybody may. I shall do so. I should be very much surprised if Mrs Bevan found anything in the application to remark upon, except the address.*

*I have not written to ask whether I may come. I am writing to tell you where I shall be. I am sixty-five years of age and I have spent eighteen years applying for things, and I have found that I do not wish to apply for this.*

*I should like it to be soon. I should like that noted.*

*Yours,*

*A.*

---

## The Martin, the third of March

Hester Bevan brings the post up at ten and puts it on the long table and goes away again, which she does on three hundred days out of three hundred and ten, because the thin foolscap envelope from Ålesund on the top of the pile looks exactly like the four hundred and nine thin foolscap envelopes that have preceded it.

Reggie Tar is sixty, and has been Director of Research of the William Martin Institute for twenty-one years, and has been reading this man's handwriting across four thousand miles for twenty-eight, and he reads the bottom of it first because he has done that since 1946.

*Yours, A.*

He has not seen that since September 1970. It was in two letters and then it stopped, and he did not ask why, and it did not come back.

He reads the top.

---

The first thing he does is object.

It is an extremely good objection and he has it fully formed inside about forty seconds: the Bay is a small place, the Institute is ten miles off, there are three hundred people in that town who know his face, and a man of Spilsbury's history taking a house there is a thing that will be *noticed*—

And then he turns the page and finds it answered in advance, in the man's own hand, at length, in the order he would have put it.

He reads it twice.

Then he gets up from the long table and goes into the office and takes the diary off the shelf. Not this year's. The whole run. 1954 to 1975.

---

It takes him about twenty minutes, standing at the shelf with the diaries open one after another.

Saturday. The Bay. Bank. Post. Pobil.

Saturday. The Bay. Post. Pobil. The Colonel.

Saturday. The Bay. Chandler, the boat account. Pobil.

Nine hundred and something Saturdays, give or take the hurricanes. Every one of them entered. Every one of them seen by everybody at the Bay, the post office clerk and the bank and Alleyne on the boat and the woman who sells fish at the steps; and not one living soul has thought about any of them for one second in twenty-one years.

He puts the last diary back on the shelf, and stands there with his hand on it.

*Nobody has ever looked at it twice and nobody is going to begin now.*

It was never the Saturday. It was never the Bay. It was the form, and the file, and a man coming up a jetty on paper. He has known that in the abstract since about 1958 and has never once stood at a shelf and counted the Saturdays.

---

Hester finds him there at a quarter to eleven.

"Dr Tar."

"I need to send a cable."

"There is a form for that."

"I know there's a form for that, Hester." He turns round. "I'd be grateful if you'd send it yourself. Now, if you would. It's four words."

She holds her hand out for it.

He writes it on the pad on the desk and tears the sheet off and gives it to her, and Hester Bevan reads it, and then reads the bottom of it for rather longer than the top, which is the only part of it anybody could possibly remark upon.

*YES. FIRST APRIL. REGGIE.*

He has signed cables *TAR* since 1938.

"It is a correct cable," says Hester, eventually, and goes down with it, and on the stairs she stops for a moment with her hand on the rail and thinks about an office door left ajar and a veterinary officer with a dead lamp saying *sign it Reggie* four years ago, which she was not supposed to have heard and did, and which went nowhere, and which has just arrived.

---

## The first of April

The mail boat from Kingston is due at a quarter past three and is twenty minutes late, and Reggie Tar stands at the top of the steps at the Bay for the whole of the twenty minutes with his hands in his pockets, in front of the fish woman and the Customs and Alleyne's nephew and about forty other people, none of whom has the faintest interest in him.

He comes down the gangway last, with two cases and a deed box and a barometer.

He is sixty-five. He is thinner than in London. He is wearing the loud tie.

"You're wearing the tie."

"It is the only tie I possess that the Norwegian state has not seen," says Alec Spilsbury, "and I thought it would be improper to arrive in one it had."

"I thought it was a Christmas present from the office."

"It was a Christmas present from the office. The office was Solveig. She has since told me what was being implied and I am now obliged to wear it for the rest of my life."

---

They do not touch. It is the steps at the Bay at a quarter to four on a Tuesday.

They carry the cases up the Pelican road between them, three houses above the Customs, and it is a small white house with a green door and a verandah on the harbour side and a relined tank and about four hundred books left by a clergyman, and Alec puts the barometer down on the table in the front room and stands and looks at it.

"There are nine pounds a month in this," he says. "And I have a receipt."

"I know you have. You said so in the letter."

"I should like to say so again, out loud, in the room," says Alec Spilsbury. "Nobody has any claim on this house but me. It is not the Institute's and it is not yours and nobody applied for it. I rented it from a man in an office above a chandler's and I paid him in advance."

"I know."

"I have never had a thing in my life I did not have to apply for," he says, "since about 1938."

---

Reggie has to go back on the half past four. It is a Tuesday and there is a board paper due on the Wednesday and he is the Director of Research and he has been exactly as good at that job for twenty-one years as it is possible to be, and that is not going to stop because a man has taken a house on the Pelican road.

That is the arrangement. Both of them understood it from the first page.

"Friday," says Reggie, at the door.

"I know when Friday is."

"I'm telling you anyway."

"Then I am glad to be told," says Alec, "and you will miss your boat, and I have waited eight and a half years, and I can manage three more days, and I should prefer not to be made to watch you run down that road."

---

## Friday

It is the fourth of April, and Reggie Tar takes the Friday boat to the Bay at half past four in the ordinary way, as he has on something over nine hundred Fridays, and walks up from the steps past the Customs, and turns up the Pelican road instead of going on to the Pobils', which is the only difference anybody could have noted and which nobody does.

The green door is open.

Alec Spilsbury is on the verandah in his shirtsleeves with the harbour in front of him and a book of the clergyman's on his knee, not reading it.

He stands up.

"You came up the road."

"I came up the road," says Reggie.

"I heard you from the Customs. You have a very particular walk. I have heard it on a jetty for eighteen years and I heard it on the road from about a hundred yards off and I have been sitting here with this book open at the same page since ten past five."

---

He goes in first, and Reggie shuts the green door behind them, and they are in the front room with the barometer on the table and nobody on earth with the smallest claim on any of it.

"There is something I should like to give you," says Alec. "Before anything. I should like it done first."

He goes to the deed box on the shelf and opens it, and it has in it four hundred and nine letters in their envelopes, in order, and on top of them, squared, eight other envelopes, which he takes out and puts on the table in a row.

1967. 1968. 1969. 1970. 1971. 1972. 1973. 1974.

"Those are yours," says Alec Spilsbury. "They were always addressed to your Institute. I filled one in every April, at the desk, in about eleven minutes, and did not post it, and I should like you to have them, because they have been in a drawer in Norway for eight years with nobody to read them and you are the only person to whom they are of any interest whatsoever."

---

Reggie Tar picks up the first one.

He does not open it. He does not need to. He knows the form; he signed the minute that created it. He turns it over and looks at the date in the man's hand, *19.iv.67*, and at the address, and at the fact that there is no stamp.

Then he picks up the second one. Then the third.

It takes him a long time to get to 1974, and he does not say anything at all during any of it, and Alec stands by the table with his hands behind his back and does not say anything either, and there is a boat going out of the harbour and somebody laughing down by the Customs.

"You filled them in every year," says Reggie, finally.

"Until last year."

"And didn't post them."

"And did not post them," says Alec. "Because the letter I sent instead was true every year, and the form would have been true as well, and I could not see a way to post both. It seemed to me that one of them had to be the one that was false and I could never decide which."

---

Reggie puts the eighth one down on top of the other seven.

"May 1967," he says. "I had your letter at ten past ten in the morning. I knew inside ten minutes that the reason you'd given me wasn't the reason."

Alec goes very still.

"And I didn't write and ask. Not because I didn't have the sentence. I had the whole sentence. I'd have put *you went in 1964 with a chartering dispute on* and *tell me the actual reason*. I had it word for word and I've had it every day since." He is looking at the envelopes. "I didn't write it because I was afraid you'd answer *I have had enough*, and then I'd have had it in your hand in a file for the rest of my life."

"I had not had enough."

"I can see that." He puts a hand flat on the eight envelopes. "I have been able to see it for about the last four minutes."

"Reggie—"

"Eight years, Alec. I could have had you back in any one of eight Aprils for one sentence and a stamp."

"You could," says Alec Spilsbury, "and so could I, for a stamp, and I did not, and we are going to be quite precise about the fact that it was both of us, and then we are going to stop being precise about it, because it is a Friday and I have been standing on this floor since ten past five."

---

## Later

The lamp is on, because the lamp has been on since October 1958 and a small white house on the Pelican road is not going to be the first place in seventeen years where it is not.

"You are looking at me."

"I'm allowed."

"I am sixty-five."

"I know you are. I'm sixty, which is close enough to be alarming, and I've been told I'm hard to stand next to, which I find hurtful." Reggie is on one elbow, and has been for some time, and has a hand flat in the middle of him and has not moved it. "London. That was the last time I was this close to you, across a table, and I've thought about it every day since and the table was the main thing I thought about."

"I am thinner."

"You're thinner and you've got a scar I don't know."

"The gall bladder. March of 1970."

"I know," says Reggie. "I found out at a drinks table from a man called Berge, and I said *much better, thank you*, and I've never told you that."

Alec Spilsbury looks at him for rather a long moment.

"Then that goes in the precise part," he says, "with the envelopes. And we shall not do it tonight."

---

"Ask me," says Alec.

"I know."

"Once. You know how—"

"I know exactly how. I've known since November of 1950 and I've known it every day for eight years with nothing whatever to do with it." Reggie takes the wrists — one in each hand, up, against the headboard of a bed that belonged to a clergyman, which has never once been used for anything of the kind and which neither of them can think about for more than a second without laughing — and holds them there. "What do you want."

"I want to be kept," says Alec Spilsbury.

---

It is not what he meant to say. Reggie can see him hear it.

"I meant — I intended to say something about the — I had a perfectly good answer prepared, it was about the shoulder—"

"Say the first one again."

"I want to be kept," says Alec, with his wrists up and his face gone entirely to pieces for about a second and a half, "which is not a thing a man of sixty-five says, and I withdraw it."

"You don't. It's entered."

"You have not got a book."

"I'll get one," says Reggie Tar. "I will go out tomorrow morning to the stationer's by the Customs and buy a book, and it'll be the first thing in it."

---

He is not quick.

He is not quick because there is no boat to catch and no fortnight running out and no date on the far end of any of it, and he finds, somewhere around the shoulder with the old strain in it, that he does not know how to be slow on purpose without a clock behind him and has to learn, and that learning is the best thing that has happened to him in eight years.

He takes the shoulder and works it under his thumb until it gives, and gets a noise that was not approved first. He takes the collarbone and the hollow under it. He finds the scar with his mouth, on the right side, under the ribs, a clean line four inches long, and stays there, and Alec Spilsbury says his name very quietly and not in any order.

"That's mine as well now."

"It is not *yours*, it is a surgeon's in Ålesund—"

"It's mine. I found out about it at a drinks table. I've had it longer than you have, in every sense that matters." He does not move his mouth. "Keep still."

"I am keeping still."

"You are *not* keeping still, you've been not keeping still since about the collarbone, and I've got both your hands and I'm not letting go of either of them."

And Alec laughs — the whole of him, with his wrists up and his eyes shut — and says, "No, I know you are not," and Reggie feels it go through him and thinks, clearly and for the first time since the July of 1966: *there. That noise. Nobody in Ålesund had it. Nobody at a drinks table. That's mine.*

---

He keeps the wrists the whole time. He asks twice, because he always has, and both times he is told to go on in a voice that is losing its grip by degrees. At some point Alec Spilsbury says, "Friday," as though it were the answer to something, and then says it again, and then does not say anything recognisable for some time.

And afterwards — rather a long time afterwards, with the harbour lights going out one by one below the verandah and the lamp still on — Reggie Tar lies with his face against a man's shoulder in a clergyman's bed on the Pelican road, and feels a hand come up and settle in the back of his hair and stay there, and goes to sleep.

He sleeps for nine hours.

At some point in the first of them, Alec reaches over, without moving the other hand, and puts the lamp out.

---

## The form

He posts it on the eighth of April from the post office at the Bay, with a Jamaican stamp on it, which costs four pence.

Hester Bevan opens it at the long table on the tenth, in the ordinary bag, with the letter opener in her hand, and it is one foolscap sheet, the same foolscap sheet she designed in 1958, and she knows every line of it and could fill it in herself in the dark.

*Application to consult deposited material of the Institute by a qualified person not being a member of the staff.*

*Applicant: A. Spilsbury, Ph.D.*

*Present appointment: none.*

*Address:*

She stops there.

*The Pelican Road, the Bay.*

---

She has had nine of these, from 1958 to 1966, and eight blank Junes of *nil* in section four since. This is the tenth she has received and the eighteenth he has filled in, though she does not know the second figure and will not for some years.

*Material to be consulted: the whole of the deposited material relating to the 1956 series and to the Vigo returns, and the Bristol archive deposited in 1969, with a view to the preparation of a monograph. Duration: indefinite. Days of access required per week: two.*

"Dr Tar," says Hester Bevan.

"Declared," says Reggie, without looking up.

"It is going in the minute in those words and you will initial it."

"Read it out, Hester."

She reads it out. All of it, including the address. Pascual Pobil, at the end of the long table, puts his pen down about halfway through and does not pick it up again.

"Qualified?" says Pascual.

"He appears to be qualified," says Hester Bevan, and then, since there is nobody in the long room at that moment except the three of them: "Two days a week. The Tuesday and the Thursday boats. I shall enter it in section four in June, and the first line will say *one* for the first time in eight years, and I am going to enjoy that very much."

---

## Chumley, the fifteenth of May

He comes out on the Tuesday and Thursday boats and works in the long room from half past eight until four with the 1956 boxes, and within three weeks he is an entirely ordinary fixture of the building, in the way a man who is plainly doing something tedious with great care becomes invisible to everybody who is doing something else.

Chumley has been out since October 1964. He comes to the sill now perhaps six or seven times a year, at no hour anyone has been able to predict, and has not been since February.

On the Thursday of the third week, at twenty to four, Hendy comes up the path at a dead run, which he has been told not to do since 1962.

"Dr Pobil — sir — he's at the sill."

---

Everybody goes down. Alec goes last, and stops on the stone six feet back from the gate, which is where he has always understood a man in his position stands, and has stood, at this gate, on every occasion since 1958 that anything mattered.

The animal is lying in the shaded half at the seaward end with his head toward the gap. He is enormous. He is scarred along the left flank from something in the open water that nobody will ever know about, and he is old in the way Gamma was old in 1953, and he takes nothing, because he has not needed to for years; he simply lies there, at the sill, where he was fed as a calf, and looks at the people on the stone.

Pascual is at the rail with both hands on it.

He looks round, once, and sees where Alec is standing.

"Come here."

"I am perfectly well where I—"

"You're no good six feet back. You never were." He moves along the rail and makes a space at it, the width of a man. "He's been out ten and a half years, and you designed every day of it, and you have never once laid eyes on him. Come and look."

---

Alec Spilsbury comes to the rail.

He stands there beside the veterinary officer who has not forgiven him and never will, and looks down at an animal whose release he planned in a room in Ålesund in 1958 and wrote a twenty-one-day provisioning schedule for against the advice of the Director of Research, and after a while he puts one hand flat on the coping stone, and leaves it there, and does not say why, and nobody asks.

Chumley stays for fourteen minutes. Hendy has the watch. Then he goes out through the gap without any hurry at all.

"Thank you," says Alec.

"Don't. I didn't do it for you." Pascual takes his hands off the rail. "I did it because you'd have stood there six feet back for the rest of your life, and I'm sixty-one and I'm not watching that for another twenty years."

"I know you did not do it for me."

"Good," says Pascual Pobil, and goes up the path.

Reggie Tar, who has been on the far side of the gate for the whole fourteen minutes, has not said a single word, and does not, and will not ever be able to say why that is the moment of the whole year he would keep if he were allowed only one.

---

## Pascual, a Friday in June

It takes him ten weeks to have it certain, because he is a veterinary officer and he does not publish on one season's data.

He is on the cay four nights a week and at the Bay three, and he does the late round at eleven, down the path from the long room past the huts to the jetty, as he has done since 1958; and he has looked at the window of the hut at the top of the row every night he has been on the island for seventeen years because it is on the path and he cannot help it.

Monday, on. Tuesday, on. Wednesday, on. Thursday, on, at one in the morning, he can see it from the jetty.

Friday, dark.

Saturday, dark.

Sunday he comes back on the late boat, and it is on again at midnight.

Ten weeks. Not one exception. A periodicity of seven days with a dark phase of two.

---

At the four o'clock on the thirteenth of June, Hendy — who is thirty-five and is senior technician and still does not know when to keep quiet — says:

"Dr Pobil, you've been in an extraordinarily good mood for about a month."

"I've identified a cycle."

"In the animal?"

"In a light source," says Pascual Pobil, and returns to the returns.

"What light source?"

"A fixed one. On this island. With a period of seven days." He signs the sheet. "It's very regular. I've been keeping a chart. Two dark phases a week, Friday and Saturday, beginning about the first of April. Before that it had no dark phase at all for about eight and a half years."

Hendy looks blank. Miss Pryce, who is Dr Pryce now and is out for the season, looks at the ceiling. Hester Bevan, at the end of the table, does not look up and the corner of her mouth does something.

Reggie Tar, at the other end, says, "Pascual."

"What? It's science."

"It isn't science. You haven't controlled for anything."

"I've controlled for the weather, I've controlled for the hurricane season, I've controlled for Marguerite's birthday and the Colonel's memorial," says Pascual Pobil, "and it's the most robust finding I've had since 1954, and if you want me to stop publishing it you know perfectly well what to do about the other five nights."

---

## Coda: the thirtieth of September, 1980

Reggie Tar retires as Director of Research of the William Martin Institute for Marine Studies on the thirtieth of September 1980, having held the post for twenty-six years, and there is a dinner at the Bay, and a speech from the Chairman that is almost entirely accurate, and one from Hester Bevan that is entirely accurate and four minutes long and makes the Chairman cry.

He is given a barometer.

"We have a barometer," says Alec, at the house on the Pelican road, at eleven o'clock that night.

"We now have two barometers."

"We shall have to hang one in each room," says Alec Spilsbury, "and compare them, and I am going to enjoy that very much," and takes the second barometer out of his hands and puts it on the table beside the first, and they do not discuss the hut at the top of the row at all, because there is nothing left to say about it.

---

Pascual Pobil is sixty-six and still does the late round at eleven on the nights he is on the island, because nobody has ever been able to stop him doing anything, and on the first of October 1980 he comes down the path from the long room past the huts to the jetty with the lamp in his hand.

The window at the top of the row is dark.

It is a Wednesday.

He stands on the path for a while. Then he goes down to the jetty, and takes the late boat to the Bay, and walks up past the Customs and past the Pelican road without looking up it, and lets himself into his own house, and goes upstairs, and wakes his wife.

"It's dark."

"Pascual, it is half past twelve—"

"It's dark on a *Wednesday*," says Pascual Pobil, sitting on the end of the bed with his boots still on. "Twenty-two years.
