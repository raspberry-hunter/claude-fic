# The Refinement

The March mail brings four things that matter, and they are all in one envelope, in a hand like a diagram.

The first is that the Navy party is confirmed for May. The second is that he is coming with it. The third is a paragraph about a buffered formalin which is not about formalin, and which Reggie reads four times standing up in the mail room with his face doing nothing at all, because there are people in the mail room.

The fourth is on the second sheet, under a rule drawn with a straight-edge, and begins: *As to your boy and the animal.*

---

He had written about it in January, because it was the most interesting thing in the Western Hemisphere and there was exactly one person on earth to tell.

He had written: that the postgraduate Cholmondeley, twenty-three, sent out by a supervisor who wanted him elsewhere, had been put on Beta in September because nobody else had the patience; that the boy sits on the rail for nine hours at a stretch; that the animal now comes to the near end of the pen when the boy's shadow crosses the concrete and will take fish from nobody else if the boy is on the station; that the food logs show a twenty per cent difference; and that he, Reggie, did not have a mechanism and did not like not having one.

The reply is four sentences long and he can hear every one of them spoken.

*As to your boy and the animal. You have a bond of unknown strength and no way whatever to measure it, which is not a finding, it is an anecdote. There is one instrument available to you and you know what it is: take the boy away, keep the record, and put him back. If the curve does what you think it will do, you will have the only quantitative account of the thing in existence, and if it does not, you will have saved yourself a year of telling people a story about a shadow on concrete.*

And then, at the bottom, in the same upright unjoined letters: *Nineteen months on the twenty-eighth. I find I am counting.*

---

He reads both sheets sitting on the edge of the sea wall with his shoes off.

He is aware — he would like this recorded, if only in his own head, where all his records are kept — that the two halves of that letter are the same letter. It is not that the man loves him in one paragraph and is a cold instrument in the next. It is that the appetite and the affection come out of the same place and always have, and that Reggie has known this since a corridor in 1946, and has never once been able to get one without the other, and has never wanted to.

The other thing he is aware of, and puts down, is that the design is right.

He knows it is right in the first four seconds, which is how he always knows. Take the boy off. Keep the record. Put him back. There is no other way to get the number and the number matters, because if the bond is real at that strength then every pen in the world is wrong and the tables are wrong and Alpha in her shed in 1952 was not renal failure at all, or was renal failure at the end of something else.

He sits on the wall for about twenty minutes.

Then he puts his shoes on and goes up to the laboratory and writes the protocol, and it takes him twenty minutes, and it is a good protocol.

---

Pascual finds out on the Tuesday.

"You're moving the boy to Gamma's pen."

"For a fortnight. Eleven days, if the curve moves early."

"Why?"

"Because I want to know what he does without him. Nobody's ever taken the number."

"*Ay.*" Pascual puts both hands on the rail and leans on it. "And what do you think he'll do?"

"He'll go off his food. Possibly a good deal more than that."

"And you'll write that down."

"I'll write it down."

"And when he's down four pounds and won't come up the shallow end, what then?"

"Then I'll have a number," says Reggie, "and every establishment holding these animals will have to look at it, including this one."

Pascual is quiet for a moment. The tide is coming in over the sill and Beta is doing his long slow circuit, and up at the second pen Charles is doing something conscientious with a bucket.

"I don't like it."

"I know."

"It isn't the same as not knowing the mechanism, Reggie. You've got a healthy animal and you're going to make him ill on purpose and write down how ill."

"Yes."

"At least you say it," says Pascual, and goes to get his bag.

---

It runs from the twenty-ninth of April.

Beta goes off his food on the second day. On the fourth he stops coming to the shallow end at all, and lies at the deep end with his tail under him, and Reggie stands on the concrete with his notebook and feels the floor of the last eight years tilt very slightly under him, because he has seen an animal lie like that before, in a concrete bay in England, at four degrees, with black paint on the glass.

He writes it down. His hand is steady. It has always been steady.

By the sixth day the boy is a wreck and pretending not to be, and Jean has taken to putting food in front of him, and Pascual has begun watching Reggie across rooms in a way that Reggie is careful not to notice.

On the seventh day Beta stops moving in the water at all for four hours, and Charles asks — quietly, in the laboratory doorway, having clearly rehearsed it — whether he might be allowed to walk past the pen. Not in. Past.

"No," says Reggie.

"It's only that he can see the path from the deep end, sir, and if he —"

"Cholmondeley. No."

And the boy says, "Yes, sir," and goes.

---

The verandah is on the eleventh day and everybody is standing there when it happens: Jean, John, Hester with her tea, Charles at the far end holding his own elbows.

Pascual says it in front of all of them, which is his way, and he says it in about fifteen words, and there is nothing in it that Reggie can argue with.

"You certainly see it's a medical issue now. He's not doing any good with Gamma, and he's needed here with Beta." And then, holding his eyes: "You have your results, Reggie. You don't need more."

He has his results. That is the thing. He has the whole descending curve, ten days of it, ruled off down the left in his own hand, and it is the finest piece of work of its kind in the world and it will be in print by Christmas and every pen from Naples to Monterey will have to answer it.

And a man four thousand miles away wrote *keep the record*, which does not mean ten days, it means the record, and Reggie can hear exactly how that would be said, without heat, at a fire, with a glass.

"Two more days," he says.

He watches Pascual's face go through it. He watches Hester Bevan put her cup down without a sound, which is the loudest thing that happens on that verandah.

"Two days, and then the boy goes back and we take the return curve. If there isn't one, I've been wrong about the whole of it, and I'll say so in print."

"And if there's no animal to put him back to?"

"Then I'll have that as well," says Reggie, "and it'll be the most expensive datum anyone here has ever produced, and I'll still have produced it."

Nobody says anything.

He does not stop it.

At some time after four that afternoon Beta gets himself over the lip of the pen and onto the concrete, sixty feet of it, dry, in the sun. It is Charles who finds him. It is Charles who is lying in the shallows holding the animal's head up when the rest of them come down the path at a run, and who will not be moved off him, and who says nothing at all to Reggie, then or afterwards, about whose two days those were.

By the morning Beta is passing urine the colour of port.

---

That is the ninth. What happens after the ninth is Pascual, twice a day, with his own hands.

Reggie keeps out of the sick bay. He tells himself it is because the animal is a medical case now and the medical man is the medical man, and this is true, and it is not the reason, and he knows it is not the reason at the time and not merely afterwards.

He stands out at the pen at night instead, more than once, in the dark, with the rain coming and going.

On the sixth night of it he catches himself doing something he has not done since England: standing at the rail at half past ten, looking at the water, thinking clearly and without any particular distress about what a return curve would look like if you had two animals and could take one away and put it back at will.

He stands there for some while after he notices.

Then he goes back up to his room, and takes out the March letter, and reads the four sentences again, and then reads the last line again — *nineteen months on the twenty-eighth; I find I am counting* — and puts the whole thing in the drawer and does not answer it.

---

The May packet comes on the eleventh.

It is thin. It is thin because it is mostly official: the Navy party, the dates, the itinerary, the names of the two American officers, and the composition of the scientific attachment, which is one man.

The private sheet is four lines and one of them is *at last*.

And then the postscript, under the rule, in the upright hand, which is not a threat, because he does not threaten, and is not a request, because it is not addressed to anybody with the power to refuse:

*Your animal will be the first fit adult I have had in front of me in eight years. I should like a fortnight with him before anything is decided about the site, and I should like the pen left exactly as it is until I have seen it. If the boy is willing he should stay on; if he is not, it makes no difference to me, since we shall by then have the separation curve and can produce the effect at will.*

Reggie reads that sitting on the same sea wall with his shoes on.

*Produce the effect at will.*

He sits there and does the arithmetic the way he was taught, which is to say honestly, with the figures written down, on both sides of the page.

On one side: the finest scientific mind in England, four thousand miles of ocean crossed, nineteen months, *at last*, and a fortnight in which the two of them would work together in the same room for the first time since the twenty-ninth of November 1950, which is a thing he has wanted so badly and so continuously that he has had to ration thinking about it.

On the other side: a fit adult animal, in a pen, with a boy who loves him, in front of a man who has never in his life been able to stop wanting to know.

And under that, in the hand he keeps for the things he does not say aloud: *he will take him back to England, and there will be a bay, and there will be a partition, and there will be paint on the glass, and I shall be the one who tells him how to do it, because I am the one who knows how, and I will do it well, because I did it well for seven years.*

He gets up off the wall.

---

He has eight days. He uses them to be certain about the animal, which is the only part of it that is science.

He gets the fitness from Charles without asking for it directly, because the boy will time anything for the pleasure of being asked: two and a half knots sustained, round the pen, on four separate days. Nine pounds up. Water clear on the ninth day of Pascual's treatment; the stiffness gone on the fifth. He checks the arithmetic himself twice and once more on the last morning.

The chart he does not do alone, because he cannot, because Jean is the only person on the station who can lay a bearing off a sounding line without making a hash of it.

He asks her on the seventeenth, in the office, with the door open, for the deep-water line off the second head and the tide for Wednesday night.

"Which hour?"

"Half past nine."

"High water's twenty-one forty. You'll want the gap before it turns."

"I know."

She rules the line and gives it to him and does not ask what it is for, and he is aware that not asking is a decision and that she has made it in about two seconds, standing up, with a pencil in her hand.

"Dr Tar."

"Miss Leslie."

"When it's written up," she says, "put the soundings down as mine."

"I will," says Reggie.

And on the morning of the nineteenth he sends for Cholmondeley.

---

It is the worst thing he has ever done and it takes four minutes and he does it beautifully.

He does not order the boy. He does something far more efficient than ordering: he tells him the truth, in the coat, warmly, as one man of science to another, with his hand on the back of a chair.

He tells him that a scientific attachment arrives on Thursday. He tells him what is proposed for the animal, and he does not exaggerate one word of it, because the plain truth is quite sufficient. He tells him that it is not in his own power to prevent it once the party is ashore, which is true. He tells him that the animal is fit, and gives him the figures the boy took himself, and lets him see him check them.

And then he says that there is a gap in the reef, and a chart on the table, and a tide at half past nine tonight; and that if Beta were put beyond the reef with a bearing and a running start, nobody on this island could be blamed for an animal that got itself out of a pen once before.

He watches the boy's face come up out of eleven days of misery into the most terrible pure joy he has ever seen on a human being.

"Tonight, sir?"

"Half past nine."

"Tonight."

"Yes."

"And you've got the tide?"

"On the table."

"I'll want the chart, then, and I'll want to swim him out past the second head, or he'll turn in at the gap and come back in on the ebb —"

"Then swim him out past the second head."

"You're *sure*, sir? Because if it's the Navy, and they've come all this way —"

"I am sure," says Reggie.

"Right," says Charles, and then, with his whole chest, "*Right.*"

---

He does not ask himself, that afternoon, whether the boy will come back.

He would like that on the record too. He does not ask it. He goes over the bearing with him twice, and makes him repeat the tide, and tells him to take the light and to keep it dry, and at no point in any of it does he put to himself the question that a man might put, which is what a twenty-three-year-old does at midnight two miles out in eight hundred fathoms when the animal he loves turns and goes.

He does not put it to himself. He is very good at not putting things to himself; he has had the coat since he was thirteen and it works inwards as well as out.

Afterwards he will have a great deal of time to consider whether not asking is the same as not knowing.

---

At nine o'clock he walks down to the beach and stands well back, up by the sea grape, where he cannot be seen from the water.

They come down the sand together in the dark. The boy is talking the whole way, low, steady, the way he does, and the animal hauls beside him the way he does, and they go into the shallows together, and there is a great deal of white water for about a minute, and then two dark shapes going out on the flat, one very much faster than the other and holding back for it.

They cross the gap at about twenty past nine. He times it, because he times everything now.

He stands there long after there is anything to see.

Then the rain starts, so lightly that for the first minute he takes it for spray, and Reggie Tar turns and walks up the north path past the generator with an envelope in his breast pocket that came on the eleventh and has not been answered, and in it, in a hand like a diagram, four lines, one of which is *at last*.
