# Habituation

The day book for the long tank is a ledger with a canvas spine, and it goes back to 1943, and Reggie has never once read it from the beginning.

He reads it on the eleventh of October 1949, because the Director has asked for a summary of the programme to date and the summary has to have numbers in it, and the numbers are in the front of the ledger in four different hands.

Received since April 1943: forty-one.

In the tank at the date of writing: seven.

He does the subtraction on his blotter, which is absurd, because he can do it in his head, and he has done it in his head, and he does it again on the blotter in pencil and looks at the figure for a while.

Then he writes the summary. It takes him an hour and forty minutes and it is a good summary. He has a gift for them.

---

Nineteen has been in bay two since the March of 1948, which makes him the longest-held animal in the building.

He is a male, a little over nine feet, with a healed break in the left forearm that predates him — he came in with it — and a white scar across the shoulder that Reggie's predecessor put there. He eats well. He has never damaged himself on the walls, which is unusual: four of them have, and two of those did it badly enough to have to be written off.

He is also, as of this autumn, the best subject in the programme, and the reason is habituation.

It goes like this. The immersion series wants a cannula in the forearm, and getting a cannula into an animal that does not want one is a four-man job with a net and a board, and the struggle is itself a variable: an animal that has fought for six minutes is no use for a resting baseline. Reggie has spent two years fighting that variable with drugs, which introduce their own.

In August he tried something else. He fed Nineteen at the same hour, from the same bucket, standing at the same place on the walkway, and on the fourth day he tapped the rail twice with the handle of the net before he fed him, and on the ninth day Nineteen came up at the tap.

By the end of September Nineteen came up at the tap, rolled onto his side at the edge of the bay, and stayed there.

By the middle of October he is doing something Reggie has not trained him to do at all. When Reggie comes to the rail with the tray, Nineteen comes up, and looks at the tray, and puts his left arm out of the water and lays it along the coping stone, forearm up, with the elbow bent, in exactly the attitude a man adopts for a blood test.

The first time he did it the readings were the best resting series in four years.

Reggie wrote *habituation complete* in the day book, and underlined it, and went two doors down and stood in the doorway of the next room until Spilsbury looked up, and said, "He gives me the arm," and Spilsbury put his pen down and said, "*Does* he," in the voice he keeps for genuinely interesting things, and came and looked.

That was the tenth of October. That was the day before Reggie read the front of the ledger.

---

He tells himself, for about three weeks, that the two things are not connected.

---

Nicholson says his mild thing on a Thursday in the corridor outside the lift, with a rack of electrodes under his arm.

"I've told Hollis I'd rather not," he says. "The nerve work. On yours, I mean. On the animals."

"Rather not?"

"Rather not."

Reggie shifts his folder to the other arm. He is late for the Director and he can feel the lateness in his shins.

"It's four days' work, Frank. Nobody else in the building can do it."

"That's what Hollis said." Nicholson looks at the lift gates. He is greyer than he was in 1947 and he has taken to a cardigan under the white coat. "Mine watches the door, you know."

"Which is yours?"

"Twenty-six. I only had him a fortnight, for the conduction work, and he watched the door the whole time. Not the tank, not me. The *door*."

He says it apologetically, as if he were confessing to a superstition.

"They're habituated to the routine. They learn the sequence, that's all."

"He knew which of us came in by which door and what each of us did. He'd go to the bottom for Hollis and come up for me, because I didn't do anything to him that hurt. Then I did, once, and he didn't come up again."

The lift comes. They get in. The boy shuts the gates.

And Reggie puts on the coat.

He is aware of doing it, which is new. He hears it come out of his own mouth in his own voice — the light one, the warm one, the one that likes you: *My dear man, of course it's foul, it's perfectly foul, it was foul in the War and it's foul now; do you know what we got out of the third subject alone? Six hundred men in the water off Norway, Frank, and a table that tells a captain how long he's got to find them. I'd give a great deal not to need it. Four days. Give me four days and I'll do the holding myself, and you needn't come near the bay after.*

It takes eleven seconds and Nicholson says yes before the lift reaches the ground floor.

He says it kindly. That is the thing about Nicholson; he is a decent man, and decent men do not like to be difficult twice. He says, "Four days, then," and then, at the gates, "You're very good at that, Tar," and it is not an accusation, and Reggie carries it about with him for the rest of the day like a stone in a shoe.

---

That is the Thursday. On the Friday he works out what the coat is for.

He is in the Director's office, being charming, about money. It goes beautifully. It always goes beautifully: he brings the Director on a little, and lets him arrive at the conclusion himself, and comes away with the next series approved, four more animals authorised for the spring, and an extra technician.

Walking back up the stairs with the minute in his hand, he thinks: *four more.*

And then, on the half-landing, in the middle of a perfectly ordinary Friday, the thing turns over in him like a body in water.

He has had the coat since he was thirteen. He knows exactly where he got it: he built it in a school corridor, out of necessity, because a small clever boy who is wrong in the way he is wrong learns very early to be liked so that nobody looks at him closely. It was armour. He has always understood that it was armour. It is why he can do a sherry party in his sleep, and why he has been asked to things, and why nobody at the Labs has ever once wondered about him and Spilsbury, which is what it was built for.

What he has not understood, until the half-landing, is that armour is not all it is. The coat does not only stop people looking at him. It makes them give him things. He put it on in a lift yesterday and it got him Nicholson's four days. He put it on this morning and it got him four animals in the spring.

It is a tool, and he has been using it all his life, and what he uses it for now is to be allowed to go on.

He stands on the half-landing for some time.

Then he goes up to the second floor, and puts the minute on Spilsbury's desk through the open door, and says, "Four more in the spring, and a technician," and Spilsbury says, "You had better take the technician and not give him to Hollis," and Reggie says, "Obviously," and goes and does his afternoon.

---

The control is his own idea. That is the part he will not be able to put anywhere afterwards.

The bitumen has been on the partitions for two years and the variance has stayed down, and in November the Admiralty man asks the obvious question, which is whether the screens are still doing anything, or whether the animals are simply habituated to captivity, in which case the paint is costing them the pair comparisons for nothing.

It is a fair question. Reggie says so. He designs the test that evening in half an hour, and it is a good test, and it is very simple: chip a window in the paint on one partition, four inches square, at mid-depth. Leave it two hours. Take the readings on both sides. Paint it out again.

They do it on the fifteenth. He does it himself, with a chisel and a mallet, kneeling on the walkway above bay two, and the technician holds the lamp.

Nineteen is at the window before Reggie has finished chipping. So is Twenty-two, on the other side.

He had expected that. Novelty; a change in the wall; anything would bring them. He starts the clock and goes down the outside stair to the viewing gallery, where the glass in the end wall of each bay lets you see in, and stands in the dark with his notebook to watch them get bored.

They do not get bored.

They come to the four-inch window and they stay at it for two hours. They do not move. Twenty-two puts her hand flat on the glass at the bottom left of the window and Nineteen puts his hand on the other side in the same place, and they hold them there; and every so often one of them takes the hand away and puts it back, and the other one takes hers away and puts it back, and this goes on and on and on, for one hundred and nineteen minutes, in four degrees, in the dark, with a chisel-cut square of light between them.

The readings are ruinous. Pulse up in both, respiration up, core down in Nineteen because he has stopped using the surface. In experimental terms it is an unambiguous result: the screens are doing a great deal, and every number he has taken in two years is only clean because of them.

At one hundred and nineteen minutes he goes back up and paints it out.

He does it himself. He does not give it to the technician. He mixes the bitumen and he goes along the walkway and he puts the brush in and the first stroke closes the top of the window, and Nineteen comes up out of the water to the height of his chest, which Reggie has never seen him do, and hits the coping stone with his hand — not at Reggie, not a display, just the flat of a hand on wet stone, once, hard — and the sound of it goes round the tank hall and comes back off the roof.

Reggie's hand is quite steady on the brush. He has always had good hands. He finishes the square. Two coats, the second at four o'clock, because one coat lets a shadow through.

In the day book he writes: *15.xi. Partition window trial, bay 2/3. 119 min. Variance data attached. Window closed 16.10, second coat 16.00. Screens retained.*

He is very careful about the times. His hands do the writing and the times are correct and his face does whatever it is doing and he does not look at it.

---

Nineteen's terminal work is on the twenty-second, because the series has to end with tissue, and there has never been any question about that. It has been in the protocol since 1948. Reggie wrote the protocol.

He does it himself, as he always does, because he is the fastest in the building and speed is the whole of kindness in this room. The animal is on the board. The premedication is in. Reggie has a hand on the shoulder above the old white scar, and he is talking, low and level, the way he has talked to this animal every morning for twenty months, about nothing, about the weather, about the Director, because it steadies him and it steadies the animal and he has never examined which of those matters more.

And Nineteen, who is drugged, and on a board, and out of the water, puts his left arm out and lays it along the edge, forearm up, elbow bent.

Nobody trained him to do that.

Reggie stands there with the tray at his elbow and the technician waiting and he cannot, for four seconds, make his hand pick anything up.

Then he does. He is quick and he is clean and it takes under a minute, and the tissue is the best he has ever taken, and at no point does anything go wrong. That is the thing he will have to carry. Not that it was botched. That it was not.

---

He does not go to Alma Terrace. He goes to Spilsbury's, which is against procedure on a Tuesday, and he does not have anything ready to say when he gets there.

"You're early."

"Yes."

"There is no fire. I have not been in."

"I don't want a fire."

Spilsbury looks at him for about two seconds, and then goes and lights the fire anyway, with two shillings in the meter, and takes Reggie's coat off him and hangs it up, and puts him in the chair nearest the hearth, and stands behind him and puts a hand on the back of his neck, at the nape, and leaves it there.

They stay like that for a while. The fire ticks.

"Nineteen went today."

"I know. I read the board."

"He gave me the arm."

"Yes," says Spilsbury. "You told me in October. It is a remarkable —"

"On the *board*, Alec."

The hand on his neck does not move. Behind him, Spilsbury is silent for a count of four.

"Ah," he says.

"He was on the board and premedicated and he put his arm out for me. Nobody taught him that."

"No."

"It isn't a trick, and it isn't the tap. He thought he knew what was coming. He thought it'd be the cannula and he was *helping* —"

"Yes."

"And I —"

"You did it properly," says Spilsbury, "in under a minute, and he did not know, and the tissue is the best in the building. I have seen the sections. Hollis has been in twice to look at them."

"Don't."

"You would rather I said something else?"

"I don't know what I'd rather," Reggie says, and puts both hands over his face.

---

They talk for a long time. He has never talked to anybody like this in his life and he has no idea how it is done, and the only reason he can do it at all is that the man is standing behind him where he cannot be seen.

He gets it out in pieces: the window in the paint and the two hands in the same place for one hundred and nineteen minutes; the ledger in the front, forty-one in and seven left; Nicholson's twenty-six watching the door and knowing which man came through it; the coat on the half-landing and what the coat is for.

Spilsbury listens. That is his gift and it is not a small one. He does not interrupt, he does not soothe, he does not once say *you mustn't think like that*. When Reggie stops he asks three questions, and all three are about the window trial, and the third one is so exactly the right question — whether they held the same position or merely the same region of the glass — that Reggie answers it out of pure reflex, and finds that the answer is *the same four square inches*, and hears himself say it, and stops.

"You see it too," he says. "Don't you. You've seen it all along."

"I have seen that they are more than the literature says they are. Yes."

"Since when?"

"Since about 1944."

Reggie turns round in the chair.

"*Nineteen forty-four.*"

"It has not made any difference to what I do," says Spilsbury, "and I am not going to pretend to you that it has."

---

Here is what he says, afterwards, sitting on the hearthrug with his back against Reggie's shins, which is where he goes when he intends to say things.

He says that the work is the only work of its kind in the country, and that if it stops here it will be done elsewhere, by men who will not screen a partition to get their variance down because they will not notice the variance; that the Admiralty table has been in every ship's standing orders since 1948 and there is no way to know which men are alive because of it, which is precisely what makes it worth doing; that the animals already dead are dead either way, and the only question available is whether the deaths purchase anything.

He says it well. He says it without any heat at all, which is the thing that makes it stick, and Reggie, who has been arguing for two days with the inside of his own head, finds that every single one of these propositions is one he has already used on himself, and that they were better when Spilsbury said them.

"And there is one more," Spilsbury says, "and you will not like it."

"Go on."

"You are not doing this because of the Admiralty. You have never done anything because of the Admiralty. You are doing it because you want to know the answer, and there is no other way to get the answer, and you have not yet met the thing you want more than the answer."

Reggie looks at the back of his head.

"That's a filthy thing to say."

"It is true, and you have said it to me at this fire, with your own mouth, in about February."

"I know."

"Then it is not filthy. It is a measurement."

---

And then Spilsbury does the other thing, and it is the thing he has no instrument for at all.

He turns round on the hearthrug and puts his arms over Reggie's knees and says:

"If you want to stop, stop."

"Don't be absurd."

"I am not being absurd. Listen to me. There are three tables in the mortality work you've been doing on the side, and they're good, and they'd take you two years and no animals at all."

"Alec —"

"I can put you in front of the Director in a week. I can have the programme handed to Hollis, who is a plodder, and who won't be given the spring allocation, and it'll run down in eighteen months. Nobody will ever know it came from you."

"You'd be running it alone."

"Yes."

"With Hollis in my room."

"Yes," says Spilsbury, and then, after a moment: "That is the part that is expensive. I have costed it. I am telling you it is available."

The fire ticks. Somewhere below, the widow's wireless.

And Reggie sits in the chair with his hands on the arms of it and looks at the only person he has ever been able to say any of this to, and discovers, with total clarity, that he is not going to take it.

Not because it would not work. It would work. Because the spring allocation is four more animals and the isolation data are not finished and Twenty-two is still in bay three and there is a question at the end of it that he can very nearly see.

"No," he says.

"Very well."

"You're not going to argue?"

"You've just spent two hours telling me what it costs, and you have looked at it, and this is your answer. I'm not going to sit here and pretend I have a better instrument than you have."

He puts his chin on Reggie's knee. In the firelight he looks about thirty.

"I will ask you again."

"When?"

"When I think you will say something different."

"And in the meantime?"

"In the meantime," says Spilsbury, "give me the terminal work."

Reggie does not understand him for a second.

"All of it," Spilsbury says. "The boards. You can have the design, the series, the numbers, the sections, everything you want. I will hold the knives. I am not fast, and it will be worse for them for about forty seconds, and I have costed that too."

"That's —"

"It is available as well."

"You'd do that?"

"It does not cost me anything," says Spilsbury.

---

He takes it.

Not that night. He says, "I'll think about it," and thinks about it for nine days, and on the first of December he stands in the doorway between their rooms and says, "All right," and Spilsbury nods once and writes something in his book.

The work does not stop for an afternoon. The question at the end of it stays where it is, about four inches beyond his fingertips.

---

That night, though, before any of that, they go to bed in the cold room and Reggie cannot be still.

"Don't talk to me."

"All right."

"I mean it. Not one number."

"All right."

"I want —" He stops. He is on his back in the dark with his hands over his eyes. "I don't want to be in my head. Can you get me out of my head."

There is a silence.

"Yes," says Spilsbury.

He is thorough about it. He is thorough about everything, and he does not say a word, which is worse and better; he simply begins at Reggie's shoulders with both hands and works down, slowly, taking his time, until Reggie's breath changes; and then keeps going, and does not permit any hurry at all, and when Reggie tries to take over he takes both his wrists and puts them back on the pillow, once, without comment, and Reggie makes a noise into the dark and stops trying.

"*Alec* —"

"Mm."

"I said don't talk."

"That was not talking."

"Do it again."

He does it again. And again, and again, for what feels like an hour, until there is nothing left in Reggie's head at all — no ledger, no window in the paint, no forearm on the coping stone — nothing but a mouth at the back of his neck and hands holding his wrists down and the enormous animal fact of being warm and wanted and known; and when it finally breaks over him he says something that is not language, and holds on so hard he leaves marks, and afterwards he cries for about ninety seconds, silently, into Spilsbury's shoulder, and Spilsbury says nothing whatever about it and does not let go.

"Sorry."

"No."

"That's not what that was for."

"I know exactly what it was for," says Spilsbury, into his hair. "I gave it you anyway."

"Why?"

"Because you asked me. Go to sleep."

---

He sleeps like the dead and wakes at six because it is the procedure.

The Labs are warm this year; the boiler was replaced in the spring. The tank hall is at four degrees as always. Twenty-two is in bay three, on the bottom, at the end away from the black partition, very quiet, with her tail under her.

At half past eight Reggie walks in through the ground-floor door, which is the door Hollis uses, and stops.

Then he goes back out, and round, and comes in by the side door, which is his own, and he does not admit to himself why he has done it; and he takes his coat off, and puts it over the back of the other chair, and goes up to the second floor to write the isolation protocol for the spring.
