# In Pairs

The cohousing paper goes in on the second of January 1955 and is in print by the end of March, and it is the best thing he has ever had his name on, and his name is third.

Pobil is first, because it is his observation. Leslie is second, because she kept the feeding logs that make it a finding instead of an anecdote. Tar is third, because somebody has to do the statistics and the covering letter.

Pascual is disgusted about it for a fortnight.

"You did the analysis."

"You did the observation."

"I watched two animals get on with each other and said so at dinner."

"For nine months, twice a day, in a notebook," says Reggie. "Stop arguing, it's printed."

The effect is not small. Two animals housed together, in a pen of adequate size, with a sight line and no barrier: food intake up by a fifth, weight maintained, no self-injury in either, and — this is the part that will do the work — a mortality in the first twelve months that is less than half what it is in the solitary literature.

The letters come in from everywhere that summer. Naples. Monterey. A man in Sydney who wants the pen dimensions. It is the first time in his life that Reggie Tar has published something that makes an animal's life better, and he finds it does not feel the way he expected. It feels like getting off a train.

The letter from England comes on the third Tuesday of April and he reads it on the sea wall with his shoes off.

*This is the most useful thing anybody has published on these animals in twenty years and you know it, so I shall not go on about it. Two questions. First: the fifth-month dip in your Table 2 is either a feeding artefact or the most interesting thing in the paper and you have not said which, because you do not know. Second: have you the raw intake sheets, and may I have them.*

*I am glad about this. I want you to know that I am glad in the ordinary way, which is not a way I have much practice in.*

He sends the raw sheets on the next boat.

---

In September he takes his leave in England for the first time in three years.

A week. Nine days, if you count the boats. He tells the station it is the Zoological Society and a dentist, and both of those things are true, and he spends five of the nine days in a first-floor flat in a street off the Fulham Road with the curtains open because the back overlooks nothing at all.

Alec is forty-three and going grey at the temples in a way that Reggie finds outrageous and does not say so, and has bought — this is the thing that gets him, out of everything — a second armchair.

"You've never had a second chair."

"I have now."

"Alec, you've had one chair since 1946. It's a *principle* with you. You said a second chair invites people."

"Yes," says Alec, and puts the kettle on, and does not look round.

Reggie stands in the middle of the room with his hat still in his hand and has to wait a moment before he can say anything else.

---

They do very little. That is the astonishing luxury of it. They read; they walk on the Heath one afternoon four feet apart talking about nothing; they argue about the fifth-month dip for two and a half hours with the raw sheets all over the floor and get precisely nowhere and enjoy it enormously.

And on the fourth night Reggie wakes at two in the morning and finds himself awake for no reason, warm, with a hand on his back, and lies there taking an inventory the way one does at two in the morning, and thinks: *this is the whole of it. There is nothing else I want. Nine years and I have never wanted anything else.*

He turns over. Alec is not asleep either.

"How long have you been awake?"

"About an hour."

"Doing what?"

"Looking at you," says Alec, "which I do not get to do at four thousand miles, and which I intend to go on doing, so you may either go back to sleep or be useful."

"Useful."

"Useful."

So Reggie is useful.

He is slower about it than he used to be, and better, and he takes his time, because he has learned over nine years that this man will not ask twice and must be given the chance to be asked. He puts his mouth to the shoulder first, because it has always been the shoulders, and then to the inside of the upper arm, and then he takes both wrists and puts them up above his head against the headboard and holds them there, and Alec's breath goes out of him all at once like a door coming open.

"*Ah* —"

"Yes?"

"Yes."

"Say it properly."

"*Yes*," says Alec, "please," which is a word Reggie has heard from him four times in nine years, and every one of them in this position.

He takes a very long while over it. He talks the entire time — nonsense, mostly, interspersed with the things he only says in the dark, and with a running commentary on what he is doing, in the flat voice he uses for reading out figures, because he found out in 1947 what that voice does to this man and has never had a reason to stop. He feels Alec come apart under him in stages, the way a difficult knot does: shoulders, then hands, then breath, then all of it at once, with his head back and Reggie's name in his mouth, twice, and then nothing but a sound.

Afterwards Alec says, into his hair, "One hundred and thirty-one."

"You were counting?"

"I was not counting. I know."

"That's worse. That's much worse."

"It is," Alec agrees comfortably, and falls asleep first, which he has done perhaps twice in nine years, and Reggie lies in the dark in a flat in Fulham with a second armchair in the next room and is so happy he cannot stay still.

---

It is on the sixth day, over breakfast, that the engagement is mentioned.

It is mentioned the way a man mentions the rates. Alec has taken a consultancy with a firm in Hertfordshire — biological supply, mostly, with a research side — three days a month, advising on husbandry and record-keeping, since they have money and no method and he has method and no money.

"Which firm?"

"Dewar and Lecky."

"Never heard of them."

"You would not have. They are commercial." A pause, and the toast goes on being buttered. "They have some of the animals."

"How many?"

"Nine, when I first went. More now."

And Reggie — sitting in a warm kitchen in Fulham on the sixth of nine days, with three days left, in the middle of the only week of his year — says, "Ah," and asks nothing else.

He is aware of the decision as he makes it. That is the part he will be unable to explain later, even to himself: it is not that the question does not occur to him. It occurs to him fully formed, with its consequences attached, and he looks at it for about two seconds and puts it down, because there are three days left and a second armchair in the next room.

He has spent nine years learning to read this man. Reading is not the difficulty. It has never once been the difficulty.

---

He goes back in October and the trouble with the tables starts in November, and it starts, as these things do, with somebody being helpful.

A man in Naples writes to ask whether the forty-one-month median in the 1951 monograph should now be read against the cohousing result, since — he puts it very politely, in the third paragraph — *all the animals in your series were held alone, were they not?*

Reggie reads that sentence standing up.

He goes through the whole series again over four nights, twenty-six establishments, four countries, every ledger he was allowed to see, and it is not close and it does not require judgement. Of the two hundred and eleven animals in the survival series, the number housed with another of their own kind, at any point, for any period, is four.

The monograph does not say so anywhere. It does not say so because in 1951 it did not occur to him to record it, because in 1951 he did not know it was a variable, because in 1951 he had spent four years in a building where the variable had been painted out with two coats of bitumen at his own suggestion.

His table is not a table of how long these animals live in captivity. It is a table of how long they live alone.

He takes it to Pascual on the fifth night because he cannot hold it up by himself.

"So it's wrong."

"It's wrong."

"How wrong? Is the curve the wrong shape, or are the numbers just —"

"The shape is probably right for solitary housing. The number's meaningless as a general figure and it's been quoted as a general figure in every paper in the field for four years, including ours."

Pascual puts his glasses on, which he does for reading and for nothing else, and looks at the sheets for a long time.

"Then you write to the journal."

"It'll take the tables out of the literature."

"Good. They're wrong."

"It'll take about half of my standing out with them," says Reggie, "and I'd be lying if I said I hadn't thought about that for two nights before I came down here."

"Ay. And now you've said it out loud, and it's a stupid reason, and you know it's a stupid reason." Pascual takes the glasses off. "Write the letter. I'll read it. Then we'll have a drink, and tomorrow you'll be a man with one good paper instead of two, and there are worse things to be at your age."

They are at it until two in the morning. The retraction runs to six hundred words and is a model of its kind, and Pascual makes him take out the third paragraph, which is the one where he is being noble about it.

It goes off on the December boat.

---

The other letter comes on the third Tuesday of January 1956.

It is four sheets, and the first three are the best correspondence he has all year: a genuinely funny account of a row at a committee; two pages on the fifth-month dip, which Alec now thinks is a feeding artefact and says so with figures; a note that the retraction is correct, that it will cost him, and that the cost is the price of the correction and not of the error, which is a distinction Reggie sits with for some time.

The fourth sheet begins under the rule.

*You will want to know, and you have not asked, so I shall tell you. D. & L. have had fourteen animals in since the June before last. Eleven of those have come in since the spring, and since the spring they have been coming in two at a time, because I gave them your paper and told them that a solitary animal is a wasted animal, which is true, and which has halved their first-year losses. Their controls remain rubbish and I have told them so in writing on three occasions.*

*I set the figures out plainly because you would get them eventually from somebody else and I would rather they were in my hand.*

He reads it on the sea wall. He reads it four times. Then he walks up to the laboratory and sits down and does not do any work for two hours.

Fourteen. Eleven since the spring. In pairs.

He can do the rest of it himself, and he does: a firm that took nine animals a year now takes them two at a time because they live longer that way, which means more of them in the water off the shoals with nets round them, which means that the paper with Pobil's name first and Leslie's second and his own third — the only thing he has ever made that reduced an animal's suffering — has doubled the number of animals in pens in England.

Both things are true. That is what undoes him: not that he was wrong, but that he was right, and that being right is what did it.

---

He writes the letter that night and it is the shortest he has ever sent.

*Leave them. Resign the engagement. I am not asking as a colleague.*

It goes on the January boat and the answer comes back on the third Tuesday of February, and he knows what is in it from the weight of the envelope, because it is one sheet.

*No.*

*I have considered it for the four days it took me to be sure I was not merely refusing out of habit. If I go, they will engage a man who will not tell them their controls are rubbish, and the animals now in that building will be worse off in ninety days. That is the whole of my reasoning and I have not dressed it up.*

*You have asked me for a number of things in nine years. You have never before asked me for the work, and I had begun to believe you never would, and I find I am not able to give you that one, and that I do not intend to lie to you about the reason. I would give you very nearly anything. That is not the same as anything, and you have now got the measurement, which I imagine you did not want.*

*I am sorry. I am aware that this is a word I have not written to you before.*

Reggie sits with it on the sea wall until the light goes.

The extraordinary thing — and he turns it over for a week, and it is still the case at the end of the week — is that he does not want to end it, and is not tempted to, and is not pretending. He has known the shape of this man since a cold passage in 1946 and has never once been misinformed. He simply has the dimension now. There is a place where the giving stops, and he has walked up to it and put his hand on it, and it is exactly where he would have said it was if anybody had asked him to guess.

What he had not known, and now does, is what it feels like to stand on his own side of it.

---

In February the retraction appears, and two men whose work he admires write to say he was brave, and four say nothing, and one — a man in Monterey who has built a career on the forty-one-month figure — writes something Reggie declines to show to anybody.

Pascual reads it anyway, off the desk, being Pascual.

"*Hijo de —* who is this fellow?"

"Somebody who now has to re-do six years."

"Then he should re-do six years and keep his mouth shut."

"He should," Reggie agrees.

"Are you all right?"

And Reggie, who is not, and who has an envelope in his breast pocket with one word in it on the first line, says, "Yes," in the ordinary voice, and is aware of Pascual looking at him for about a second and a half longer than the answer warranted.

"Hm," says Pascual, and goes and gets the buckets, and says over his shoulder, "Four o'clock feed. Come and hold the gate, you're no use up here."

So Reggie goes down and holds the gate.

Gamma comes to the near end with the calf beside her, the two of them shoulder to shoulder in the shaded half of the pen, which is a thing no pen in England has, because no pen in England was built by a man who had read a paper with three names on it.

He stands there with his hand on the rail and watches them eat, and does the only arithmetic he is fit for that afternoon, which is: two animals here, fed, together, alive; fourteen in Hertfordshire, in pairs, in a building with rubbish controls and the best husbandry adviser in England coming three days a month.

He cannot get it to come out. He works at it all through the feed and it will not come out, and Pascual, beside him, unaware, says, "Look at her. *Look* at her. Did you see him take that from her mouth? Write that down, Reggie, that's the fourth time this week."

And Reggie writes it down.
