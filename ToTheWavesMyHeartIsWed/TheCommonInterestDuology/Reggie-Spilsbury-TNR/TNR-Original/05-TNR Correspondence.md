# Correspondence

The launch comes round the head at four in the afternoon and the whole station is on the jetty, because, he learns later, the Colonel likes a thing done properly.

Pascual is thirty-nine years old, he has been travelling for nine days, his shirt is stuck to him in four places, and his instruments are in a crate that he is not letting out of his sight. There are five people on the planks. He sorts them in about the time it takes to hand up the crate.

The Colonel: old soldier, kind, delegates everything, will be no use at all and no trouble either. The wife — Mrs Bevan, administrator, and she is the one who actually holds the place, that is obvious in four seconds, it is in the way the others stand relative to her. A young woman with red forearms and her sleeves rolled, who looks at the crate rather than at him, which he likes very much. A thin man in spectacles who is introduced as being on the technical side.

And the Director of Research.

"Dr Pobil. *Splendid.*" A hand out, a grip, the whole thing done in one movement. "You've had nine days of boats and you'll be wanting a bath and a drink and neither of them will be what you're used to, and I'm afraid the bath is the worse of the two. Bevan, is that the last of it? Then let the man get off the jetty."

He is small, sandy, in his middle thirties, in spectacles, and he is pleasant in a way that arrives complete, like something poured out of a jug.

And Pascual, who has been off a boat for ninety seconds, takes against him immediately and thoroughly.

It is not reasoned. It is the same thing he feels about a horse dealer at a fair: not *this man is lying to me*, but *this man is extremely good at this and does it to everybody*. Thirty years of hearing men be charming about animals they have not looked at.

"You'll want to see the pens tonight rather than the morning, I imagine," says Dr Tar.

"I'd like that."

"Thought so. Jean will take you. Don't let the Colonel give you the tour, he's very proud of the generator."

And he is right, which is the irritating part. Pascual does want to see the pens tonight.

---

The pens are good. That is the first surprise.

They are better than he expected, better than the Lisbon aquarium and much better than the place in Florida, and somebody has thought about the gate mechanisms with actual animals in mind. The seaward pen has a shaded end. The rails are padded where an animal would come up against them.

The second surprise is in the office, on the Thursday, when he asks for the medical files and Mrs Bevan gives him everything without a murmur and does not ask why he wants it.

The third surprise is Specimen Alpha.

---

He reads the Alpha file in one sitting with the shutters open and the moths coming in, and by the end of it he is standing up.

Four years old. Female. Brought in June 1951, and dead on the nineteenth of September 1952, eight weeks before Pascual landed. Declining for nine days before the end, out of the water for most of that time in a slatted crate lined with wet sacking, in a shed, at an air temperature nobody recorded.

The necropsy is by J. M. Leslie and is, for a person with no training, astonishing: careful, hedged, honest about what she could not do. She writes *I do not know how to open the pericardium without spoiling it and so I have not.* She writes *there was no vet and no time to send for one.*

There was no vet. That is the sentence he takes down the path with him, past the generator, to the light in the laboratory window, at twenty past ten at night.

"Tar."

"Pobil. Come and look at this, I've got a salinity series that makes no —"

"Alpha was nine days in a box."

Dr Tar straightens up from the bench.

"Nine days," says Pascual. "On sacking. In a shed. With nobody trained within four hundred miles, and she was four years old, *hombre*, four, she'd have had twenty years in front of her, and somebody here decided that a crate in a shed was husbandry —"

"I decided it."

"Then it's you I'm talking to."

"Yes."

"Why was she out of the water?"

"Because I wanted hourly respiration counts and I couldn't get them in the pen."

Pascual has his mouth open for the next thing and it goes out of him like air out of a bladder.

"*Ay.* Dios. At least you said it."

"There isn't much point in saying anything else. You'd have got it out of the file by Saturday."

"I had it out of the file tonight." He sits down on the corner of the bench, which he has not been invited to do. "You know what killed her?"

"Renal failure, on Miss Leslie's material."

"Renal failure, *and* nine days of being handled, *and* — was there another animal here that year?"

"Two. Beta came in the March and one that died in the boat."

"And where was Beta while Alpha was in the shed?"

"In the seaward pen. Fifty yards away."

Pascual makes a noise. He does not, at that moment, have anything to attach it to; it is thirty years of stockmen and stables and the flat animal fact that you do not separate a beast from its own kind and then write down its respiration as though the number meant anything.

"I don't like it," is all he can get to. "I can't tell you the mechanism. I don't like it."

And Dr Tar, small and sandy in the lamplight at ten at night, takes his spectacles off, and rubs the bridge of his nose, and says, "No. Nor do I," and it is a completely different voice.

It is flat, and tired, and about nine years older than the one on the jetty.

"There," says Pascual. "That one. Use that one, it's better."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Nothing. Where've you put your buckets?"

---

By the end of the second week he has stopped disliking him, which is a nuisance, because by the end of the second week he has also noticed the other thing.

He notices it in the surf at the end of the sand, where Tar swims at six in the morning, badly, with great determination, and comes out and stands with his hair flat and his glasses in his hand, blinking, looking about nine years old instead of nine years older.

Pascual, standing in the water up to his knees with a bucket in his hand, thinks: *ah. No. Not that.*

He has had this before. Twice, badly. It does not get better for being ignored and it does not get better for being fed, and the only thing he has ever found that works is the work, of which there is a great deal here, and a temper, of which he has plenty.

So he says nothing whatever, and puts it where such things go, and gets on with the pens.

---

The mail comes by the estate boat every third Tuesday, and it comes in a canvas sack, and the whole station goes silly for two days.

Jean gets her father's letter and a magazine about birds. The Colonel gets six weeks of *The Times* and reads them in order, on the correct days, which Pascual finds so funny that he has to go outside. Mrs Bevan gets documents. Pascual gets nothing for the first three months, and then a great deal from his sister in Toulouse all at once.

And Dr Tar gets a fat envelope with an English stamp and a hand on it like a diagram.

It is a very particular hand: small, upright, absolutely even, the letters not joined, the sort of hand a man uses for labelling slides. Pascual clocks it the first time because Tar, who has been talking to Bevan about the boat schedule, stops talking in the middle of a word.

Then he puts the envelope in his breast pocket and finishes the sentence, and goes on working until six, and is not in the laboratory that evening at all.

---

In February one of the letters is on the bench under a beaker, and Pascual, moving the beaker because it has his own stain in it, reads two lines of it before he understands what he is holding.

They are about fixative.

He stands there with a beaker in one hand and reads the whole of the first page before he can stop himself, and it is, from top to bottom, a technical letter: a paragraph on a buffered formalin at a particular strength, a query about whether the postal delay is spoiling the blocks, a short and rather funny complaint about a man at a journal, a recommendation of a German paper with the reference given in full, and, at the bottom, a line to say that the second box arrived on the fourteenth and was exactly right and that he is much obliged.

There is no *dear*. There is no *yours*. There are two initials at the end.

Pascual puts it back under the beaker, squarely, where it was.

He does not have anything you could call evidence and he is not looking for any. What he has is a man who stops in the middle of a word, and a hand like a diagram, and a technical letter that a person had kept, and a face — because he saw the face on the jetty on the Tuesday, from forty feet, for about a second, before the charm came back over it like a shutter.

"*Bueno*," says Pascual, to a beaker, in an empty laboratory. "Well. That's that, then."

And he goes and does the four o'clock feed.

---

The thing he minds is not the fact. He has no view on the fact; he has never had a view on the fact, having spent his own life on the wrong side of it.

The thing he minds, for about a week, in a low and shabby way that he is not proud of, is the arithmetic of distance: that whoever writes in that hand is four thousand miles away and gets a letter every third Tuesday, and that Pascual Pobil is here, in the same building, holding the buckets.

He gets over it by doing the pens twice a day and shouting at the generator.

---

At Christmas the Colonel produces rum and the whole station sits out under the awning, and at about half past ten there are only two of them left, and Pascual has had enough to be honest and not enough to be stupid.

He does not plan what he says. He has never been able to plan a sentence in his life and he does not start at Christmas; it simply comes up out of him sideways.

"You should get somebody out here," he says. "That's what I think. I'm telling you, a man can't work at this pitch with nobody in the house."

"I've got the whole station in the house."

"You've got staff in the house. That's not the same and you know it isn't."

And Tar turns his glass round on the table twice, and looks out at the water, and then puts the coat on.

Pascual watches him do it and he has never seen it from the inside of the conversation before. It is very fine work. He is warm, he is funny, he says something extremely charming about the Colonel's rum, and he asks Pascual about Toulouse, and he asks it *well*, with real curiosity, so that twenty minutes go by and Pascual has told him about his sister and her hopeless husband and the boy with the bad chest, and then the lamp is out and the evening is over and they are both standing up.

At the bottom of the steps Tar says, "Goodnight, Pascual," and puts a hand briefly on his shoulder as he goes past, which is the first time he has touched him at all.

Pascual stands in the dark for some time.

"*Ay,* hombre," he says, out loud, to the sea. "All right. All right."

It is not a refusal, that. He has had refusals and they feel different. It is a door held shut by a man with his back against it and a smile on his face, and it is done so kindly, and so completely, that there is nothing at all to be angry about, and Pascual — who can be angry about most things — finds that what he mainly is, is sorry.

Not for himself. That passes. Something else.

---

He works out what the something else is in April, over the tables.

Tar's monograph comes out in England in the January and nine copies arrive in the March, and Tar hands them round the station with a face on him like a man distributing summonses. Pascual takes his to the hut and reads it in two evenings with his glasses on, which he wears for reading and nothing else.

It is very good. That is not in question. It is the first honest account of captive mortality in the animals that anybody has produced: three tables, a survival curve, and a discussion written by a man who plainly wanted to be wrong and could not manage it. The median is forty-one months. The modal cause of death is written as renal and hepatic failure. There is a line at the end of the discussion which says that the figures are what they are and that the author's own hands are in them.

He takes it down to the laboratory on the Thursday.

"Where did you get the data?"

"Ledgers. Twenty-six establishments, four countries, everything back to 1936 that anybody would let me see."

"And who kept the ledgers?"

"The establishments."

"The people who did it," says Pascual.

"Yes."

"So where a man has killed an animal badly, what's in the ledger?"

"Renal failure," says Tar, "in almost every case. Renal or hepatic. It's the commonest true finding and it's also the commonest thing to write down when you'd rather not write anything else, and there is no way on earth to separate the two from the outside."

"You'd better put that in."

"It's in. Page four, in the small type, where nobody will read it."

Pascual leans on the bench.

"Then your forty-one months isn't a median. It's a ceiling."

"It's a ceiling."

"And your causes aren't causes —"

"My causes are a list of what people are prepared to write in a book," says Tar.

And there it is again: the flat voice, the one from ten at night in November, and the thing Pascual has been failing to name since Christmas gets a name at last, on the Thursday afternoon, at the bench.

It is not the letters, and it is not the coat.

It is that this man has, somewhere behind him, a room he does not talk about, and the monograph is a man building a wall out of numbers between himself and that room, one table at a time, and doing it very well, and living the rest of his life in a technical letter every third Tuesday.

"Tar," he says. "Whose is the handwriting?"

Tar does not look up from the bench.

"A man I worked with."

"Ah," says Pascual.

And that is all either of them says about it.

---

The gale comes on the ninth of October 1953 and takes the roof off the store shed, thirty feet of the seaward pen fence, and most of Pascual's opinion of Dr Reginald Tar.

It arrives at four in the morning. By ten past four Pascual is in the water in the seaward pen with a rope round his waist and Beta going round him in circles like a shell from a gun, and the surge is coming over the sill, and the gate — the seaward gate, the one that lets an animal out into eight hundred fathoms — is working on a broken hinge, and there is precisely one man on the station who is any use at all, and he arrives at a dead run in his pyjama trousers with a crowbar.

What follows takes two hours and ten minutes and is the best two hours he has had in years.

They get it done by shouting. Tar, in the water up to his chest, hangs on the gate with the crowbar as a lever while Pascual gets the chain through; Pascual goes under three times and comes up with the chain and loses it twice; Tar holds the gate on his own shoulder — on his *shoulder*, a man who weighs nine and a half stone — for as long as it takes, which is longer than it should be, because Pascual's hands are cut and slipping.

"*Otra vez!* Again! Hold it, hold it — "

"I am holding it —"

"Higher, boy, put your back in it — "

"I'm five foot six and it's the *sea*, Pascual —"

"Then be five foot six harder!"

And Tar laughs, in a gale, in the water, with a gate on his shoulder, and gets it higher; and the chain goes through; and Pascual gets the pin in with the heel of his hand and two blows of a crowbar, and they lie on the sill in eight inches of surf like a pair of dead fish while the sky goes grey.

"Is she all right?"

"She's fine. She's *magnificent.* Did you see her go? She went round the whole pen four times and never touched the fence, in that."

"I saw."

"You're bleeding."

"So are yours."

"Mine are supposed to," says Pascual, "I'm a vet," which is not even slightly logical, and Tar laughs again, lying on his back in the surf with his spectacles gone somewhere and his hair in his eyes.

Then he says, "That was the best fun I've had since 1950," and stops laughing rather abruptly, and gets up, and goes to find his glasses.

---

Afterwards, in the morning, with the shed roof in the palms and Jean walking the fence line with a notebook, Pascual sits on an upturned crate outside the sick bay and takes stock, in the way he does, which is not a procedure and takes about four minutes.

He is not in love with the man. He would like to be very clear about that, chiefly with himself, and he is aware that the clarity is doing a certain amount of work.

He likes him. That is the part he did not see coming in November. The man is quick, and he is funny, and he does not pretend, at the bench, at ten at night, when the coat is off; he takes a correction the way a good farrier takes one, which is to say instantly and without sulking; and there is nobody else within four thousand miles that Pascual can hand half a thought to and get the other half back improved.

And he is carrying something. Pascual has no idea what it is beyond the shape of it, and he knows where the man's letters come from, and he knows what he is not going to do about any of it.

So: work, then. Animals, and the pens, and the rows, and the rum at Christmas, and a man to shout at across a rail who shouts back and is usually wrong and sometimes right.

It is a great deal more than he had in Florida. He is, he finds, sitting on a crate with his hands bandaged in the middle of a wrecked station, entirely content.

He is wrong about one thing, which is that he thinks this is settled.
