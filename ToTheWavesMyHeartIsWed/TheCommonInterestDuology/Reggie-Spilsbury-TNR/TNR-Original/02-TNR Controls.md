# Controls

On the eleventh of February the Government turns off the electricity, and at a quarter past three in the afternoon the long tank begins to freeze.

Reggie is on the second floor when the pumps stop. He hears it through the floor: not a noise but the end of one, the thud that has been going on under his feet for two years simply not arriving. He sits with his pen above the page and waits for it, and it does not come, and he says, "Spilsbury," to the open door.

"I heard."

"The generator's meant to cut in."

"The generator," says Spilsbury, from the next room, in the voice of a man reading from a document, "has had no diesel since Thursday, because the Ministry's allocation for the quarter was used in January, because January was cold."

"Who told you that?"

"Nobody. I went and looked at the tank."

Reggie is already on his feet and getting his coat off the back of the chair. It is minus six outside. It has been below freezing for sixteen days together, and the canal behind the Labs is solid enough that boys are walking on it, and the long tank on the ground floor holds five animals that breathe air, in water at four degrees, under a roof that is not much warmer than the street.

Without the pumps to keep it moving, the water will skin over. After that it will set.

"How long have we got?"

"I have no idea," says Spilsbury, coming through the door with his own coat on and his hat in his hand. "Shall we find out?"

---

They find out. It is fourteen minutes.

That is how long it takes a hole the size of a dinner plate, broken with a boat hook in the surface of bay three, to close over again with a skin that will hold a pencil. Spilsbury times it with his watch, standing on the walkway above the bays with a torch, and then times it again, and gets thirteen and a half.

"So we break them every ten," Reggie says.

"Every ten. Five bays."

"Two of us."

"There's nobody else. Hollis has gone home, Nicholson has gone home, and the night man is sixty-eight."

"Then it's two of us."

Spilsbury looks at him along the walkway. The torch is under his chin and it makes his face look like a lantern at Hallowe'en, and he is, Reggie is almost sure, trying not to laugh.

"Until the current comes back."

"Which is when?"

"The wireless says tomorrow. The wireless said that yesterday."

"Right," Reggie says, and hands him the coal shovel. "You take one, three and five. I'll have two and four. And if you fall in I'm not coming after you."

"Noted."

---

It is the stupidest night of his life and he would not give a minute of it back.

By six o'clock it is fully dark and the only light in the tank hall is two storm lanterns and a torch, and the breath of five animals coming up in white plumes at the holes, and the breath of two men coming up in white plumes on the walkway. By seven Reggie has lost the feeling in both his feet. By eight he has worked out that the ice closes fastest at the corners, where the water is stillest, and that if he breaks the corners first the middle holds for twelve minutes instead of ten, and he shouts this down the hall, and Spilsbury shouts back, "*Good*," and writes it down.

By nine he has had the idea about the paraffin stove.

"We heat buckets. Not hot. Tepid. Pour them in at the corners. It'll buy us minutes."

"You'll crack the glass."

"Not at twenty degrees. It's thermal shock at forty, and I'm not an idiot."

"You've never been an idiot," says Spilsbury, "you've been a man with a paraffin stove," but he goes and gets the stove.

It works. It buys them seven minutes a bay. Reggie carries the buckets and Spilsbury pours, because Spilsbury has the steadier hands, and between rounds they sit on the walkway with their backs to the rail and their knees up, drinking tea from the same flask, and Spilsbury talks about the fuel figures and the Minister's arithmetic and the precise number of tons of coal that are at this moment frozen solid in wagons in sidings in Durham, and it is not funny, it is a national emergency, and Reggie laughs until he has to put the flask down.

"You've been reading the *Times* in the lavatory again."

"I read the *Times* where I like."

"You know the number of wagons."

"Somebody ought to."

At a quarter past ten Reggie leans out over bay four to get at the far corner and his boot goes on the rime on the walkway and he goes in up to the waist.

---

The cold is not like cold. It is like being hit.

For about two seconds he cannot breathe at all, and he knows exactly why, because he has the whole of it written down on the second floor, with timings: the gasp, the involuntary hold, the heart trying to climb out through the ribs. It is extremely interesting and he is not in a position to take notes.

Then there is a hand in the back of his collar and another under his arm, and he is on the walkway on his hands and knees, water pouring off him, and Spilsbury is kneeling in front of him saying, "Tar. *Tar.* Breathe out. Out first."

He breathes out. It comes out of him in a shout.

"Good. Again."

"I'm — I'm all —"

"You are all right. You were in it for six seconds. Get up."

"The *bay* —"

"The bay will keep for four minutes. Get up."

He gets up. Spilsbury has his overcoat off before he is fully standing and puts it round him, over the wet one, and then takes it off again and takes the wet one off first, and then the jacket under it, working fast, with his hands perfectly steady; and puts the dry coat back on him and buttons it up to the throat, all six buttons, as if Reggie were four years old. Then he takes Reggie's face in both hands and looks at him, very close, in the lantern light, and Reggie sees that Spilsbury's hands are steady and his face is not.

It is the first time he has seen that. It goes into him like the water did.

"I'm all right," he says. "Alec. I'm all right."

"I know. I counted."

"Counted what?"

"Your pulse. It is a hundred and forty and coming down." Spilsbury lets go of his face. "Sit by the stove. I will do two and four."

"You'll be doing all five."

"Yes."

"In your shirtsleeves."

"It will be very instructive," says Spilsbury, and goes, and Reggie sits by the paraffin stove in a coat that smells of Spilsbury's tobacco and watches him walk down the walkway in the lantern light in his waistcoat and shirt, with a boat hook, at minus six, and is so happy he could be sick.

---

He watches the animals too, because he is sitting still for the first time in seven hours and there is nothing else to look at.

They have learned the holes. That is the first thing. By midnight, all five of them come up at the holes within a minute of the ice being broken, as though they know the sound of the boat hook — which they must, since they are not stupid — and go down again, and wait.

The second thing is Four and Five.

They are in adjacent bays, with the glass partition between them, and every time Reggie looks down with the torch, both of them are at the partition. Not at the hole. At the glass. Four has one hand flat against it. Five is on the other side, lower down, very still.

"Alec," he says, when Spilsbury comes back past the stove. "Look at the partition in four."

Spilsbury looks.

"Hm."

"They've been like that since the pumps went off."

"Display," says Spilsbury. "They're territorial. It's in every account. A strange animal on the other side of a wall at a bad moment."

"Or they're cold, and they want to be nearer something warm."

"Through glass?"

"They don't know it's glass is doing it. They know there's something there."

"Write it down," says Spilsbury. "With the time. We will see if it persists when the pumps come back."

Reggie writes it down: *01.10. 4 & 5 at partition, both. 4 — hand to glass.* Then he goes and breaks the ice in bay two, because it is his turn, and Spilsbury sits by the stove in the other coat, the wet one, which he has put on because it is better than nothing, and says nothing about it at all.

---

The current comes back at seven minutes past six in the morning.

They are both on the walkway when it does. The lights come up all at once, a long white row of them down the hall, and they both put their hands over their eyes; and then, under their feet, the pumps begin: a cough, and another, and then the thud, steady, the same as always, and the water in all five bays starts to move.

Neither of them says anything for about a minute.

Then Spilsbury says, "Well," and Reggie turns round and puts his face against the front of his wet coat, in the tank hall, in the full electric light, with the night man on his way in at any moment, and Spilsbury lets him, for perhaps four seconds, and then puts him away by the shoulders and says, "Go home. Seven. The bus."

"It's six."

"Then walk slowly."

Reggie goes to the door. He looks back once. Spilsbury is standing on the walkway looking down at bay four, and Four and Five are both still at the glass.

---

The paper is on the table in Spilsbury's flat on the ninth of March, which is a Sunday, and the snow has at last begun to go off the roofs in long wet slides that land in the street like dropped sacks.

Spilsbury has typed the fair copy himself. He does not trust the typing pool with it, for reasons that are partly security and partly that the typing pool spells *hypothermia* with an *i*. There are forty-one pages and twelve figures, and Reggie reads it through at the table, with the gas fire going and a cup of cocoa going cold at his elbow, and Spilsbury in the other chair pretending to read the *Observer*.

It is good. It is better than good. It is the half of it that can be printed — the curves, the timings, the gasp and the hold and the long decline — written up as a contribution to the survival of men in cold water, which is what it will be, because the Admiralty will read it; and the half that cannot be printed is in a locked cabinet on the second floor, where it belongs, and Reggie does not think about it much.

He gets to the title page last, because Spilsbury put it at the bottom.

There are three names on it. The Director's is first, because the Director's always is. Reggie's is second. Spilsbury's is third.

He sits and looks at it.

"This is wrong," he says.

"It is not wrong."

"You're the senior man. You ran the programme. It's your design."

"It is your series."

"It's *our* series —"

"It is your series," says Spilsbury, not looking up from the *Observer*. "You ran the third subject and the fourth and the fifth. You found the corner effect with a boat hook at half past eight at night. You found the probe drift. The design is mine and the design is a page and a half, and the Director will not read past the abstract, and the man whose name comes second on a *Lancet* paper at twenty-eight is a man who will be asked to things."

"And the man whose name comes third at thirty-five?"

"Has been asked to things already," says Spilsbury, "and did not enjoy them."

Reggie puts the page down.

He is aware of a great many things at once and not all of them are comfortable. He is aware that this is generous, and that Spilsbury is not a generous man in the ordinary way, and that it is not being done in the ordinary way either, since it has been done without a word and put at the bottom of the pile. He is aware of his own face getting hot. He is aware that he would like, very much, to get up and cross the room and take the *Observer* out of the man's hands and do something that would make it impossible for him to go on pretending to read it.

He does that.

"Tar. I was reading."

"You weren't. You've been on the same column for twenty minutes. It's about pigs."

"It is about the pig census."

"*Alec*," says Reggie, and sits down across his knees, and takes his glasses off for him, and Spilsbury stops talking about the pig census.

---

Afterwards — a good while afterwards, on the hearthrug, with the fire down and the paper fanned all over the floor where it went when the table got knocked — Reggie lies with his head on Spilsbury's chest and says, "The partitions."

"Mm."

"Four and Five. They were at the glass again on Thursday. And Friday. It's every day now, even with the pumps on. Their readings on those days are all over the place. Pulse, core, all of it. It's noise."

"I know. I have been looking at the variance."

"It's the other animal, isn't it. They can see each other and it winds them up."

"It is almost certainly that."

"So we screen them."

"We screen them," Spilsbury agrees, as if Reggie had asked for the salt. "Paint the glass. Black. Both sides. Then every bay is its own control, and the variance comes down, and the next series is clean."

"Monday?"

"Monday."

Reggie lies there and feels it go through him: the small, hard pleasure of a problem closed. He has loved this since he was at school — two people who know what they are doing, looking at the same bad number, and seeing the same fix in the same second, and not needing to discuss it. He did not know until this winter that there was a way of having it that was better than this, and now he is lying on a hearthrug having it.

"You know what I like about you," he says.

"I have a list of hypotheses."

"You never make me explain anything."

Spilsbury's hand comes up into his hair and stays there.

"No," he says. "I don't."

---

In May the landlady at Reggie's lodgings announces that she is selling up and going to her daughter's in Worthing, and he has until the end of June.

He takes it to Spilsbury as good news.

"There are two bedrooms here."

"There is a bedroom and a box room with a bed in it."

"Which is two bedrooms. There are men sharing flats all over London. There's no housing. There are three chaps from Hollis's section in one place in Kilburn with a *gas ring*."

"There are," says Spilsbury.

"So?"

"So no."

Reggie stares at him.

He had not expected it. He had worked it out in his head on the bus, and it was perfectly sound: the housing shortage, the two colleagues, the box room, the rent halved. He has been imagining it for a week, in some detail — mornings, chiefly. He had thought Spilsbury would object to the mornings.

"Why not?"

"Because two unmarried men of our ages in a flat with one bed in use is a thing a policeman can count."

"There's the box room."

"There are the neighbours. There is the landlady, who comes back from Eastbourne. There is the coalman, and the milkman, and the woman across the road who has the upstairs front and nothing whatever to do."

"Alec, nobody is *looking* —"

"Nobody is looking until somebody is. After that, everybody always was. It is extraordinary how that works."

He says it perfectly mildly. It is his ordinary voice, the one he uses for the pig census and the diesel allocation, and there is nothing in his face at all.

Reggie opens his mouth to argue, and then does not, for a reason that he does not stop to look at. It is something about the mildness. It is like putting a hand on a wall and feeling a machine running behind it, very large, very steady, that he did not know was there.

He does not ask what it is. He has the curious sense, for about a second, that if he asked he would be told, and that he would rather not be told on a Tuesday in May with the window open.

"All right," he says. "Then where do I go?"

"Number fourteen, Alma Terrace. First floor back. Two rooms, own gas, own door, widow on the ground floor who is deaf in one ear and goes to her sister's on Sundays. Four minutes' walk. Thirty-five shillings."

Reggie looks at him for a long moment.

"You checked."

"I check a number of things."

"When did you check?"

"On Thursday. You told me about Worthing on Wednesday."

"I told you it was good news."

"It is good news," says Spilsbury. "It is four minutes' walk."

And Reggie laughs, because there is nothing else to do with it, and goes across the room and kisses him, and forgets about the machine behind the wall.

---

The *Lancet* takes it in June, and prints it on the twenty-sixth of July, and the Director gives a sherry party in the library on the second floor, because the Director likes a thing done properly.

It is hot. It has turned, since the spring, into the hottest summer anybody can remember, which the English are treating as a personal affront, and the library windows are open and the sherry is warm.

Reggie is very good at sherry parties.

He does not know where he got it. School, probably; his father, possibly. It is a manner, and it fits him like a well-cut coat: he can take a man by the elbow, and turn him to the Director, and turn him away again without the man feeling turned, and say something about the Admiralty that makes the Director laugh. He does it for forty minutes, with his name second on nine copies of the *Lancet* lying about the library tables, and he is aware of Spilsbury at the far end of the room, by the window, talking to nobody, watching.

Nicholson finds him by the bookcase.

"Tar. Well done. Well *done*."

"Thank you. It was mostly Spilsbury's."

"It's never mostly anybody's, and your name's second, so don't be modest, it doesn't suit you." Nicholson is a big fair man of forty with a kind face and ink on his cuffs. He is the best electrophysiologist in the building and the only man in it who says good morning to the night porter by name. "Margaret sends her regards. She says you're to come to supper again, and bring Spilsbury, since you're joined at the hip."

"Are we?"

"The door," says Nicholson, laughing. "That door of yours. Never shut. The rest of us are consumed with envy. My section can't sit in a room together without somebody throwing a slide rule."

"We don't throw things. We shout."

"I've heard you," says Nicholson comfortably. "The whole corridor's heard you. Come to supper. Margaret will want to hear about the ice. And I've done a new one of the canal, frozen — I went out on the Sunday with the box. I'll show you. It's not very good."

"I'm sure it's very good."

"It's terrible," says Nicholson happily, "but I like doing them," and goes off to get more sherry.

Reggie looks down the room.

Spilsbury is still at the window. He is looking at Nicholson's back. His face has nothing in it at all, which is what his face usually has, and Reggie does not know why, standing in a hot library with a glass of warm sherry, he should feel the machine again, behind the wall, running.

---

The offprints come on the first of August, a stiff brown packet of fifty, and he takes them home to Alma Terrace and puts them on the table by the window and looks at them for some time.

Spilsbury comes at ten, by the back way, as he does on Tuesdays and Fridays and occasionally, against his own procedure, on a Wednesday.

It is still hot. It is hotter at ten at night than it was at noon, in the peculiar way of London in a heatwave, and the air in the first floor back is thick and still and smells of the plane trees, and Reggie has the window up as far as it will go and is sitting on the sill in his shirtsleeves with an offprint in his hand when the door opens.

Spilsbury looks at him, and at the window, and shuts the door behind him, and crosses the room, and shuts the window.

"It's ninety degrees in here."

"About eighty-four."

"I'll *die*."

"You will not die. You will be warm. The widow's sister's house backs onto this yard, and it is Friday, and she has her windows open too."

"She's deaf."

"The *widow* is deaf. The sister is not."

"You've checked the sister."

"I check a number of things," says Spilsbury, and takes the offprint out of his hand, and reads the first page, standing there, all of it, with his lips very slightly pressed together; and Reggie sits on the sill and watches him read it and feels the heat come up in his own face that has nothing to do with the weather.

"It reads well," says Spilsbury.

"You typed it."

"You wrote it." He puts the offprint down on the table with the others, square to the edge. "Come here."

---

It is too hot for anything, and they do it anyway.

Reggie gets Spilsbury's jacket off him and it is damp through at the back. He gets the waistcoat off, and the tie, and it is like unwrapping a parcel that has been left in the sun. He puts his mouth to the side of Spilsbury's neck and it is salt, and he says so.

"You taste of the sea."

"I taste of the Northern Line."

"Stop being accurate."

"I shall try," says Spilsbury, and does not, at all, for the next hour.

They end up on the bed with the sheet kicked off onto the floor and not a stitch between them, and even so there is sweat running down the hollow of Reggie's back, and Spilsbury follows it with one finger, slowly, from the nape to the base of the spine, as though it were a line on a chart.

"*Oh* —"

"Hold still."

"I can't hold still when you — Alec, that's — no, don't stop, *there*, do that again —"

"You will want to be quieter. The sister."

"Damn the sister."

"Tar."

"I'm being quiet. This is quiet. You've no idea how quiet this is."

And Spilsbury laughs — he really does laugh, into the small of Reggie's back, a short sound Reggie can feel more than hear — and Reggie turns over on the damp sheet to see it, because he has heard that laugh perhaps four times and never once seen it; and catches the tail of it, and pulls him down, and kisses it off his mouth.

He has learned a great deal since November. He knows now that Spilsbury likes to be held down, which nobody at the Labs would believe, and that he will not ask for it, and that if Reggie takes both his wrists and puts them flat to the mattress above his head he goes quite still and his eyes go dark. He knows that Spilsbury's shoulders are the most sensitive place on him, and that the inside of his upper arm is the second. He knows that the man will count his own pulse aloud, if he is not stopped, and that the way to stop him is to put a mouth over his.

He does all of it. He takes his time. It is too hot to do anything fast.

"Tell me," he says, low, against the inside of Spilsbury's arm.

"Tell you what."

"What it's doing. You always tell me. Tell me now."

"It is —" Spilsbury stops. It is extraordinary to watch. His throat moves and nothing comes out, and his hands, held flat under Reggie's, turn over and close on Reggie's fingers. "It is a hundred and twenty. Rising."

"What else?"

"Reggie."

He says it like that, just the name, and Reggie feels it go through him from the scalp down.

"What else, Alec?"

"I can't —" A breath. "I can't give you a figure."

"Good," says Reggie. "*Good*," and lets go of his wrists, and puts both arms round him instead, and does not let him give any more figures for a considerable time.

---

Afterwards the heat is worse, and neither of them cares.

They lie on the bare mattress with one foot touching, because that is as much as can be borne, and the street is quiet and the plane trees are quiet, and there is a train somewhere a long way off. Reggie's hair is stuck to his forehead. Spilsbury's glasses are on the floor, where they went.

"Are you happy?" Reggie says, to the ceiling.

It is not a question he has asked anyone before. It comes out of him without any drafting at all.

Spilsbury is quiet long enough that Reggie thinks he is asleep.

"Yes," he says. "Considerably more than I had budgeted for."

"Is that good?"

"It is very good. It is also a variance I cannot account for, and I find that I don't mind." He turns his head on the mattress. Reggie can feel him do it. "Are you?"

"Yes."

"Say it properly."

"I was happy on that walkway at one in the morning with my feet dead," Reggie says. "I'm happy now. And I'm not saying it again, because it's embarrassing, and you'll write it down."

"I have already written it down."

"*When?*"

"In February. On the walkway. You were in my coat."

Reggie lies there in the heat and cannot say anything at all.

Out beyond the shut window the widow's sister's wireless is playing dance music, very faintly, and the offprints are square on the table, fifty of them, with his name second.

---

In September the partitions in the long tank are painted out, both sides, with two coats of black bitumen, so that no animal can see another.

It takes the variance down by forty per cent in a fortnight. Reggie runs the numbers three times on the second floor, because it is too good, and it comes out the same each time, and he sits back in his chair and puts a hand to the back of his neck and rubs it, twice, hard.

"Alec," he says, to the open door. "It's forty per cent."

"I saw."

"Forty. We'll have the whole of the next series clean by Christmas."

"You will have it clean by Christmas," says Spilsbury, from the next room, and Reggie can hear that he is writing it down.

Down in the tank hall, according to the day book, Four has stopped coming to the glass. For the first week he went to it every morning and lay against the black paint with one hand flat, for up to an hour, which the night man logged; and then for a shorter time; and by the end of the month he does not go at all, but lies on the bottom at the far end of the bay, very quietly, with his tail under him, and his readings are much steadier than they were.

Reggie reads that entry at a quarter to six, with his coat on, on his way out.

He notes it, because it is good, and he puts the book back on its shelf, and goes home by the bus.
