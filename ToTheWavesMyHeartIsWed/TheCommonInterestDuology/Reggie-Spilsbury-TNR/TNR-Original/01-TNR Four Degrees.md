# Four Degrees

*The Next Room is an alternate universe branching from 15-TCI Number 197. Nobody came back for a coat.*

The boiler went at a quarter past four, and by ten o'clock the whole building is at about four degrees, which Alec Spilsbury knows because he has a thermometer on his bench and has been reading it on the hour out of interest.

Four degrees is also the figure in the long tank on the ground floor, to within a tenth. He has noted the coincidence. It is not a significant one. It is November, and there is no coal.

The lights on the second floor have tripped twice since supper, because somebody at the Ministry has put six men's worth of electric fires on a circuit wired for six men's worth of lamps, and so at five to ten he is standing in the passage with his back to the wall beside the fuse box, in his overcoat, with the board open and a card of fuse wire in his fingers, when Tar comes up the stairs.

He hears him before he sees him. Tar takes stairs two at a time when something has gone well and one at a time when it has not. Spilsbury has had twenty months in which to establish this and has not yet seen an exception.

These are two at a time.

---

He is twenty-seven. He is the best man Spilsbury has worked beside, and the most wasteful with himself: he will work until two in the morning, eat whatever has been left on the end of the bench, and walk out into a building at four degrees in his shirtsleeves because he had something to say and his jacket was on the other chair.

He is in his shirtsleeves now. He has a clipboard in one hand, and he has pushed his glasses up his nose with the back of the other and left a grey thumbprint on the left lens, which he has not noticed.

"Spilsbury. Have you got a minute? You haven't, you're mending the lights. Mend them afterwards." He is already coming down the passage. "It's the third one. The third subject. I've got the whole run and it's clean, it's clean all the way down, and you said I wouldn't have it before Christmas."

"I said you would not have it clean before Christmas."

"Well, I've got it clean. Look at the curve."

He stops about two feet away. Spilsbury would put it at two feet and an inch, since he measures things, and since there is nothing whatever in the passage to look at but the man and the fuse box.

Tar does not hold out the clipboard. He puts his free hand on Spilsbury's arm instead, above the elbow, through the overcoat, and leaves it there, and says something about the third subject which Spilsbury does not hear at all.

---

He has wanted this man since about the spring of 1945, and he has known it the way one knows a temperature.

He has done the arithmetic on it a number of times since, most recently on Tuesday, and it has come out the same way every time, and he does it again now, in the dark by the fuse box, faster than he has ever done it.

On one side: a programme, a laboratory, a career, and the better part of a decade of work he is not going to be given anywhere else in England.

On the other: Tar's time, Tar's attention, and the whole of Tar's working day, every idea he has before anybody else is told about it. All of which Spilsbury already has, and all of which has cost him nothing. What is left over, once that is subtracted, is a quantity he has hitherto judged to be not worth the price.

That is the first second and the second.

In the third he observes that Tar has stopped talking.

In the fourth he observes that Tar is not looking at the clipboard, and that the hand has not moved, and that Tar knows both of those things. This is not a young man who has wandered into something in a cold passage. It is a young man who has walked forty feet in his shirtsleeves and is now waiting to see what will be done with him, and who would, Spilsbury knows with perfect accuracy, give him everything, and go on giving it, for as long as he was allowed.

He has known that for a year. It is not new either.

What is new arrives in the fifth second, and it is this: that he has done the sum a sixth time and it has come out the same, and he does not intend to obey it.

He examines this with some care, because it is not like him. He is not confused. He is not overcome; his hands are perfectly steady on the card of fuse wire, and he could give the temperature of the passage to half a degree. He simply finds that he has a preference, and that it is larger than the figure on the other side of the page, and that at thirty-four he is entitled to discover that he has one.

In the sixth second he puts the other thing into the account as well, because he is not going to leave it out merely because it is inconvenient: Tar will pay for this too, and Tar has not counted it, and Spilsbury is not going to count it for him.

He notes that. He does not revise the result.

In the seventh second he listens to the stairwell, and there is nothing in it: no door, no feet, no voice, nobody on the half-landing. The building is empty and cold and silent from the roof to the tanks.

In the eighth he puts the fuse wire into his pocket.

In the ninth he puts his free hand over Tar's hand where it lies on his arm, and holds it there.

---

"Oh," says Tar.

It is very quiet. The breath goes out of him in a small cloud in the cold.

Spilsbury has thought about a number of ways of doing the next thing, over twenty months, and he does none of them. He takes his hand off Tar's hand and puts it to the side of his face instead, at the jaw, where the skin is cold and there is a day's roughness under the thumb, and he tips his head down, and kisses him.

Tar's mouth is cold for about a second and then it is not. He makes a sound against Spilsbury's lip that Spilsbury has never heard him make and fully intends to hear again. Then the clipboard goes out of his hand and down onto the linoleum with a report like a rifle.

They both stop dead.

The building says nothing at all. Somewhere on the ground floor the pump in the long tank thuds, and goes on thudding, and that is all.

Tar starts to laugh. He puts his forehead down against Spilsbury's shoulder to do it, which muffles it, and Spilsbury stands there with his back against the wall by an open fuse box and feels a man shaking with laughter against the front of his overcoat, and discovers that the arithmetic has not accounted for this at all.

"Well," Tar says, eventually, into the wool. His voice isn't steady. "That's the series, then."

"You haven't told me the series."

"No. I seem to have lost my place."

"Page two," says Spilsbury. "You'll find it on the floor."

---

He does not do anything further in the passage, because it has a staircase at one end of it and a Ministry of Supply at the other.

"Not here," he says.

"No." Tar has picked up the clipboard. He is holding it against his chest with both arms, like a boy with a satchel, and his face is doing a great many things at once, and none of them is hidden. "Where?"

"Get your jacket."

"It's on the chair."

"I know where it is. Get it."

"And then?"

"And then you may watch me mend this fuse, because I am not leaving six men's electric fires on a bad circuit overnight in a building full of paper."

Tar looks at him. The thumbprint on the left lens catches the light from the stairwell.

"You're going to mend the fuse."

"It will take forty seconds."

"*Now.*"

"Tar. Your jacket."

He goes. Spilsbury takes the wire out of his pocket and draws the carrier and clears the old wire off the screws and cuts a fresh length, and when Tar comes back up the passage with his jacket on and his scarf round his neck he is winding it on, and he finishes it, and pushes the carrier home, and the lights in the far room come up.

"Your hands are perfectly steady," says Tar.

"Yes."

"Mine aren't."

"I had noticed," says Spilsbury, and shuts the board.

---

It is a mile and a half to the flat and it is raining the thin, flat, determined rain that London keeps for November.

He tells Tar about the bus at the corner of the second road, because it is there, and because he wants it on the record that it was mentioned.

"I know about the bus. I take the bus."

"I am telling you it is there."

"I heard you." Tar has his hands in his pockets and his collar up and his hair is flat on his forehead already. "Is that meant to be a courtesy or a test?"

"A courtesy."

"Then thank you. I don't want the bus."

They walk. Past the dark front of the Co-op, past the bomb site with the willowherb gone to brown string along the hoarding, past a man walking a dog that does not want to be walked.

"Do you know what this is?" Spilsbury says.

Tar does not pretend to misunderstand him, which is one of the things about Tar.

"Two years," he says. "If they're feeling ungenerous. I looked it up when I was sixteen, in the school library, under a table."

"Why?"

"I thought it was the sort of thing one ought to know before one needed it." He glances up sideways. The rain is on his glasses. "It's a number, Spilsbury. I know the number."

Spilsbury considers that for about half a street.

It is exactly true. Tar knows the number the way he knows the freezing point of seawater: precisely, correctly, and without the slightest weight on it. Spilsbury knows the same number and has carried it about for eighteen years at its full weight, and has seen what it does to men who come to the end of it, and could, in the next forty yards, set the weight down in front of this young man in the rain so that he could not fail to feel it.

He does not.

It is not an oversight. He has put it in the account already, at about the sixth second, and he declines to take it out again for the sake of a wet pavement.

"Yes," he says. "It is a number."

---

It is a curious thing, and he will think about it afterwards, that by the third road Tar has forgotten entirely where they are going.

It is the series. He cannot not tell it. He has held it in for nearly fifteen minutes and it comes out of him in the rain like water out of a split pipe, and Spilsbury, who has been waiting for it, lets it come.

"The first two went off the curve at thirty-one and thirty-four minutes. You saw those."

"I saw those."

"The third held. I kept expecting him to break, and he didn't break, he held at four degrees for fifty-two minutes and the whole of the decline is in the readings, every one, core, pulse, the lot, and there isn't a gap in it anywhere."

"*Him*," says Spilsbury.

"What?"

"Nothing. Go on. What did you do about the probe drift?"

"I changed the probe at twenty minutes, I'm not an idiot, and I ran the old one against the bath —"

"Against which bath?"

"The *reference* bath, Spilsbury, what do you take me for —"

And they argue about the probe, happily and at volume, for most of a street, and it is the best argument Spilsbury has had all week, because Tar is right about the bath and wrong about the interval and knows it by the end and says so; and it is only at the door of the house, when Spilsbury stops and gets out his key, that Tar stops as well, halfway through a sentence about the sampling rate, and looks at the door, and remembers.

Spilsbury watches him remember. It goes over his whole face at once, from the brow down.

"Ah," says Tar.

"Quite," says Spilsbury, and opens the door.

---

The flat is the first floor of a narrow house in a street that was knocked about in 1941 and has not been put back. It is self-contained, which is the reason he took it. The landlady lives on the ground floor, and is in Eastbourne with her sister until Tuesday.

"You checked," Tar says, on the stairs.

"I check a number of things."

"How long have you known she'd be in Eastbourne?"

"Since about the first of the month."

Tar stops on the landing and turns round and looks at him, and there it is: the smirk. The ordinary one, the public-school one, the one the man wears to seminars and to the canteen and to the Director's sherry. It is the first time Spilsbury has ever had it pointed directly at him from eighteen inches away, and he finds that at this distance it is not a smirk at all. It is delight, and it has nowhere else to go.

"Since the first of the *month*," Tar says.

"Go up, Tar. I'd like to shut the door."

---

The sitting room is as cold as the Labs. He had known it would be. There is a jar of shillings on the mantelpiece for the meter, because he is a man who keeps a jar of shillings, and he puts two in and lights the fire, and it catches with a thud and begins to tick.

Tar stands in the middle of the floor with rain running off the ends of his hair and looks at the books.

He looks at them properly, the way he looks at anything, going along the shelf with his head on one side, and Spilsbury watches him do it and is aware of something happening under his own breastbone that he does not have an instrument for and does not wish to be without.

"You've got the Krogh," says Tar. "The capillaries. The German edition."

"Yes."

"I've been trying to get that for a year."

"You may borrow it."

"I'll never give it back."

"I know," says Spilsbury, "take your glasses off, they're wet."

Tar takes his glasses off. Without them his face is younger and much less certain, and he stands blinking in the gaslight with them in his hand, and Spilsbury takes them from him and dries them on his own handkerchief, carefully, both lenses, and gets the thumbprint off the left one while he is about it, and folds them, and puts them on the mantelpiece beside the shillings.

"I can't see you," Tar says.

"You've seen me."

"That's not —" He stops. "Yes. All right. I have."

---

It is too cold to take anything off, and for some while neither of them does.

He gets Tar by the lapels of his wet jacket and backs him the three steps to the wall beside the fire, and this time he does not stop at one. He kisses him slowly and then not slowly, and Tar's hands come up inside the overcoat and get hold of the back of his jacket and hang on, and the fire ticks, and the rain goes on at the window.

"Your hands are frozen."

"Your fault. You made me mend a fuse."

"I mended the fuse."

"I *watched*. It was extremely trying."

Spilsbury gets the jacket off him. It is wet through across the shoulders, and the shirt under it is damp, and the man under that is cold enough to be shivering in fine continuous waves that he is plainly trying to control and cannot. So Spilsbury opens his own overcoat, and his own jacket, and his own waistcoat, and takes both of Tar's hands and puts them inside his shirt, flat against his ribs.

The cold of them goes into him like a blade. He does not flinch. He had thought he might, and is interested to find he does not.

"*God*," Tar says. "Sorry — "

"Leave them."

"They're like ice, you'll —"

"Tar. Leave them there."

So Tar leaves them there, spread over his ribs, and Spilsbury feels them warm by degrees against him — first the palms, then the fingers, then the tips — and Tar stands with his eyes half shut and his breath coming shorter, and the shivering in him changes by degrees into something else entirely.

"You've a heart going," Tar says, low. "Right here. Under my hand."

"Yes."

"It's quick."

"It is about a hundred and ten."

Tar opens his eyes. At that distance, without the glasses, Spilsbury does not suppose he can see very much at all.

"You're counting your own pulse."

"I count everything."

"How long," Tar says, "have you —"

"The spring of last year."

"The *spring* —" His fingers close, a little, against the ribs. "You might have said."

"I might."

"Why didn't you?"

"Arithmetic."

"And tonight?"

"Tonight the arithmetic came out the same, and I did not care for the answer."

Tar looks at him for rather a long time.

"I'll want to see the working," he says.

"Later," says Spilsbury, and puts his mouth to the side of his throat.

---

He has had twenty months to watch this man and he has watched him closely. He knows, for instance, that when Tar is pleased with a result he puts his hand to the back of his own neck and rubs it, hard, twice. He has formed a hypothesis about that and has never been in a position to test it.

He tests it now. He puts his fingers where Tar's own go, at the nape, under the wet hair, and presses, and then he puts his mouth there as well.

The noise Tar makes is not a word. His whole body comes off the wall.

"Ah," says Spilsbury, against his neck. "I thought so."

"You *thought* —" Tar's voice has gone somewhere Spilsbury has not heard it go. "You've been taking notes."

"Twenty months of them."

"Then you'd better —" A breath. "You'd better show me the rest."

---

It is not possible to undress in a room at six degrees with any dignity, and they do not try.

They do it in stages, as the fire gets the air up, and it is absurd, and Tar laughs at least twice and Spilsbury very nearly does. Tar's shirt comes off and he says, with complete seriousness, "No, that's a mistake, put it back," and Spilsbury does not put it back, and puts his hands on him instead, flat, over the shoulder blades, and then down the length of his spine to the small of his back, and Tar stops laughing.

He is lightly made. Spilsbury knew that; he had not known that he could span the whole of the back with two hands, nearly, or that the skin over the shoulders would take the heat of a palm so quickly and hold it. He puts his mouth to the collarbone, and to the notch at the base of the throat, and to the place under the jaw where the pulse is, and Tar tips his head back against the wall to give it to him and says, perfectly clearly, "*There*. Do that again. Slower."

Spilsbury stops.

He lifts his head and looks at him.

"What?" says Tar.

"Nothing." It is not nothing. It is one of the more interesting things that has happened to him. "You give instructions."

"Do you mind?"

"No."

"Because I can stop, if you'd —"

"I do not want you to stop. I want to hear everything you say." He puts his mouth back to the throat, slower, and feels the pulse under it jump. "Go on."

And Tar goes on.

He is, it turns out, the most articulate man Spilsbury has ever had against a wall, and he uses it the way he uses it in a seminar: flat, dry, specific, in exactly the voice he would use to ask for a figure. *Not there. Higher. Yes. Your hand, the other one — yes.* It goes through Spilsbury like current. He has spent twenty months listening to that voice through an open door, reading out temperatures, and he did not know until tonight that it could do this, and he thinks that he should have known, and that it is the only thing about this man he has failed to predict.

---

The bed is in the next room and the next room has no fire at all.

"We will have to go through," he says.

"It'll be freezing."

"It will be about four degrees."

"Then bring the coat."

So he brings the overcoat, and both jackets, and the travelling rug off the back of the chair, and they get in under the blankets and all the rest of it on top, and it is like getting into a river. Tar swears, comprehensively and at some length, and pushes his whole length up against Spilsbury under the covers, knees and hips and chest, and puts his cold feet on Spilsbury's shins, and Spilsbury says, "*Tar*," in a voice that he has not used since he was a boy, and Tar laughs into his shoulder until the bed shakes.

Then the blankets begin to hold the heat, and after a while neither of them is laughing.

It is dark. He can see very little: the pale shape of a shoulder, the line of a throat, the glint of the window. It does not matter. He has the rest of it by touch, and he takes his time, because he has waited twenty months and he is not a man who hurries a measurement he has waited for.

He learns the ribs, one at a time, with his thumb. He learns the long muscle at the side of the back, and that it is ticklish at the lower end and not at the upper, and that Tar will squirm and swear and then say, "No, don't stop, that was — do that again." He learns the hollow inside the hip and the hard line of the bone above it, and that if he puts his mouth there Tar's hand comes down into his hair and closes, and holds on.

"Alec," Tar says.

It is the first time. Nobody at the Labs uses it. Spilsbury is not sure anybody alive uses it. He stops with his mouth at Tar's hip and his whole attention on the word.

"Say something," says Tar, into the dark. "I can't see you. Talk to me."

"What would you like me to say?"

"I don't care. Anything. Your voice."

So Spilsbury talks.

He is not a man who talks in bed; he has not previously been in one with anybody he wished to talk to. It turns out that he talks the way he talks at a bench. He tells Tar precisely what he is doing, and what he intends to do next, and what he has observed Tar do in response; and he says it quietly, in order, with the measurements in, against the skin of a hip and a belly and the inside of a thigh, and Tar's breath goes shorter and shorter under it, and his hand in Spilsbury's hair goes tighter, and at one point he says, "Oh, you absolute *bastard*," in a voice of perfect wonder, and Spilsbury finds that he is smiling in the dark where nobody can see it.

"Come up here," Tar says, finally. "Come up here, I want — Alec. Come *here*."

He comes up.

Tar gets both arms round him, and a leg, and holds on hard, and they are chest to chest under the weight of every coat in the flat, and there is no more cold anywhere in the bed at all.

What follows is not quick, and it is not quiet, and at no point in it does Tar stop talking — *yes*, and *there*, and *don't you dare*, and once, very low, against Spilsbury's ear, *I didn't think you ever would* — and at some point Spilsbury stops counting anything, including his own pulse, for the first time in his adult life. He notices this only afterwards. At the time there is only a man in his arms saying his name, over and over, as though checking a figure; and then not saying anything, with his face pressed hard into Spilsbury's shoulder and his whole body pulled taut as wire; and then Spilsbury's own breath going out of him in something that is very nearly Tar's name, and is not quite, because at the last he says the other one.

"Reggie."

He says it once. He does not disguise to himself what it is to say it.

Under him, Tar goes perfectly still, and then shudders, all the way down, and says nothing at all for some time.

---

Afterwards the bed is warm, which is a thing Spilsbury had not dared put a figure on.

Tar lies half across him, heavy and loose, his head on Spilsbury's chest and one hand spread over his ribs in the place where it was put earlier. Spilsbury has an arm round him and the other hand in his hair, which is drying in the heat under the blankets and has begun to stand up at the crown.

"You said it," Tar says, eventually.

"Yes."

"My name."

"Yes."

"You've never said it."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I was not in a position to afford it."

Tar is quiet for a while. His thumb moves on Spilsbury's ribs, back and forth, very slightly.

"The working," he says. "You said later. It's later."

Spilsbury looks at the dark ceiling and considers how much of it to give.

"I had a great deal of you already," he says. "Your time, your whole day, every idea you have before anybody else has it. It was a very great deal and it cost me nothing, and I judged that the remainder was not worth what it would cost to take it."

"And tonight you judged differently."

"Tonight I judged the same, and I did it anyway."

"Why?"

"Because I wanted to." He says it plainly. "I am thirty-four. I have not previously wanted a thing more than it cost. I thought I had better find out what it was like."

Tar lifts his head. In the dark Spilsbury can see nothing but the shape of him.

"And what's it like?"

"I will tell you in the morning."

"Alec."

"It is very good," says Spilsbury. "I'd rather not say more than that until I have repeated it."

Tar laughs, and puts his head down again, and Spilsbury feels the laugh through his whole chest.

"What does it cost?" Tar says after a while, more quietly. "The remainder. You said it would cost."

Here it is.

He could give it all. He could give him the name of a man he was up at Cambridge with, and the name of that man's wife, and the rooms in Brighton and Oxford and Hammersmith where the thing comes due for somebody every month, and the particular tone of voice a Director uses when he says *I'm afraid there's nothing I can do*, and the weight of eighteen years of carrying the number. He could put the whole bill on the blankets between them, here, now, and watch this young man read it.

"If it goes wrong," he says, "everything I have."

"Then it'd better not go wrong."

"No."

"It's the same for me, you know." Tar says it into his chest, comfortably, already half asleep. "Same number."

"Is it," says Spilsbury.

It is the same number. It is not the same weight, and he knows it, and he has known it since the sixth second, and he lies in the dark with his hand in Reggie Tar's hair and does not say so.

---

Tar is asleep inside five minutes. He sleeps the way he works, completely, with one hand still flat on Spilsbury's ribs.

Spilsbury lies still until he is sure, and then eases out from under the hand, and gets the overcoat off the top of the pile and puts it round himself, and goes through to the sitting room, where the fire is still ticking over the second shilling, and takes his notebook out of the inside pocket.

He writes down everything. He always has, and nobody is ever going to read it.

*14.xi.46. Building 4°. Circuit 2 tripped ×3; rewired 22.05.*

*T. — third subject, clean run, 52 min at 4°. Probe changed at 20 min. Reference bath correct; interval wrong, conceded.*

*T. refers to subject as* he.

He looks at that line for a moment, and then leaves it, because it is an observation, and he does not strike out observations.

Underneath it he writes the rest.

*Did the sum in the passage. Result unchanged. Declined result.*

*T. knows the number. Does not know the weight. Did not give it him.*

*That is the operative line and I shall keep it in that order.*

He reads it through once, and shuts the book, and turns the fire down to save the meter, and goes back through to the cold room and the warm bed, and gets in, and Tar, without waking, turns over and puts the hand back where it was.

---

He wakes Tar at six, because it is the procedure.

"What procedure?"

"You leave at seven. You go home and change your shirt. You arrive at the Labs at twenty to nine by the bus. I arrive at half past eight on foot."

Tar sits up in the grey light with the blankets round him and every hair on his head standing on end, and puts his glasses on from the bedside table, where Spilsbury put them at some point in the night, and looks at him.

"You've done this before."

"I have planned it before."

"How many times?"

"Enough to have a procedure."

And there is the smirk again, at a distance of about a foot, in a cold bedroom at six in the morning, and Spilsbury has no instrument for what it does to him and does not wish for one.

"I'll want the German Krogh," Tar says. "Before I go."

"It is on the second shelf."

"I know where it is. I looked."

---

He arrives at the Labs at half past eight, on foot. The boiler man is not coming until Monday. The building is at four degrees exactly, according to his bench, and the long tank on the ground floor is the same, to within a tenth.

Tar arrives at twenty to nine by the bus, in a clean shirt, with the Krogh under his arm and the thumbprint gone from his glasses.

The door between their two rooms is a heavy one, with a brass knob that sticks. It has been shut for most of twenty months, and opened when one of them had something to say.

Tar opens it and does not shut it again. He wedges it with a bound volume of the *Journal of Physiology*, and goes to his bench, and takes his jacket off, and puts it over the back of the other chair, and begins without turning round to read out figures.

Spilsbury, at his own bench, writes them down.

The door stays open all day. Nobody passing in the corridor would think anything of it. It is only a door, between two men who work together, in a building with no heat.
