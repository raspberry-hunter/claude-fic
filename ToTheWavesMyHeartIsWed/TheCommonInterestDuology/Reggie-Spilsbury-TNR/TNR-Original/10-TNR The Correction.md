# The Correction

The launch comes round the head at four and Reggie knows before it is halfway across the bay.

It is not the cases, although there are two of them and there should be eight. It is not the absence of a Commander, or of a party, or of any of the apparatus that a man of that standing travels inside. It is the way he is standing at the rail: perfectly upright, perfectly still, with his hat in his hand, like a man who has been told to wait somewhere and has decided to do it properly.

Reggie has read him for nine years and has never been misinformed. He puts both hands behind his back so that nobody on the jetty can see what they are doing.

At the planks Alec says, "Tar," and nothing else.

He is grey. Not the temples; the whole of him, under the brown of three months in the tropics, in the particular way of a man who has been eating standing up since February.

"You'll want the guest room in the house," says Hester Bevan, after about a second and a half, in the voice she keeps for arrangements, "or Dr Pobil's hut, which is better, because the Colonel snores."

"The hut," says Alec.

And Pascual, who has said nothing at all, comes forward and picks up both cases before anyone can decide who ought to, and carries them up the path, and does not look back once.

---

He will not eat. He will not have the lamp lit until the door is wedged. He puts the smaller case flat on the table and stands behind it with both hands on the lid and says:

"I am going to give you an account. It will take some time and I would rather you did not interrupt until I have finished."

"Alec, it's ten o'clock and you've been at sea for eleven days."

"Yes."

"Then eat something and give me the account tomorrow."

"I am giving it to you tonight," says Alec, "and I am giving it to the room in the morning, and the reason it is tonight is that you are going to have to hear it twice and I want the first time to be without an audience."

Reggie sits down on the edge of the bed.

"All right."

---

It takes two hours and ten minutes and he does not interrupt more than six times, which for him is a considerable feat of engineering.

It goes in order, because he is who he is: the fifth of February, the letter, the four days he took over it; the ninth of February at twenty past four in the afternoon, and a man called Meikle, and three letters about the controls, in writing, to a firm.

And Reggie, sitting on the edge of a bed in a hut in the tropics, does an arithmetic he did not ask to be given.

"The fifth," he says. "And they sacked you on the ninth."

"Yes."

"You said no to me on the fifth of February in order to keep a post you then held for ninety-six hours."

"Ninety-six hours," Alec agrees, "and I should like to make one thing plain, since you will otherwise construct it for yourself: the reasoning was sound when I sent it and it is still sound. I did not refuse you for a job. I refused you because a worse man would have had the post, and that is still true, and they have got one now."

"And you didn't write and tell me."

"No."

"For three and a half months."

"For three and a half months. It is the only item I have ever withheld from you and I wrote down that I was doing it, and the reason, which was that you would have read the dismissal as the price of the refusal and tried to comfort me for a thing I chose." A pause. "You would also have been right, which I did not care for."

Reggie sits with that. He finds that he is not angry, which surprises him, and that what is underneath instead is so much worse that he would take the anger if it were on offer.

"Go on," he says.

---

The rest of it is the animal.

Four in the morning on the eleventh of February. One boat, one man, four hundred miles of coast. Accession one hundred and ninety-seven, male, four years, six feet two, in good condition, taken off the shoals in the spring with another animal of the same age and penned with him ever since — penned with him, Alec says, without any change of tone at all, because Spilsbury himself had told the firm in writing to pen them in pairs.

"You knew he was one of a pair."

"I knew."

"And you took one."

"Two would not go in the boat, and two would not have done in that pound. I costed it. It took me about eleven seconds and I would give you the same answer now." He stops. "That is the point at which you may say the thing you are keeping behind your teeth, if you would rather do it now than at the end."

"At the end," says Reggie.

"Very well."

The pound. Forty feet by sixty, tidal, shaded end, better than anything in England. Cut herring refused. Whole herring refused. Four species refused, refused, taken and dropped, refused. Live fish on the fifth day: taken, eleven of them in forty minutes, weights recorded.

*Feeding problem. As expected. Soluble.*

"And on the ninth day," says Alec, "I used your method."

Reggie looks up.

"Two taps on the stone with the handle of the net, at the same hour, from the same place on the wall. By the fourteenth day he came to the tap." For the first time in two hours the voice does something. "I was standing on the wall with the bucket in my hand, and I thought about a bay in England, and a forearm on a coping stone, and you in a doorway telling me he gave you the arm."

Reggie does not say anything, because there is nothing that would not be an insult.

"On the twentieth day he stopped," says Alec.

---

He gives the thirty-one days the way he gives everything, which is flatly, in order, with the dates: the refusals, the hand-feeding, the forced feeding on the twenty-sixth day taken and brought back, the diving of the pound for injury, the water twice daily, the moving of the shade, the tapping taken away and put back.

Then the sixth of May at about two in the afternoon. Then three days of post-mortem, alone, in a linen jacket, because he is extremely thorough and because the alternative was to sit down.

"Nothing," he says. "Good condition to the end; you have the weights. Clean lungs. No bone lesions, he was far too young to have been dived. Adrenals ordinary. Kidneys I should have been pleased to put in front of a class."

He opens the case, at last, and sets it out on the table: the weights, the daily sheets, the intake record, the blocks in their box, the slides in their tray, the ledger pages he brought out of the building in February with a column for weight and a column for date and no column for why.

"That is the account," he says. "I have not improved it anywhere."

---

Reggie gets up and lights the second lamp, because two hours in the dark is enough for anybody, and goes to the table and starts checking the figures.

He does it because it is what the man crossed four thousand miles to have done. He also does it because his hands need something, and because if he looks at Alec's face for another thirty seconds he is going to do something that will not help either of them.

He works for half an hour. Weights first, then intake, then the dates, cross-checked, twice. It is an immaculate record. It is the best captive record of a decline that exists anywhere in the world and it was kept by a man alone on a wall with a notebook, and every line of it is true, and none of it says what killed the animal.

Then he goes and gets the box from under his own bed and brings back the separation curve of May 1954 — Beta, eleven days, ruled off down the left in his own hand — and lays it beside the pound weights, and turns up the lamp.

"Alec."

"Yes."

"Come and look at this."

---

They stand over it together for a long time, shoulder to shoulder, the way they have stood over things since 1946.

It is the same curve. Not similar: the same, allowing for the mass difference and for the fact that Beta was pulled down by a uraemia on top of it. The intake falls away on a shoulder, holds, and then goes off a cliff between the eighteenth and twenty-first day, and after the cliff the animal will take live fish for a day or two and then stop taking anything at all and go to the bottom of the enclosure and put his face against something.

"The fifth-month dip in Table 2," says Reggie.

"It is not a feeding artefact."

"No. It's this. It's this with a slower slope, because in a pen there's usually something to look at."

"I sent you two pages in January," says Alec, "stating the contrary, with figures."

"I read them. I agreed with you." Reggie hears his own voice go flat. "I wrote back and said so. I've got the carbon."

And there it is, at half past midnight in a hut, in the worst circumstances in which any finding has ever been arrived at: the mechanism. Not husbandry. Not feeding. Not water or shade or handling. The other animal.

Reggie puts both hands on the table and looks at the two sets of figures lying side by side in the lamplight and thinks, with perfect clarity and no comfort whatsoever, that they have found it, and that it has taken a paper, a curve, a boy, two animals and nine years, and that it is the most important thing either of them will ever publish.

---

"Now," says Alec. "The thing behind your teeth. I should like it entire, please, and I should like it tonight."

So Reggie gives it to him. It is the hardest thing he has ever done and he does not soften one word of it, because he was taught not to by the man standing in front of him.

"You killed him," he says. "You took a paired animal out of a pen at four in the morning and you put him in a pound on his own, and you had the paper, and you had the curve, and you had my Table 2, and the reason you didn't see it is that you were looking at food, because food was the difficulty in front of you and you always solve the difficulty in front of you."

"Yes."

"And I gave you every one of those instruments. The habituation is mine. The separation design is yours and the animal was mine, and between us we produced a curve that would have told either of us the answer in about four minutes if we'd been willing to read it about anything except an experiment."

"Yes."

"And that isn't the worst of it," says Reggie.

Alec stands quite still.

"You didn't take him because he was the best material and they were going to waste him. That's true and it isn't why. You took him because you'd told me no four days earlier and then been thrown out of the building anyway, and you had to do something with the difference." He makes himself keep going. "You had a thing you couldn't give me and no post left to have refused me for, and at four o'clock in the morning you went down to a pen and took the one action available."

The lamp hisses.

"Say it," says Reggie, "if it's wrong."

"It is not wrong," says Alec.

And then, after about four seconds, in a voice that Reggie has heard perhaps three times in nine years: "I would be obliged if you would sit down. I find I am —"

He does not finish it, because he cannot.

---

Reggie gets him into the chair and then gets him out of it again onto the floor, because the chair is too far away from anything, and sits with his back against the bed and Alec's head against his shoulder and his arms round him, hard, the way you hold a man who has never in nine years permitted it.

It goes on for some time. There is nothing in it that could be called weeping; it is quieter than that and much worse — a sort of shuddering that comes up through the whole frame at intervals, like a building settling.

Reggie says nothing at all for a good while. Then he says the only thing he can think of that is true.

"You brought it to me."

"There was nowhere else."

"You could have burned it. Nobody knows. There's no ledger entry, no post-mortem on file, no —"

"Do not," says Alec, into his shoulder, "be stupid at me. I do not strike out observations."

"No," says Reggie. "You don't."

And he sits on the floor of a hut with four thousand miles of ocean outside it and the whole of his own culpability laid out in figures on the table over his head, and holds on, and thinks: *we made each other. That is the thing nobody will ever be able to put in a paper. He made me the man who could do that to Nineteen and I made him the man who could do that to one hundred and ninety-seven, and neither of us did it alone, and we have never once lied to each other, and here we are on the floor.*

---

At about two he gets him onto the bed and takes his shoes off and puts the blanket over him, and Alec says, without opening his eyes, "The hearing."

"Tomorrow."

"There is a way to use this. The ledger has no column for *why*, and they are moving live animals through colonial ports, and the record I have is —"

"Alec. Tomorrow."

"Yes," says Alec, and is asleep in under a minute, which he has done perhaps three times in nine years, and all of them after the worst days of his life.

Reggie sits on the end of the bed in the dark for a while with his hand on the man's ankle through the blanket, and then goes and puts the sheets back in the case in the right order, because they will be wanted at eight.

---

At ten to eight they walk up the path together, four feet apart, in the ordinary way.

Pascual is standing at the turn by the generator with two cups of coffee, which is not on his way to anywhere.

He gives one to Alec without a word — not to Reggie, to Alec, the one who has been at sea for eleven days and looks like a man cut out of paper — and jerks his chin at the office.

"They're all in there," he says. "Bevan's put the long table out. Weald's got his notebook, so talk slowly." Then, to Alec, in exactly the voice he uses to a horse that has been badly handled by somebody else: "Drink that first, *hombre*. Whatever it is, it'll still be true in four minutes."

And he walks off down to the pens, and does not look back, and has not asked one question in four years.
