# Every Item Genuine

He has costed the fortnight in some detail, on the ship, over nine days, which is the sort of thing a man does when he has nothing to read.

Fourteen days. Of those, six will go on the site survey and the Commander, which cannot be helped. Two will go on the animal, properly, with the record open. That leaves six evenings, of which perhaps four will be usable, and the arithmetic on four evenings against nineteen months is not good arithmetic and he has done it anyway, repeatedly, leaning on the rail off Antigua like a boy.

The launch comes round the head at ten in the morning and the whole station is on the jetty.

He sees him at about eighty feet.

Brown. That is the first thing, and it is absurd how much of the nine days it justifies. Brown to the collar, and thinner, and in a shirt with the sleeves rolled, standing a little apart with his hands behind his back.

And at forty feet, he sees the second thing, and it puts the whole of the fortnight into a different column.

The coat is on.

Not the ordinary working charm. The whole garment, laid on thick, at ten in the morning, on a jetty — and it is pointed at him. In eight years it has never once been pointed at him.

"*Tar!*" he says, across ten feet of water, because there is a Commander behind him and four people in front of him and the public register is the only one available. "Good God. You're brown."

---

"Before you come up," Tar says, when there is a gap. "You should have it from me. We lost Beta in the night."

Spilsbury stops walking.

"He got out of the pen and made the water, and we haven't recovered him. And one of my postgraduates went in after him and hasn't come back."

Here is a true thing about himself that he has never bothered to conceal: the first thing that arrives is appetite. An adult male, out of a pen, into open water, with a human being following him — nobody has had that. Nobody in the world has ever had that.

He gets *how dreadful* onto his face, reasonably well, for about a second and a half, and then gives it up, because the appetite is under it like a light behind a blind and it always shows.

"Out of the *pen*," he says. "Show me."

And Tar shows him — the sill, the lip, the sixty feet of concrete, the scrape of epidermis, the drag mark that the rain has nearly finished — and talks him through it fluently, warmly, in the coat, without one tremor anywhere; and it is a beautiful performance, and Spilsbury walks beside it and notes, as one notes a temperature, that he has been lied to.

Not in a word. Not one word of it is untrue. He has simply been handed a thing with a shape, by a man who is very good at shapes, and the shape is wrong.

He says nothing whatever about it. He has just come four thousand miles and there are four evenings in the column.

---

The first of them is that night, in the hut at the end of the row, with the door wedged and the lamp out.

Nineteen months.

He had intended to be careful, having formed a view on the ship about what nineteen months does to a man's judgement, and he abandons the view in the first thirty seconds, because Tar comes across the room and takes hold of him and says, "Don't talk. For God's sake don't talk yet," in a voice that has nothing left in it at all.

So he does not talk. He gets the shirt off him and puts his hands flat on his back, on skin that is warm all the way through for the first time since he has known him, and finds the ribs under it more countable than they were, and holds on.

They do not get as far as the bed for some while. There is a chair, and then the floor, and Tar is not gentle and does not want gentleness; he wants to be held down, hard, at the wrists, in a way that Spilsbury has learned to give without comment, and when he is given it he makes a sound like something being taken out of a wound.

"*Reggie.*"

"Don't. Don't say anything, just —"

"Tell me what you want."

"You. That's all. That's the whole of it."

And after that it is only breath and the sea and a man saying his name into his shoulder over and over, *Alec, Alec, Alec*, like a bearing repeated back.

Afterwards, on the boards, with the lamp still out, Spilsbury does what he does.

He counts the anomalies.

One: the hands, which have been unsteady since the jetty and are unsteady now. Two: the coat, on him, for four hours. Three: a fit adult animal that went over a lip at the top of a tide, with a boy who could time him at two and a half knots. Four: the man on the floor beside him, who will not talk, and who has not once asked him a single question about England.

He puts them in a row and looks at them, in the dark, with Tar's head on his chest and Tar's hand flat over his ribs where it goes, and he understands the shape of the thing at about half past one in the morning, entire, without effort, the way one sees the answer to a bad integral.

Then he lies quite still and says nothing.

Because a man who is being fed does not overturn the table. He has had nineteen months of a technical letter every third Tuesday. He has three evenings left in the column.

He will have to say it. He knows that. He would simply rather it were Thursday.

---

The file comes out on the Monday, at the long table, with the Commander present and Mrs Bevan pouring tea and nobody sitting down.

It is a good box. There is more real material in it than he could work through in a fortnight and every single item in it is genuine: the water record ruled off down the left, colour and volume, port wine to straw to clear; the weights; the food logs in the boy's round hand; the vet's bicarbonate notes, twice a day, initialled; the soundings, in a woman's neat figures, signed J. M. Leslie.

He reads it properly. It takes eighteen minutes, and the Commander shifts from foot to foot, and Pobil stands at the end of the table with his arms folded like a man guarding a gate.

At the ninth minute he has it.

It is not one thing. It is never one thing; it is the join between two things that were each perfectly in order. The animal's weight is up nine pounds. The animal's speed was timed at two and a half knots sustained, on four separate days, by a postgraduate, in the week before the escape — and nobody times an invalid four times in a week unless somebody has asked for the number.

And the soundings are dated the seventeenth. Off the second head. To a hundred fathoms.

Nobody surveys a deep-water line to get an animal *back*.

He turns the sheet over and looks at the reverse, which is blank, and lets his face do nothing for four seconds, and then goes on to the next item, and finishes the box.

"Thank you," he says. "May I have the water record overnight?"

"You can have it if you bring it back at eight and don't put your tea on it."

"I don't take tea."

"*Bueno*," says the vet, "then we'll get on."

---

He gets on. That is the other thing nobody understands about him.

He does six days of work in four, because the site is a genuinely good site and the survey is genuinely necessary and the fact that his own private arrangements are quietly coming apart on a jetty is not a reason to do arithmetic badly.

And for two of those days he works beside Tar in the same room, which he has not done since the twenty-ninth of November 1950.

It is exactly as it was. That is the part that is difficult. Tar reads out a figure without turning round and he writes it down; Tar is wrong about the sampling interval and is told so and concedes in four seconds; they argue about a salinity series for most of an afternoon and it is the best afternoon he has had in four years. At one point Tar laughs at something and puts a hand to the back of his own neck, twice, hard, and Spilsbury has to go and look out of the window.

He also watches the vet.

Pobil is a large, loud, warm man who is a great deal cleverer than he permits himself to appear, and who has known the handwriting on the envelopes for two years — Spilsbury establishes that on the second day, from about three seconds of the man's face when the two of them are in a doorway together, and it costs him one very bad half hour to decide that it is not a risk.

It is not a risk. That is plain by the Wednesday. The man has had the information for two years and has done precisely nothing with it, and there is only one reason a person does nothing with that for two years.

Spilsbury notes the second thing as well, which is the way Pobil watches Tar eat, and does not enjoy noting it, and does not strike it out.

---

He puts it to him on the Thursday, at the point, at ten o'clock at night, where there is nothing but rock and noise and no possibility of being come upon.

"Beta was fit."

"Yes."

"Two and a half knots. Four times in the week before he went."

"Yes."

"High water at twenty-one forty on the nineteenth."

"Yes."

"Soundings off the second head, dated the seventeenth, to a hundred fathoms, in Miss Leslie's hand."

"Yes."

"You put him in the sea."

"Yes."

"Because of my postscript."

"Yes."

The sea does what it does at the point, which is a great deal, and for a while neither of them adds to it.

"You'll want to know whether I'm going to put it in the report."

"No," says Tar. "I know you're not."

And that — the flatness of it, the sheer unhesitating certainty of a man who has read him correctly for eight years — is the first thing that gets under Spilsbury's guard all fortnight.

"You're very sure."

"I'm not sure of anything else on this station," says Tar. "I'm sure of you."

---

He says the rest of it because it is true and because nobody else will ever say it to this man, and because being told the truth by somebody who loves you is the only good that was ever on offer between them.

"He is dead."

"You don't know that."

"He was eleven days into a uraemia. He had broken his own muscle down twice. He hauled himself sixty feet on concrete on the ninth and put himself back into crisis, and then he swam two miles into eight hundred fathoms on a body that had nothing left in it." He pauses. "He was dead before the boy was."

"Stop."

"You did not save him, Reggie. You moved where he died and you sent a child out to hold his head while he did it."

And Tar says nothing at all, for about nine seconds, and then says, "I know," in a voice that Spilsbury has heard exactly twice before: once at a fire in a cold flat in 1949, and once on a walkway at one in the morning with the ice closing over.

---

"Tell me how you got him to do it."

"Charles?"

"Yes."

"I told him the truth," says Tar. "All of it. What was proposed for the animal, and that I couldn't stop it once you were ashore, and that he was fit."

"And?"

"And that there was a gap in the reef and a tide at half past nine. I gave him the figures he'd taken himself and let him check them in front of me. Then I stopped talking."

Spilsbury stands on the rock in the dark and finds that he is cold, at twenty-six degrees, in the tropics.

Because that is his own method. Every part of it. Give the man the true figures, arrange them, and then be silent and let him arrive; he has done it to committees, to Directors, to an Admiralty working party, and to a young man in a corridor in 1946 and at a fire in 1949 and on a hearthrug in 1947 with the whole of a paper spread on the floor.

He taught him that. Not deliberately; he has never taught anybody anything deliberately in his life. He did it the way water teaches a channel.

"Ah," he says.

"Say it."

"There is nothing to say. You used the instrument you had. It is the best one there is."

"He was twenty-three."

"Yes," says Spilsbury. "He was."

---

He makes the offer on the last evening, because he would not forgive himself for not making it, and because it is, on the figures, a good offer.

"Come back."

"No."

"Half the old place is gone. You would have material, money, a free hand, and nobody standing over you within four floors. I can have it done by Christmas."

"No."

"You are thirty-five. You have the best hands in this field and you are spending them on a station with two pens and a generator and a woman doing the soundings because there is nobody else."

"Alec."

"Yes."

"If I came back," says Tar, "how long before I was at a board?"

Spilsbury does the arithmetic. He does it honestly, because he has never given this man a dishonest figure and is not going to spoil an eight-year record on a beach.

"About four months."

"Yes," says Tar. "That's my estimate too."

---

He writes the report on the ship, in nine days, and it is a good report, and it is nineteen pages long and every item in it is genuine.

It states that the male, Specimen Beta, in the eleventh day of a uraemic crisis, left the pen by the same route he had used previously, entered the water, and was not recovered; that the postgraduate C. Cholmondeley entered the water in an attempt at recovery and was lost; that the Institute's husbandry is sound, its records are exemplary, and its medical man is the best he has seen outside a teaching hospital.

It does not state that the animal was timed at two and a half knots on four occasions in the week before he went. That number is in the file. Anybody may go and find it. He is not obliged to do the joinery for them.

It does not mention the soundings of the seventeenth.

In the notebook, on the second night out, he writes:

*Result of arithmetic: report the anomaly. The case is complete and would be believed.*

*Declined result. Third time.*

*He was right that I would decline it, and said so on the rock, flatly, before I had decided. I find I do not mind this as much as I ought.*

And underneath, after a space:

*Cholmondeley, C. Aged twenty-three. I did not ask his Christian name until the fourth day, and then I asked the vet and not Tar, because I did not wish to see Tar's face while he answered.*

*Charles.*
