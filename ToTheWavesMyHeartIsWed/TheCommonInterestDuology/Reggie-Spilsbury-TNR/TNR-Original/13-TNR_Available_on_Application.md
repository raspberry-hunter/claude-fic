# Available on Application

They take him across to the Bay on the twenty-eighth in Alleyne's boat and Mr Purdie reads every word of it aloud, because he is seventy-one and has been a magistrate since before the Colonel came out and has never in his life witnessed a document he had not first heard himself say.

*Deposited with the Institute this 28th day of March 1957 by Dr A. Spilsbury: the anatomical material of one specimen of N. aestuarius, being the animal designated accession 197 in the ledger of its former holders; nine blocks, four jars of wet tissue, one plate sequence, and the daily record of 51 days. The depositor retains no interest and reserves no condition, save that the material be available on application to any qualified person.*

Reggie stands at the back of a hot little room with his hat in his hands and listens to a man of seventy-one read that out at the speed of a metronome, and thinks: *he has given away the only thing he owns and he has done it in a form that cannot be taken back, and he has put four words in it so that anybody in the world can have it, and he did not consult me about a syllable of it.*

Alec signs. Purdie signs. The clerk blots it.

On the boat going back Alec says, "That is the part I was worried about," and takes his hat off and turns his face into the wind for the whole of the crossing, and looks about nine years younger, and Reggie sits four feet from him in front of Alleyne's boy and cannot touch him.

---

He says it to Pascual first, which he has thought about for a week and which turns out to be the only sensible order.

They are in the long room on the evening of the twenty-ninth with the shutters down and John's pens on the bench between them, and Reggie has not sat down.

"I'm going to go with him."

Pascual puts the slide down.

"Say it properly."

"I'm going to resign and go with him." His voice does something in the middle of it and he does not try to correct it. "Not tomorrow. In the summer, after the paper's out and Hester's got the schedule application in, because the Institute doesn't lose anything by my going in August that it doesn't lose by my going now —"

"It loses the man whose name is on the paper."

"Jean can carry the paper. You can carry the licence. Hester's carried this station for twenty years and I've been on it for four and a half." He has both hands on the back of a chair. "And I'm not going to stand on a jetty tomorrow and watch a man get into a boat with twelve hundred pounds and no reference and a solicitor writing to every society in England, and then go up to my hut and have my dinner."

Pascual looks at him for a long time.

"Then go," he says.

Reggie stops.

"What?"

"I said go." Pascual takes his glasses off and rubs the bridge of his nose. "You thought I'd fight you. I'm not going to fight you. *Hombre*, I've watched you for four and a half years and I've known since February of 'fifty-three, and if you tell me tonight that the thing you want is to get on a boat in August then I'll write the letter to Hester myself and I'll do the north pen and I'll be angry about it for about two years and then I'll get over it." He puts the glasses back on. "I'm not the one you have to ask."

"You're the one I wanted to ask."

"I know. That's not the same thing and you know it isn't."

---

He goes up the row at about ten.

Alec is at the table in the hut at the top of the row with the case half packed and a stack of offprints tied with string, and he looks up, and Reggie says it standing just inside the door.

"I'm coming with you."

Alec puts the string down.

"No."

"You haven't heard it."

"I have heard it since the twenty-fourth of February," says Alec. "You have been assembling it for five weeks. You have been assembling it badly, because you leave the room when you are assembling something, and you have left the room eleven times."

"Then you'll know the arithmetic's good."

"The arithmetic is not good. Sit down."

"I'll stand."

"Then stand," says Alec, "and I shall do it in order."

---

He does it in order.

The paper needs an author with a post; the Martin needs a Director of Research on the day an inspector comes; the licence application has four names on it and one of them is already under a solicitor's letter and the others must be as dull as possible. Two men leaving a small station in the same year, for no reason, to the same place, is a sentence somebody writes down. There is no laboratory at the other end and no bench and no animals and no work, and Reggie at forty is the best field man of his generation and would be, within a year, a man who used to be one.

"That's four arguments and you know I've got answers to all four."

"You have answers to three."

"Which one haven't I got an answer to?"

"The last one," says Alec, "and it is also not the real one."

Reggie comes and sits down on the end of the bed after all.

"Say the real one."

---

Alec turns the chair round.

He does it slowly and he takes his glasses off and holds them, which he does once or twice a year, and Reggie watches him arrive at the edge of something and decide, visibly, to go over it, and has seen him do that exactly twice before in fourteen years.

"On the eleventh of February last year," says Alec, "I took an animal out of a pen in which he was doing perfectly well, because I had a good reason, and the reason was good, and it was the best reasoning I have ever done in my life."

"Alec —"

"He was dead in fifty-one days and there was nothing on the post-mortem." He is holding the glasses in both hands. "I am not going to do it twice."

The room is entirely silent.

"That's not the same thing," says Reggie.

"It is the identical thing and you would see that it was if it were anybody else's life, and you would say so to me across a bench in about four seconds."

"It isn't the same, because I'm not an animal in a pen, I'm a man of forty who's telling you what he wants —"

"You are a man of forty who is doing extremely well," says Alec, "in the only place he has ever done extremely well, with a woman who fights for him and a man who feeds him and four hundred yards of reef and two healthy animals and a paper that is going to change what is believed; and I am the other thing in the pen, and if I take you out of it I will have to watch, and I know the shape of the curve, and I know it better than any man alive because I made it."

Reggie finds he has put his face in his hands.

"That's the filthiest argument you've ever used on me."

"Yes," says Alec.

"You've been saving it."

"Since the ninth of February."

"And there's no answer to it."

"There is no answer to it," says Alec, "and I have looked for one, and I should very much have liked to find one, and I did not."

---

They sit with it for a while. At some point Alec puts the glasses down on the table and comes and sits on the bed, not touching him, a hand's breadth off, which is worse.

"I'd have come," says Reggie, to the floor.

"I know."

"I'd have gone anywhere. I want that on the record. Not as a nice thing to have said — on the record, in the book, in the hand." He wipes his face with the heel of his hand, roughly, the way you do a window. "Because in about six years you're going to be somewhere cold with a paraffin stove telling yourself I was being sentimental in March of 'fifty-seven, and I want it written down where you'll have to look at it."

"It will be written down."

"Tonight."

"Tonight," says Alec. "In order. Both sides of the page."

---

They get to it at about midnight, which is the only part of the whole business Reggie has no complaint about.

He does most of the talking, because one of them always does and it is not the other one; and because he has worked out over ten years that Alec, who can lie still and take almost anything without a sound, cannot take being told the truth about himself at close range, and goes to pieces at it, quietly and completely, every single time.

So he tells him.

He tells him with his mouth against the hinge of his jaw that he is the best mind Reggie has ever been near and that this has never once been the reason. He tells him, with a hand spread flat between his shoulder blades and the other in his hair, that he has watched him take a filthy winter without complaining and that it was not admirable, it was frightening, and never to do it near him again. He gets Alec's wrists and puts them up above his head against the iron of the bedhead, one in each hand, the way they have done since a cold flat in 1947, and holds them there, and says into his throat: "Still," and Alec goes still, and stays still, and breathes like a man doing it deliberately.

"Reggie."

"I know."

"I should like —"

"Say it."

"I should like it slowly," says Alec, "and I am aware of what I am asking for and why, and I should like it anyway."

So it is slow.

And at about the point where Alec stops being able to manage his own voice, with his wrists still up and his head back and the lamp out because tonight neither of them can do the other thing, he says a sentence that he has never said in ten years in any position whatever, which is: "Don't let go."

Reggie does not let go.

Afterwards Alec lies with his face in the side of Reggie's neck and one hand shut in the front of his shirt, and does not move for a long time, and his pulse comes down through about twenty beats while Reggie counts it with two fingers and does not tell him the number, and neither of them says anything at all, and the boat is at half past nine.

---

In the morning Pascual gives him the reference.

It is one sheet, typed by Hester on the Institute's paper and signed in Pascual's own hand, and it begins:

*I have worked beside Dr Alec Spilsbury for ten months and I have no reason whatever to like him.*

It goes on for eleven lines. It says that he is the most careful recorder of a number the writer has met in twenty years of practice; that he dissects better than anybody the writer has watched; that he has never once in ten months altered an observation to suit an argument, including observations that finished him; and that the writer, who is a veterinary surgeon and not a friend, would put any animal in his care on any day of the week and has done so.

It is signed *P. Pobil MRCVS, Veterinary Officer.*

Reggie reads it twice at the end of the jetty with his back to the station and has to walk down to the water's edge and stand there for a minute.

Because he could not have written it. That is the whole of it, and it took him four weeks in February to understand it: a reference over R. Tar's name is the one piece of paper on this earth that would do Alec more harm than no reference at all, and so the only man on the island who could sign a true sentence about him is the one who does not like him.

He goes back up and finds Pascual at the sink.

"Pascual."

"Don't."

"I'm going to."

"I know you are." Pascual does not turn round. "Say it quick, then, because I've got a pen to do and I'm not in a fit state."

"Thank you."

"*Bueno*," says Pascual, to the sink. "It's all true. That's the only reason it's any good."

---

The whole station goes down at nine, which is completely unnecessary, and Reggie is savagely grateful for every one of them.

John shakes his hand and cannot get a word out, and Alec tells him his hatching is better than his own ever was, which is not true, and which John will carry for forty years.

Jean says, "Your appendix two is wrong about the tide and I'm correcting it in proof," and Alec says, "Good," and looks, for a moment, extremely pleased.

The Colonel says something about the roof that neither of them will remember afterwards and both of them will think of at odd times for the rest of their lives.

Hester gives him an envelope with the deposit receipt in it and says: "You will write to this Institute twice a year, Dr Spilsbury, because I intend to know where the depositor of that material is at all times, and I shall be extremely tiresome about it."

"Noted," says Alec.

And Pascual shakes his hand and says nothing whatever, and Alec says, "The calf will be off her within eighteen months and you will want the protocol written before it happens and not after," and Pascual says, "I've started it," and that is the whole of their goodbye and both of them are entirely satisfied with it.

---

Then there are about ninety seconds at the end of the jetty with five people standing at the top of it being tactful, and Alleyne's boy holding the painter and looking at the sky.

"Vigo, then," says Reggie.

"Vigo first. There is a firm there that loses thirty-eight per cent of its holding stock in the first fortnight and does not know why, and I do."

"And then?"

"Then wherever they lose it next."

"That isn't an address."

"No," says Alec.

"I want the fortnight."

Alec looks at him.

"The first fortnight of October 1958," says Reggie. "Not *when we can*. Not *as soon as it's sensible*. The first fortnight of October 1958, somewhere both boats touch, and we settle the place in a letter in the spring, and you write the date in the book tonight and I'll write it in mine before I go up the path." He is holding his hat too hard. "Eighteen months. You can do eighteen months. You cannot do an open interval, you have never been able to do an open interval in your life, and if I let you get on that boat without a number you'll do the arithmetic every night for four years and you'll come out at *never* one night when you're tired, and I know you will, because I've watched you do it."

Alec does not say anything for about three seconds.

Then he takes out the notebook and the pencil, standing up, at the end of a jetty, in front of five people being tactful, and writes it.

"First fortnight of October 1958," he says. "Place to be settled by letter in the spring. Entered."

"Say the rest of it."

"There is no rest of it."

"Say it anyway."

"I shall be there," says Alec Spilsbury, "unless I am dead," and puts the notebook away, and gets into the boat.

---

It goes out past the reef at about twenty to ten and there is a point at four hundred yards where the hat is still visible and a point at six hundred where it is not, and Reggie stands at the end of the jetty and finds that he cannot make himself leave it, and then that he can, because Pascual comes down and takes his elbow and says, "Four o'clock feed," and does not let go of the elbow for the first thirty yards.

---

On the fourth of April, at about six in the evening, he is at the north pen with the sheet and the tin, doing the thing that Alec did every day at four for ten months, and he is copying the weights across and he has got the pencil in his hand.

And he thinks, with no warning at all: *the lamp.*

He stops with the pencil on the sheet.

The twenty-fourth of February. Eleven o'clock at night, inside the door, with his hand still on the bolt: *leave the lamp*. Two days after Pascual came up the row with his sleeves down. Three weeks before the deposit. Five weeks before the boat.

*Because I have had ten years of you in the dark and it seemed to me that I had better have one of the other thing.*

He had already decided. He had decided by the twenty-fourth of February at the latest, and he had gone on arguing about the title page for another month, and he had let himself be beaten about the title page, and he had been pleased about being beaten, and the whole time the sum was done and in his pocket; and he had asked for the lamp in order to take a measurement away with him, and he had said so, out loud, at midnight, in almost exactly those words, and Reggie had called it a horrible answer and gone to sleep with his hand on the man's ribs.

He puts the pencil down on the sheet in the tin.

The calf comes up under her arm at the near end and takes a whole fish out of her mouth, which is the thing, which is the entire thing, and there is nobody at the rail to hold the gate.

He stands there for a while.

Then he picks the pencil up, because the animals eat at four whatever is happening, and there is a sheet, and the sheet has to be kept; and he writes the weights, and the date, and, under the date, the number of days remaining until the first fortnight of October 1958, which is five hundred and forty-nine.

He works it out twice.

He is not going to be wrong about it.
