# Feeding Artefact

He posts the refusal on the fifth of February and it is four days before he is dismissed.

He would like that recorded somewhere, in the way one records an interval. He had taken four days over the writing of it, because he had to be certain he was not merely refusing out of habit, and he had got to the end of the four days sure that the reasoning was sound: if he goes, they engage a man who will not tell them their controls are rubbish, and every animal in that building is worse off inside ninety days.

That was the reasoning. It was correct. It is still correct.

On the ninth of February, at twenty past four in the afternoon, a man called Meikle — commercial director, forty-one, pleasant enough — tells him that the Board is grateful and that the engagement is concluded, and that the accounts department will settle to the end of the quarter.

He asks why, because he always asks.

"Because you write things down," says Meikle. "Three letters about the controls, Dr Spilsbury. In writing. To a firm."

"You asked for an opinion."

"We asked for an adviser. You've given us a file."

And Spilsbury stands in a commercial office with his hat in his hand and understands, without any particular heat, that he has been dismissed for keeping a record, which is the only thing he has ever done consistently in his life.

He walks down to the jetty afterwards and stands looking at the holding pens for about half an hour, and does the arithmetic on the previous week, and gets the answer he already had.

Four days. He had refused Reggie the one thing he has ever asked for, in order to keep a post that he then held for ninety-six hours.

---

He does not write and tell him.

That is a decision, and he makes it on the tenth, sitting on the end of a bed in a boarding house with the notebook open and the pen uncapped, and he writes down the fact that he is making it, because he does not strike out observations.

*10.ii.56. Told on the 9th. Have not written to T. Reason: if I write this week he will read the dismissal as the cost of the refusal and he will attempt to comfort me, and I am not able to be comforted for a thing I chose. Also: he would be right.*

Then, underneath:

*This is the first item I have withheld from him in nine years. Noted as such.*

---

There are nine animals in the holding pens on the tenth of February and he knows every one of them by number, because he wrote the accession ledger they are entered in, and the ledger has a column for weight and a column for date and no column at all for *why*.

One hundred and ninety-seven is a male of four years, six feet two, in good condition, taken off the shoals in the spring with another animal of about the same age, and accessioned with him, and penned with him ever since, because Spilsbury told them in writing to pen them in pairs, and they did it, and their first-year losses halved.

He is aware of that. He would like it understood that he is aware of it at the time and not afterwards.

He goes down at about four in the morning on the eleventh with the man he has arranged things with and a boat, and he stands on the planks in the dark and does the sum properly, on both sides.

One animal can be taken in that boat. Two cannot: not with a man and gear and four hundred miles of coast to run, and not into a pound that will hold one adult well and two adults badly. He has costed a great many things in his life and this one takes eleven seconds.

He takes the better animal and leaves the other where he is.

Then he puts him in the boat and takes him off the place, and nobody attempts to prevent him, and nobody ever writes to him about it.

---

The pound is stone, on a coast he has no intention of naming, in a place where a shilling still buys silence and the man he has arranged things with can net live fish at dawn.

It is forty feet by sixty, it is tidal, it has a shaded end, and it is — he says this to himself on the first morning, standing on the wall with the light coming up — a better pen than anything in England.

The animal will not eat.

That is the whole of it, from the first day, and it is not a surprise to him. An animal moved is an animal off his food; it is in every account there is, including Tar's, and it resolves in four days or seven.

Cut herring: refused. Whole herring: refused. Four local species offered whole, refused, refused, taken and dropped, refused.

On the fifth day he stops offering dead fish altogether and has the man net live ones, and puts them into the pound at the seaward end, and stands on the wall, and watches a four-year-old male take eleven of them in forty minutes.

He writes the weights down that evening with a very steady hand.

*Feeding problem. As expected. Soluble.*

---

Here is the thing that he has gone over since, more than any other, and will go over for the rest of his life.

He had the paper. He had given it to a commercial firm himself and told them to pen in pairs. He had two pages of his own working on the fifth-month dip in Table 2, and he had sent those two pages to the author in January with the conclusion clearly stated, which was that the dip was a feeding artefact.

He had, further, the separation curve of May 1954, which exists because he suggested it in a letter over a straight-edged rule, and which shows in ten days of an animal's own figures exactly what happens when you take away the other one.

He had all of it, in his hand, in his own writing, and he looked at a male in a stone pound eating live fish and taking weight and concluded that the difficulty was husbandry, because the difficulty in front of him was plainly food, and because he is a man who solves the difficulty in front of him.

He would put it to anybody who asked: given the same material he would reach the same conclusion. That is not an excuse. It is the finding, and the finding is that he is capable of it.

---

For nineteen days it goes well enough to be called going well.

He works from first light. He nets, or the man nets and he carries; he weighs; he records; he tries four separate methods of getting the animal to take fish that are not alive, because live netting cannot be sustained through a season and he is thinking about the season.

And on the ninth day he tries Tar's method, because it is the best instrument there is and he has watched it used.

He taps the stone twice with the handle of the net before he puts the fish in, at the same hour, from the same place on the wall.

By the fourteenth day the animal comes to the tap.

He stands on the wall the first time it happens with the net in his hand and the fish in the bucket and finds that his chest has gone tight, and that he is thinking of a bay in England at four degrees and a forearm laid along a coping stone, elbow bent, in the attitude a man adopts for a blood test, and of a young man in a doorway saying *he gives me the arm* in a voice like a boy on Christmas morning.

He does not write that down in the record. He writes it in the other book.

---

On the twentieth day the animal stops.

He stops eating, first — refuses live fish, which he has never refused — and stops going round the pound, and by the afternoon he is at the seaward end, on the bottom, with his tail under him and his face against the stone.

Spilsbury tries everything, in order, and keeps the record.

He varies the fish. He varies the hour. He tries hand-feeding and gets no response, and forced feeding on the twenty-sixth day and gets it taken and brought back. He has the man dive the pound and check for injury: nothing. He checks the water twice daily for temperature and clarity. He moves the shade. He takes the tapping away, in case it has become aversive, and puts it back, in case it has not.

On the thirty-first day he sits down on the wall at four in the afternoon and writes out, on a clean sheet, every cause of decline in the literature, and goes through them one at a time, and strikes out each one against his own observations, and arrives at the bottom of the sheet with nothing struck in.

The word is not on the sheet. He does not write it because it is not, on his evidence, a candidate: the animal is not injured, not infected, not starved, not cold, and Tar's own paper is about feeding and weight and first-year loss, and Tar's own separation curve was published nowhere and exists in a box on an island, and the only animal Spilsbury has ever seen do this before was in the eleventh day of a uraemia and was written up as one.

He writes to the island on the fortieth day. It is four sheets and three of them are about a fixative for gill tissue, which is a subject on which he has strong views, and the fourth is about nothing at all, in the way that they have written to each other since 1952.

He does not mention the animal.

---

The fish pound is a good pound and the water is clear and there is a shaded end, and for thirty-one days a four-year-old male in good condition sits on the bottom of it with his face against the stone and will not be moved, and on the sixth of May, at about two in the afternoon, he dies.

Spilsbury is on the wall when it happens. He has been on the wall, on and off, for most of eleven days, in a linen jacket, with a notebook.

He does the post-mortem himself, over three days, because he is extremely thorough and because there is nobody else and because the alternative is to sit down.

Good condition to the end; the weights are in the book and anybody may check them. Clean lungs. No bone lesions — far too young to have been dived. Adrenals ordinary. Stomach empty; gut empty; nothing in the mesentery; kidneys perfect, which is the joke of it, kidneys he would be pleased to put in front of a class.

There is nothing whatever on the post-mortem.

He sits down on the wall on the evening of the third day with his hands not quite clean and understands three things in about a minute and a half, in the following order.

That he has killed this animal.

That he did it by removing him from another animal, in the spring, at four in the morning, having costed the boat and the gear and the coast.

That he has had the mechanism in his own hands for a year and called it a feeding artefact in writing, to the man who could have told him.

---

He gets very drunk on the fourth day. It is the second time in his adult life and he does it alone, properly, with a bottle, and it does not do anything for him except spoil the following morning, which he supposes is what it is for.

Then he washes, and shaves, and pays the man off in full and something over it, and packs the blocks and the slides and the weights and the ledger sheets he brought out of the building in February, and finds that all of it goes into two cases.

In the shipping office at Kingston on the eleventh of May the clerk asks him where he wants to go.

He has been asking himself that for five days and has not been able to arrive at it, which is unlike him, and standing at a counter with a pen in his hand he discovers that the answer is not a decision at all. It is simply where the material has to go, since he cannot publish it and there is one place on earth where it will be read properly, by people who will not stop reading it when they get to the part that is about him.

There is also the other thing, and he is not going to pretend to himself about it, at a counter, at his age.

He would like to be in the same room as Reggie Tar when he says it out loud. Not to be comforted; there is no comfort available, and he would decline it if it were offered. He wants to say it to the one man alive who will take the whole account, and check the figures, and not flinch, and then tell him the truth about it, which will be the worst thing anybody has ever said to him, and which he will believe, because it will be said by somebody who has never once told him an untruth.

He asks for the sailing that gets him there soonest and is told it is the thirtieth.

---

That night, in a room over a bar, he opens the other book and writes the last of it.

*6.v.56, 2 p.m. approx. 197 dead. P.M. 7th–9th. Nil.*

*Cause: removal from paired housing, 11.ii.56, by me.*

*I had the paper. I had the curve. I had the fifth-month dip and I sent him two pages in January saying it was a feeding artefact.*

*It is not a feeding artefact.*

He looks at that for a while. Then he writes, in the same hand as the rest:

*Correction to be delivered to the author personally.*

And, after a space, at the bottom of the page, the only line in nine years of notebooks that he thinks afterwards about cutting out, and does not cut out, because he does not strike out observations:

*I have nothing left to give him but this, and it is the worst thing I have ever had, and it is the first thing I have ever had that he could not get anywhere else.*
