# The Column

The application comes up from the Bay on the ninth of July and Hester puts it on the long table at breakfast with the letter opener still in her hand.

"Dr Pobil, you are witness. Dr Tar, you are the Director of Research and it is your decision and I intend to watch you make it."

"Read it out."

"*Application to consult deposited material,*" says Hester. "*Applicant: A. Spilsbury, Ph.D., Statens Havforskningsstasjon, Ålesund. Material sought: accession 197, nine blocks, four jars, plate sequence, daily record. Purpose: comparison with wet material from northern holding facilities. Duration: twelve days.*"

"Qualified?"

"He appears to be qualified."

"Then he may have it," says Reggie, "in the ordinary way," and goes on with his egg, and Pascual says, "That's five years running and you two do this every single time," and Hester says, "It is a correct procedure, Dr Pobil, and it is on the file, and the file is the reason he is permitted to walk up that jetty at all," and puts the form in the cabinet, and locks the cabinet, and this is the fifth July and nobody at this table has ever once said the other thing out loud.

---

He comes in on the eleventh at about four.

The station has changed and it has changed in the direction of noise. There are three students on the cay now — Codrington, who is from Barbados and is going to be extremely good and knows it; Miss Pryce, who is Jean's, on loan from Bangor for a year and has already corrected Reggie twice in public and been right both times; and a boy called Hendy who does the tide gauge and eats more than the other two together. John has two darkroom lads and a proper safelight and no longer permits anybody in there at all.

Jean has been gone two years. She has a group at Bangor and three students of her own and two more next year, and she writes to the Martin about once a month in a hand that has got worse with responsibility, and she sent a cable in May which read, in its entirety: MY SECOND YEAR HAS FOUND YOUR ERROR IN APPENDIX TWO AGAIN STOP LESLIE.

Alec comes up the jetty with his case and his hat and stops in front of the three students, who have been drawn up by Pascual in a line, which Reggie did not know about and could kill him for.

"Dr Spilsbury," says Pascual, "these are my students. This one thinks he is going to be a better anatomist than you."

"I said I'd *like* to be," says Codrington.

"He is not going to be," says Alec, "but he may get within about fifteen per cent of it if he is put in front of enough material and nobody flatters him," and shakes his hand, and Codrington goes scarlet with pleasure and does not stop talking about it for two days.

---

Pascual is getting married on Saturday and has been impossible since about March.

Dr Marguerite Sealy is the district medical officer at the Bay. She is forty-six, she trained at Edinburgh, she has run that district single-handed for eleven years, and she was not impressed by Pascual Pobil for approximately fourteen months, which Reggie considers the single most important fact in the recent history of this Institute.

---

Reggie met her in the November of 1960, on the verandah, with Pascual standing between the two chairs like a man waiting for a shell to land.

He had been dreading it for a fortnight and had said so twice, in the tone of a man who has thought about it properly: *you'll be clever at her, and she'll think you are laughing at her.*

It took about four minutes.

"You are the one he calls Reggie."

"I am."

"Then I have a question, and he will not answer it." Dr Sealy put her bag down by the chair leg and her hat on top of the bag, and looked at him over a glass of lemonade. "In March he came into my surgery with a hand that had been open five days and a temperature of a hundred and one. He had strapped it himself. He had strapped it *well*. And he told me he had been busy." She took a mouthful. "Is that a thing he does?"

"Every time."

"Thank you."

"He did it in 'fifty-four with a torn calf and worked nine days on it, and he did it in 'fifty-seven with a tooth for six weeks, and when I sent the boat over for a doctor in 'fifty-nine he went down to the jetty in his dressing gown and told the boy to turn round in the channel."

"*Ay*, that is not —"

"Be quiet, Pascual. We're consulting."

And Dr Marguerite Sealy put the glass down on the boards and laughed, once, from somewhere well down in her chest, and said: "Go on then, Dr Tar. From the beginning, and take your time. I have the whole afternoon and I have been waiting eight months for somebody who has the notes."

Pascual Pobil stood on his own verandah with his arms folded and his ears scarlet for an hour and twenty minutes and did not leave, and did not stop them, and did not for one second want them stopped.

They have been a joint enterprise ever since. She writes to Reggie about once a month.

In 1961 she wrote to him about a shoulder, and put at the top of it: *In confidence, and only until Thursday. I am going to tell him myself and I had much rather it came from me than from you.* It did come from her, on the Thursday, in her own surgery, in detail; and she told him about the letter at the end of it, and Pascual has been threatening litigation ever since.

---

He gets about nine opportunities a day and he takes every one of them.

"Pascual."

"No."

"I haven't said anything."

"You're about to say something."

"I was going to ask whether you'd finished the mortality sheet."

"I finished it on Tuesday and you know I did." Pascual has his back to him at the bench and his ears have gone red. "Say the thing."

"I only wondered," says Reggie, "whether you'd like the afternoon off on Thursday."

"Why."

"Because the mail boat goes to the Bay on Thursday and there's a woman on it."

"*Ay, Dios* —"

"And because a man who irons a shirt at half past five in the morning to go and look at a tide gauge is a man in trouble, and I can see the ironing board from my window, Pascual, I have been able to see the ironing board from my window since February."

Pascual turns round with the forceps in his hand.

"I liked you better when you were miserable," he says.

"No you didn't."

"No," says Pascual. "I didn't."

---

---

There is one other thing and he did not understand it at the time.

In April she came over on the Thursday boat with a bag of limes for Hester and got him on his own on the verandah for about ten minutes, and asked him, in the manner of a woman making conversation, whether Dr Spilsbury's summer visit was a fixed sort of thing.

"More or less. He applies in the spring, he comes in July."

"Always July."

"Always July. The material's here, the Norwegian station shuts in August, and he can't come in the winter because of the boats."

"Could it move?"

"It could move a week either way if there were a reason for it." He was pouring something and did not look up. "Why?"

"Because we are choosing a date," said Dr Sealy, "and I should like it to fall inside the fortnight that man is on this island."

Reggie put the jug down.

"That's an odd thing to want."

"It is not what I want. It is what Pascual wants." She took the glass out of his hand. "He asked me in March. He was extremely awkward about asking, and he has not the first idea which week anybody is arriving, because he is hopeless with dates and has been hopeless with dates for as long as I have known him. So I am doing it, because I do the dates."

"He could have asked me himself."

"He could have," she said, "and he did not, and I am not going to tell you why, and you are not going to ask me. I think we understand one another very well, Dr Tar."

And she drank her lemonade and talked about the dispensary at the Bay for six minutes and got the four o'clock boat back.

Reggie stood on the verandah afterwards with the jug in his hand for a considerable time.

He wrote to Alec that night and moved nothing, because nothing needed moving; and he put in the letter that Dr Sealy had been over and had said something to him that he had not understood one word of, and had said it in a manner suggesting that he ought to have.

---

The decision comes up on the boat on the twelfth, addressed to Mrs H. Bevan, and she reads it standing in the office doorway and then puts her hand out for the doorframe, which Reggie has not seen her do in nine years.

The Order is made. The species goes on the schedule with effect from the first of January 1963. Taking from the wild is prohibited except under permit; holding is permitted under licence only; and there is a paragraph — paragraph nine — which provides that no animal shall be held otherwise than in the company of at least one other of its own kind, save on the written certificate of a veterinary surgeon, and for not more than fourteen days.

Twenty-two years.

They get her into the chair and Codrington runs for the Colonel and somebody produces brandy from somewhere at ten past eleven in the morning, and Hester Bevan drinks about a teaspoon of it and then says, "Now sit down, all of you, because I want to say who did it and I am going to be accurate," and they sit down, all of them, including the students, including the boy Hendy who has been on this island for five months and has no idea what is happening.

She is accurate. It takes her four minutes. The soundings and the paper and the curve and the hearing and the ledger and the four months; the boy Cholmondeley, by name; Jean Leslie, by name, twice.

And then she says: "And the reason it was made this year and not in 1975 is the third annex, and the third annex is Dr Spilsbury's, and I should like that said in this room while he is in it, because he will certainly not say it himself and I have seven weeks of correspondence proving that he tried very hard to keep his name off the submission entirely."

"That is not accurate," says Alec, from the end of the table.

"Which part."

"It was nine weeks."

---

The third annex is four hundred and eleven pages and Reggie has read every one of them twice.

It is the holding and mortality returns of eleven commercial establishments in four countries over five years and something over sixty thousand animals — not this species, mostly; food fish, crustaceans, two cephalopod fisheries — with the densities, the pairings, the group sizes, and the first-year losses, kept daily, by hand, by a man who was in the building because they paid him to stop them losing stock.

And in the middle of it, in appendix 3(c), are the statutory returns of Dewar & Lecky Ltd for the years 1957 to 1961.

They are public. They are public because a licence condition was written into their permit in September 1957 requiring them to file, and it was written in because a woman in the West Indies was tiresome to four separate offices for eleven months, and a man in a room over a chandler's in Vigo has been reading them as they came out for four years.

"You made them keep books," says Reggie, on the path, on the evening of the twelfth, "and then you read the books."

"Mrs Bevan made them keep books."

"And you read them."

"They were in the public series and they cost one and threepence," says Alec. "I should like it understood that there was nothing clever about it whatever. Anybody in England could have done it. Nobody did, because nobody was looking at a shelf of trade returns and thinking about a dead animal, and I was, because I had nothing else to think about for four years."

Reggie stops on the path.

"Do you know what Pascual shouted at you across that table in May of 'fifty-six?"

"I am aware of most of what Dr Pobil has shouted at me."

"He said: *there is no column for it* — you said that, and he said *then make one, hombre, you had a pencil.*" Reggie has his hands in his pockets and is looking at the water. "Six years. Four hundred and eleven pages."

Alec does not say anything for a moment.

"I had not connected the two," he says, at length, "and I find that I do not entirely care for the connection."

"Why not?"

"Because it suggests I have been taking instruction from a veterinary surgeon since 1956," says Alec, "and I should rather it were arrived at independently," and Reggie laughs the whole of the rest of the way up the path.

---

They are married on the fourteenth at eleven in the morning in the Anglican church at the Bay, which is small and hot and full.

Jean is not there. Jean has nine weeks of transect work on the Menai Strait with three students and a tide that does not consult her, and she wrote in June to say that she would not hear of the date being moved months on end for her convenience, and would they please get on with it; and then she sent, at what must have been ruinous expense, a floral arrangement of such dimensions that Alleyne's boy had to make a second crossing for it, and which stands at the chancel step looking like something that ought to be in a licensed pen of its own. She will come in the spring. She has said so twice and Jean Leslie does not say a thing twice unless she means it.

Her people are there and there are a great many of them. Mr Purdie, who is seventy-six now and has known Dr Sealy since she was born, gives her away and gets through it. The Colonel is in a suit nobody has ever seen. Hester cries from the second hymn and does it the way she does everything, upright, without a sound, and would knock down any man who mentioned it.

Pascual Pobil stands at the front of a church in a grey suit with his hair flattened and cannot stop his face doing what it is doing, and Reggie stands at his shoulder with the ring in his waistcoat pocket and thinks, with perfect clarity and no bitterness at all: *good. Good. Let him have every single bit of it.*

They say it out loud, in front of ninety people, and the ninety people say it back at them, and somebody's aunt shouts something from the back in the middle of the vows and the whole church laughs.

---

At the schoolroom afterwards there is a table down the length of it and a man with a guitar and the students get at the rum and Reggie is asked to speak.

He gets up and puts his glass down and does not use any of the four things he had prepared.

"I met Pascual Pobil in November of 1952," he says, "and inside about two days I understood that I had been sent a man who was going to tell me I was wrong, in public, to my face, at least once a week, for the rest of my life."

"Twice," says Pascual.

"Twice a week. He was right about Alpha on his second night on this island and I didn't listen, and he was right in 1954 and I didn't listen, and he was right in 1956 and by then I had learned to, and there is a paragraph nine in a Colonial Office Order because of it." He turns the glass round on the tablecloth. "I have watched that man work for nine and a half years and I have never once seen him do a thing that was easy instead of the thing that was correct. Not once. And I want to say, in front of his wife and her family and everybody on this island, that whatever anybody else here thinks they know about him, I have had the best of it, and I've had it for nine years, and I'm handing it over today with extremely bad grace."

He puts the glass down again, because he has not finished.

"And I'm going to say one more thing, which is that she is far too good for him."

He waits for the noise to stop.

"I'd like it minuted that I am not being funny. Marguerite Sealy has run this district on her own for eleven years. Eleven years, one motor car, no partner, no relief and no night off, and every person in this room knows exactly what that means, and about a third of you have been carried in to her at some point and are standing here now." He looks down the table at her. "She took fourteen months to decide about him. Fourteen months. I want the young men present to write that down and keep it. And she is the only human being I have ever watched out-argue Pascual Pobil and make it stick — she has done it repeatedly, she did it twice about me, and she was right on both occasions and I have not entirely forgiven her."

"You have not," says Dr Sealy.

"I have not. Give me nine years."

He lifts the glass.

"Dr Sealy. Dr Pobil. Two people who have each been entirely formidable on their own for twenty years, and who are going to be a very great deal better than that together."

And from the far end of the table Codrington, who has had three rums and has spent the whole week reading a Colonial Office Order, shouts "PARAGRAPH NINE!" and is immediately sat on by Hendy; and about six people in that schoolroom understand it, one of whom is at the end with his hat on his knee; and Hester Bevan laughs out loud for the first time in twenty-two years.

Pascual gets up and hugs him in front of everybody in a way that would be entirely unremarkable in a man from Zaragoza at his own wedding, and says something into his shoulder in Spanish that Reggie does not catch and does not need to, and Dr Sealy watches the two of them with her chin on her hand and an expression Reggie cannot read at all and will think about later.

---

They get back to the cay at about ten and everybody goes to bed except the students, who go on being audible until two.

Alec is sitting on the step of the hut at the top of the row with his jacket off and his collar open, and Reggie sits down beside him on the step, six inches away, in the dark, which is what is available.

"You did that well."

"I meant all of it. I especially meant the bit about Marguerite."

They sit for a while. Somebody down at the huts is playing the guitar badly.

"I know you did. So did every person in that room, which is precisely why it worked." Alec turns his glass round. "For the avoidance of doubt, she is exactly as good as he is, and she has known that for about eleven months, which is why she took fourteen to decide."

Then he sets the glass down on the step.

"There is one thing you did not know," says Alec, "and you were the only person in that room who did not; and I have been deciding for six years whether it was mine to say, and I have decided this evening that it is, because it is finished and because it finished well."

Reggie goes still.

"Go on."

"You know that he was in love with you."

"For about six months," says Reggie. "'Fifty-three. It went off. It was a small station and I was the only —"

"Until the spring of 1957," says Alec.

---

The guitar stops. It starts again in a different key.

"No."

"Yes."

"Alec, that's four and a half years —"

"Four years and some months. I could give you the quarter and I am not going to, because I have a shape and not a date, and I will not pretend to a precision I have not got." Alec is looking straight out at the water. "I watched him for ten months in 1956 and 1957 and I have watched him for twelve days a year since, and it went out over the winter of 'fifty-six and it was gone by about the April."

"How."

"Because a man cannot maintain it against the sight of the thing itself at four feet for ten months," says Alec. "And he has never once, in all of it, been anything but decent to me."

Reggie puts his head back against the doorframe.

He is doing the arithmetic. He cannot stop doing it. May 1956 and a man coming up a jetty with two cases in his hands, making a decision about the huts on the path in four steps and not looking back once. February 1957, a chair pulled out backwards in the long room: *I've known since February of 'fifty-three and I've never needed a third line.* March 1957, at a sink, with his back turned: *say it quick, then, because I've got a pen to do and I'm not in a fit state.*

*Then go,* he said, in the long room, on the twenty-ninth of March 1957. *I'll write the letter to Hester myself.*

"He carried your cases up to my hut," says Reggie.

"He did."

"He put them at the foot of my bed. He *squared* them — he told me he squared them, he was proud of it."

"Yes."

"And then he wrote you a reference."

"He wrote me the only reference I have ever had that was worth the paper," says Alec, "and every line of it was true, and I have had it in the same pocket since 1957, and I am going to have it when I die."

---

"Alec. The date."

"Which date?"

"*This* date. The fourteenth of July." Reggie is sitting forward now with his elbows on his knees. "She came over in April with a bag of limes and got me on my own for ten minutes and asked me whether your summer visit was a fixed thing, and whether it could move, and I thought she was making conversation. It wasn't her. He asked her in March. He asked her to put his wedding inside the fortnight you're on this island and he wouldn't ask me himself and she wouldn't tell me why."

Alec does not say anything for rather a long time.

"I did not know that," he says.

"No."

"He has never forgiven me."

"I know he hasn't."

"He is not going to, either, and he should not." Alec has both hands round the glass. "He stood at a table in 1956 and listened to a man describe taking an animal out of a pen at four in the morning, and he will carry that until he dies, and he has been perfectly correct about it for six years and has never once let it near the work."

"And he still moved his wedding."

"He moved his wedding so that I would be standing in the room."

"Why would he —"

"Because you are not separable from me," says Alec, "and he understood that a very long time before you did, and he has decided to take the whole of it, on the grounds that the whole of it is what there is." He turns the glass round once. "That is not forgiveness. It is a good deal more useful than forgiveness and a great deal harder to do."

---

Reggie sits on a step at half past ten at night with his hands hanging between his knees.

"I've been ragging him about Marguerite since March."

"You have."

"Every day. Twice a day. The ironing board —"

"And he has enjoyed every second of it," says Alec, "which I know because I have watched him enjoy it, and because a man in his position does not go on saying *say the thing* to a man he wants to be spared by. He wants it exactly as it is. He has wanted it exactly as it is since about 1958 and you have been giving it to him without knowing you were, which is the only way it would have been any good."

"Then I'm not going to say anything to him."

"No."

"Not ever."

"Not ever, and not differently either," says Alec. "You will not be kinder to him tomorrow than you were on Thursday. You will get up and you will ask him whether he ironed anything, and he will tell you to go to the devil, and that is what he is owed."

Reggie sits with that for a while.

"You've had this for six years."

"I have."

"And you never once —"

"He never asked me a question in four years," says Alec, "and when he finally asked me one it was about a man with a card and a clerk. I was not going to be the first to be careless."

---

They sit with the guitar for a while. It stops for good at about eleven.

"You would have been better with him," says Alec.

Reggie turns his head.

"Say that again."

"You would have been better with Pascual Pobil." He says it in the voice he uses for reading out a weight. "Not because he is in the building, though he is. Because he argues with you. He has argued with you twice a week for nine and a half years and it has made you better at the work and better in yourself, and you stood up in a schoolroom this afternoon and said exactly that in front of ninety people and every word of it was true."

"And you don't."

"We do not. We never have. We agree." The glass goes round once in his hands. "And when we agree we go faster, and there is no brake anywhere in the circuit. I wrote you four sentences in the March of 1954 and you ran them inside six weeks, and an animal went over a lip and a boy went into the water, and the reason that happened is not that either of us was stupid. It is that there were two of us and we were pointed the same way."

"That's not a finding."

"It is an obser—"

"It is *not a finding*, and you of all the men on this earth know it isn't." Reggie has got up off the step. "You've got one observation and no control and you're publishing. There isn't a second arm. There has never once in nineteen years been a second arm, because from about the March of 1943 there was nothing else available to me and there was never going to be, and you cannot compare a thing with a thing that does not exist and then hand me the difference."

Alec looks at the water.

"That is a fair objection."

"It's a fatal one and you'd throw it out of a journal in four seconds." He sits back down, rather hard. "And you've got the design wrong anyway, because I've had both. That's the whole point of the last ten years. I've had Pascual arguing with me twice a week the entire time and I've had you, and you're proposing to improve me by removing one of them, and no man alive has ever improved an animal by taking something out of the pen."

Alec does not answer that for a moment.

"Go on," he says.

"And you've got the wrong year for the brake." Reggie turns to look at him. "1954 is your evidence. Take 1956. You were dismissed on the ninth of February and you were on this island on the thirtieth of May with two cases and every single thing you had, and you stood at that table in front of Hester Bevan and a Colonel and told them, in order, with nothing in it to make you look better, and it finished you, and you knew it would before you opened your mouth."

"That was correct."

"Of course it was correct. Would you have done it in 1946?"

Alec is quiet.

"No," he says, at length. "In 1946 I should have kept the book and been right about it in private for the rest of my life, and it would have been the most comfortable thing I ever did."

"So there's your brake. It just isn't the kind you were looking for." Reggie puts his elbows back on his knees. "You want to run it the other way, Alec? Run it. That man in 1946 with the book and the private rightness and the comfortable life. Is he better?"

Alec is quiet for a long moment.

"No," he says. "He is a considerably worse man and I should not care to meet him."

"Then it's a rotten paper."

"It is a rotten paper."

"It's worse than a rotten paper. It's an unanswerable question, because you can't get the man out to test him." Reggie's voice does something and he lets it. "There's no version of me that wasn't made by this. Nineteen years. You'd have to take you out of me first."

And Alec Spilsbury puts his hand, flat, on the step between them, six inches from Reggie's, in the dark, where nobody can see it and where Reggie can.

---

He goes in and gets Alec up off the step and does not say anything at all for the first quarter of an hour, and gets the collar the rest of the way open, and the shirt off him, and puts his mouth to the place under the jaw where the pulse is, which is slower than it was in 1946 and which he has now been counting for sixteen years.

"Reggie."

"I know."

"You are not doing this because of what I told you."

"I'm doing it because you're fifty and you've got your collar open and you've been sitting on my step all evening being decent about somebody you have every reason not to be decent about." He gets the man's wrists and puts them up and holds them there, one in each hand, the way they have done since 1947. "And because I've got nine days left and I'm not wasting any of them thinking about 1956."

"That is good reasoning."

"It's excellent reasoning. Be quiet."

And Alec is quiet for about forty seconds, which is his record, and then is not quiet at all; and the lamp is on because the lamp has been on since October of 1958 and neither of them has ever mentioned it again.

---

He told him on the Friday, in the office, with the door shut, in the voice he uses about four times a year.

"You aren't doing the pens on Sunday."

"I do the pens."

"You aren't doing the pens on Sunday. Or Monday. And you aren't doing the seven o'clock on Tuesday either." Reggie put both hands flat on the desk. "Codrington and Hendy take the seven, Miss Pryce takes the four, I do the sheet and the drugs, and if there's anything clinical I'll send the boat over for you and you can come across and swear at me. That is not a discussion, Pascual. That's the Director of Research, and I have waited nine and a half years to say a sentence like that to you and I intend to enjoy every syllable of it."

Pascual looked at him for a moment.

"*Bueno*," he said.

---

So at seven o'clock on Sunday morning Reggie comes down the path with a bucket in each hand and finds Pascual Pobil at the north pen with his sleeves rolled.

He stops dead.

"You are not serious."

"I'm not doing anything. I'm standing here."

"You have got a bucket in your hand."

"I'm holding it. There's a difference and you know there is." Pascual does not turn round. "Codrington put it down on the wrong side of the gate and it would have gone in."

"I have been the Director of Research of this Institute for eight years —"

"And you've never once made a rule stick with me and you aren't going to start on a Sunday."

"You got *married* on Saturday."

"I know when I got married," says Pascual. "I was there."

---

Reggie puts his buckets down and comes and stands at the rail beside him, because there is no point in any of the rest of it, and because he has just seen what the man is actually looking at.

The female is at the far end and she is a long time coming.

"She's slow at the turn."

"I know she is."

"She was slower yesterday. Hendy wrote it down and I read the sheet at six this morning before anybody was up." Pascual has both hands on the rail. "I wasn't here yesterday, Reggie. I want to see her myself, once, with my own eyes, and then I'll go up and have my breakfast like a married man and you can have the whole week."

And there is nothing whatever to be said to that, so Reggie says nothing, and they stand at a rail at seven in the morning and watch an old animal come the length of a pen.

She gets there. Her son comes with her the way he has come with her every day of his life, shoulder to shoulder in the shaded half, seven years old and a foot longer than she is, and he waits while she takes the first fish and then takes one out of her mouth a second later, which is the thing, which is the entire thing, and which somebody has written down at this rail, twice a week, without missing a week, since February 1956.

"All right," says Pascual.

"All right?"

"All right. She's slow and she's eating and she's not doing anything else, and I'll look at her properly on Wednesday." He carries the bucket round to the correct side of the gate and stands there holding it, because putting it down would be a concession. "You'll have the protocol out of the cabinet."

"It's been on my desk since Thursday."

"*Bueno.*"

---

Dr Marguerite Sealy comes down the path at about twenty past seven in a straw hat, at a walking pace that has emptied a good many waiting rooms, and stops at the rail on Pascual's other side.

"Dr Tar."

"Dr Sealy."

"Is he holding a bucket?"

"He says he is holding it and not carrying it."

"Yes," she says. "He said that about the hand in March," and Pascual makes a noise like a man stepping into cold water.

"I came down to look at an animal —"

"You came down at ten to seven with your shoes in your hand so as not to wake me, which was thoughtful, and which I should like the Director of Research to know about because I think it does you credit." She takes the bucket off him without any fuss at all and hands it to Reggie. "We go to Tobago on the eighth of September for three weeks. He has asked me twice already what arrangements will be made about the pens."

"Three times," says Reggie.

"Thank you. Three times." She puts her hand through Pascual's arm. "Say good morning to Dr Tar."

"Good morning to Dr Tar," says Pascual, with immense dignity, and lets himself be taken up the path, and is talking before they have gone fifteen yards, and Reggie can still hear the shape of it at the top when he can no longer hear a single word of it.

He is grinning like a lunatic. He is aware that he is grinning like a lunatic. He stands at a rail with a bucket in each hand at half past seven on a Sunday morning and cannot get his face to stop, and would not particularly want it to.

---

The students come down for the seven o'clock at about half past, in a body, arguing.

Codrington wants to know why the male has never been weighed in the water and is told, twice, by Miss Pryce, who has read the protocol and has been the second observer since June and does not know what anybody expects her to see, which is the part neither Reggie nor Pascual likes and which both of them insisted on.

Up at the long room the shutter is half up. There is a man in there at this hour with four hundred and eleven pages and a pencil and eight days left of twelve, making a column; and in April there is Bergen, second week, entered and written in two books; and in the cabinet in the office there is a document fourteen pages long in two hands, initialled by both of them, which has been waiting five years for a week that has not come yet.

They are going to be ready. That is the whole of it and it is not nothing: they are, in all probability, the first people in the history of the world who are going to see it coming.

"Sheet, sir?" says Codrington.

Reggie gets the tin down off the post and takes out the pencil, and writes the date.
