# Positive Vetting

Spilsbury hears it at twenty past nine on Monday morning, from the Director's secretary, who is crying.

He does not ask her anything. He goes up to the second floor, and into his own room, and shuts the door — his own door, the corridor one — and stands with his hand still on the knob and lets the information arrange itself.

Saturday night. Camberwell. A public lavatory off the Walworth Road. Two officers in plain clothes who were there for exactly that purpose, because it is October and the Division likes a return before the end of the quarter.

Frank Nicholson. Forty-three years old, the best electrophysiologist in the building, a wife called Margaret, a house in Lee with a greengage tree in it, and a box of watercolours he takes out on Sundays.

Spilsbury stands there for about ninety seconds.

He has read this bill before. He read it in 1933, when he was twenty-one, on a man who had taught him partial differential equations; and again in 1938; and he has carried the figure since he was sixteen and has never once been under any illusion about the size of it. So there is nothing here he did not know.

What there is, is the arrival.

He takes his hand off the knob and opens his notebook and writes, *9.20 a.m. F.N. Camberwell, Sat. night. Charged.* Then he puts the book in his inside pocket, and goes through the connecting door into the next room, where Tar is at the bench with a pipette in his hand, and says, "Put that down."

---

There are things that have to be done in the first four hours and he does them in three.

He telephones nobody. He writes nothing down that is not already written. He walks to the bank in the dinner hour and takes out the private deposit box he has held since 1939 and puts his own notebooks into it — all nine of them, four years of measurements and a great deal else — and signs the form and walks back, and is at his bench by twenty past two.

He does not cut a single page out. He has never struck out an observation in his life and he is not going to begin with these.

At half past four he goes to Alma Terrace with his own key, while Tar is still in the tank hall, and stands in the middle of the first floor back and looks at it with the eyes of a man from the Division.

There is nothing. That is not luck, it is four years of procedure: no letters, no photographs, no book with an inscription. There is a spare shaving brush in the cupboard, which is nothing, and two cups on the draining board, which is nothing, and on the table a marked-up typescript of the isolation paper with corrections in two hands, which is nothing at all, and which he stands and looks at for some while.

Then he washes one of the cups and puts it away and goes home.

---

Tar comes at nine and is white to the lips.

"He's been fined. Thirty pounds. He was in the magistrates' court this morning and the *Kentish Mercury* had a man in it, so it'll be in on Friday, and Hollis says Margaret's gone to her mother's, and the Director's had his room cleared."

"Sit down."

"I can't sit down."

"Then stand. Have you eaten?"

"Alec —"

"Have you eaten?"

"No."

There is a chop, because he bought two on Saturday and the cold weather has held. He cooks it and puts it in front of him and Tar eats about half of it, mechanically, and puts the knife and fork together and says:

"They've taken the keys off him and they haven't let him back in the building and he did four days of nerve work for me last autumn and I made him do it."

"You did not make him do anything."

"I did. In the lift."

"Eat the rest of that," says Spilsbury.

---

The men from the Ministry come on the Wednesday and stay three days, and what they are doing has a name now, or is about to have one: the whole service is being gone over, department by department, and it is not really about Frank Nicholson at all. It is about Fuchs, who confessed in January, and about a general apprehension in Whitehall that the Establishments are full of men whose private arrangements make them liable to pressure.

They are perfectly civil. That is what people never understand about them afterwards. They have a list of everybody who worked with Nicholson in the past two years, and they take each man in turn into the small committee room on the first floor, and they ask him about his associations.

Spilsbury goes in on the Thursday afternoon and comes out fourteen minutes later. He is very good at this. He answers in complete sentences, gives them three facts they did not have and one they did, expresses the opinion that Nicholson's judgement had been deteriorating for a year — which is untrue, and which he says because it is what a man in his position would have noticed if it had been true — and is thanked.

Tar goes in after him.

Spilsbury waits in the corridor, which he has no business doing, and reads the noticeboard for nineteen minutes.

Then the door opens and Tar comes out, and the coat is on.

It is a magnificent thing to watch and he has never been able to watch it from four feet away before. The young man who comes out of that room is sunny, unhurried, faintly amused; he is holding the door for the junior of the two Ministry men and saying something about the Walworth Road, and the man is laughing — he is *laughing* — and the senior one says, "Much obliged, Dr Tar," in the voice of a man who has found nothing because there is nothing to find.

Tar comes down the corridor. He goes past Spilsbury without a flicker, which is correct, and says, "Half two in the tank hall, then," which is also correct, and goes up the stairs.

At the turn of the stairs, where the window is, and where nobody on either floor can see, he puts one hand flat against the wall for a second before he goes on up.

---

On the Friday the *Mercury* has it, seven lines on page five, with the name and the address and the amount.

On the Monday two men in the canteen who have known Nicholson for nine years agree that there had always been something.

---

Tar asks his question on the second Sunday, in the flat, with the fire on.

"Is this what you've been carrying?"

"Yes."

"All of it? All the time? Since before me?"

"Since I was sixteen."

"And you never —" He stops. He is sitting on the floor with his back against the chair and his knees up and he has not touched his tea. "In November. In the passage. You knew all of this and you knew I didn't, and you let me walk it."

"Yes."

"*Why?*"

Spilsbury considers a number of answers, and discards all of them but the one that is true, because Tar is entitled to that much and because he has never told this man a lie and does not intend to start in the fifth year.

"Because if I had set it in front of you in the rain," he says, "you might not have come up the stairs."

Tar puts his head back against the chair and looks at the ceiling for a long moment.

"You absolute swine."

"Yes."

"You'd do it again."

"Tomorrow," says Spilsbury.

And Tar laughs — one short, wrecked, entirely genuine laugh — and reaches up over his own shoulder without looking, and Spilsbury takes his hand, and holds it, and nobody says anything for about four minutes.

---

The thing about Nicholson comes up again three days later, in a form Spilsbury has not budgeted for.

"He'll have nothing," Tar says. "No position, no reference, no Margaret, thirty pounds down, and a box of paints."

"Yes."

"Somebody should send him something."

"No."

"Alec."

"Think about what you are proposing. A cheque in your hand, to that name, at that address, in this month. It is a line drawn between you and a convicted man by the only instrument the Division actually possesses, which is paper."

"So we do nothing."

"We do nothing that can be traced."

"That's the same sentence with more words in it."

And it is. Spilsbury notes that it is, and says so, and goes to bed, and lies awake for forty minutes with the arithmetic in front of him, which comes out — it comes out clearly, it is not even close — as *do nothing*.

On Thursday he writes to a man in Southport whom he taught in 1937 and who now runs a firm that makes optical parts, and mentions, in the fourth paragraph, that a first-class technical draughtsman with an unfortunate personal history is likely to be at liberty, and that the man's hand and eye are exceptional, and that he, Spilsbury, would take it as a kindness. He signs it and posts it himself.

Then he arranges, through his own bank, a standing quarterly payment to a Southport address, from an account in a name that is not his, the way one does it: not because it is safe, but because it is safer.

He does not tell Tar he has done it. He puts it in the book, in three lines, with the figures.

*Result of arithmetic: do nothing. Declined result. Second time.*

---

Tar says it on the fourth of November, at Alma Terrace, at about half past ten at night, standing up, with his back to the window, having plainly been rehearsing it for days.

"I'm going to leave the Labs."

Spilsbury puts his glass down.

He had known something was coming. He had catalogued it: the quietness since the second week of October, the two evenings Tar cancelled, the Bureau's letter he left face-down on the table, the fact that he had stopped saying the word *spring*.

What he had budgeted for was the fear. That is what he had prepared for and it is a thing he could have argued with, because fear is a quantity and there is a great deal one can do about a quantity: procedure, distance, a year apart, Oxford, Cambridge, care.

"Because of Nicholson," he says.

"No."

"Tar —"

"No. Listen. *Listen.*" He has both hands up. "Wednesday, the lorry came in. The new four, the spring allocation brought forward. I went down to the yard because I wanted to see them come off, and they got the doors open, and I stood there in the rain and I was —"

He stops. He looks, for a moment, about nineteen.

"I was *happy*, Alec. Pleased as anything. Four new ones. New baselines, and the big female, and I'd got half the protocol written in my head before they had the tailgate down."

"Yes."

"Don't say *yes* like it's a reading."

"It is a reading."

"I know exactly what I did to Nineteen," Tar says. "I've known for a year. I've had it in front of me every morning for a year and it hasn't stopped me for a single afternoon, and on Wednesday I found out why, and it isn't that I think the Admiralty needs it and it isn't that somebody worse would do it. It's that I like it. I'm good at it and it's the most interesting thing in England and if I stay in that building I will keep doing it until they carry me out."

The fire is not lit. It is very cold in the room and neither of them has noticed.

"You could take the tables," Spilsbury says. "You have three of them. Nobody would put you within four floors of the tank hall. I'd see to it myself, and there would be no animals within a mile of you."

"And the hall would be downstairs."

"Yes."

"With Twenty-two in bay three and a new female in bay one and a protocol I wrote." Tar shakes his head, once. "I'd be in that hall by Friday. You know I would. You'd find me there at ten at night with a clipboard, *helping*."

And Spilsbury, who has spent four years being the only person in the world who tells this man the truth about himself, discovers that he cannot get round this one, because it is the truth about this man, and Tar has arrived at it without assistance, and has said it out loud, standing up, in his own rooms, at half past ten at night.

He has been waiting a year to ask the question again. He had a form of words ready. He was going to ask it in the spring.

Tar has answered a different question, which is the one Spilsbury never put to him, and the answer is correct.

"Where," he says.

"The Bureau. Oxford. They've offered me a room and two hundred a year and no laboratory at all, and I'm going to write the tables, and after that I don't know."

"Two hundred is not enough."

"It's enough."

"It isn't enough, and I shall look at it, and you will let me." He hears his own voice do something and takes it back under control. "When?"

"I said the end of the month. I've told the Director I'm going. I haven't told him why and I'm not going to."

"No," says Spilsbury. "Don't."

---

That is the whole of the conversation and it takes nine minutes.

Afterwards Tar sits down on the edge of the bed, in his overcoat, in the cold, and says, "Sixty miles," and Spilsbury says, "Fifty-eight," and Tar says, "*Fifty-eight*, then," and puts his face in his hands, and laughs, and then does not.

"I'm taking the door," he says, into his hands. "That's what I'm doing. I'm taking the door away from you and going to Oxford to be a better man in a room with no animals in it, and you'll be in the next room to *Hollis*."

"Probably."

"Say something. Tell me what it costs. You always cost everything; cost this."

Spilsbury stands in the middle of a cold room in a house in Alma Terrace and finds that he can, in fact, cost it, immediately, to two figures, and that he is not going to say it out loud.

Because this is the part. Here it is, the actual centre of the thing: the young man on the bed has got himself, unassisted, to the one position from which he can be a decent man, and the price of it is the next room, and the door, and the ordinary Tuesday, and the sound of a voice through a doorway reading out numbers — which is, when he does the sum honestly, the whole of what he has.

And he is not going to put one ounce of that on the scale, because the moment he does, Tar will pick it up and carry it, and Tar carrying it means Tar staying, and Tar staying means Tar in that hall with a clipboard at ten at night.

He has spent four years declining to tell this man what things cost.

For the first time in his life he declines to do it as a kindness, and he is aware, standing there, that the two things look identical from the outside and that he will never be able to demonstrate the difference to anybody, including himself.

"It costs less than you think," he says.

"Liar."

"I have not told you an untruth in four years."

"Then that was the first one," says Tar, "and I'll allow it," and holds out his hand.

---

Later, in the dark, under everything they own, Tar says:

"Ask me for something."

"I have what I want."

"You haven't. You've never asked me for one thing since the fourteenth of November 1946." His hand is flat on Spilsbury's chest, over the ribs, in the place where it goes. "I've got three weeks. Ask me."

Spilsbury lies still.

It is a curious sensation, being asked. He turns it over and finds that he does not have a procedure for it, and that there is something he has wanted for approximately four years and has never once said, because to say it is to hand somebody an instrument, and because he has generally preferred to be the man holding the instruments.

"Hold my wrists," he says.

There is a pause of about two seconds.

"*Alec.*"

"You have done it. Twice. Both times without being asked, and both times I —" He stops, which is not like him. "Both times it was the best thing that had happened to me that year."

"You could have said."

"I am saying."

And Tar rises up on one elbow in the dark and takes both his wrists, one in each hand, and puts them up above his head, flat, and holds them there; and then leans down close and says, very quietly, in the voice he uses for asking a colleague for a figure: "Like that?"

"Yes."

"Tighter?"

"Yes."

"Say it."

"Tighter," says Spilsbury, and hears his own voice come apart on the second syllable, and does not care in the least.

What follows is slow and extremely thorough and Tar does not let go of him once. He talks the whole way through it, which Spilsbury has always been grateful for and has never told him; and at the end he lets one wrist go in order to get a hand under the back of Spilsbury's neck, at the nape, and holds him there instead, and says his name — not *Alec* but the whole of it, *Alexander*, which nobody has used since his mother died, in a voice with nothing in it but tenderness — and Spilsbury, who counts everything, loses count of everything, for something between forty and ninety seconds.

Afterwards Tar lies on his chest with his ear over the ribs and says, "A hundred and thirty."

"A hundred and twenty-six."

"You can't possibly —"

"I can," says Spilsbury, "and it's coming down," and puts his hand in his hair and keeps it there until he is asleep.

He does not sleep himself for some time. At about two he gets up and goes through to the other room and writes it down, because it happened and he does not strike out observations.

*4.xi.50. T. leaves at end of month. Reason: himself. Correct.*

*Asked him for something. First time. Note: the cost of asking is much lower than my estimate, and I have been wrong about this for four years.*

He looks at that for a while, and then adds one more line, in the same hand as the rest.

*The other figure is not going in this book.*

---

Tar's last day is the twenty-ninth of November and it is entirely ordinary, which is the only way to do it.

He clears the bench. He hands the isolation protocol to Hollis with every courtesy, and the day book, and the key to the viewing gallery. He is charming to the Director for eleven minutes in the Director's office and comes out with a reference that will open any door in England. He shakes hands with the night porter and remembers his wife's name.

At twenty past five he stands in the connecting doorway between their two rooms with his coat over his arm.

"Right," he says.

"Yes."

"Friday week. The train gets in at ten past seven."

"I shall be at the barrier."

"Don't be at the barrier. Be at the corner."

"I shall be at the corner," says Spilsbury.

Tar looks at the doorway itself for a second — at the door, standing open against the wall where it has stood open since the fifteenth of November 1946, with the paint worn off the edge where a bound volume of the *Journal of Physiology* used to go.

Then he says, "Goodnight, Spilsbury," in an ordinary voice, for the corridor, and goes.

---

Hollis moves in on the Monday.

He is a pleasant man of fifty-one with a pipe he is not allowed to light in the building, and he is perfectly friendly, and at about ten o'clock on his first morning he says, "Draught in here, you know," and gets up and shuts the connecting door.

Spilsbury looks at it for a moment.

"Yes," he says. "Quite right."

---

Twenty-two's terminal work is on the twelfth of December, because the isolation series has to end with tissue, and it has been in the protocol since 1948, and Tar wrote the protocol.

Spilsbury does it himself. That was the arrangement and there is nobody to release him from it, since the man he made it with is in Oxford, and would not release him anyway, and does not need to be told.

He is not fast. He has never been fast; his hands are steady and his hands are not quick, and there is a difference of about fifty seconds between his own work at a board and the work of the man who used to do it, and he has costed that, and he pays it out in full, with his hand on her shoulder, talking to her, low and level, about the weather.

He times it, because he times everything.

Ninety-four seconds.

He goes up to the second floor afterwards and sits down and writes the figure in the book, and looks at it, and does not strike it out.
