# The Other Animal

He made the decision about the huts on the path, with a case in each hand, and it took him about four steps.

Hester had said the guest room or Dr Pobil's hut. Pascual went past his own door without slowing and put the two cases down inside the one at the end of the row, which is Reggie's, at the foot of the bed, square, the way you set down something a man is going to want in the dark; and came out, and shut the door, and went down to the pens.

Nobody has said one word about it since. Nobody is going to.

---

The animals eat at seven whatever is happening in the office, so he does the feed first and comes up wet to the elbows at twenty past eight, and the long table has been out since before he got there and they have started without him, which is correct.

The Colonel is at the head of it saying what the meeting is for, which is a thing he does and which Pascual has never seen another man of science do at all. Hester is at his right with the pad. Jean has the window and her sleeves rolled. John Weald is at the far end with his notebook open and three pencils laid beside it in a row, because John has decided this is a thing that gets written down properly, and John is right.

Reggie is standing. That is the first thing Pascual does not like. Reggie sits at meetings; Reggie tips a chair back at meetings and is insufferable in it.

And Spilsbury is on his feet at the middle of the table with both hands flat on the wood, in a clean shirt, shaved, looking like a man who has been assembled out of paper and posted.

"Doctor," says Pascual, and takes the chair at the end nearest the door.

"Dr Pobil."

"You ate?"

Spilsbury looks at him for about a second.

"I did not."

"After," says Pascual, and folds his arms. "Go on."

---

It takes an hour and a quarter and Pascual does not interrupt it once, which is not like him, and he is aware of not being like himself the whole way through.

It goes in order. That is the thing about it. The fifth of February and a letter; the ninth of February at twenty past four and a man called Meikle; three opinions about the controls, in writing, to a firm, which is the whole of why the engagement was concluded. Then the tenth. Then four in the morning on the eleventh, and a boat, and an accession number.

He does not improve any of it. There is no sentence anywhere in the hour and a quarter that is there to make him look better, and Pascual has sat through a great many accounts given by men about animals that died in their care and he knows exactly what those sound like, and this is not one.

That is its own kind of provocation and he has to sit on his hands about it.

What he watches instead is Reggie. Reggie has heard this before — you can see him hearing it the second time, half a beat ahead, his weight going onto the other foot before the bad part arrives — and at about the fortieth minute he takes his glasses off and does not put them back on for the rest of it, and Pascual, who has told that man four times in four years to keep his glasses where he can find them, says nothing at all.

---

The heat gets out at the pens. It was always going to be the pens.

"Say that again," says Pascual.

"He was taken off the shoals in the spring with another animal of the same age, and penned with him from the day he came in."

"You knew that when you went down there."

"I knew it."

"At four in the morning."

"At four in the morning."

"And you took one."

"I took one."

"And left one."

"And left one."

"Two animals that'd been shoulder to shoulder in a pen for a year." Pascual has his hands flat on the table now and he can hear his own voice going up and does not stop it going up. "And you wrote the weight down, and you wrote the date down, and what you'd just done to the pair of them goes nowhere at all —"

"There is no column for it," says Spilsbury.

"Then make one! *Hombre*, you had a pencil —"

"Yes."

"Don't say yes to me."

"It is the answer," says Spilsbury.

And there it is: the thing he cannot get his hands round, because the man will not hold still and be argued with. Pascual sits back in the chair, hard enough that it complains, and rubs a hand down over his face, and lets the rest of it go out of him. It takes about four seconds and everybody in the room can see it happen, and he does not care.

"At least you say it," he says.

"It is the cheapest thing I have."

"It isn't worth nothing, either. Don't be clever about it at this table." He picks up the water jug and pours a glass and pushes it down the wood at the man, which takes it most of the way. "Fifty-one days. Go on."

---

The fifty-one days he gives flat, the way the rest of it came: the refusals, the four species, the live fish at the seaward end and eleven of them taken in forty minutes on the fifth day, the two taps on the stone at the same hour from the same place on the wall.

Jean makes a small sound at the window at the two taps and puts her hand over her mouth.

Then nineteen days of it going well enough to be called going well, and the twentieth, and thirty-one days on the bottom at the seaward end with his face against the stone, and the sixth of May at about two in the afternoon.

Then three days of post-mortem, alone, in a linen jacket.

"Nothing," Spilsbury says. "Good condition throughout; the weights are in the book. Lungs clean. No bone lesions — far too young. Adrenals ordinary. Kidneys I should have been pleased to put in front of a class."

"Stomach."

"Empty. Gut empty. Nothing in the mesentery."

"Liver."

"Nothing."

"I'll want your blocks," says Pascual. "Not now. After."

"They are in the smaller case."

"I know where they are. I'm the one who carried it."

---

Reggie puts the box on the table at about half past nine and the room changes temperature.

It is a shallow deal box with a lid that does not fit, and it has been under a bed in a hut for two years, and what comes out of it is a sheet of squared paper with a curve ruled off down the left in a hand Pascual would know upside down at forty feet, because he has been reading that hand on drug charts since 1952.

Reggie lays it beside the pound weights and turns them both to face up the table, and steps back, and does not say anything, which is his way of saying a great deal.

Hester gets up and comes round. The Colonel puts his spectacles on. John comes the whole length of the room.

It takes Pascual perhaps twenty seconds.

"It's the same curve."

"Yes."

"Not close. The same." He has both hands on the table edge and he is leaning over it and he can hear the generator going through the floor. "There, and there. It holds, and then it goes off a cliff."

"Between the eighteenth and the twenty-first day."

"And after the cliff he'll take a live fish for a day or two, and then he won't take anything at all, and down he goes and puts his face against something."

"That is what it does."

"And yours is slower."

"Mine is slower," says Spilsbury, "because in a pen there is generally something to look at."

Pascual straightens up.

"It's not the food," he says. "It was never the food. It's the other animal."

Nobody says anything for a moment. Reggie has his arms folded and is looking at the table.

"You said that to me in November of 'fifty-two," he says. "In the laboratory. About ten at night, with a beaker in your hand."

"I said I didn't like it."

"You said you couldn't tell me the mechanism and you didn't like it." Reggie still does not look up. "You were right and you had no way of getting at it and you've been right ever since, and it's taken four years and two dead animals to hand you the instrument."

"Three," says Pascual.

"Three."

"Alpha was in a shed fifty yards from Beta for nine days, and I'd told you so by my second night on this island."

"I know."

"Good."

---

He puts two fingers on the left-hand column of the 'fifty-four sheet, where the intake figures run down in a round careful hand that is not Reggie's and is not his own.

"This is Charles."

"The food logs are his. Nine months of them."

"Then his name goes on it." Pascual looks up the table at nobody in particular, which is how he does this. "Not thanked at the bottom. On it, the way Jean's soundings went down. I'd like that settled this morning and not in October when somebody's counting names."

"Yes."

"Say it properly."

"His name goes on it," says Reggie.

Hester writes something on the pad. Over by the window Jean has turned round and is looking out at the water with her arms folded, and nobody says anything to her either.

---

It is Hester who opens it, and she does not know she is opening it, because she is the only person in the room who will hold a thing up by the corner and ask a plain question about where it came from.

"Dr Tar. Where does this sheet come from?"

"The separation trial. April and May of 'fifty-four."

"And whose design was it?"

And Pascual has his hand out for the sheet and his mind on the eighteenth day and hears, from the middle of the table, in the flat unhurried voice that has not altered once in an hour and a half:

"Mine."

---

He does not explode. He would like that understood, chiefly by himself: he sits in a chair at the end of a long table with his hand still out and does not explode, and the room notices, and the room's noticing is the worst part of it.

"Say it again."

"I put it to him in a letter in the March of 'fifty-four," says Spilsbury. "Four sentences."

"Four sentences."

"He had a bond of unknown strength and no way whatever to measure it. There is one instrument available in that situation and he knew quite well what it was." A pause. "Take the animal away. Keep the record. Put him back."

"And he did it."

"He did it."

"And I stood on that verandah and told him to stop."

"I am aware that somebody did."

Pascual turns his head.

"You told me it was yours."

"I didn't say it was mine," says Reggie.

"You let me think it."

"Yes."

"For two years."

"Yes," says Reggie, and looks at him, and does not do the other thing, the warm thing, which Pascual would have put a hand through the table for.

"Right." Pascual gets up. The chair goes back and John catches it without appearing to move. "Four minutes. Don't anybody say anything useful."

---

He stands at the corner of the verandah with both hands on the rail and looks at the water and does the arithmetic, because the arithmetic is the only thing in his head that will hold still.

Fifty-one days.

He gets to the bottom of it in about ninety seconds and goes back in, and does not sit down, and says it from the door.

"There's another one."

Everybody looks at him.

"The other half of that pair. He's in a pen in Hertfordshire and he's been on his own since the eleventh of February, and that's a hundred and ten days today, and the record on this table says fifty-one."

The Colonel takes his spectacles off.

"You are saying he is already dead."

"I'm saying nobody in this room knows, and that's worse." He comes down the side of the table. "Doctor. Is there a way to ask?"

"Not from me."

"Why not?"

"Because I am the man who took him," says Spilsbury, "and the last document with my name and that firm's name on the same sheet of paper is a letter telling them their controls are rubbish. Anything I write now goes into a file and stays there."

"Then somebody else's got to write."

"I'll write," says Reggie.

"Today."

"No."

Pascual stops walking.

"In September," Reggie says. "Not June. Not July."

"Why not?"

"Because in September it's an ordinary letter." He picks his glasses up off the table and puts them on. "On the Institute's paper, from the man who wrote the paper they've had in their office since last spring, asking for first-year losses on animals held singly and in pairs. Nobody ever remembers getting one of those."

"And this month?"

"This month it's a line drawn between this Institute and that firm in the month their adviser went missing. The only instrument anybody needs for that is paper."

Pascual stands in the middle of the floor with his mouth open for about a second and a half.

It is a good answer. It is a better answer than the one he came in with, and he takes it the way he takes a correction, which is on the spot; and underneath that, in a place he does not go into at a table, he registers that the sentence did not arrive in Reggie's voice.

"September," he says. "All right. And I'd like it read to me before it goes."

"Obviously."

---

They do the rest of it in two hours and it is the best two hours of work he has had in a year, and he is not proud of enjoying them.

John says the useful thing first, which is his way: every sheet in both cases photographed before dark, two sets, one set off the island on the next boat, and the negatives somewhere that is not this building. Hester says she will speak to the bank at the Bay about a box. John says he will need the long room and the shutters and nobody to open the door for four hours, and gets it.

Jean has been at the window doing something with a pencil for twenty minutes and comes to the table with a list.

"Forty tons of frozen herring," she says. "You can't land that anywhere in the world without four people writing it down."

"Go on."

"And you can't ship a live animal through a British port without an entry, a manifest, a licence and a certificate. Every one of those is paper with a date on it, in somebody's office."

"Broker," says Reggie.

"Broker keeps books."

"And the feed's the soft part, because they can't stop buying it —"

"— and they can't buy it quietly, no. Nine ports, I make it. Possibly ten."

"Do the nine."

"I'm doing the nine."

Pascual looks from one of them to the other and gets his own oar in.

"And the ice," he says. "Forty tons of frozen anything needs a cold store, and there's four between here and Kingston. I've bought fish out of three of them since 'fifty-two, and the men know me."

Jean is writing before he has finished.

Hester has not written anything for some minutes. She has her pen down and her hands folded on the pad, and she waits until it goes quiet, which it does, because it always does.

"I am going to say the difficulty," she says, "and then we will have had it said, and nobody need be surprised by it in November."

"Say it," says the Colonel.

"Every exhibit on this table was produced by a man sitting at it. Dr Spilsbury's record is a confession. Dr Tar's curve is a confession. There is no third party in it anywhere."

"And no animal," says Jean.

"And no animal in this building that anybody can point to and say *look what was done to him*, because both of ours are fat and well and swimming about forty yards from this window. That is an argument for the other side, and I have watched it be one for twenty years."

"They'd say he does perfectly well in a box," says the Colonel.

"They will say precisely that, and a man who does not wish to schedule this animal will be within his rights."

"So we're nowhere," says Jean.

"We are not nowhere. We are somewhere disagreeable." Hester picks the pen up again. "We have the only quantitative account of this that exists, and we have the mechanism."

"And?"

"And two men who'll be taken apart for having it, and no way of putting it in front of anybody that doesn't cost us both of them."

---

And Pascual, at the end of the table with his arms folded, sees the fifth thing, and says nothing whatever about it.

It is not the exhibits. It is the room.

Because there will be a room, in Kingston or somewhere worse, with a table and a man behind it who has been given a file and a reason to want it not to be true; and that man will look at a Director of Research and at a dismissed consultant who crossed four thousand miles to hand him a dead animal, and he will not start with the curves. He will start with how the two of them know each other, and he will go on with it, patiently, in a civil voice, until he finds something, and there is something to find, and it has been there since before Pascual came to this island.

He has known whose the handwriting was since the February of 1953.

He has not asked one question in four years and he is not going to begin at a long table in front of the Colonel; so he keeps his face where it is, and looks at the water jug, and puts it where such things go.

"Right," he says. "Then that's the morning. Doctor, you're at my bench at two and we'll do your blocks, and between now and then you'll eat something, and I don't care what."

"I am not particularly —"

"I didn't ask you."

---

He gets him at the north pen at four, because he is not going to be in a room at two with a man who has had nothing since Kingston.

"Hold the gate."

"I beg your pardon?"

"The gate. There, with your hand. Hold it and don't let it come back on the chain." Pascual hands him the second bucket, which is heavier than it looks and is meant to be. "You're no use to me standing about with your hat."

So Spilsbury holds the gate.

Gamma comes to the near end at the sound of the bucket and the calf comes with her, the two of them shoulder to shoulder in the shaded half, and Pascual feels the man beside him go entirely still.

He does not look round. He has some manners.

"Six months old in the August," he says, and puts the fish in. "He's had her since the day he was born and he'll have her for years yet, because I built this pen and I'm not moving either of them out of it while I'm alive."

The calf comes up under her arm and takes a whole fish out of her mouth, which is the thing, which is the entire thing, and Pascual has been writing it down twice a week since February.

Beside him, Spilsbury does not say anything for a long moment. Then:

"They do that."

"That's five times this week."

"At that age."

"At that age. And they'll do it with a full bucket sitting right there in front of them."

"Then it is not hunger."

"It isn't hunger and it isn't food, and I've given up trying to make it be either." Pascual throws the last of the herring long, to make her go for it. "Write it down if you like. There's a pencil in my top pocket and I want it back."

There is a pause of perhaps three seconds. Then a hand comes across and takes the pencil out of his pocket, and Spilsbury turns half away with the gate still on his shoulder and writes on the back of something out of his own coat, standing up, for about a minute and a half.

Pascual watches the appetite arrive in the side of the man's face and does not care for it in the least.

He has seen that particular expression on exactly one other person, at this rail, in this light, and he knows where it goes when nobody puts a hand on it.

But the animals are eating, and the pencil is going, and the man has his weight on a gate for somebody else's benefit, which is the first useful thing he has done on this island; so Pascual lets it run, and holds out his hand for the pencil at the end, and gets it back.

"Four o'clock tomorrow," he says. "Same gate."

"Yes," says Spilsbury.

"And eat something, *hombre*, before I go and tell Hester on you."

He does not turn round to see what that does to the man's face, because there are two buckets to rinse and the light is going, and because some things are better left to get on with themselves in the dark.
