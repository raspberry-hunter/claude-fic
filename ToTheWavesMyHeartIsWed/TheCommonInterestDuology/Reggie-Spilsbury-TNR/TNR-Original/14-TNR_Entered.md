# Entered

The boat from Vigo is a day late, and Reggie spends that day finding out what he is like when he cannot do anything about anything, and does not think much of him.

He walks the sea front four times. He buys a hat he does not need. At about two he goes into a bar and has one glass of wine and gets out of there quickly, because he can see precisely where that goes and he is not going to arrive at the quay tomorrow with a head on him.

At six he goes back up to the Rosário and lies on the bed in his shirtsleeves with the shutters half over and does the arithmetic he has been doing since the fourth of April 1957, which is now finished, which now has nothing in it, and finds that he cannot stop doing it, and that it keeps coming out at one.

---

He is on the quay at half past seven in the morning because the office said eight and the office has been wrong once already.

It comes in at twenty past nine. It is a filthy little coaster with a list on her and a deck cargo of drums, and there is a man at the rail in a linen jacket and a hat, standing perfectly still while everybody round him is not, with both hands on the rail, looking at the town.

Reggie stays where he is. He has thought about this for five hundred and forty-nine days and about nine of those thoughts involved doing something at the water's edge in front of thirty people, and he does not do any of them; he stands with his hands in his pockets and lets the man come down the ladder and along the stones and stop in front of him.

"Tar."

"Spilsbury."

"You have a hat."

"I've got an idiotic hat. I bought it yesterday because you were late."

"I was not late. The boat was late." Alec puts his case down and looks at him for a long moment in the full morning light, from about a yard, with his face doing absolutely nothing at all, which Reggie can read the way other men read print. "You are brown to the eyes and you have put on nine pounds and there is grey in it now on the left side."

"There's grey in it on both sides and you're being polite, which frightens me." Reggie picks up the case before the man can stop him. "Come on. It's eight minutes and it's uphill and the woman gave me the room at the back, which she did on purpose and which I did not ask her to do."

"What did you ask her?"

"For two rooms," says Reggie, "like a coward," and Alec makes a small sound that is as close as he comes to laughing in public, and they go up the hill.

---

The first hour is bad and he had not expected it to be bad.

They have written to each other something over forty times in eighteen months and Reggie could sit an examination on the last two years of the man's life, and it turns out that this is not the same as being in a room with him. They put the case down. They talk about the passage from Vigo. Alec stands by the window and does not take his jacket off. Reggie hears himself ask, in the voice of a man being introduced to somebody's aunt, whether the food on the boat was tolerable.

Then Alec says, to the window, "This is intolerable."

"Yes."

"I have been composing sentences since Tuesday."

"So have I. I had about six good ones on the quay and they've all gone."

"I had eleven," says Alec, "and they were better than yours," and turns round.

---

It is four in the afternoon before they get any further than the bed, and there is no lamp in it anywhere, because the shutters are half over and the room is full of yellow light off the whitewash across the street and there is nothing to turn out.

That is the thing Reggie keeps arriving at and having to stop over.

He gets the jacket off him and the shirt and puts him back against the bolster and takes a very long time about the whole of it, and he can see every single thing; he can see the grey coming in over the ear, and the mark on the collarbone where a crate went over in Vigo in January and which he has read about in a letter in February and now puts his mouth to; he can see the man's face when he does that, which is not a face Alec Spilsbury has ever let anybody see in daylight, and which goes to pieces immediately and completely.

"Don't," says Alec.

"Don't what?"

"Look at me while you do that."

"No," says Reggie. "That's the whole arrangement. You had a lamp in February of 'fifty-seven and I'm having October."

And Alec laughs — properly, with his head back on the bolster, a real one, which perhaps four people alive have ever heard — and says, "That is entirely fair," and puts a hand up into Reggie's hair and pulls him down.

He is thinner than he was. Reggie learns the ribs again with his thumb and says the number out loud, which is unkind, and Alec says, "There is a fish stew in Vigo which I have been unable to master," and Reggie says, "You'll eat here if I have to do it with a spoon," and Alec says, "I should like to see you try," and Reggie says, "*Please* try me," and after that neither of them says anything sensible for some time.

He talks the whole way through it, because he always does.

He tells him what he looks like in this light, in order, with the measurements in, because that is a joke between them now and because it works better on him than anything else in the world. And when Alec's hands go up of their own accord to the iron of the bedhead — not asked, not put there, just going, the way a hand goes to a rail — Reggie takes hold of both wrists and says, "Still," and Alec is still, and says his name three times in about ninety seconds and cannot stop himself, and afterwards lies with his forearm over his eyes and his chest going like a man who has run somewhere.

"Reggie."

"Mm."

"I am forty-six years old."

"You're forty-six years old and you just said my name like a tyre going."

"Yes," says Alec, with enormous dignity. "That is what I am remarking on."

---

They do not leave the room until the following afternoon, and then it is only because the woman comes and knocks and says something about the bed linen that neither of them fully follows, and they go out into the town like men let out of somewhere.

It is a good fortnight. He wants that written down somewhere, because in about nine years he is going to want to look at it.

They eat at a place on the Rua do Castanheiro four times and the woman there decides on the second evening that Alec is ill and begins bringing him things, and Alec — who has spent his entire life declining to be fussed over by anybody — eats every scrap of it, every time, and is furious about it, and goes back.

They walk up into the hills on the fourth day and Reggie gets sunburnt across the back of the neck like a boy of nineteen and Alec is unbearable about it for four days and buys him a worse hat.

They have one real argument, on the seventh, about a map, and it lasts twenty minutes and is entirely about the map, and Reggie is right, and Alec concedes at the end with the words "You have done the arithmetic correctly and I dislike it," which is the sentence he uses about once a year and which Reggie has never once heard without wanting to put his hands on him.

They sleep late, which Alec has not done since 1939 and says so on the fifth morning in a tone of genuine scientific interest, as though reporting a novel finding in an unfamiliar organism.

---

The work comes up in pieces, the way it does when two people have it in common and are trying, on purpose, not to spend the fortnight on it.

He tells him on the second evening, in about four sentences, that the paper came out in the February and has been cited eleven times already, and that the fourth name on it is C. A. Cholmondeley, and that there was a note from the boy's mother in June which he has brought and which Alec reads standing up in the street under a light, because he is not a man who puts a thing in his pocket to read later.

He tells him on the fifth day, walking, that Dewar & Lecky got their licence in the September of 'fifty-seven — and that it is written with a condition in it that animals of this species be held not fewer than two together, which has never appeared in a licence in the history of the British Empire, and which Hester Bevan got by being tiresome to four separate offices for eleven months.

"That is not a schedule," says Alec.

"It isn't a schedule. Hester says 1962 and Hester is never wrong about dates, she's only wrong about people."

"Their solicitor stopped writing in the February."

"I know he did."

"I should like to know why."

"Because the paper came out," says Reggie, "and a man with forty-one citations looks like a man being persecuted instead of a man being corrected, and Lecky is far too good at his job to go on doing something that isn't working."

Alec thinks about that for about a hundred yards.

"That is a pity," he says. "I had grown rather attached to the correspondence. It was the only post I got that was addressed to me personally."

"That," says Reggie, "is the worst thing you have ever said to me," and Alec looks extremely pleased with himself for the rest of the afternoon.

---

And on the ninth day Reggie gets the rest of it out of him, which takes about forty minutes and is like drawing a nail.

There is a firm in Vigo and a firm in Aberdeen and a Norwegian outfit at Ålesund who pay him in kroner and by results; he has a room over a chandler's; he has four hundred pounds more than he started with, which he says in the tone of a man confessing to something. He writes anatomical descriptions three nights a week at three guineas the thousand words and can do six thousand in an evening and has begun to find it restful.

And he has, in a case under a bed over a chandler's in Vigo, the holding and mortality records of something over forty thousand food fish across eleven commercial establishments in three countries, kept daily, by him, by hand, with the densities.

Reggie stops in the middle of the street.

"Alec."

"It is not a scientific dataset. It is a set of trade books."

"It's the largest series of its kind that exists on this earth and you have been sitting on it since the spring of 'fifty-seven —"

"I have not been sitting on it. I have been *compiling* it, which is a different verb, and I intended to say something about it when it was finished."

"When's it finished?"

"In about nine years."

"Then you'll write the first part this winter and send it to me in January," says Reggie, "and I'll put Jean on the tidal stuff and Pascual on the mortalities and you'll be first name on it and I don't want to hear one single word."

And Alec opens his mouth, and looks at him, and shuts it again.

"Very well," he says.

---

On the twelfth evening Reggie finds the notebook.

It is not hidden. It is on the table under the offprints with the pencil through the loop, the way it has been on every table in every room this man has ever occupied, and Reggie has never once opened it in twelve years and has never been told not to.

Alec is downstairs paying the woman for the week.

He opens it at the back, because that is where it is, and reads the entry of the twenty-fourth of February 1957 standing at a table in Funchal in his shirtsleeves with a towel still round his neck.

*Result: go.*

*Accepted. Not declined. First time. Note that the reason is the same reason as all three of the others, and that I can no longer tell whether that is a defence.*

He reads it three times.

Then he turns the page, because his hand does it without him, and the next entry is dated the fourteenth of May 1958 — five months before this fortnight; before the letters that settled the town; before either of them had the slightest idea whether a fortnight would be bearable or an unendurable thing neither of them would ever risk again — and it says:

*Second fortnight of April 1960. Place to be settled by letter. Entered.*

He sits down on the end of the bed with the book open on his knee.

---

When Alec comes up he is still sitting there, and Alec sees the book, and stops in the doorway with his hand on the frame.

Neither of them says anything for about three seconds.

"April of nineteen-sixty," says Reggie.

"Yes."

"You wrote it in May."

"I did."

"Before you knew."

"I knew," says Alec.

"You did not know. You had eighteen months of letters and a coaster from Vigo and no earthly idea whether the two of us could do a fortnight in a room, and you put the next one in the book in May anyway." His voice is not doing what he wants it to. "You said you couldn't tell whether it was a defence."

"I cannot."

"Give me the pencil."

---

Alec takes the pencil out of the loop and gives it to him and does not ask what for, which is the single most extraordinary thing he does in the entire fortnight.

Reggie turns back to the twenty-fourth of February 1957 and writes, under the last line, in a hand that is not as steady as he would like and which will be perfectly legible in forty years:

*It is not a defence. It is a finding. — R.T., Funchal, 8.x.58.*

Then he hands the book back and does not watch him read it, because there are some things you do not stand and watch, and goes and looks out of the window at the whitewash across the street until there is a hand flat between his shoulder blades, where it goes.

---

They have two more days.

They spend the first of them doing nothing whatever and the second one badly, because the last day always is, and they both know it and neither of them mentions it, and Alec packs at eleven in the morning for a boat at six in the evening, which is absurd, and which Reggie lets him do because he understands exactly what it is for.

At about four Alec says, from the chair, in the voice he uses for reading out a weight: "Ålesund in the summer."

"What about it?"

"There is a marine station and a man there who has been in my correspondence since June, and he does not read the Zoological Record and does not care what anybody in London thinks about anybody. It is a small place and it is extremely cold and the work is entirely on cod."

Reggie sits up.

"Are you telling me you've got a bench?"

"I am telling you," says Alec, "that in about two years I may have a bench, and that Ålesund is nine days from the Martin by mail and cable and not five weeks, and that a man may go to Norway in the summer to look at a holding facility without one single person in the world writing a sentence down about it."

"Two years."

"Possibly three."

"Right," says Reggie.

And that is all either of them says about it, because it is a projection from a small sample and they both know better; and Reggie takes it out to the quay with him at six o'clock and puts it in his coat next to the other thing.

---

On the stones, in front of forty people, they shake hands.

"First fortnight of October 'fifty-eight," says Reggie. "Entered and closed."

"Entered and closed."

"Second fortnight of April 1960."

"Entered," says Alec. "Five hundred and fifty-one days."

"Five hundred and fifty."

Alec stops with his hand still in his.

"Do it again," he says.

"I've done it four times. It's five hundred and fifty. You've gone wrong on the leap year, which is a thing you have never done in your life and which I intend to write to you about at length."

And Alec Spilsbury stands on a quay in Funchal with his hat in his other hand and looks at him, and says, "Then I shall want it in writing," and gets on the boat.

---

Reggie walks back up the hill in the dark and does not go into the bar.

He goes up to the room at the back, which the woman gave him on purpose, and sits on the end of the bed for a while with the shutters open, and then gets out his own book and writes the date, and under the date the number, and works it out twice, because he is not going to be wrong about it.

Five hundred and fifty.

Then, because the light is still on and there is nobody to see him do it, he writes one more line underneath, which has nothing to do with the arithmetic at all.

*He was late by a day and I got through it. Note that for next time.*
