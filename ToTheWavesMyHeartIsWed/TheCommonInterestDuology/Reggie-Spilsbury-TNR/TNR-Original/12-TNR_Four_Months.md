# Four Months

It takes Hester Bevan four minutes on the fourteenth of November to establish that he is not going to Kingston, and he concedes at about the third, which is unlike him, and concedes because she is right.

"You removed a live animal from a licensed holder's pen without leave."

"I did."

"And kept it in a place you decline to name."

"I do."

"Then in that room you are not a scientist, Dr Spilsbury. You are a man they can spend the whole morning on instead of spending it on a curve." She squares the pad to the edge of the table. "Dr Pobil takes the record. You stay on this island."

"Yes."

"You are not going to argue."

"You have done the arithmetic correctly," he says, "and I would rather be useful than present."

She looks at him for a moment longer than the answer requires.

"That," she says, "is the first sentence you have said in six months that I have liked."

---

On the morning of the hearing he walks down to the harbour office before breakfast and hands in four words.

> USE IT ALL STOP
> SPILSBURY

It costs him one and eightpence. The clerk counts it twice, which is correct procedure, and Spilsbury stands in the doorway afterwards and looks at the water and observes that there is nothing whatever for him to do between now and four o'clock in the afternoon, and that this is the first day since the fifth of February on which that has been true.

He does the day badly. He would like that recorded. He reads nine pages of a monograph on gill vasculature and retains none of it; he goes twice to the long room and does not sit down; at one point he finds himself standing in the doorway of the office looking at the empty long table, which is a thing he cannot account for and does not attempt to.

At four he goes down to the north pen, because there is an arrangement and the arrangement has held since the thirty-first of May.

Pobil is not there. Pobil is in a room on the first floor of the Secretariat, and it is a quarter past nine in the evening in London, and Spilsbury stands at a rail with a bucket in his hand and holds a gate open for nobody.

The female comes to the near end at the sound of the bucket. The calf comes with her.

He puts the fish in and writes the weights on the sheet in the tin, in the book kept by a man who will be five hundred miles away until Thursday, and signs the line *A.S.* the way he has been told to, and goes up.

---

The boat comes in on the twenty-second at about four and Spilsbury is not on the jetty, because the whole station is on the jetty and one of him would be conspicuous in it.

He is at the end of the long room with the shutter half up when Tar comes through the door at a run, which he has not done since 1949.

"Four months."

"Say it in order."

"Averill reserved. Inspector in the new year. Licence extended to the thirty-first of March."

"On its existing terms?"

"On its existing terms. And he's put the word *welfare* in a minute where it's never been in the history of that office." Tar has his hat still in his hand and is doing something with it that will ruin it. "And Pascual took Lecky apart on the dates."

"On the dates."

"On the *dates*, Alec. He got up and said the last entry was the sixth of May, and there was a man from Agriculture in that room who said *yes* out loud."

Spilsbury sits down on the stool.

"Then it cannot have been written to fit," he says.

"It cannot have been written to fit."

"Because on the sixth of May there was nothing for it to fit."

"That's the sentence. That's very nearly the sentence he used."

And Spilsbury, who has spent nine days believing that the only thing he had left to give anybody was going to be thrown out of a room in Kingston on the ground that he is the man who produced it, finds that he has to take his glasses off and clean them on his handkerchief, which takes about fifteen seconds and is the only instrument available to him.

"Four months," he says.

"Four months."

"It is not a win."

"It isn't a win. It's an inspection and a word in a minute book." Tar comes and sits on the end of the bench, close enough that the shutter would have to come down before either of them did anything else, and does not do anything else. "And they aren't going in a lighter in January."

"No."

"And the Colonel wants to tell Hester himself, so you're to look surprised at dinner."

"I shall be magnificent."

"You'll be awful. You have one face."

"I have several," says Spilsbury, "and you have seen two of them," and Tar laughs at that with his whole chest, in a room with the shutter half up, four feet away, and it is the best sound Spilsbury has heard in a year.

---

The first letter is returned to him in the January.

It is not a letter to him. It is a letter *from* him — an offprint request he sent to a man in Naples in October, which comes back in the Institute's mail with a note on the bottom in a strange hand regretting that the writer does not feel able to correspond at present.

He reads it standing at the rack and puts it in his pocket and says nothing to anybody for nine days, and then Hester puts the solicitors' letter on the long table in front of four people, because she does not permit that sort of thing on this station.

The relevant part is paragraph five.

*We are instructed further that our client has written, and will continue to write, to such institutions and societies as it considers appropriate concerning Dr A. Spilsbury and the circumstances of his departure from its employment.*

"How many," says Pobil.

"All of them," says Spilsbury. "It is not expensive to do and there is no hurry about it. A man in Nassau with a typewriter can finish me in four afternoons."

"That's what you've got to say about it."

"It is precisely what I should do in their position and I have no complaint whatever."

Tar does not say anything at all.

Spilsbury does not look at him, because there are three other people in the room and because he already knows, to about two figures, what is happening in the man's chest, and because the one thing he is not going to do in front of Jean Leslie is check.

---

The agent comes in the third week of January, and they do not find out about him until the ninth of February.

Pobil brings it. He comes up to the long room at about eight in the evening with his sleeves down, which is how Spilsbury knows before he opens his mouth that this is not about an animal.

"A man went to Alleyne's clerk in September and again last month. Card from an insurance agency in Nassau. Asked nine or ten questions."

"About the licence."

"About the boats, and the cold store, and who signs for the fish." Pobil puts both hands on the back of a chair. "And one about the staff. Whether the doctors live in."

The room does what rooms do.

Spilsbury finds that he has put the forceps down, squarely, beside the jar, and that his hands are perfectly steady, and that somewhere under his sternum something very large has changed gear.

"Which doctors."

"He didn't say which. That's the whole of it and I've had it four days and I've been deciding what to do with it, and what I've decided is to bring it to you before I bring it to him."

"Why."

"Because he'll do something clever," says Pobil, "and you'll do something correct, and one of those is worth having tonight."

---

There is a silence of perhaps four seconds.

"You have not asked me a question in four years," says Spilsbury.

"No."

"You are going to ask me one now."

"I'm not going to ask you anything." Pobil pulls the chair out and sits down on it backwards, with his arms along the top of it, which is how a man sits who intends to stay. "I've known since February of 'fifty-three. I read two lines of a letter off a bench by accident and I've never needed a third."

"Then say what you came to say."

"You're careless." Pobil says it flatly, without any heat in it at all, which from him is the alarming register. "Not him. He's had a coat on since he was thirteen and it's a good one. You."

"Go on."

"You look at him about a second and a half longer than a man looks at a colleague. You do it at the four o'clock feed. You did it on Christmas Day in front of nine people."

"And nobody remarked on it."

"Nobody in this station noticed it except me, and I notice everything that's in front of me. It's what I'm paid for."

"That is a fair observation."

"It's not an observation, it's a bill."

"Go on."

"Then here's the rest of it. The huts." Pobil rubs a hand down over his face. "I put your cases in that one on the thirtieth of May because I thought it was the kind thing, and I'd do it again tomorrow, and it was the stupidest thing I've done since I came here."

"Yes."

"So you move. Tomorrow, not next week, and nobody helps you carry anything." He gets up. "There's a spare at the top of the row and something wrong with its roof, and you'll make a fuss about the roof, and I'll show you where. And you stop being at that rail at four."

"No."

Pobil stops with his hand on the chair.

"The animals eat at four," says Spilsbury, "and they eat at four whatever is happening in the office. You told me that yourself in May."

And Pobil looks at him for a long moment, and then makes a noise that is not quite a laugh and is not anything else either.

"*Ay,* hombre," he says. "All right. Four o'clock stands. Everything else goes."

---

He moves his case up the row on the tenth and complains about the roof to the Colonel on the eleventh, and the Colonel comes and looks at it and is delighted, because the Colonel likes a fault he can do something about.

Tar takes it correctly, which is to say he takes it in about nine seconds and says nothing until they are alone.

"Whose idea."

"Pobil's."

"He's right."

"He is right."

"I know he's right," says Tar. "I'd like it on record that I said so before I said the other thing."

"Say the other thing."

"No."

"Reggie."

"I said no," says Tar, "because if I say it I shall have to look at it, and I'm not going to look at it in the fortnight before an inspector arrives," and he goes and does his afternoon, and that is the whole of it, and Spilsbury writes both halves of it down.

---

The paper is on the long table on the twenty-fourth of February in draft, and it is the best thing either of them will ever have anything to do with.

It is short. It is fourteen pages and two figures and it says one thing: that the decline described in the captive literature as renal, hepatic or inanitional is, in a defined class of cases, the consequence of separating an animal from a conspecific with whom it has an established association, and that the effect is reproducible, quantifiable, and has a shape.

There are two curves in figure one. One of them is fifty-one lines long. The other is the record of eleven days in April and May of 1954, with the intake column in the round careful hand of a postgraduate who is dead.

Spilsbury reads the whole draft twice and then turns to the title page, which Tar, who learned it somewhere, has put at the bottom of the pile.

There are four names on it.

"Take mine off," he says.

---

"No."

"Tar —"

"No."

"You have not heard the reasoning."

"I don't need the reasoning. I know the reasoning." Tar has his arms folded and his back to the bench and he has gone perfectly still, which in him means the opposite of what it means in anybody else. "The reasoning is that a paper with your name on it can be answered with a solicitor's letter instead of a figure."

"That is the reasoning."

"And it's sound."

"It is sound."

"And the answer is still no."

Spilsbury takes his glasses off.

"Give me yours, then."

"Strike your name and that record's fifty-one lines from an unnamed man at an undisclosed pound." Tar picks the title page up off the pile and holds it. "No provenance. No witness. The whole of what it's worth is that a named man put his name on a thing that finished him."

"That is sentiment."

"That's Pascual's argument in a room in Kingston in November, and it won four months."

"He won four months with the dates."

"He won four months with the dates because the dates were in *your book*." He puts the sheet down. "You cannot have it both ways, Alec. Either the book is worth something because of who kept it or it's worth nothing at all."

Spilsbury sits with that for a moment.

It is a good argument. It is, in fact, his own argument, run back at him across a bench at twenty past nine at night by a man who has had thirteen years in which to learn how he does it, and there is a great deal of pleasure in being beaten with it and he declines to let any of it show.

"There is a second reasoning," he says.

"Go on."

"They will not stop at the journals. When the paper is out they will begin on the men whose names are next to mine, and one of those names is on the licence application of a colonial institute."

"I know."

"You do not know. You have not looked at it."

"I've looked at it," says Tar.

"When."

"On the ninth of February. In the sick bay. About four hours, after Pascual came up the row with his sleeves down." He says it perfectly evenly. "Both sides of the page, the way you taught me. And it came out at exactly what you think it came out at."

"And?"

"And I'm not taking your name off my paper," says Tar. "Get another argument."

---

Here is what Spilsbury does not say, at twenty past nine on the twenty-fourth of February, standing at a bench with his glasses in his hand.

He does not say that he agrees.

He does not say that the sum has been in front of him since the ninth of February and that he has run it every night since and that it comes out the same way every time and that the answer is not *take my name off the paper*. It is one step further along than that, and it is not a thing that can be done at a bench, and it will require a boat.

He has declined the result of an honest arithmetic three times in his life and he has written down each one and the reason, and every one of them has been for this man.

He does not decline it this time.

He notes that as well, silently, standing there; and then he puts his glasses back on and says, "Very well," and lets the man have his paper, and watches him be pleased, which takes about two seconds and comes up over the whole of his face from the brow down.

---

He asks for it that night.

That is not his habit. He has asked for a thing twice in ten years, both times in November, both times having worked out in advance precisely what it would cost him to say the words; and he stands inside the door of the hut at the top of the row at eleven o'clock with his hand still on the bolt and says, "Leave the lamp."

Tar stops with his fingers on the wick.

"It's on."

"I know it is on."

"Alec, it's *on* and it's been on since I came in —"

"Leave it on," says Spilsbury. "That is what I am asking you for."

---

Tar takes his hand off the lamp.

He does not make a joke, which he could, and which would be deserved. He comes across the boards instead and stands in front of him and looks at him for a good four seconds in the full yellow light, at about a foot, with the whole of his face doing what it does when nobody is watching, which is everything at once.

"Right," he says. "Then I want to say something first and you're not to be clever about it."

"I shall try."

"You've had a filthy winter and you've been extraordinary about it, and I've watched you be extraordinary about it for three months and hated every day of it." He puts both hands flat on Spilsbury's chest, over the ribs, where they go. "So the lamp stays on, and I'm going to look at you the whole time, and you may count whatever you like."

"That is not fair."

"It isn't," Tar agrees, "and it's what's happening," and kisses him.

---

It is slow, because Spilsbury will not be hurried at a measurement he has waited for, and because he has decided — at some point between the bench and the bolt — that he is going to have the whole of this and take it away with him.

He gets the shirt off him and puts his mouth to the collarbone and to the notch at the base of the throat and to the place under the jaw where the pulse is, and Tar tips his head back against the boards to give it to him and says, "*There*. Slower," in exactly the voice he uses to ask a colleague for a figure, and Spilsbury laughs against his throat, which is a thing perhaps four people alive have heard.

"What."

"You said that in 1946."

"I meant it in 1946."

"You said *do that again, slower*, and I stopped, and I have thought about it approximately once a week for ten years."

"Once a *week* —"

"It is an estimate," says Spilsbury, "and it is conservative," and gets him to the bed.

In the lamplight the man is brown to the collar and pale below it and there is a good deal more of him than there was in 1946; he eats now, which is Pobil's doing and which Spilsbury has never thanked him for and never will. He learns the ribs again with his thumb, one at a time, because the count has changed since September. He puts his mouth to the hollow inside the hip and the hard line of the bone above it, and a hand comes down into his hair and closes and holds on, and Tar says his name, and it is not steady.

"Talk to me."

"You can see me."

"I know I can see you. Talk anyway." A breath that goes wrong in the middle. "That's — Alec, God, that's — don't stop. Say what you're doing. You always say what you're doing."

So he says what he is doing.

He says it the way he says anything, quietly and in order, with the measurements in; and when he gets to the part he has not said before — when he says, against the inside of a thigh, in the flat voice he uses at a bench, that he has wanted this man since the spring of 1945 and has not stopped for a week since — Tar makes a sound like something being taken out of a wound and gets a fistful of the blanket and holds on to it as though the bed were moving.

"*Please.*"

Spilsbury stops.

He lifts his head. He is forty-four years old, in a hut on a cay, in lamplight, with his glasses somewhere on the floor, and he has heard that word from this man five times in ten years and has been in the same position for all five.

"Say it again."

"Please."

"Once more."

"Alec, *please* —"

And he gives him what he asked for, and he takes a long time about it, and does not permit any hurry at all; and at the end Tar has both hands in his hair and his back off the bed and his eyes open, because he was told the lamp was staying on and has kept his side of it, and he says, right at the last, with nothing left in his voice whatever: "*Alexander*."

Nobody has used it since his mother died except this man.

Spilsbury does not say anything for some while, because there is nothing he could say that would be an improvement.

---

Afterwards Tar lies half across him, heavy and loose, with one hand spread over his ribs in the place where it goes, and the lamp still burning because neither of them has got up to it.

"One hundred and thirty-four."

"You cannot possibly —"

"I can. It's coming down." Tar's thumb moves, back and forth, very slightly. "Why the lamp?"

And there it is, at about midnight, in the ordinary way, from a man who is nine-tenths asleep and has never once been misinformed about him in thirteen years.

Spilsbury looks at the ceiling.

"Because I have had ten years of you in the dark," he says, "and it seemed to me that I had better have one of the other thing."

"That's a horrible answer."

"It is a true one."

"It's horrible *because* it's true, that's what makes it horrible." Tar shifts, and gets an arm right round him, and takes hold of a handful of the back of his shirt where the shirt is not. "Don't do that again."

"Do what?"

"Whatever that was. Whatever you were doing while you were doing that." His voice has gone somewhere. "I could feel you at it. You were three feet behind your own eyes the whole time, and I let it go, and I'm not letting it go tomorrow."

Spilsbury puts his hand in the man's hair and keeps it there.

"Tomorrow, then," he says.

"That's not a yes."

"No," says Spilsbury. "Go to sleep."

And Tar, who sleeps the way he works, completely, is gone inside five minutes with his hand flat where it was, and does not ask again, and would have been given a true answer if he had, which is the part Spilsbury will have to carry.

---

At about two he eases out from under the hand and gets his coat off the chair and goes and sits at the table with the lamp turned down to save nothing at all, because there is no meter on a cay, and takes out the notebook.

He writes the date.

*24.ii.57. Paper in draft, 11 pp, 2 figs. Four names. Argued to have mine struck; refused; the argument used against me was mine and was correct. T. has already done the sum himself — 9.ii, sick bay, four hours — and reached the same figure and put it aside.*

*He will not put it aside twice.*

Then he sits for a while with the pen not moving, and then writes the rest, because he does not strike out observations and has never yet begun.

*Arithmetic, ninth of February onward, run every night since.*

*On one side: the four o'clock feed, the bench, the next room, and the whole of what I have.*

*On the other: a man with a card asking Alleyne's clerk whether the doctors live in; a solicitor with a letter-writing habit; an inspector in March; and R. T.'s name on the licence application of a colonial institute, four lines under mine.*

*Result: go.*

He looks at that for some time. It is not a long word and it has taken a fortnight.

Then he writes the last line, and it is the fourth entry of its kind in ten years of notebooks, and it is the only one that does not say what the other three say.

*Accepted.*

*Not declined. First time. Note that the reason is the same reason as all three of the others, and that I can no longer tell whether that is a defence.*

He shuts the book and puts it in the inside pocket, and turns the lamp out, and goes back through and gets in, and Tar, without waking, turns over and puts the hand back where it was.
