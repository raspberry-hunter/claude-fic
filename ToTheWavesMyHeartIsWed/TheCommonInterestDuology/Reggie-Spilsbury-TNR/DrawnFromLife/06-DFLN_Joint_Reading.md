# Joint Reading

## How it starts

It starts, as everything on this station starts, with a piece of equipment.

Dr Sealy's microscope is a 1938 Watson with a cracked substage condenser and no oil objective, and she has been reading blood films on it for eleven years and getting about sixty per cent of what is in them, and she knows it, and on the third of December she says so in a letter to Hester Bevan in one sentence without any apology in it at all.

Hester brings it to breakfast.

"Dr Sealy asks whether she may use the Institute's microscope on Thursdays. She proposes to bring her own films, her own slides and her own oil, and to pay for the time at whatever rate the Institute cares to set."

"What rate do we set for a microscope," says the Colonel.

"We have never set a rate for a microscope because nobody has ever asked."

"Set it at nothing," says Reggie, "we're not a commercial house. Pobil, you'll have to show her the stage, it's got the wrong clip on it since March."

And Dr Pobil says, "I can do that," in a voice of such perfect flatness that Nora puts her cup down in order to look at it properly, and nobody else at the table hears a thing.

---

## Thursdays

The first one is the twelfth of January and it lasts two hours.

By the sixth of April they run from about half past nine to the last boat, and there are two chairs at the far bench instead of one, and the second one does not go back under.

Here is what they are actually doing, which Nora gets in pieces over four months and eventually understands better than either of them supposes.

Dr Sealy brings films from the district — blood, mostly, and a certain amount of other material — and Dr Pobil cuts and stains anything that will not go on a slide flat, because he is a better histologist than either of them thinks he is and she has no one at all. Then they read them together, which is the part that matters: two people at one instrument, one looking and one writing, and then swapping, and a result that neither of them signs alone.

Nora, who is at the far end of the same room for most of it, gets used to the noise of it inside about three weeks: the long silences, the click of the stage, and then:

"There."

"Where."

"Field nine. Come off it and let me put the pointer on."

"I have got field nine and there is nothing in field nine but a rather tired-looking —"

"Dr Pobil, move."

---

## The list

She does not go looking. It runs.

That the second chair came out from under the bench on the nineteenth of January and has never gone back.

That he has started keeping a second notebook, small, in his breast pocket, in a hand he does not use for the station — smaller, slower, more upright, the hand of a man taking care — and that it comes out on Thursdays and on no other day.

That in February he begins arriving for the nine-thirty boat at nine twenty-five, when he has arrived for every boat since 1952 at a quarter past, and Nora would defend in any court that this is the single most incriminating fact in the whole inventory, because it means he has calculated how long looking eager takes.

That in March there is a second lamp at the far bench which nobody ordered and which is not in the inventory and about which Hester Bevan has said nothing whatsoever.

That his English gets worse on Thursdays. Not much. But the *ay* comes out, and the *bueno*, and twice in April he says an entire sentence in Spanish by accident and has to say it again, and Dr Sealy does not look up and says, "I got about half of that and the half I got was rude."

And that on the eleventh of May he tells her she is wrong.

---

## The eleventh of May

It is about a film and Nora does not follow the medicine of it at all.

What she follows is that Dr Sealy says a thing, and Dr Pobil says, "No," flat, without a preamble, in the voice he has used on Reggie Tar across a verandah in front of the entire station once a month for eight years —

"— no, that is what it looks like and that is not what it is, and I have seen it four times in goats and you have not seen it at all, and I am not going to agree with you at eleven o'clock in the morning in order to be pleasant. Cut it again and cut it thinner."

— and there is a silence of about three seconds, and Nora at the far bench does not breathe.

And Dr Marguerite Sealy says: "Then cut it thinner and show me."

And he does, and he is right, and she looks at it for a long time and then says, "Yes. All right. I had that wrong and I have had it wrong for two years and there is a man at the Bay I owe an apology to," and writes it down; and neither of them says anything else about it, and they go on to the next slide.

Nora sits at her end of the room with a plate in front of her that she has not touched in twenty minutes.

Because that is not a courtship. Nobody in the history of the world has ever courted anybody by telling them to cut it thinner.

It is something else and she has watched it for three years at close range in a different pair of people: it is two people agreeing, without ever discussing it, that the work between them is going to be more important than either of them minding.

*Oh,* she thinks. *Oh, you're done for, both of you.*

---

## The early boat

In June Dr Sealy starts coming on the early boat.

There is no reason for it. The films do not go off faster. It puts four extra hours on her day at each end and she has a district to run and she is forty-six years old.

She simply begins, on the eighth of June, arriving at ten past seven in the morning instead of half past nine, and she does it every Thursday thereafter, and she never once remarks upon it, and neither does he, and it is the first thing Nora has seen from *her* side that will not go in any other column.

The other one comes on the twenty-ninth.

They are all on the verandah at six and Reggie is being extremely funny about a letter from a society in Ghent, and Dr Sealy is in the second chair with her hat on her knee, and Pascual gets up to fetch something, and as he goes past the back of her chair she puts two fingers on his sleeve — barely, for about half a second, the way you touch a doorframe going through it — and takes them off again, and goes on listening to Reggie.

Nobody sees it.

Nora sees it, and looks immediately at her own hands, and finds she has to put the glass down.

---

## The eleventh of July

Alec Spilsbury comes up the jetty at four in the afternoon with a case and a hat, for the fourth consecutive summer, twelve days, accession 197 in the ordinary way.

He is greyer. He is in extremely good spirits. He has a station in Vigo that has stopped losing thirty-eight per cent of its holding stock and a paper coming out of it, and he tells the whole jetty about it before he has put the case down.

It takes him, by Nora's estimate, about thirty hours.

She watches him get it on the Wednesday morning, at breakfast, while Dr Sealy is not even on the island: Pascual says something about Thursday, and looks at the clock, and Alec Spilsbury goes on buttering his toast and does not change expression by one degree, and Nora — who has had three years of practice — sees him put it down somewhere and file it.

On the Thursday he comes into the long room at about eleven, takes one look at the far bench, and goes straight back out again with his hands behind his back.

---

## The verandah, the fifteenth

He finds her at about half past nine with the lamp between them, which is what they do now.

"Mrs Weald."

"Dr Spilsbury."

"I am going to tell you a thing rather than ask you one," he says, "because we did the other way round in 1958 and it was a disgrace and I have thought about it since."

"Go on."

"Pobil is in love with the doctor at the Bay. She is in love with him. Neither of them has said so. It will hold until about the autumn and then one of them will do something about it, and it will be her, because he has decided it would be presumptuous and she has decided nothing of the kind."

"That's very good for thirty hours."

"It is four days," says Spilsbury, "and it is not good at all, it is the second-easiest reading I have taken in my life, and the reason I am telling you rather than asking you is that I should like to know why nobody else in this building has taken it."

"Hester's taken it."

"Mrs Bevan took it in January, I should think, and bought a lamp." He turns his glass. "I am not asking about Mrs Bevan."

Nora looks out at the dark for a moment.

"He's been at it eleven years," she says, "and he's very good on numbers."

"He is the best man on numbers I have ever worked with."

"And Dr Tar told Dr Pobil on a path in November, as a kindness, that she wasn't having him. And he has not revised it since, because nothing has come in that he counts as data, because what's come in is a chair and a lamp and a boat that arrives two hours early."

Alec Spilsbury puts his head back and laughs, almost soundlessly, for about eight seconds.

"He will find out in the spring," he says, "and he will be *insufferable*."

"He'll be worse than insufferable. He'll be delighted, and he'll go about telling everyone he always thought so."

"He will tell *me* he always thought so. He will write it to me in a letter. I shall keep it."

---

Then he stops laughing, and they sit for a while.

"You are not going to tell him," says Nora. "Between now and the spring."

"No."

"May I ask why not? Because I've got my reason and I'd like to know if it's the same one."

"It is not mine to tell," says Spilsbury, "which is presumably yours as well and is the least of it." He sets the glass down on the boards. "The rest of it is that I should have to explain how I knew, and I should do that rather well, and Reggie would then ask me how long I have been able to read that man; and the answer is since about February of 1956, and he has never once asked me what I saw in that year, and I have spent five years being extremely glad of it."

The generator changes note.

"Dr Spilsbury —"

"I am not asking you anything, Mrs Weald. I want that clear." He is looking straight out. "I formed a view in 1957 and I have carried it since and you declined to confirm it on this verandah three years ago, and I have thought about your declining it a good deal more than I have thought about the view. I am not going to put you through it twice."

"Thank you."

"I will say the one thing." He turns the glass round once and stops. "Whatever was or was not the case, that man has been decent to me for five years without a single lapse, in a situation where nobody on earth would have blamed him for one, and I have never found a way of saying so that would not be an insult." He puts both hands on his knees. "And now he is going to be extremely happy, entirely without my help, and I find I am glad about it in a way that is nearly unmanageable, and there is no one at all I can say that to."

"You've just said it."

"Yes," says Alec Spilsbury, after a moment. "Yes, I suppose I have. That was badly done of me as well."

"No, it wasn't."

---

## The eighteenth, at dinner

It comes up because the Colonel asks whether Dr Sealy is coming on the Thursday, and Reggie — carving, cheerful, in front of nine people — says:

"She'll come on the Thursday. She comes every Thursday. It's the instrument."

"Is it," says Hester Bevan.

"It's the instrument and it's an hour of Pobil's time and she pays for neither, and she has been perfectly straight about it from the start, which I respect." He puts the plate down in front of Alec. "Pascual, I said a thing to you on a path in November and I'm going to say it once more and then I'll leave it alone for good. Sixteen months. It's a long time to be coming over on a boat about a condenser."

Nora does not look at anybody.

Dr Pobil says, "*Bueno*," and goes on with his dinner.

And Dr A. Spilsbury, Ph.D., of Vigo, puts his napkin down beside his plate, and says, "Forgive me," in a perfectly steady voice, and gets up and goes out onto the verandah, and does not come back for four minutes.

"Is he all right?" says the Colonel.

"He's got a cough," says Reggie. "He's had it since Tuesday."

---

## The twentieth

She sees the last of it from the door of the dark room and she is not supposed to see it at all.

The two of them are at the north rail at about six in the evening. Pascual has a bucket. Spilsbury has his hat in his hand. They have been doing the protocol for the old female for three days and by all accounts have not agreed about a single line of it.

And Spilsbury says something — four or five sentences, not long — and then stands there.

Pascual Pobil does not answer for a while.

Then he puts the bucket down on the concrete, which is the most that man has ever done in front of anybody, and says something back, about a sentence and a half of it, and puts his hand out.

And they shake hands at a rail at six o'clock in the evening over a bucket, which they have not done at a greeting or a parting in five years of greetings and partings, and then they pick the protocol back up and go on disagreeing about line four.

Nora goes back into the dark room and shuts the door and stands there in the red light for a minute with her hand flat on the bench.

She never finds out what was said. Neither of them ever mentions it. She is still thinking about it in 1990.

---

## The twenty-third

The boat is at half past nine and the whole station is on the jetty.

Hester gives him an envelope. John gives him a plate, unnumbered. Nora gives him the Oporto man's new address and he says "*Good*" with real pleasure. The Colonel says something about the roof.

And Pascual shakes his hand and says: "The old girl. February or March, I think, and not later."

"You will want somebody at that rail at seven in the morning who is not you."

"I know what I want. I have not got it."

"Then write to me in the autumn and I will tell you what we did at Vigo, which was also wrong, and you may do the opposite," says Spilsbury, and that is the whole of their goodbye, and both of them are entirely satisfied with it.

Then ninety seconds at the end of the jetty with everybody at the top of it being tactful.

---

## The first of August

"You've been odd all week," says John.

"I know."

"Is it a thing you can say?"

"It's a thing I can say," says Nora, "which is why I've been odd," and sits down on the step next to him.

So she tells him about the second chair and the early boat and two fingers on a sleeve for half a second, and John Weald listens to all of it with his elbows on his knees and does not interrupt once, and at the end of it he says:

"He'll be married inside a year."

"He'll be married inside a year."

"And Reggie will be the last man on this island to know."

"Reggie will be the last man in the *hemisphere* to know," says Nora, "and I have thought about it a great deal and I have decided it's the best thing about him."

John turns his head.

"Go on."

"He's had that man at four feet for nine years," she says, "and in nine years it has never once occurred to him to look at him and wonder what's going on in there, because he has never in his life thought of a person as a thing to be worked out." She flattens her hands on her knees. "It's how he got everything wrong for four years about Jean, and it's how he got it right in about nine seconds when she told him. He isn't watching anybody, John. He's just *waiting to be told*, and he'd believe anybody who told him anything, and I find I would not have him altered by a single degree."

John does not say anything for a bit.

"That's the kindest thing anybody's ever said about him."

"Don't tell him."

"I shan't tell him," says John. "I shall draw it."

---

The register reaches seven hundred and ninety-four on the Saturday.

And at the far bench there is a second chair which is not in the inventory, and a second lamp which is not in the inventory, and on the Thursday there will be a boat at ten past seven in the morning with nothing on the manifest to explain it; and none of that is going in any book in this building, and it is all perfectly true, and Nora Weald puts the cloth over the case and goes up to her supper.
