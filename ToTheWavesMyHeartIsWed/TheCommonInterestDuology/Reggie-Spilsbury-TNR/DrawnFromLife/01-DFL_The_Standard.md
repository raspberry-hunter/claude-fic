# The Standard

## The errand

The instruction is Reggie's and it takes about forty seconds on the jetty on the third of June.

"You are not going to be able to stop them screening it at a hundred and twenty."

"I know, sir."

"Then don't fight that. Fight the one that matters, which is the sequence, and if they set a single plate out of order I want to hear about it by cable." Reggie has his hands in his pockets. "You'll be in the room. That's the whole of it. Nobody has ever once been in the room."

"Yes, sir."

"And John." He is looking at the launch and not at John. "Come back."

---

Jean is going as far as London and no further, for the reading, with the paper in a case she has not let anybody else carry since Tuesday.

"Six weeks," she says on the deck. "You'll go soft."

"I shall not go soft."

"You'll go soft. There'll be pavements." She squares the case on her knees. "Your mother will feed you and you'll sleep in a bed with a wall round it and by August you'll have forgotten what the generator sounds like, and then you'll come back and it'll take you a fortnight to get right and you'll be unbearable about the light."

"I'm never unbearable about the light."

"John Weald," says Miss Leslie, "you are the most unbearable man about light that I have ever met, and it is the only thing about you that anybody could possibly dislike, and I'd not have it otherwise."

---

## Hitchin

His mother has painted the back room.

That is the first thing, and it takes him two days to work out why it bothers him: she has painted it a colour he would not have chosen and would not have been asked about, and she has done it in April, and it is *fine*, and he is twenty-seven years old and has been away for three years and has no business having an opinion about a back room in Hertfordshire.

On the second evening Barbara comes over with the baby and there is a pie, and at about nine o'clock, with the baby asleep upstairs and his mother collecting the plates, Barbara says:

"You're free on Saturday, aren't you."

"I'm at the printer's till one."

"After one, then."

"Barbara."

"There's a tea at the Coles'," says Barbara, "and Enid Roach will be there, and I'm telling you now so that you can't say afterwards that I sprung it on you."

"You have sprung it on me."

"I've sprung it on you with four days' notice, which is not springing." She picks up the salt. "You needn't do anything. You needn't say anything. You are to stand in a room and be spoken to, and if it is dreadful you may go home at half past five and I shall never mention it again."

His mother comes back in for the other plates and does not look at either of them, and does not say one word, and John understands, standing at his own mother's table with a fork in his hand, that this has been discussed.

---

Miss Enid Roach is twenty-four and has a very good face — a short upper lip and the eyes set rather wide, which he would draw in about four lines and would enjoy drawing — and at twenty past three she asks him what it is like out there, and he tells her, and at twenty-five past three he sees the exact moment it stops going in.

It is not her fault. He has got to the pens. He can hear himself getting to the pens.

He stops at twenty-nine minutes past, which he thinks is rather good, and asks her about the school where she teaches, and she tells him about it for a quarter of an hour, and it is interesting, and he listens properly, and at the end of it neither of them has anything else.

"You're very easy to talk at," she says, kindly.

"That's not the same thing."

"No," says Miss Roach. "It isn't."

---

There are two more.

Sylvia Barnes, on the eleventh, who is charming and quick and laughs at everything including three things that were not jokes, and who at one point puts her hand on his sleeve, and he notices only that she has a ring-guard on the wrong finger and wonders about it for the rest of the afternoon instead of listening.

And a Miss Pargeter, on the twentieth, at the Prentices', who is perfectly pleasant and who says, about halfway through, that she couldn't be doing with the heat; and who is right, and who is the only honest one of the three, and John thinks about her kindly for years afterwards.

Barbara says nothing at all on the way home until they are at the corner.

"It's not that you're shy."

"I am shy."

"You are shy in the way that a door is shut," says Barbara. "It opens. I have watched it open. What you are is *not interested*, and that's a different animal, and I can't do anything about it with a tea."

## The summer meeting

The paper is read on the twenty-fifth of June and takes fifty minutes, and J. E. Leslie reads it herself, standing up, in a room with about eighty people in it and a lantern at the back.

The plates go up one at a time, in order, as the record; there is a moment at the fourth one when the room changes, and John, who is standing at the side because he could not sit down, feels the change go across the back of his neck like weather.

In the discussion a man near the front says that he must congratulate Mrs Leslie on a very clear exposition.

Jean says, "Doctor, and the exposition is Mr Weald's; the plates are the paper and I have only written underneath them. Next question."

And then a thin man three rows back stands up and says that he would like to ask about plate nineteen — about the hatching on the ventral surface, and whether what he is looking at is a real change in the animal or a change in the draughtsman — and Jean says, "Mr Weald is here. Ask him."

Eighty people turn round.

John Weald stands in the aisle of a room in Regent's Park with his hands at his sides and hears himself say:

"It's real, sir. I changed my pen in November of 'fifty-five and you can see the join in the sequence at four hundred and one, but nineteen is after the change and so is the one before it, and the difference between them is nine days and the animal."

"How do you know it is the animal?"

"Because I drew the same square foot of her every other day for two years," says John, "and if I'd been doing it to her I'd have done it to the calf as well, and I didn't."

The thin man sits down and writes something.

Afterwards Jean finds him by the stairs with her case under her arm and her hat already on, because she is catching the six o'clock.

"Well," she says.

"I talked too fast."

"You answered the only question in the room that mattered and you answered it in about thirty words." She puts the case down and straightens his collar, which she has never done in five years and will never do again. "John. Six weeks. Go and be a person in a city."

"I'm going to a printer's."

"Yes," says Jean. "I did know that. Go on."

## Bowling Green Lane

Benfield & Coe do process work for four learned societies and have done since 1889, and the front office smells of ammonia and hot metal and the river, and Mr Ronald Benfield is a pleasant heavy man of about forty who calls him *young man* twice in the first minute.

The first pulls come on the second of July and they are good.

They are better than good. John takes them to the window and holds them up one at a time and the screen is finer than he was promised and the blacks are holding, and he says so, and Mr Benfield says that they pride themselves.

And then he gets to nineteen.

He looks at it for a long moment. He puts it down on the bench, and puts the original plate beside it, and looks again, and what he feels come up the back of his throat is not anything he has felt since the first of October last year.

"Somebody's been at this."

"At which?"

"Nineteen. The ventral hatching." His finger is on it and his hand is not steady. "There were sixty-one strokes on that surface and there are about forty now, and they've been *opened out* — look at it, look at the two together, somebody has sat down with a tool and taken a third of the information out of a primary record, and I should like to know which of your men did it, because I read that plate out to eighty people in Regent's Park on Tuesday and I stood up and said it was the animal."

The room has gone quiet in the way a workshop goes quiet.

"I did that," says a woman at the far bench.

She has not turned round. She is thirty-one or so, in a brown overall, with her sleeves buttoned at the wrist and a loupe pushed up into her hair, and she finishes the stroke she is making before she puts the tool down, which John notices at the time and thinks about for the rest of his life.

Then she turns round.

"Nora Benfield. And I took twenty strokes out of that surface because your original is at eighteen lines to the inch and this firm is screening at a hundred and twenty, and at a hundred and twenty your sixty-one strokes go to grey mud and the reader gets a smudge and no information at all. You'd have had forty-eight pounds of blocks and nothing on them."

"You altered the record."

"I preserved it," says Miss Benfield. "There's a difference and you of all people ought to know what it is."

"Nora," says her brother.

"It's plate *nineteen*, Ronnie."

---

She goes to the drawer and comes back with two proofs, and puts them on the bench side by side, and does not hand them to him.

"That one's yours untouched at a hundred and twenty. That one's mine."

He looks at them for about nine seconds.

The untouched one is mud. It is exactly mud; the whole ventral surface has gone to a flat grey with a smear where the pen crossed itself, and there is no ridge and no fan and no interval, and a man in Regent's Park looking at it would have seen nothing at all and would not have asked.

Hers has the ridge. Hers has the fan. Hers has the nine days in it.

"Ah," says John.

"Yes."

"I said *which of your men*."

"You did."

"That's the worse one," he says. "The other thing was wrong and I'll take it back, but that one was — that's the worse one, and I'd like to say so before you do, because I've had it said about me for two years and I know exactly what it costs and I have just gone and done it to somebody in their own workshop."

Miss Benfield looks at him.

"Sit down, Mr Weald," she says. "You've gone a colour."

---

## What she did with the sequence

It takes an hour and a half and he does not say more than forty words in it.

She has done the twenty-two plates over eleven working days and she has done them in the order they are numbered, because on the second day she worked out that they were a series and not twenty-two separate jobs; and she has kept a sheet of what she took out of each one and why, which she has not been asked for by anybody in nineteen years and which she keeps anyway.

She fetches it. It is ruled in pencil, four columns, in a small upright hand.

John reads it twice.

"You've matched the treatment across the sequence."

"Of course I have. If I open out nineteen and I don't open out twenty, then the difference between nineteen and twenty is me." She takes the sheet back and squares it on the bench. "And then your nine days are my Tuesday afternoon, and that's no use to anybody."

There is a silence of about four seconds.

"Nobody has ever said that to me," says John. "About my own work. In five years. Except one man, and he's a scientist, and he's the reason there's a sequence at all."

"Then he was right and you may tell him I said so."

"I shall tell him you said so and he will be insufferable about it."

And Nora Benfield laughs — once, short, down in the chest — and goes back to her bench, and John Weald sits on a stool in a process shop in Clerkenwell at half past eleven on a Tuesday morning and does not want to be anywhere else, and is perfectly clear-headed about it, and is also extremely frightened.

---

## The line at the foot

He writes to the Institute on the fourth and it is four lines, because he learned that from somebody.

*Dear Dr Tar — The blocks are excellent and the sequence will stand. The retoucher has matched her treatment across all twenty-two and has kept a written record of every stroke removed, which I enclose a copy of. I should like her name printed. J. Weald.*

The reply comes on the nineteenth and is one line and is not from Reggie.

*Mr Weald — Tell them the Institute requires it. H. Bevan.*

He tells them. Mr Benfield says it is not done; John says that the plate sequence running as the primary record of a paper is also not done, and that it is being done in November, and that he would rather not have to write to the Society about a second unusual thing but that he will.

At the foot of the plate list in the printed paper, in six-point, there is a line that no journal in London has ever carried.

*Blocks by N. Benfield, Benfield & Coe, London.*

She looks at it for rather a long time when it comes, in a way he recognises, because it is what he did with a title page in 1954.

---

## The cay

He does it on the seventh of August, on a bench on Clerkenwell Green, badly.

What he has brought is the portfolio, and what he says first is: "I'm going to show you the place and I'm going to show you the bad parts first, because otherwise it isn't honest and you'd find out in October."

"That's a very odd way to begin a thing."

"It's the only way I know how to begin it."

So he shows her the bad parts.

The generator, which runs from six in the morning until ten at night and which you hear in your teeth for the first month. The hut, in section, with the measurements on it: nine feet by eleven, one bed, one press, one table, one looking-glass, cracked. The rain in September, which he has drawn twice because the first one was a lie. Juan's brother killing a rat behind the fish house. The mosquitoes, which he has drawn at twelve diameters, because he cannot help himself.

The mail, which comes on a Wednesday and sometimes does not.

The fact that there is one doctor and she is at the Bay and it is four hours in a boat.

The fact that there is no press, no trade, no society, nobody within four hundred miles who knows what a stipple tool is, and that if the firm here goes to Pettifer's in the spring, as her brother has agreed and has not told her, then what is out there instead is five hundred and forty plates in four boxes and a sequence with no gaps in it since April 1956 and a Department that has written down in a file that the collection ought to be deposited to a standard, and nobody on that island who can do it.

"How do you know about Pettifer's?"

"Your brother told me on the second of July," says John, "in the front office, because he thought I was somebody who wouldn't mind."

Nora Benfield sits with her hands in her lap on a bench on Clerkenwell Green.

"Go on, then," she says. "The good parts."

---

The good parts take twenty minutes and he does not hurry them.

At the end of it she says: "Those two men in the corner of the fire-pump drawing."

"That's not in the book. That's a private one."

"I didn't ask what it was. I asked who they are." She has it in both hands. "Because the rest of the sheet is a joke and that corner isn't, and whoever drew it was very fond of both of them and knew something he wasn't saying."

John looks at the gravel for about six seconds.

"That's the Director of Research," he says, "and that's the veterinary officer, and they are the two best men I will ever work for, and I'm not going to say anything else about them, ever, to anybody, and I'd like you to know that about me now rather than later."

"Yes," says Nora. "I'd rather worked that out."

---

## Terms

She has four questions and she asks them in order and she has plainly written them down somewhere, which he finds almost unbearable.

Is there a post, or is there only a husband. — There is a post. Mrs Bevan will make one and it will be in the minute book and it will have a salary in it, and he has already asked, and the answer came in nine days.

Whose name goes on the collection. — Hers, on everything she does, in the accession record, in her own hand.

What happens if she hates it. — Then she says so, and they go, and he means it, and he has thought about what it would cost and has decided he does not care what it costs.

And the fourth she does not ask for a while.

"Mr Weald. Why me."

"Because you finished the stroke before you put the tool down," says John. "Because you kept a sheet nobody asked you for. Because you took twenty strokes out of my plate and could tell me the reason for every one of them, and because you'd read the sequence by the second day when there are four men in London with letters after their names who read that paper in June and thought the pictures were illustration."

"That's the work."

"It isn't only the work."

"No," says Nora. "It isn't. Say the other part, then, since we're doing it on a bench."

And John Weald, who has been hopeless at three teas in four weeks and has never in his life been able to start anything, finds that he is not shy at all, which surprises him considerably.

"Because I have looked at you for six weeks," he says, "which is what I do instead of talking, and I have got the way your hand goes up to the loupe and the way you stand at the light and the thing your mouth does when somebody says *young man*, and I have not drawn any of it, not once, not on a corner of anything — and I have never in my life *not* drawn a thing I wanted to look at."

"Why haven't you?"

"Because I'd have had to ask you to sit for me, and then it would have been about a drawing," says John, "and I didn't want it to be about a drawing."

---

It is Clerkenwell Green at about half past four in the afternoon and there are two clerks and a boy with a barrow at the other end of it.

Nora takes the portfolio off his knees and closes it, carefully, and ties the tape, because it is a working portfolio and she is a working woman and neither of them is going to leave plates open on a bench.

Then she puts her hand along his jaw, which he does not expect at all, and he hears himself make a small sound.

"Your hands are cold," he says.

"It's the ammonia. They're always cold." Her thumb is at the corner of his mouth. "Is this all right?"

"It's —"

"John."

"Yes," he says. "God. Yes."

She kisses him with one hand cold on his jaw and the other flat on his chest where she can feel what his heart is doing, and she keeps it there afterwards, and does not remark on it, which is the kindest thing anybody has ever done to him.

He has his eyes shut. He has both arms round her and his face in her hair, which smells of ammonia and hot metal and the river, and which will smell of those things in his memory for fifty years, and he is thinking, with the one part of his head that is still running:

*I shall never draw this. Not once. There will be no plate of this and no number on it and it will not be in the book.*

"Say something," she says, into his collar.

"There's a boy with a barrow looking at us."

"Let him look," says Nora Benfield. "He's not in the sequence either."

## Hitchin, the fourteenth of September

His mother takes it very quietly, which he expects, and then goes out to the scullery for longer than the kettle requires, which he does not.

When she comes back she has her face in order.

"Barbara will say she did it."

"Barbara will say she did it for the rest of her life and I shall let her."

"Yes," says his mother. "Let her." She puts the pot down and sits, and puts her hands flat on the cloth. "She's older than you."

"Four years."

"And she'll work out there? At her trade?"

"At her trade, with her name on it, and I've got that in writing from a woman who has never once written down a thing she didn't mean."

His mother nods at the tablecloth for a while.

"Your father used to say you'd be all right as long as somebody found you," she says, "and I've thought about that a great deal these three years, and I never could see how anybody was going to, out there, with the fish."

"Nobody found me," says John. "I found her. I made rather a mess of the first part."

"Well," says his mother, and pours the tea. "You would."

---

They sail on the twenty-eighth. There are two cases, a crate of books, a portfolio, and a japanned tin box with four stipple tools, three scrapers and a loupe in it, which does not leave her hand from Hitchin to the jetty.
