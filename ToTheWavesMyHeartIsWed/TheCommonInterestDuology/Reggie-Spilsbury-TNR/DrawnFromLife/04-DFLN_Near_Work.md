# Near Work

## The twelfth of August 1959

She notices it on a Wednesday afternoon with plate 611 under the loupe and the light good.

She has been at it forty minutes, which is nothing — she has done four hours at a stretch since she was seventeen — and the edge of the stroke will not hold. It sits, and then it softens, and she blinks and it comes back and sits for a bit less than it did before.

She puts the tool down and looks out of the window at the water for a minute and then goes back, and it is fine for about twenty minutes, and then it is not.

She does not mention it to anybody. It is August; it is hot; the lamp is filthy; she cleans the lamp.

---

## The gap

Dr Pobil is standing at the end of the long room with the feed book open, not writing in it, and he has been standing there for rather a long time.

Nora is at the far bench and does not say anything, because she has learned that in this building you let a man finish.

He turns three pages back. Then four more. Then he puts his finger on a line and keeps it there, and does not move for about fifteen seconds, and then he lets out a breath that has more in it than a breath.

"Mrs Weald."

"Dr Pobil."

"What is today."

"The ninth of September."

"The ninth of September." He closes the book on his finger. "Do you know when I last went down to the pens at an hour when there was no round due? Not for the animals. Not because anything was wrong."

She does know, more or less, and she is not going to say so.

"No."

"The twenty-second of March," says Pascual Pobil. "I have checked it three ways. There is nothing in the book, there is nothing in the sheets, and I remember the night, because it rained." He puts the book down on the bench very carefully, the way you put down something that is not yours. "Five months and two weeks. And I did not notice it going."

He stands there.

"*Ay*," he says, to nobody in particular.

And Nora Weald, who has been carrying this since February of last year and has never said one word of it to her husband, puts her elbows on the bench and does not go to him and does not say anything kind, because he has not asked for anything kind and it would be the worst thing available.

"That's a long time to not notice something," she says.

"It is a very long time."

"Is it a good book?"

"It is an excellent book," says Pascual. "It has never once been wrong in eight years and I am extremely annoyed with it."

Then he picks it up and goes down to do the five o'clock, which is due.

---

They never discuss it again. Not once, not obliquely, not in eleven years.

But he says, at the door, without turning round: "You have known since about the spring of 'fifty-eight."

"February."

"February." A pause of about two seconds. "*Bueno.* Thank you for the two years, Mrs Weald."

---

## The autumn

Here is what nobody warns you about, and what Nora gets to watch at four feet for about four months: that it is worse afterwards.

Not sad. Worse in a more practical way. A man who has organised eleven hours a day of his life around being reliably in the same room as somebody, for six years, and has done it so well that nobody ever saw the scaffolding, takes the scaffolding down and finds he is standing in a great deal of space.

He is at a loose end at seven in the evening for the first time since 1953. He does not know what to do with a Sunday. He is, for about six weeks in October and November, mildly and continuously irritable in a way that the entire station notices and puts down to the licence renewal.

Reggie puts it down to the licence renewal.

Reggie puts it down to the licence renewal so thoroughly that in the third week of November he corners Nora in the dark room doorway with his glasses in his hand and says, with real concern and in complete good faith, "Mrs Weald, has Pobil said anything to you about the schedule? He's been off since the autumn and he won't have it from me."

"He's all right."

"He is not all right. I have known that man for seven years and he is —" Reggie makes a gesture with the spectacles that means nothing at all — "*flat*. He was flat all through October. He was flat at the Colonel's birthday."

"Dr Tar."

"Yes?"

"Ask him something about the calf protocol," says Nora. "Not about how he is. About the protocol, and then argue with him for an hour."

Reggie looks at her for a second and then says, "Right," and goes away and does exactly that, and it works, and he never once asks her why she knew that.

---

She tells John nothing. John, who has been doing this since May 1954, asks her nothing.

But in December he comes back from the pens and says, "Pobil's come right," in the tone of a man reporting a weight, and she says, "Yes," and that is the entire conversation, and she understands later that it was also the whole of it.

---

## Jean

Jean Leslie leaves on the fourth of February 1960.

It is Bangor, in the end, and it is a group of her own and two students and a laboratory the size of the long room, and the letter that did it came in the October and was the eleventh in the folder, and Nora is the second person in the building to be told and the first to be told the salary.

"It's not as much as the Naples one."

"It is four hundred miles from a decent tide and it has a man over me who published a wrong table in 1951 and has never withdrawn it," says Jean, "and they wrote to J. E. Leslie about the salinity series in August of 'fifty-seven and they have gone on writing for two and a half years, and Nora, they *asked*."

"Yes."

"You told me that on the boat and I was extremely rude about it."

"You were."

"Well," says Jean Leslie, and then does not say anything else for a moment. "I shall write to you about paper. Nobody else out here will care about paper."

The whole station is on the jetty, because the Colonel likes a thing done properly. Reggie shakes her hand and then does not let go of it and says four words that nobody else hears. Hester gives her an envelope. John gives her a plate, unnumbered, and goes scarlet.

Nora cries about it on the path afterwards, which surprises her considerably.

---

## The Bay, the twenty-eighth of March

The dog comes up from the Bay side on a Monday and the whole thing takes two days and Nora is not there for any of it.

What she gets is the shape.

A child of seven, bitten on the forearm on the Saturday. An animal that went off and was found dead on the Sunday and about which two people gave two accounts. And a district medical officer with a course of Semple vaccine in a cold box and a decision to make about whether to put fourteen injections into a seven-year-old, which in 1960 is not a small thing to decide and is not a small thing to decline either.

Dr Pobil is sent for because he is the only man within four hundred miles who can open a head properly, and he goes over on the Monday afternoon and does not come back until Tuesday night.

---

He comes up the path at about nine with his case and he is grey with it, and Nora is on the verandah with the lamp because she is waiting for John.

"Did you get it?"

"I got it." He sits down on the top step, which he has never done. "Negative. There was nothing in the brain, and I looked at eleven sections and I looked at them twice, and the child does not have the course."

"That's good."

"That is very good and I am going to feel it in about four hours." He puts the case down beside him. "The doctor over there wanted me to start at seven o'clock on Monday evening on the sections I had at seven o'clock on Monday evening."

"And you wouldn't."

"I would not, because at seven o'clock on Monday evening I had four sections from one hemisphere and a fixation that was nine hours old, and if I had said *negative* on that and been wrong, that child dies in about six weeks and nothing on this earth would have stopped it." He is looking at the boards. "So I said I would have an answer at four in the afternoon on Tuesday and not before, and she said — she is a very direct woman, Mrs Weald — she said that she had a child in front of her and I had a piece of tissue in a jar, and that she would like me to remember the difference."

"That's hard."

"It is not hard, it is correct," says Pascual. "She was correct and I was also correct and we could not both act, and she gave way because it was my material, and she was in that surgery for twenty-one hours with the mother while I was at a bench being right slowly." He rubs his face with both hands. "And at four o'clock I gave her the answer and she said *thank you, Dr Pobil*, in a voice you could have kept fish in, and I do not believe she will ever think much of me, and she should not."

Nora says, "What's her name?"

"Dr Sealy," says Pascual. "Marguerite Sealy. She has run that district on her own for eleven years and I have never heard of her, which tells you a good deal about this Institute."

And he picks the case up and goes to bed.

---

## What she has, by June

It starts as nothing and Nora would swear in a court that she was not looking.

That there are four trips to the Bay between March and June and that the reasons for all four are good and that in the previous eighteen months there were two.

That he has had his hair cut by somebody other than Juan.

That in May he asks her, at the bench, apropos of nothing whatsoever, what one writes a proper letter on, and whether the Institute has anything that is not the flimsy; and that she gives him four sheets of her own laid and does not say one word about it, and he says "*Bueno*" and goes away, and she puts her head down on the bench for a moment afterwards.

That he has started arguing less and asking more, which in that man is a symptom.

That he is *nervous*, which she has not seen in three years — not at the inspection, not at the licence, not with a dead animal on a slab and a magistrate coming.

And that on the ninth of June he takes a box of slides to the Bay on a Thursday for no reason that appears anywhere in any book in this building, and comes back on the last boat, and does not mention it at dinner.

---

She does not say anything to anybody, obviously.

She does, once, in July, watch Reggie Tar look straight at the man putting on a clean shirt at four in the afternoon on a weekday and say, "You're never going over there again, what is it now, the licence?" — and get "the licence" for an answer — and go back to his tide gauge entirely satisfied.

Nora goes into the dark room and shuts the door and laughs until her eyes run.

---

## The ninth of September

She goes to the Bay because of the plates, in the end, and because she has been going round it since August of last year and it has stopped being possible to go round.

She can do about twenty-five minutes now. Then the edge softens and it does not come back properly until she has been off it an hour, and in July she put a stroke on 634 that she had to take off again, which has happened to her four times in twenty-two years and twice of them were this summer.

She tells John on the Tuesday, because she has had a rule since 1958 about what she keeps from him and this is not one of the things covered.

"Right," he says. "Thursday boat."

"It might be nothing."

"It might be nothing and you've been holding the loupe up with your other hand since about May," says John Weald, "which you have never once done in three years, and I have been waiting for you to say so since the fourteenth of June, and I am coming with you as far as the harbour and no further because you would hate it."

---

The district surgery at the Bay is two rooms and a bench outside with nine people on it and a clerk who takes your name.

Dr Marguerite Sealy is about forty-five, with grey coming in at the front, and she has a way of walking that has emptied a good many waiting rooms, and when Nora comes in she does not look up for about four seconds and then does, and the look goes over her once, top to bottom, in the way Nora herself looks at a case that has come in off a boat.

"Mrs Weald. From the Institute."

"Yes."

"Sit down and tell me what you do all day, and don't leave anything out because you think it isn't medical."

So Nora tells her what she does all day.

---

It takes fifty minutes, which she is later given to understand is about four times the district average, and there is a great deal more of it than she expected: the lamp, the light, the drops, the card at arm's length and then at eighteen inches, a trial frame and a box of lenses that live in a cabinet and have plainly not been out since Easter.

And a considerable amount of attention paid to things that are not her eyes at all, which she does not understand until the end.

"Right," says Dr Sealy at last, and sits back. "I am going to tell you what you have not got, first, because that is what you came for and you have been too proud to say so."

"I've not said —"

"Mrs Weald, you are thirty-four years old and you have come four hours in a boat about a symptom you have had for thirteen months. You did not come for lenses. You came to be told it is not the other thing." She caps her pen. "It is not the other thing. There is nothing in either of them. The discs are clean, the fields are clean, you have no pressure that I can detect with what I have got here, and whatever you have been rehearsing at four in the morning since the summer, you may stop."

Nora sits in a wooden chair in a district surgery and discovers that her hands have gone unreliable.

"Take a minute," says Dr Sealy, and writes something, and does not watch her do it.

---

"What you have got is nineteen years of near work and a pair of eyes that have started charging you for it, and they will go on charging you, and it is not a disease and there is no treatment and you are going to be doing fine work at sixty."

"Not the way I do it now."

"No," says Dr Sealy. "Not the way you do it now, and I am not going to pretend otherwise, because you have been straight with me for fifty minutes and I dislike being lied to in either direction." She turns the pad round. "Here is what you do. You go to Kingston, to this man, and you do not let him refract you to a chart. Charts are at six metres and you do not work at six metres. You take your loupe, and your tool, and a plate, and you make him do it at *your* distance, and if he tells you that is not how it is done you tell him it is how it is done for you and you give him my name."

"Lenses made to my working distance."

"Made to your working distance. Two pairs, because you will break one." Dr Sealy taps the pad. "And you halve the sitting. Twenty-five minutes and then you get up and look at the horizon for five, and you will hate it in October and by March you will be doing more work in a day than you are doing now, which is the part nobody believes until they try it."

"That's very good advice."

"It is ordinary advice, it is simply that nobody gives it to women who work with their hands, because nobody asks them what they do all day." She caps the pen again. "One more thing and then you may go."

"Go on."

"You are thirty-four, you have been married since 1957, and there is nothing in the history you have given me about children, which in my experience means either that there is nothing to say or that there is a great deal."

Nora looks at the window for a moment.

"We've not prevented anything since the wedding," she says, "and nothing has happened, and I stopped counting the months in about the spring of last year because the counting was worse than the thing."

"Yes."

"And I have got a trade I am good at, and a husband I would not swap for a child if somebody put it to me in those words, and I would like one, and I have decided I am not going to spend the next ten years waiting at a window." She puts her hands flat on her knees. "That is the whole of it and I have never said it out loud."

Dr Marguerite Sealy looks at her for a moment.

"Then I shall not make you say it again," she says. "There is a good deal I do not know and cannot find out with what is in this building, and some of it could be looked at in Kingston and some of it could not be looked at anywhere by anybody, and if you ever want that conversation you may have it from me in an hour at any time and I shall not raise it with you otherwise. Does that do?"

"That'll do."

"Good," says Dr Sealy, and stands up, and then, at the door, with her hand on it: "And Mrs Weald — you may tell the Institute that I have a laboratory bench with nothing on it and a microscope that I am told is better than the one they have."

---

## On the step

It is the last thing said and Nora thinks about it for the whole four hours home.

Because it was not said the way the rest of it was said. It came out at the door with her hand on it, faster than the rest, and Dr Marguerite Sealy — who has run a district on her own for eleven years and who does not, as far as Nora can tell, do anything by accident — had gone very slightly pink across the cheekbones.

And Nora had said, "I'll tell them," and had not asked which of them, and had got out onto the bench in the sun with nine people on it and stood there.

*Well,* she thinks, on the boat, with the water going by and the pad in her bag.

*Fourteen months, is it.*

---

She gives Reggie the message about the microscope at dinner on the Friday, straight, with the bench and the laboratory and all of it, and watches the table.

Reggie says, "That's a good offer, actually, we've nothing east of here at all," and goes on eating.

The Colonel says something about the Bay road. Hester writes it down.

And at the other end of the long table Dr Pascual Pobil puts his fork down and picks it up again, and says nothing whatsoever, and does not look at anybody, and goes on with his dinner; and Nora Weald butters a piece of bread and thinks that there is not one single column in any book in this building for what she has just watched, and that this time she does not mind in the least.
