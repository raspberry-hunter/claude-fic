# In Duplicate

## Kingston

The man's name is Mr Fyffe and he has a shop on Harbour Street with a brass plate and he does not want to do it.

"Madam, the refraction is taken at six metres. That is the standard."

"I know it's the standard. I don't work at six metres."

"Nobody works at six metres. That is not what a refraction is *for* —"

"Mr Fyffe," says Nora, and puts the japanned box on his counter and opens it, "that is a stipple tool, and that is a scraper, and this is a plate off a paper that was read at the Zoological Society in 1957, and the distance between my eye and that surface when I am working is nine and a quarter inches, and it has been nine and a quarter inches since 1941, and I should like my lenses made for nine and a quarter inches and I am perfectly happy to pay twice."

There is a pause.

"Nine and a quarter," says Mr Fyffe.

"With the loupe in. I've brought the loupe."

And Mr Fyffe of Harbour Street, who is sixty and a tradesman and has been standing behind that counter for thirty-four years being told what the standard is by people who do not work with their hands, looks at the plate for rather a long time and then goes and locks the front door and turns the sign round.

"Sit down, Mrs Weald," he says. "This is going to take us two hours and I am going to enjoy it."

---

They stay three nights because the boats are what they are, in a hotel with a ceiling fan and a bathroom along the corridor and sheets that somebody else has to wash.

John is peculiar about it for the first evening — he does not know what to do in a room with nothing in it to keep — and then gets over it at about nine o'clock and is, for two and a half days, a man on a holiday, which Nora has never once seen and had not known was available.

They eat things neither of them cooked. They walk up to the Institute of Jamaica and he is rude, quietly and at length, about the mounting of everything in it. On the second afternoon it rains so hard that the street runs and they stand in a doorway on Duke Street with her back against his front and his chin on the top of her head and do not say anything for about twenty minutes.

"I could get used to a bathroom," she says.

"You could not."

"I could get used to a bathroom for about nine days."

"Nine days," John agrees. "And then you'd want to know what the light was doing at the cay and whether Hendy had been in the dark room, and you'd be unbearable on the boat."

On the last night the fan does not work and they leave the shutters open onto the street, and there is nobody in the corridor and nobody on the path and no generator, and it is the first time in three years they have been somewhere nobody can hear them, and they make rather free with it.

"You're laughing."

"I'm not laughing."

"You are laughing, I can feel you —"

"I'm laughing," says Nora, "because there's a *tram*," and puts her hand over her own mouth, and John Weald puts his face in her shoulder and shakes.

---

## The fourteenth of October

The lenses come up on the Wednesday boat in two cases with a bill that would have kept Bowling Green Lane in gold size for a year, and she takes them into the long room and shuts the door and does not let anybody in for fifty minutes.

Then she comes out onto the path.

"Well?" says John, who has been sitting on the step outside doing nothing at all for fifty minutes and pretending to sharpen something.

"Forty minutes," she says. "Clean through. I stopped because I said I'd stop."

And he gets up off the step and takes hold of her with both arms in the middle of the path at half past four in the afternoon, and Juan goes past with a bucket and says nothing, and Hester, who is at the office window and has been watching the door since four, writes something in a book and does not say what.

---

## The arrangements

Dr Sealy is asked to the Institute for the Saturday, on Institute paper, over Hester's signature, which is Pascual's doing and which took him, Nora estimates, about two months to arrange in such a way that it did not look arranged.

Reggie is told a fortnight in advance and is delighted, because there is a bench and a microscope in it.

And Pascual dreads it for a fortnight and says so twice, both times to Reggie, in the tone of a man who has thought about it properly and got an answer he does not like:

"You will be clever at her. And she will think you are laughing at her."

"I shall be nothing of the kind."

"You will be clever at her within four minutes, *hombre*, you cannot help it, and she has had eleven years of men being clever at her across a desk, and she will put you in a drawer with the rest of them and she will be perfectly polite about it for the whole afternoon."

"Pascual."

"And she will not come back," says Pascual, and goes out, and Reggie stands in the long room with his glasses in his hand looking as though somebody has handed him an instrument he cannot read.

---

## The nineteenth of November

She comes up the path at ten past three in a straw hat at a walking pace that Nora recognises instantly, because she has sat on that bench at the Bay.

Pascual meets her halfway down and brings her up, and there are two chairs on the verandah and a jug of lemonade and Hester has put out the good glasses, and Nora is at the far end of the boards with a case of plates on her knees, being furniture, which she has got extremely good at.

Reggie stands up.

Dr Marguerite Sealy puts her bag down by the chair leg and her hat on top of the bag and looks at him over the top of the glass.

"You are the one he calls Reggie."

"I am."

"Then I have a question, and he will not answer it."

---

Nora watches the next four minutes with the case of plates going soft on her knees.

Because it is not an introduction. It is a woman opening a file.

She does not ask him about the Institute, or the animals, or the paper, or any of the nine things a person asks a Director of Research on a verandah. She asks him whether a man who strapped his own open hand for five days and came in at a hundred and one and said he had been busy is a man who does that *every time*; and Reggie says "Every time" without a half-second in it; and she says "Thank you," and Nora sees the whole shape of it arrive at once.

*Eight months,* she thinks. *She has been carrying that since March and there has been nobody to ask.*

And Reggie — who has been warned twice, who has spent a fortnight deciding not to be clever — is not clever at all. He simply opens the drawer.

"He did it in 'fifty-four with a torn calf and worked nine days on it, and he did it in 'fifty-seven with a tooth for six weeks, and when I sent the boat over for a doctor in 'fifty-nine he went down to the jetty in his dressing gown and told the boy to turn round in the channel."

"*Ay*, that is not —"

"Be quiet, Pascual. We're consulting."

And Dr Marguerite Sealy puts the glass down on the boards and laughs — once, short, from somewhere well down in her chest — and says:

"Go on then, Dr Tar. From the beginning, and take your time. I have the whole afternoon and I have been waiting eight months for somebody who has the notes."

---

It runs an hour and twenty minutes.

Pascual Pobil stands at the rail with his arms folded and his ears scarlet for the whole of it and does not leave, and does not stop them, and — this is the part Nora would put in a column if there were one — does not for one second want them stopped.

At about half past four Reggie gets up to fetch the '57 file because he cannot describe the tooth without the dates, and Nora looks up from her case of plates and finds that Dr Sealy is looking straight at her.

"Mrs Weald."

"Dr Sealy."

"Forty minutes?"

"Forty-five now. Fifty on a good lamp."

"And the man in Kingston did it at your distance?"

"He locked the front door and turned the sign round."

"*Good,*" says Marguerite Sealy, with enormous satisfaction, and picks her glass back up.

---

## Ten minutes

They get them at about six, when the men have gone down to the pens to show her the old female and Hester has gone in about the supper and the two of them are left on the boards with the light going orange.

They talk about the lenses for four minutes, properly, because both of them would rather.

Then Dr Sealy says: "May I be improper."

"Everybody on this island keeps asking me that."

"It is a small island." She turns the glass round. "I have had eight months of a man on the other side of a channel about whom I have no information at all, and this afternoon I have had an hour and twenty minutes of a Director of Research who is very good on dates and entirely useless on the only question I actually have, and you have been sitting at the end of these boards the whole time, and you are the one who looks at things."

"That's not a question."

"No," says Marguerite Sealy. "Here is the question. In March I had a child in front of me and he had a piece of tissue in a jar, and I told him so, and he would not be hurried, and he was right and I was right and only one of us could act, and I gave way because the material was his. And I have been at that for eight months, Mrs Weald, and I cannot get it to come out, because both of those are true and I do not know how to be a person who thinks both of them at once."

Nora sits with that for a second.

And she does the thing she always does, which is to consider the material before she puts a mark on it — what is hers to say, what is not, and who it belongs to.

And she decides that what she has is about Dr Sealy and not about Dr Pobil, and that she is therefore at liberty.

"He came up that path on the Tuesday night at about nine," she says, "and sat down on the top step, which he has never done in eight years, and he told me the answer was negative and then he told me why he had refused you. And then he said —" she gets it right, because she has a good memory for a line — "that you were correct and he was also correct and you could not both act, and that you gave way because it was his material. And that you had been in that surgery for twenty-one hours with the mother while he was at a bench being right slowly."

The light on the boards has gone the colour of weak tea.

"He said *twenty-one hours*."

"He said twenty-one hours."

"How did he know it was twenty-one hours?"

"I should think," says Nora, "because he asked somebody, and wrote it down, and has not mentioned it to a living soul in eight months except a woman with a case of plates on her knees who happened to be on the verandah."

Dr Marguerite Sealy does not say anything at all for about six seconds.

Then she says, "Ah," in a completely different voice, and puts her hand flat on the arm of the chair, and looks out at the water for a while.

"That is not an answer to my question."

"No," Nora agrees. "It's only a fact that was going spare."

---

They do not say one further word about him, either of them, ever.

What they do instead is spend twenty minutes on Mr Fyffe's trial frame and the light in the district surgery and whether the Institute's microscope is in fact better than Dr Sealy's, which it is not, and about which Dr Sealy is extremely pleased.

And then, at the top of the path, with the bag in her hand:

"There is a thing I am not going to ask you about, Mrs Weald, and I want you to know that I am not asking it on purpose, and that the hour remains available at any time."

"I know it does."

"Good," says Marguerite Sealy, and goes down to the boat.

---

## After

They stand on the jetty at seven o'clock, the whole station, because the Colonel likes a thing done properly, and the launch goes off towards the Bay with a straw hat in the stern.

Reggie is in tremendous spirits. He has had an hour and twenty minutes of being asked questions by somebody who wanted the answers, which is the single condition under which that man is happiest, and he does not appear to have noticed one other thing that happened all afternoon.

He says, on the path afterwards, in the voice of a man delivering a kindness:

"Pascual, I'm going to say this once and then I shall leave it. She is not having you."

"Is that so."

"That is a woman who has run a district on her own for eleven years and has no intention of running anything else, and I watched her for four hours today, and she gave you nothing. Not one inch. In eight months." He puts a hand on the man's shoulder, briefly, which he does about twice a year. "I'd rather you heard it from me on a path than worked it out in the spring."

"*Bueno*," says Pascual Pobil.

"You're not offended."

"I am not offended in the slightest," says Pascual, "and I should like it on the record that you have just told me I have no chance in exactly the voice you use for a wrong table," and goes off down to the pens, and does the six o'clock, which is due.

---

Nora walks up to the west hut and finds John on the step.

"Well?" he says.

"He's got it about eight months wrong," says Nora Weald, "and he'll go on having it wrong for about two years, and he will tell everybody, and he will be so pleased with himself."

"Should somebody tell him?"

"No," she says, and sits down beside him, and takes his hand and puts it in her lap and keeps it there. "Because it isn't ours, and because in about 1962 something is going to happen at the Bay that he has not budgeted for, and I intend to be sitting somewhere I can see his face."

---

## The twenty-sixth

The register reaches six hundred and eighty-one on the Saturday, and she does the week's entries in one sitting, in forty-five clean minutes, at nine and a quarter inches, with the good lamp.

At the back, on the page headed *Conditions of storage*, under the flood entry from 1958 and the note about the Oporto paper, she rules a new line and writes:

*14.x.60 — Lenses, two pairs, made to working distance 9¼ in. with loupe (Fyffe, Harbour St, Kingston). Sittings reduced to 45 min. Output restored. N. Benfield.*

It is accurate, it is complete, and nobody reading it in 1990 will know anything whatever about a hotel room with the shutters open, or a tram, or a man sitting on a step outside a closed door for fifty minutes pretending to sharpen something.

Which is, she has come to think, the correct way round.
