# Noted as Such

## What she was told in England

John told her about Dr Tar and Dr Spilsbury on the eighth of August 1957, on the bench on Clerkenwell Green, in about nine words, while she was looking at a drawing of a man at a bench with a shade over the lamp.

"That's Dr Spilsbury. He was here ten months. He's gone now."

"Gone where?"

"Nobody knows and nobody asks." John turned the sheet over. "And the Director of Research put him on that boat in March and has been about eighty per cent of a man ever since, and that is the last thing I am going to say about it to anybody for the rest of my life."

So she arrived in October knowing.

What she did not arrive knowing, and what takes her from Christmas until the sixth of February to get hold of, is the other one.

---

## The sixth of February

It is not a look. She wants that on the record, if only her own, because she has read novels and she came out here half expecting to catch somebody at a rail with his heart in his face, and it is nothing whatever like that.

It is the spectacles.

Dr Tar takes them off about forty times a day, because he cannot think and see at the same time, and he holds them in his left hand and gestures with them, and they get fogged and salted and thumbed, and he does not clean them; he simply goes on holding them and squinting.

And about half the time, without either of them stopping talking, Dr Pobil takes them out of his hand, does them on his shirt tail, and puts them back into it.

Nora has watched this happen perhaps twenty times since October and has never once thought anything about it at all.

On the sixth of February she is at the far bench and she sees Dr Pobil do it while Reggie is halfway through a sentence about a tide gauge — and she sees that Reggie does not break the sentence, does not look down, does not say thank you, and *does not notice*, because the spectacles arrive back in his hand as reliably as gravity and have done for four years.

And she sees the other man's hands afterwards.

He puts them flat on the bench, one and then the other, and leaves them there for about two seconds before he picks the slide up.

Nora Weald stands at the far bench with a scalpel in her hand and thinks, with complete and unwelcome clarity: *oh, you poor man.*

---

## What she has, by March

She does not go looking. She would like that recorded too. It simply runs, the way it has run since she was fifteen, and by the middle of March she has a list and she wishes she had not.

That the evening round used to be at six and went to five in 1955, which is when the water samples moved to five; and that nobody asked for that and nobody minutes the rounds.

That there are two cups above the long-room sink and one of them is chipped and it is not Dr Pobil's, and he washes both.

That in a room of three he is *Reggie* and in a room of six he is *Dr Tar*, and that this is Dr Pobil's line and not Dr Tar's, and it is drawn in exactly the place a man would draw it who had thought about it once, carefully, some years ago, and never revisited it.

That when the Director of Research talks about Dr Spilsbury — which he does about twice a week, unguardedly, at the bench, to the one person on this station he is certain of — the veterinary officer puts down whatever is in his hand and gives him the whole of his attention and asks a question, every time, without fail, and the questions are good ones.

That he has been doing that for a year.

And that when it is over he goes down to the pens, at whatever hour it is, and does a round that is not due.

---

## What it is not

It is not moping. She wants that straight, because she spent about three weeks getting it wrong.

Pascual Pobil is forty-five years old, he runs the only licensed drug cupboard within four hundred miles, he has taken a station through an inspection and a hearing, he eats well, he sleeps, he is extremely funny about the Colonel, and he argues with Reggie on the verandah in front of everybody roughly once a month and wins about half.

What he is, as far as Nora can tell, is *organised*.

He has arranged a life in which he is reliably within forty feet of a particular man for about fourteen hours a day, in which he is the person that man says things to first, and in which nothing whatever is ever asked for; and he has been running it, without a single visible failure, for four or five years; and it is the most sustained piece of craftsmanship she has seen since she came here, and she is including her own cases.

It is also, she thinks — standing in the dark room at the end of March with a safelight on and a husband three feet away and her arms folded — an appalling amount of a man's one life.

---

## The thing she does not say

"You've gone quiet."

"I've been quiet since Tuesday and you've been too polite to ask."

"I have been too polite to ask since Tuesday," John agrees, "and I'm going to go on being too polite for about another day and a half, and then I shall ask, so you may as well use the time."

She does not use the time.

It takes her two more days to get to what is actually stopping her, and when she does she finds it is a simple, professional sentence and she does not like it at all: *it is not mine.*

She is the person in this building who writes things down. She has five hundred and eighty-odd entries in a register in her own hand and a rule she made up on the second of July 1957 and has never broken, which is that a record with nobody's name on it is not a record, it is a rumour. She believes it. She has built a marriage and a career on people being named.

And there is one fact on this island that must never be entered anywhere, in any column, in any hand, including to her husband, who is the only person she is allowed to be accurate in front of — because the fact is not hers, and the man it belongs to has spent four years keeping it, without help, extremely well.

So she says, on the Thursday, "It's nothing. I'm out of shelf paper and Pettifer's have sent the wrong grade," which is true, and which is the first time in her married life she has said something true in order not to say something else.

She thinks about that for a long time.

---

## The application

It comes on the eleventh of April in the ordinary bag and Hester opens it at breakfast with the letter opener still in her hand and stops.

Then she reads it out, in her ordinary voice, from the top.

"*Application to consult deposited material. Applicant: A. Spilsbury, Ph.D., care of the harbour office, Vigo. Material sought: accession 197 — nine blocks, four jars, plate sequence, daily record. Purpose: comparison with holding-stock mortality, Iberian and Atlantic coasts. Duration: twelve days. July if convenient to the Institute.*"

Nobody at that table says anything.

Nora looks up because she cannot help it, and Dr Tar has gone entirely still with his cup halfway, and Dr Pobil is not looking at Dr Tar; he is looking at the tablecloth with his jaw set in a way that has nothing whatever to do with his face.

"Well," says the Colonel. "Is he qualified?"

"He appears to be qualified," says Hester Bevan.

"Then he may have it," says Reggie, "in the ordinary way," and puts the cup down, and it goes into the saucer at an angle and makes a noise, and he says "Damn," and nobody remarks on any of it.

---

Afterwards, on the path, Jean falls into step with her.

"You saw what that was."

"I saw a man drop a cup."

"That's not what I mean and you know it isn't." Jean has her folder under her arm, because she takes it to the Bay on the second Friday now and has stopped pretending otherwise. "He wrote that clause himself. March 1957, in a magistrate's room at the Bay, in a document he did not consult one living soul about. *Available on application to any qualified person.*"

"Ah."

"Fourteen months ago that man gave away everything he owned in a form that could not be taken back," says Jean Leslie, "and he put one sentence in it, and none of us understood at the time that the sentence was a door, and he has just knocked on it." She adjusts the folder. "Hester understood. Hester understood in the magistrate's room and has never said so, and I would give a very great deal to know what that has been like for her."

---

## The eleventh of July

He comes up the jetty at about four with a case and a hat and he is smaller than Nora expected and considerably more frightening.

"Mrs Weald. Spilsbury." He puts the case down to shake her hand, which nobody else on this island bothers to do. "You are the plate collection."

"I'm the officer in charge of it."

"That is what I said." He is looking past her at the long room. "The interleaving. Was that yours or was it done before you came?"

"Mine. October."

"Then you have saved between forty and sixty sheets of the most important sequence in this field," says Dr Spilsbury, "and I should like to see the register before I see anything else, including the material I have come four thousand miles about, and I am aware that this is an unusual request and I am making it anyway."

And he does, and he reads eleven pages of it standing up, and asks her four questions, all of which are the right ones, and one of which nobody in London ever thought to ask her in nineteen years.

She decides, on the spot, that she likes him very much and that she would not care to be examined by him twice in one week.

---

## The twelve days

What she expects is that she will watch two men be careful.

What she actually watches is two men be careful, and one other man be magnificent, and it is the third one that takes the skin off.

Because Pascual Pobil does not absent himself, which would be the easy thing. He is there for the whole of it. He puts Spilsbury on the bone series on the second day because it is the best use of him and says so in front of everyone; he argues with him about the fixative on the fourth and wins; he takes him down to the pens at six in the morning on the sixth and gives him twenty minutes with the old female entirely alone, which Nora only learns about because she was on the path and saw him walk away up it.

On the ninth day he says, at the long table, in front of the Colonel and Hester and Jean and John and Nora: "Dr Spilsbury, you are wrong about the calf and you were wrong about him in 1954, and the record is in that cabinet and I should like to do this properly after dinner."

And they do it properly after dinner, for two hours, and it is one of the best things Nora has ever sat through, and Reggie hardly says anything at all because he is enjoying it too much to interrupt.

---

And at intervals through all of it she watches Reggie Tar be visible.

That is the only word she can find for it. He is not different in any way she could put in a column — he works the same hours, he is rude to Hendy about the tide gauge, he spills things. It is that he has come up to full brightness, the way a lamp does when somebody finally cleans the glass, and she had not known until the twelfth of July that she had been looking at him through soot since October.

And the one who has been looking at him through soot for sixteen months, and who has now seen what the difference is, is standing at the other end of the bench cleaning a slide.

---

## The seventeenth, on the verandah

He finds her at about half past nine with the lamp between them and everybody else gone up.

"Mrs Weald. I am going to ask you something improper and you are at liberty to send me away."

"Go on."

"Pobil." Spilsbury turns his glass round once. "You have been here nine months. You look at people; I watched you do it to me on the jetty and you were about seventy per cent right inside a minute, which is better than most. So: he is well?"

"He's very well."

"That is not what I asked and you know it is not." He does not look at her. "I watched him for ten months in 1956. I had rather a lot of time and very little else to do with it. He was carrying something and I am satisfied about what it was, and I formed the view before I left that it had gone out over that winter — that it was finished by about the April."

The generator is running. Somebody down at the huts is putting a bucket away.

"And you want me to confirm it."

"I want to know whether I was right," says Alec Spilsbury. "I have been carrying the conclusion for fifteen months and I should prefer to know if I am carrying a wrong one."

---

Nora Weald sits in a wicker chair on a verandah in July with her hands round a glass and does the thing she does, which is to consider the material properly before she puts a mark on it.

She could tell him. He would take it well; he would take it better than almost anybody; he is the one man on this island who could hear it and never once let it show.

And it is not hers.

It has never been hers. It belongs to a man who has kept it for four or five years with no assistance from anybody, who has never asked one person on this station to carry any part of it, who put Spilsbury on the bone series because it was the best use of him, and who took him down to the pens at six in the morning and then walked away up the path.

And the thing about a fact of that kind, which she understands as well as anybody alive, is that the moment it is entered anywhere — in a column, in a letter, in one sentence on a verandah to a man leaving on Thursday — it stops being his and becomes everybody's, and it can never be taken out again.

She has built her whole working life on entering things.

"Dr Spilsbury," she says, "I'm not going to answer that."

He looks at her for the first time.

"No," he says. "No, of course you are not."

"I'd like to say why."

"I would rather you did not, because I am extremely good at this and you will tell me more than you intend." He sets the glass down. "That was badly done of me and I knew it was before I sat down. I am going to say one thing and then we will talk about the shelf paper."

"Go on."

"Whatever it is that you are not answering," says Alec Spilsbury, "the fact that this station now contains somebody who will not answer it is worth a great deal more to all of them than the answer, and I shall think about that in Vigo with some satisfaction."

And then he asks her about the shelf paper, and it turns out he knows a man in Oporto who can get unbuffered lining paper in quantity, and he writes the address on the back of a receipt, and it arrives in November and is the best paper she handles for nine years.

---

## The twenty-second

The boat is at half past nine.

The whole station is on the jetty, because the Colonel likes a thing done properly, and because after fifteen months nobody is going to be anywhere else.

Hester gives him an envelope. Jean says something about appendix two and he says *good* and looks extremely pleased. John has done a plate of the old female and has not numbered it and does not say anything at all when he hands it over, and Spilsbury looks at it for rather a long time and then puts it inside his coat instead of in the case.

Pascual shakes his hand.

"The gate," he says. "August or September. You said eighteen months in 'fifty-seven and it's going to be fourteen."

"Then you will want the protocol out of the cabinet and not written in a fortnight."

"It's been on my desk since June."

"*Good*," says Spilsbury, with feeling, and that is the whole of their goodbye and both of them are entirely satisfied with it, and Nora, watching from six feet away, has to look at the planks.

Then there are ninety seconds at the end of the jetty with everybody standing at the top of it being tactful, and two men four feet apart in front of Alleyne's boy, doing nothing whatever, for a minute and a half.

Reggie comes back up the jetty afterwards with his hat in his hand.

He gets about halfway and Pascual, who has not moved, says something to him, one sentence, very quietly, and Nora is close enough to hear it and wishes she were not.

"Come on. There's an hour of light and the old girl's off her feed."

And Reggie Tar says, "Right," in a voice that is not much of a voice, and goes down the path with him, and they are at that rail until dark.

---

## The twenty-fourth

She tells John in the end, and she tells him nothing.

They are in the west hut with the shutter open and she has her head on his chest and his hand is going up and down her spine the way it does.

"There's a thing I've had since February," she says, "and I'm not going to tell it to you, and I want you to know I've got it, because I said in the winter that you were the one person I'm allowed to be accurate in front of and I meant it and I've been sitting on this for five months and it has felt like lying."

John's hand does not stop.

"Is it about somebody in this building?"

"Yes."

"Is it theirs and not yours?"

"Yes."

"Then it isn't lying," says John Weald, "it's the other thing, and I've been doing it since May of 1954 and I can tell you exactly how it goes: it stops feeling like lying at about the third year, and then you miss the feeling, because the feeling was the part that meant you hadn't got used to it."

She lies there for a while.

"That's horrible."

"Yes," he says. "I'd rather you had it than not, though."

"Why?"

"Because there are nine people on this island," says John, "and until about February there were two of us doing that and now there are three, and it is very slightly easier, and I have not been able to say so to anybody, and I have just said it to you."

She gets a hand into his hair and holds on for a bit.

"John."

"Mm."

"I'm going to want the lamp on in a minute."

"Why?"

"Because I want to look at you," says Nora, "and because I have had four months of a man at the other end of a bench who has arranged his entire life around being in the same room as somebody and cannot say one word of it, and I have a husband nine feet from the door, and I find I am not going to waste an evening of that on being tired."

---

Afterwards, when the lamp is out again and he is nearly asleep, she lies looking at the ceiling and thinks about the register in the press in the long room, five hundred and ninety-one entries, four columns, one hand.

There is no column in it for this. There is no column for it anywhere, in any book, in any building, in this hemisphere or the other one, and there is not going to be; and a man in Vigo will go on believing something that is not true for as long as nobody corrects him; and she is the only person alive who could correct him and has decided not to, and there is nowhere she can even write down that she decided.

*Noted as such,* she thinks, in the dark, to nobody.

And then she turns over and puts her cold hand flat on her husband's back, and goes to sleep.
