# Revised Estimate

## The twenty-first of September

She is at the far bench with plate 802 and the good lamp and she has been furniture for so long that neither of them remembers she is in the room.

It is a Thursday. It is about half past eleven. Dr Sealy has been at the microscope for forty minutes and Dr Pobil has been writing, and they have not said anything for a while, which is ordinary.

Then Dr Sealy sits back from the eyepieces.

"Dr Pobil, take your eye off that and listen to me, because I am going to say something and then I am going to catch the twelve o'clock boat whatever you answer, and I have arranged it that way on purpose."

Nora's hand stops.

"I am forty-six years old," says Marguerite Sealy. "I have run that district on my own for eleven years and I am extremely good at it and I have never once wanted anything I could not do myself. I have come over on that boat every Thursday since January and for the last four months I have been getting up at half past four in order to do it, and I have not been getting up at half past four for a microscope."

There is a silence.

"Now. I think you have known that since about the summer and have decided it would be presumptuous, and you have decided it on your own, without consulting me, which is the only thing you have done in eighteen months that I have found genuinely irritating." A chair moves. "So I am consulting you. Is it presumptuous?"

Nora Weald sits at the far bench with a stipple tool in her hand and her ears roaring and has never in her life wanted so badly to be somewhere else.

"*Ay, Dios* —"

"That is not an answer, and I have twenty minutes."

"Marguerite."

"Yes."

"Sit down," says Pascual Pobil, in a voice Nora does not recognise at all. "You are not catching the twelve o'clock."

---

She gets out on her hands and knees behind the benches with a case of plates, which she is thirty-five years old and far too old for, and she makes it to the door and out onto the path, and neither of them notices her go, and she is fairly confident that neither of them has noticed anything else that has happened in that building since 1957.

She stands on the path in the sun with her heart going.

Then she goes and finds John in the dark room and shuts the door and tells him, and John Weald laughs so hard he has to sit down on the floor.

---

## What happens next

Nothing whatever happens next, for about five months, which is the funniest part.

Dr Sealy goes on coming on Thursdays and going back on the last boat, mostly. Dr Pobil goes on running the drug book and the pens and the protocol. They are perfectly correct with each other in front of everybody. There is no announcement of any kind.

And the entire station, which contains one of the best observational scientists in the field, notices that Dr Pobil has been in extremely good spirits since the autumn, and accounts for it as follows.

*The Colonel:* the licence.

*John:* nothing, out loud, ever.

*Hester Bevan:* nothing out loud either, but in November she has a second bed put into the spare room at the bungalow against an inventory, and when Nora looks at her she looks back and says, "Miss Benfield, I have kept records for thirty-three years," and goes on with the wages.

*Dr R. Tar, Director of Research:* the old female.

"He's been a different man since September," Reggie says at dinner in December, with a fork. "It's Gamma. He had her down for February and she's come up four pounds and her rate's back and he's been walking about like a man who's won something."

"Mm," says Nora.

"It's a great pity about the other business, but that's how it goes. Sixteen months. Eighteen now." He helps himself to potatoes. "I said to him in November of last year that she wasn't having him and I was right, and I take no pleasure in it whatsoever."

Across the table, Dr Pascual Pobil says, "*Bueno*," and eats his dinner, and does not look at anybody, and Nora has to put her napkin to her mouth and think very hard about shelf paper.

---

## February

Gamma comes through the February.

That is the other thing that happens that winter and it is not a small one. She is old and she is slow at the turn and Pascual had her down for February or March and not later, and he wrote to Vigo in the autumn and got four pages back about what they did wrong there and did the opposite; and on the ninth of February she eats the whole bucket, and on the twelfth he sits down on the concrete rather suddenly and stays there for a minute.

"She has bought a year," he says. "Perhaps two. I would not go further than that in writing."

"Then don't put it in writing."

"*Ay*, Mrs Weald, I put everything in writing," says Pascual. "It is the only reason anybody knows anything."

---

The engagement is on the twenty-fourth of February and Nora hears about it on the twenty-sixth from Marguerite, in the long room, in about four sentences, because Marguerite Sealy does not do a thing by halves and has apparently decided that Nora is on the list of people who get told before the list of people who get announced at.

"There is a ring and it came from Kingston and it is perfectly nice and I shall wear it."

"Congratulations."

"Thank you. There is also the other thing and I am going to tell you about it because I have got to tell somebody and I am not going to tell my sister." Dr Sealy puts her bag down. "He gave me a notebook."

"The little one. Out of his breast pocket."

"You know it."

"I've watched it come out every Thursday for fourteen months," says Nora, "in a hand he doesn't use for anything else."

"It is every joint reading we have ever done. Every one. January 1961 to this month, in order, with the date and the result and which of us saw it first." Marguerite Sealy is looking at the bench. "And on about a third of them, Mrs Weald, he has written down that I saw it first. Where I did. In his own hand. Where nobody was ever going to read it."

"Yes," says Nora. "That'll be it."

"I have been a doctor for twenty-one years and no one has ever once written down that I was right." She picks the bag back up. "I made rather a fool of myself about it and I am not going to describe that, and I should like to look at a slide now, please."

---

## The twenty-eighth of February, at dinner

It comes up because the Colonel asks about the summer, and Reggie — carving — says:

"Spilsbury's put in for July as usual and Hester's had it in the cabinet since the fourteenth. Pascual, you'll want the protocol out before he's here, he'll be unbearable about line four."

"It has been out since January."

"Good man." Reggie puts the plate down. "And while I think of it — I'm going to say a thing and then I shall leave it for good."

Nora sets her knife down very quietly.

"Two years next month," says Reggie, kindly. "Two years, and a boat every Thursday, and I have watched that woman for two years and she has never given you one single inch, and there comes a point —"

"We are getting married in July," says Pascual.

---

The best four seconds of Nora Weald's life.

Reggie Tar stops with the serving spoon in the air.

"You're —"

"The fourteenth. At the Bay."

"You're getting *married*."

"That is what I said."

"To *Dr Sealy*."

"*Ay*, to Dr Sealy, who else would I be marrying, there are four women on this island and three of them are in this room —"

And then it goes off like the fire pump.

The Colonel is on his feet. Hester Bevan says "Well" in a tone that, from her, is a brass band. John has gone scarlet and is shaking Pascual's hand across the table and knocks over the water. Reggie has abandoned the serving spoon entirely and is saying *when* and *how long* and *why did nobody* at a volume that brings Juan in from the path to see what has broken.

And in the middle of it Nora sits with her hands in her lap and watches Dr Pobil be shouted at by four people at once and try, for about ninety seconds, to look like a man enduring something.

He gives it up at the end of the ninety seconds. She sees him give it up. He sits back in his chair with his arms folded and lets them do it to him, and he is the colour of a brick from the collar upward, and he is *beaming*.

---

## The arithmetic

Reggie does it out loud on the verandah afterwards, twice, with a pencil.

"March of 1960. That's when she first came over, I had it off Hester. March 1960 to July 1962." He is writing on the back of an envelope. "That's twenty-eight months. Two years and four months."

"It's a long time," says the Colonel.

"It is an *extraordinary* time. That woman held out for two years and four months and she never gave him a thing, not a look, not an inch, and I watched her do it across a table about nine times a year." He taps the envelope. "And he never said a word about it to a living soul and just kept getting on the boat. Two years and four months."

Nobody at that table says anything at all.

Hester Bevan, who bought a lamp in March of 1961 and had a bed put in in November, looks at the tablecloth.

John, who was told in September, looks at the sea.

Nora, who was on her hands and knees behind a bench on the twenty-first of September 1961 with a case of plates, drinks her lemonade.

And Dr Pascual Pobil says, "It was a very long two years and four months, Reggie," in a tone of profound suffering, and pours himself another glass.

---

She watches Marguerite get offered it, once, in March, and decline it.

They are on the verandah and Reggie is doing the twenty-eight months again for her benefit, with the envelope, because he has kept the envelope; and he says, "Two years and four months, and I want you to know I told him in November of 1960 that he hadn't a hope, and I said it to be kind."

And Dr Marguerite Sealy, who got up at half past four on Thursday mornings from June 1961 and sat down at a microscope on the twenty-first of September and said *I am consulting you*, looks at him over the top of a glass of lemonade for about two seconds.

"Did you," she says. "How thoughtful."

And that is all, and she never says one word of it, then or later, in the whole of her life; and Nora catches her eye for about a quarter of a second across the boards and looks away first.

---

## March and April

The teasing runs for four and a half months and does not let up for one day.

Reggie Tar has spent nine years at four feet from that man with nothing whatever to say to him at breakfast, and he has now been handed the single richest seam of his life, and he works it like a man on piece rates.

He reinterprets everything. He is wrong about all of it and it makes not the slightest difference.

"The *shirt*. July of last year. You came in to breakfast in a clean shirt on a Thursday and I thought, hello, he's had a letter from the Department."

"I am always in a clean shirt."

"You are never in a clean shirt, you are a veterinarian." Reggie turns a page. "And the boat. That woman was coming over at ten past seven in the morning and I said to Hester, that's a very keen woman about a condenser."

"You said no such thing."

"I said it in my own mind," says Reggie, with dignity, "where it counts."

He writes it to Vigo. Nora sees him doing it on the fourth of March, at the long-room bench, taking his time over it, and she has to go out onto the path.

---

The reply comes in the second week of April and Reggie reads it at the breakfast table and then reads it again with his glasses off.

"That's the whole of it," he says. "He's sent four pages on the fixation and then two lines at the bottom. Listen to this. *As to the other matter: I congratulate them both without reservation and I note that you always thought so. You have said so to me at some length. I have put your letter in the tin with the 1949 one.* What tin? What 1949 one?"

"I couldn't say," says Hester Bevan.

"There isn't a *tin*," says Reggie.

Nora gets as far as the doorway before she has to hold on to the frame, and Pascual Pobil says, from behind his cup, without any expression whatsoever, "Perhaps he keeps a record, Reggie. Some people do."

---

## What she notices about being teased

Two things, and neither of them goes in a book.

The first is that Pascual Pobil starts mentioning Marguerite at dinner when there is no reason to. Not often. Once or twice a week, dropped into a gap — the district, the surgery, a case she had at the Bay — and Nora clocks it the third time and thereafter counts them, and they are always, without exception, mentioned at a moment when Reggie is within earshot and has his mouth free.

He is *loading the gun*. He has been loading it since the first of March.

The second is a thing he says to her at the far bench in April, when she asks him — because by then they are people who can ask each other about one thing and one thing only — whether Reggie is driving him mad.

"He has been at four feet from me for nine years," says Pascual, cutting, not looking up, "and in nine years he has never once had anything to say to me at breakfast that was not about an animal or a form." He changes the blade. "He now has something. He has it every morning. He is extremely pleased with it and he has got every single detail of it wrong."

"And you're not going to correct him."

"I am not going to correct one word of it," says Pascual Pobil, "until the day I die."

---

## The limes

On the eleventh of April Dr Sealy comes over on the early boat with a bag of limes, which is not a thing she has ever done, and gets Reggie on his own on the verandah for about ten minutes.

Nora watches it from the path with a case under her arm.

It is not a long conversation and it is not an important-looking one. Reggie is cheerful and expansive. Marguerite has her hat in her hand. At one point he goes off and comes back with the cabinet key and shows her something, and she looks at it and nods, and puts her hat on, and goes down to the pens to find Pascual.

And Nora Weald stands on a path in the sun with a case of plates and finds, for the first time in four and a half years, that she cannot read a thing she has just watched.

There is no reason for the limes. There is no reason for the early boat in April. There is no reason for a woman who has asked that man a total of one question in two years to have wanted ten minutes of him on a verandah with the cabinet key.

She thinks about it on and off for three months and gets nowhere, and writes nothing down, and on the fourteenth of July she will be standing at the back of a schoolroom at the Bay when it arrives all at once.

---

## The twenty-eighth of April

She has not said anything to John because she does not believe it.

She stopped counting in the spring of 1959, and she has been thirty-four and thirty-five and is now thirty-six, and she has had two false alarms in five years and she is not going to be a woman who has a third.

So she goes over on the Thursday boat with Marguerite's slides in a box as cover, which she is aware is ridiculous, and sits on the bench outside the district surgery with nine other people, and gives the clerk her name.

It takes forty minutes and about half of it is silent.

"Yes," says Dr Sealy at the end of it. "You are, and I would put you at about nine or ten weeks, and I am going to tell you the rest of it in order and you are going to sit there and let me."

"Go on."

"You are thirty-six and this is your first and that is late, and there is a form of words for it that I am not going to use because it is unkind and it is also accurate, and the thing it means is that you will be seen more often and not less. You will see me every month until July and every fortnight after that. You will go to Kingston in August and again in November and I will write to the man myself. You will stop lifting those cases and you will find somebody else's arms, and I do not care in the least how proud you are about it."

"Right."

"And there is a figure I am not going to give you, because you are the sort of woman who would put it in a book and look at it, and knowing it would not change one single thing you do between now and November." Dr Sealy caps the pen. "If you ask me for it I will give it to you, because I do not lie to patients. Do you want it?"

Nora sits in the wooden chair for a while.

"No," she says.

"Good."

---

They are quiet for a minute.

"Fourteenth of July," says Nora.

"The fourteenth of July, and you will be about four and a half months, and you will be at the back of that room in something loose, and I am not going to say one word to anybody and neither are you." Marguerite Sealy stands up. "I have waited two years and four months, apparently. I can wait eleven weeks."

And then, at the door, with her hand on it, in an entirely different voice:

"Estimated date, Mrs Weald. Around the twenty-ninth of November, give or take a fortnight, and it is an estimate and not a promise and I want you to hear me say so."

"Around the twenty-ninth of November."

"Go home," says Dr Sealy. "Tell your husband. Take the early boat back, there's a swell coming."

---

She tells him on the step of the west hut at about seven in the evening with the light going orange, and she does it badly, in about six words, because she has been rehearsing it for four hours on a boat and every version was worse.

John Weald does not say anything at all for a long moment.

Then he puts both hands over his face, and Nora sits down next to him on the step and puts her arm through his and holds on, and the generator runs, and down at the pens somebody drops a bucket and swears in Spanish.

"Nine or ten weeks," he says eventually, from behind his hands.

"About that."

"That's — " He stops and does it again. "That's February. That's the fortnight the old girl came right. That's the week I was in the dark room until two every night with the copy set."

"Yes," says Nora. "I've done that arithmetic too and it's not in a book either."

And John takes his hands down, and his face is a complete disgrace, and he says, "Right," and then, after a moment, "Right," again, in a slightly different voice, and does not manage anything else for some time; and they sit on a step on the twenty-eighth of April 1962 and watch it get dark over the water.
