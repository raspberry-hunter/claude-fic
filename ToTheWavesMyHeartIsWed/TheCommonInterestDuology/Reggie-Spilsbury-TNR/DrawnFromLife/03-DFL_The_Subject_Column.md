# The Subject Column

## Staff

The minute book has her under *Staff* and it has her twice.

*2.ix.57 — Post created, Officer i/c plate collection and reproduction. Salary as minuted.*

*18.xi.57 — Mr J. C. Weald and Miss N. Benfield married at the Bay, 16th inst. No alteration to either post.*

"No alteration to either post," says Nora.

"There is no alteration to either post," says Mrs Bevan. "Is there."

"No."

"Then that is what the book says." She turns the page. "There is a separate question, which is what you sign. You may sign Weald, which is ordinary; or you may sign Benfield, which is what is printed at the foot of a plate list in London and what is on every entry in your own register since October. I have no view. I should like it settled by Friday so that it is settled once."

She settles it in about four seconds and then feels obliged to explain herself, which nobody has asked for.

"It's not sentiment. It's that a register wants one hand and one name from end to end, and if it changes halfway a man in 1980 has to stop and work out whether that's two people."

"Yes," says Hester Bevan. "That is the correct reason and I am glad it was yours."

---

## What she is like about things

It takes John about a month to work out that his wife looks at people the way a foreman looks at a machine that somebody else has been running.

Not the composition. He does the composition. What she gets is the *wear*.

Where the thumb goes. Which side of a bench a man stands on and whether the varnish is gone there. Which chair has been moved and put back wrong. Whose cup is the chipped one and whether anybody minds. She has spent nineteen years in a shop where you could tell who had used a scraper last by three inches of handle, and she cannot switch it off, and on a station of nine people it runs all day like a tap she cannot find.

So this is what she has by Christmas, and she has not gone looking for any of it.

The Director of Research does not clean his own spectacles. He takes them off and holds them, and about half the time somebody takes them off him, does them on a shirt tail, and puts them back into his hand; and the somebody is always the same one, and neither of them has ever looked at the other while it happens, and it happens in front of the Colonel.

There are two cups on the shelf above the long-room sink that nobody else uses. Not reserved. Just never washed up into the general stock, for four years, by anybody.

There is a stool at the far bench that lives eighteen inches from the end and not under it.

Dr Tar says *Pascual* in a room with three people in it and *Dr Pobil* in a room with five, and the line is at four, and he has plainly never worked out that he does it.

And Dr Pobil, who does not touch anybody at all — who shook her hand on a jetty and has not been within two feet of her since, who examines an animal with his whole forearm and a person with his eyes — puts his hand on the back of Reggie Tar's neck on the path at night when he thinks the path is empty, and has done it twice that she has seen, and both times the other man has stopped walking for about a second and a half before going on.

---

## The fourteenth of December

It is not a large thing and she does not see the whole of it.

They have been married four weeks. She has come up to the verandah at six for the mail with her hands full of shelf paper, and John is on the step, and she puts the paper down on the rail and takes his face in both hands and kisses him, in the open, at six o'clock, the way you do when you are four weeks married and have not yet stopped being surprised at being allowed to.

And then she picks the paper up and turns round and Dr Pobil is at the other end of the verandah with the post bag.

He says good evening perfectly normally. He asks whether the second case has swelled in the damp and she says not yet and he says to tell him the day it does. He goes on into the office.

It takes her until about half past eight that night to be able to name what his face had done, because it was not disapproval and it was not embarrassment and it was over in under a quarter of a second.

It was the face of a man watching somebody spend money in a currency he does not have.

---

She thinks about it for a week and she does not stop being careless, because the alternative is to start performing on her own verandah, and she is not going to do that to John or to herself or, when she gets it straight, to them.

But she does notice, from the fifteenth of December onward, that she is the only person on this station who touches her husband in front of people. Jean does not touch anybody. The Colonel shakes hands. Mrs Bevan once put her palm flat on the Colonel's shoulder blade going through a door and Nora nearly dropped a plate.

Nine people in a building, and two of them are the only ones with anything to hide, and the effect of it has gone through the whole house like damp through a wall, and not one of them could tell you when it started.

---

## Number 214

She reaches it on the nineteenth of January, working backwards through 1954, and she stops because it breaks the pattern and her job is patterns.

Two hundred and thirteen is Gamma at the rail. Two hundred and fifteen is Gamma and the calf at the rail. And between them, dated the same fortnight, at an hour written in the corner in John's square capitals, is a beach.

No animal. No rail. Low water on the seaward side, the light coming in flat from the left, a drag of weed and two birds, and it is very beautiful and it is completely pointless, and it is the only plate in five hundred and forty-two that is not of the subject.

She takes it up to the dark room.

"John. What's two-fourteen?"

He has his back to her at the tank. He does not turn round and he does not answer, and the extractor is going, and Nora Weald stands in a red room with a plate in her hand and watches her husband's shoulders and works it out in about six seconds, because she is married to him and because she has seen the fire-pump drawing.

"Ah," she says.

"Yes."

"What time was it?"

"Twenty to six. End of May, 'fifty-four." He is still not turning round. "I came up the path to get the light and a door shut and I turned round before either of them knew there was anybody on the path, and I went down to the beach and drew it, because I had to be somewhere and I had an easel."

"And you numbered it."

"I number everything," says John. "That's the whole point of me."

---

## The column

The register is ruled in four columns and she has never once had trouble with any of them until now.

Number. Date. Subject. Condition.

She sits at the long-room bench for a long while with the pen not touching the paper and thinks about it properly, the way she would think about a plate that is going to be screened at a hundred and twenty, which is to say: what survives, what is lost, and who is going to be reading this, and when.

She could leave it out. That is the obvious answer and it is the wrong one, because a gap is the loudest thing in a sequence and the first thing an inspector's eye goes to, and because a register with a hole in it is worth nothing, and because she is not going to be the first person in this building to falsify a record.

She could write *view, no subject*, which is a lie by omission dressed as a shrug.

Or she can do her job.

*No. 214 — 28.v.54 — Seaward beach, 05.40, low water, looking NE — J. C. Weald del. — condition good; foxing nil; interleaved 19.i.58.*

She reads it back twice.

It is accurate. It is complete. It is more informative than most of the entries in the book. There is nothing in it that anybody could put in front of anybody. And it will sit in a ruled column in a bound register in a cupboard in a marine institute for the next hundred years, holding a morning in May 1954, and telling nobody anything at all.

She sits back on the stool.

*So that's what they all do,* she thinks. *That's the trick. That's Mrs Bevan's whole method and John's speech in the dark room and the four-line covering letter to London. Every one of them, all day long, telling the exact truth in a shape that can't be used.*

And it has taken her three months to see it, and she has been doing it herself since the second of July, since she wrote *twenty strokes removed, ventral, for screen* on a sheet nobody asked her for.

---

## Dr Pobil in the long room

He comes on the twenty-second, in the afternoon, when John is at the pens and Jean is at the Bay and the room is empty, which she understands afterwards was not luck.

"Mrs Weald."

"Dr Pobil."

"I would like to see the book."

She takes it out of the press and puts it on the bench and opens it at the nineteenth and turns it round, and does not say anything, because there is nothing that needs saying and because she has learned a certain amount from these people since October.

He reads it. He takes his time. He is a slow man and he is slow at this too, and his finger goes along the ruling and stops in the subject column, and stays there.

Then he says: "*Ay.*"

"It's accurate."

"It is entirely accurate." He does not look up. "How did you know to do it that way?"

"I didn't know anything. I sat there for about twenty minutes and I could see three ways of doing it and two of them were wrong for professional reasons." She folds her arms. "It only occurred to me afterwards that it was also the other kind of right."

"That is exactly how Hester does it," says Pascual Pobil. "She has never once written down a thing that was not true and I have watched her keep this station out of a file for five years with nothing else."

"John told you he'd seen you. In 'fifty-six."

"He stood in the dark room and told us both and then went to bed before either of us could answer him, which I have thought about every month since and have never mentioned to him, and would rather you did not." He straightens up. "He did not tell us there was a plate."

"No."

"Twenty to six in the morning at the end of May 1954." He is looking at the beach and not at her. "There is nothing else. You understand? Not a photograph. Not a letter — we are in the same building, there has never been one letter. Not a line in a minute book, and I have made certain of that."

"I know."

"And there is a drawing of an empty beach with a number on it in a box in this room."

"Two hundred and fourteen."

Dr Pobil stands in a long room in January with both hands flat on a bench, and Nora, who reads bodies about as well as anybody who is not a vet, watches something go through him and be got hold of and put down again.

"Thank you," he says.

"I've not done anything. I ruled a line and filled in four columns."

"*Ay*, Mrs Weald," says Pascual, and the corner of his mouth goes. "You have written the only record of my life that will ever exist and you have put it in the condition column as *good*, and I am going to think about that at intervals for the next thirty years. Do not tell me you ruled a line."

---

## The second of February

It comes round the head at about eleven at night with no warning that anybody on this island thought worth acting on, and it is not a hurricane, and it is quite bad enough.

What happens is that the sea comes over the low sill on the north side and goes down the hard and into the long room to a depth of about three inches, and the long room is where the plate cases are, and they are on the floor because Juan's brother built them for the floor because nobody built a stand.

Nora gets there at four minutes past midnight in John's coat over her nightgown and Dr Tar is already in the doorway with a lamp.

"Cases."

"They're on the floor —"

"I know where they are. Up on the far bench, both, and the bench is fixed and it is forty inches and the water is not getting to forty inches." He has his shoes off, which she notices and does not understand until later, when she sees the concrete. "John's behind me. Juan's gone for his brother. Where's the register?"

"The press."

"Is the press off the floor?"

"Four inches."

"Then that's the first thing you move and you move it now, because a register is the only part of this we can't redraw."

And she stops for about a second in three inches of seawater at midnight, because it is the first time in her life that anybody has said that to her without being told it first.

---

Dr Pobil comes through the door at eight minutes past with a hurricane lamp and no shirt and says one word, which is "North?"

"North sill, it's over," says Reggie. "Gate?"

"Gate's holding. Gamma's at the far end, she's fine, the boy is not fine and he is not in danger, and there is nothing to do for him but stand there and I am not going to stand there."

"How long have you got?"

"An hour."

"Then it's an hour," says Reggie. "Cases, then the press, then the tins in the office. Nora, tell him what's in each case."

And that is the entire negotiation, and it is about nine seconds long, and Nora Weald carries one end of a plate case across a flooded floor in the dark and understands, with total clarity and considerable irritation, that she has just watched two people do in nine seconds what she and John took a fortnight and two arguments to do about a lamp.

---

The rest of it is wet and stupid and goes on until twenty past three.

The cases go up. The press goes up. The office tins go on the desks. Juan's brother arrives with four men from the Bay side and they build a check across the doorway out of sacking and a hatch cover and it works better than anything anybody designed. The Colonel appears at about one with a bottle of something and the settled conviction that he is helping, and Mrs Bevan takes it off him and gives it to the men from the Bay side, which is the correct use of it.

The interleaving holds. Nothing in the cases gets wet.

Nine sheets of shelf paper have gone brown at the corner where the water came up under the third case before they got to it, which is nine sheets of shelf paper, and Nora sits down on the end of the fixed bench at twenty past three with her feet in the water and laughs until she has to put her head on her knees.

---

## Twenty past three

They are all on the verandah afterwards because nobody can face going to bed, and everybody is soaked, and the Colonel has got the bottle back.

John comes and sits on the boards next to her and she leans on him — whole weight, no thought about it, the way you lean on a wall — and he puts his arm round her and pushes her wet hair off her face with his other hand and leaves that hand where it is.

She is not thinking about anybody. She is thinking about nine sheets of shelf paper.

And she looks up because there is a lamp being moved, and Dr Tar is standing three feet away with the lamp in his hand, and he is looking at the two of them.

He is thirty-eight years old and he has been in the water for three hours and he has not got his spectacles on, and there is nothing whatever on his face, which is exactly the point; she has seen him pleased and furious and delighted about a number, and she has never once seen him with nothing on his face, and it is like coming round a corner into an empty room.

It lasts about two seconds.

Then he says, "Weald, your wife has been sitting in the sea for a quarter of an hour," and hangs the lamp on the hook, and goes and sits down at the far end next to Dr Pobil with about eighteen inches between them, and says something that makes Pascual put his head back and laugh out loud at half past three in the morning.

And Nora thinks: *he wasn't looking at us. He was looking at the arm.*

---

## The west hut, later

"You're not asleep."

"No," says John.

She is lying with her head on his chest and his hand going up and down her spine, slowly, the way he does when he is thinking, and the shutter is open and the sea is doing the thing it does.

"Can I say something about them and then never say it again?"

"You can say anything you like about them," says John. "That's the only thing I can give you that they haven't got. Say it."

So she says it into his collarbone with her eyes shut.

"I'm better at my trade than I was in July, because of them. Not because they taught me anything. Because I watched two people take nine seconds to do a thing that took us a fortnight, and I thought, I want that, and I have got about forty years to get it." She flattens her hand on him. "And there is a morning in May 1954 in a box in that room and it has a number on it and it is the only thing there is, and I put *good* in the condition column, and I have thought about it every day since Tuesday."

John does not say anything for a while.

"I drew the beach because I couldn't draw the other thing," he says. "I want you to know I've always known that."

"I know you've always known that."

"And I'd not have said it to anybody but you."

"No."

"That's the bit I'd not worked out about being married," says John Weald, to the ceiling, in the dark. "I thought it was going to be the — the company. Somebody at the table. It's not. It's that there's one person you're allowed to be accurate in front of."

Nora lies still for a moment.

Then she gets up on her elbow, and gets a hand into his hair, and kisses him properly, and takes rather a long time about it; and he makes the small sound he makes and his hand goes flat and hard at the small of her back and pulls, and she comes over him, and the shutter is open and the lamp is out and there is nobody on the path at half past four in the morning.

"Say that again," she says, later, against his mouth.

"Which part —"

"You know which part."

"That you're the one I'm allowed to be accurate in front of."

"Yes," she says. "*Oh.* Yes. That one."

---

## The fourth of February

The stands go in on the Tuesday: two of them, teak, forty-two inches, built by Juan's brother to her drawing, at a cost of eleven pounds which Mrs Bevan finds in about a day and a half and does not say where from.

The register gets a new page at the back, ruled in three columns, headed *Conditions of storage*, with the date of the flood and the height of the water and the nine sheets of shelf paper and what was done about it.

And at the foot of the entry, because it is accurate and because it is the only place in the building it will ever be written down, she puts:

*Cases removed to the fixed bench by Dr R. Tar, Dr P. Pobil, Mr J. C. Weald, J. Aguilar and his brother, between 00.04 and 00.40, in three inches of water, without loss.*

Reggie reads it a fortnight later, standing at the bench with his glasses in his hand, and says nothing at all about it, and goes out.

Twenty minutes after that she finds she has been left, on the corner of the bench, a folded slip in a hand she does not yet know well, which says:

*Aguilar is Juan's mother's name. His brother goes by Peña. You should have both and I have had it wrong in the wages book since 1954, which somebody might have told me. R.T.*
