# On Approval

## The last ten hours

There is a folder in Dr Leslie's case and it comes out somewhere off Port Royal, at about the fourth hour, when it has become clear that neither of them is going to sleep.

"You may as well," says Jean. "You've been looking at it since Southampton."

"I've been looking at the case."

"You've been looking at the folder inside the case, Miss Benfield, and I've watched you do it, and you're very good at not asking, and I've had eleven days of it and I'd rather you just asked."

So Nora asks.

There are nine letters in the folder and they are in date order, which she likes, and they are all answers, which means there were nine letters going the other way, and those are not in the folder because you do not keep your own.

Bergen. Plymouth. Two from Canada. One from a man at Aberdeen who has plainly written it in ten minutes on a Friday. One from a Institute in Naples that is enthusiastic and offers nothing. And two that are quite different from the rest, because they are not replies at all: they are approaches, off the paper, from people who heard it read in June and wrote to J. E. Leslie afterwards without knowing what they were writing to.

"That one thinks you're a man."

"That one thought I was a man until the fourteenth of August and then wrote again to say he hoped I would not think his first letter presumptuous, which I do not, because it was the only honest one I got." Jean takes it back. "It's the second letters you want to read. The first ones are about the work."

"And the second ones?"

"Are about the arrangements," says Jean. "Whether there is suitable accommodation. Whether I have dependants. Whether I would find the position congenial." She squares the folder. "Nobody has asked me once what the salinity series cost me to get."

Nora sits with that for a while. The engine changes note and goes back.

"Do you want to go?"

"I want to be *able* to go," says Jean Leslie. "That's not the same and I'm perfectly clear about the difference and I'd thank you not to tell anybody I said either of them."

---

"May I say the tactless thing," says Nora, "since we've eleven days in and about six hours left."

"You may say it."

"John calls you Dr Leslie and so does Mrs Bevan in her letters. Who doesn't?"

The light off the water has gone the colour of weak tea. Jean Leslie looks out at it.

"Everybody else," she says. "For four years. And I've never once said anything, because when I got it there was an animal dying and it seemed a small thing to raise, and then it was six months old and it was worse to raise, and now it's four years old and I should look ridiculous."

"You wouldn't."

"I should look as though I minded."

"You do mind."

"Yes," says Jean. "I mind very much, and I have got a folder in a case, and I can add those two facts together as well as anybody."

---

## The jetty, the ninth of October

The whole station is on the planks. All of it, at four in the afternoon, and later she learns that this is not for her at all and that the Colonel simply likes a thing done properly, and by then she is fond of it.

She sorts them while the crate is going up, because that is what you do in a new shop.

The old soldier: kind, formal, will make a speech and will be no trouble. The wife beside him, small and upright, who is the one who actually holds the place — it is in the way the others stand relative to her, and it takes about three seconds. A big slow dark man at the back with his sleeves rolled who is looking at the japanned box in her hand and not at her face, which she likes enormously. A very brown boy of about nineteen holding a rope.

And a small sandy man in spectacles who comes forward with his hand out already moving.

"Miss Benfield. *Splendid.* You've had ten hours of that boat and there's a bath which is worse than the boat, and I am told I'm not to talk to you about the collection until Friday, which I intend to observe up to about Thursday morning."

"Reggie," says Mrs Bevan.

"That's the whole of my speech. Bevan, is that the last of it?"

John hands her down onto the planks and does not let go of her hand for about four seconds longer than is required, in front of six people, which she has not expected of him and which she thinks about at intervals for the rest of the week.

---

The big man comes over last, when the others have started up the path.

"Pobil," he says. "Veterinary officer. You have carried that box off the boat yourself."

"It's got my tools in it."

"*Ay.* Good." He looks at it a moment longer. "Weald has been unbearable since July. I want you to know it was the good kind."

"What's the bad kind?"

"The bad kind is a man who tells you," says Dr Pobil. "The good kind is a man who does not tell you anything and reorganises the dark room four times."

---

## The bungalow

Hester Bevan settles it standing in her own hall with Nora's case still at the door, in under a minute, which Nora will come to understand is how everything in this building is settled.

"You'll sleep here. Second room on the left, it has a wall to the sea and the window catches."

"Mrs Bevan, I'd not want to put you —"

"You are not putting me anywhere. I have thought about this since August and I should like you to hear the reasoning rather than the instruction, because you are going to be dealing with me for some years and you may as well know how I work." She sets her hands on the hall table. "You are not married to Mr Weald. You have told him you will not be until you have decided, and he has told me so in a letter, and I think better of both of you for it."

"Thank you."

"That is not a compliment, it is a fact and I am coming to the difficulty." Mrs Bevan does not blink. "There is an inspector who was on this island in January and will be again. There is a parish four hours away that has had opinions about this establishment since 1948. And there is a Department file with our name in it. If you sleep in the west hut, Miss Benfield, then what is true — which is that a skilled woman has come out to take a post and to make up her mind at her leisure — will not be what is said, and what is said is the only thing that ever reaches a folder."

"So I sleep in your house."

"You sleep in my house, and the post is in the minute book with a salary attached, and both of those are on paper before you unpack." She picks up the case herself, which Nora is not fast enough to prevent. "And when you have decided, you will tell me, and I shall be extremely pleased, and I shall say so once and then not mention it again."

---

The post is in the minute book. It has a title, which is *Officer in charge of the plate collection and reproduction*, and it has a salary, which is less than she was getting at Bowling Green Lane and is her own.

She reads the entry twice standing at the office desk.

"It's in your hand."

"It is in my hand and it is dated the second of September and it was minuted at a meeting with three people present," says Mrs Bevan. "If you go home in November it stays in the book. I am not going to rule it out."

---

## Four boxes

They are in the long room under the far bench and there are four of them and they are japanned and they are wrong.

She gets them open on the Friday morning with the shutters back and the light coming off the water onto the ceiling, and then she stands there for about a minute and a half not touching anything.

Five hundred and forty-two sheets. In order. Numbered, dated, in one hand for the first four hundred and then the same hand with a better pen, no gaps since April 1956 and only three before that; and they are stacked flat, unsleeved, with nothing between them, in a box with a metal hinge, on an island where the air comes off the sea.

"Mr Weald."

"Yes?"

"Come here and look at the bottom sheet of box three."

He comes. He looks. He goes white.

"That's foxing."

"That's foxing, and that's a bloom on the ink where it's been face to face with the back of the one above, and there's about nine of them gone that way and they are all early, which means they are the ones you can't do again." She puts her hand flat on the bench. "John. This is the best sequence in the world and it is in four biscuit tins."

"I know what it's in."

"Then why —"

"Because there was nowhere else to put it and I asked three times in 1955 and there was no money," says John, and then, "and because I did not know about interleaving until about four minutes ago, and I have been keeping this record for five years, and I am going to be sick."

"You are not going to be sick." She has him by the wrist. "You are going to go and find me paper. Not cartridge — anything unsized and unbuffered, lining paper, shelf paper, I don't care what it was for. And I want to know what's in the store and what comes on the Wednesday boat and who I ask."

"You ask Mrs Bevan."

"Then we'll go and ask Mrs Bevan," says Nora Benfield, "and John, look at me — nine sheets in five years, out here, with no case and no advice and nobody who knew. There are men in London with proper cabinets who have lost more than that in a wet spring. You kept it."

---

She works for nine days and does not go down to the pens once, which the whole station notices and nobody remarks on.

At the end of it there are five hundred and forty-two sheets interleaved with shelf paper cut to size, in two cases built by Juan's brother out of a packing crate to her drawing, with a cloth over each, and a register.

The register is the part she is proud of. It is ruled in four columns and it is her hand, and the first entry is *No. 1 — 14.vii.51 — Specimen Alpha, ventral — J. C. Weald del. — condition good, foxing at lower edge 4mm, interleaved 18.x.57.*

She signs the foot of the page *N. Benfield* and dates it, because a record with nobody's name on it is not a record, it is a rumour.

---

## The fourth night

It is not the heat and it is not the insects and it is not even the generator, which stops at ten and is the only merciful thing on this island.

It is that at about half past eleven on the fourth night she wakes up and cannot hear anything at all.

No river. No drays on the Clerkenwell Road at five. No trams, no gulls off the wharf, nobody's boots. Just water doing the same thing to the same rock, over and over, and her own ears ringing with the absence, and the realisation — flat, factual, arriving all at once at the bottom of the bed — that she is four thousand miles from every single person she has known for thirty-one years, on the word of a man she has spent six weeks with, and that the Wednesday boat does not always come.

She does not cry, because she is not a crier. She sits up with her arms round her knees until about two and then gets up and lights the lamp and finds, on the table, a thing that was not there at eleven.

It is a half-sheet in soft pencil, pushed under the door at some point in the evening, and it is the bench on Clerkenwell Green: two clerks at the far end, a boy with a barrow, and the corner of a portfolio with the tape tied, and nobody sitting on the bench at all.

On the back, in the small square draughtsman's capitals: *NOT IN THE BOOK.*

She goes back to bed with it and sleeps until six.

## Miss Leslie

It happens on the twenty-third, in the long room, and Nora is there because she is always in the long room now and has become furniture.

"Miss Leslie, have you the October figures —"

"Doctor."

Dr Tar stops with his hand on the cabinet.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Doctor Leslie," says Jean, not looking up from the table. "Since June of 1953. I don't raise it and I'm raising it, and I'd like it in front of Miss Benfield because she asked me about it on the boat and I gave her a cowardly answer."

The room is very quiet. John has stopped ruling a line.

And then Dr Tar does a thing that Nora has not seen a man of his sort do in her life. He does not laugh it off and he does not become elaborate. He takes his hand off the cabinet and he stands there and he thinks about it, visibly, for about four seconds, and what comes over his face is not embarrassment, it is *interest*, of the same kind he wears at the bench when a number is wrong.

"I have been doing that," he says. "Haven't I. For four years."

"Four years and four months."

"In front of Sturrock. In front of Averill's office, in November, twice." He takes his spectacles off. "And on the covering letter of the '55 retraction, which went to London."

"Yes."

"Good God," says Reginald Tar, with considerable feeling, and sits down on the corner of the bench.

"It's not an apology I want."

"You are not getting one, they're worthless." He is rubbing the lenses on his shirt and not seeing anything. "You're getting a correction, which is different, and I'm going to do it the way we do corrections here, which is in the record and in the open and about four years late. Hester keeps every minute since '52. It's going in the book at the next meeting, with the date I started getting it wrong, and I am going to have to say the number of times out loud."

"Reggie —"

"Jean," says Dr Tar. "I called your plates illustration for two years," — he does not look at John, and John has gone scarlet — "and I called Pobil's histology *the slides* until he took me apart on a verandah about it. I appear to be a man who gets the names of things wrong for years at a stretch and only ever finds out because somebody finally loses patience. I'd rather know. It's the single most useful thing anybody does for me."

He puts the spectacles back on.

"October figures?"

"On the end of the bench, where they have been since Monday."

"Thank you, Dr Leslie," says Reggie, and takes them, and goes out, and is heard to say *four years* to himself on the path.

---

Jean rules a line and does not look up for a while.

"Well," she says.

"That went better than you thought."

"That went *exactly* as I thought, which is what I've been so cross about." She puts the pen down. "He was never going to mind. Not for one second. He'd have changed it in 1953 if I'd said it in 1953, and I have spent four years and four months being angry at a man who was simply not looking, and there is nobody to be angry with, and I find I should have preferred a villain."

"There's a folder in a case."

"There is a folder in a case," says Jean, "and it is not about Reggie, and I have known that since about the fourteenth of August, and I have not yet worked out what it is about."

"I can tell you what it's about."

"Can you."

"It's about wanting somebody to have had to *ask* for you," says Nora. "Nine letters is nine people finding out that you exist. That's all the folder is for. You'll go to Bergen or you won't, and either way it'll be because somebody wrote to a person called J. E. Leslie about a salinity series and not because anybody out here noticed in time."

Jean Leslie looks at her for a long moment.

"You've had that speech ready since Port Royal."

"I've had that speech ready since about 1948," says Nora. "It's not yours. It just fits."

---

## The rock, the second of November

There is a flat rock past the store, above the high line, which is where people on this station go when they want to be out of the building, and John takes her there after dinner with a lamp they do not light.

It has been a month. She has said nothing about deciding and nobody has asked, which she is aware has cost Mrs Bevan something and John a great deal more.

"You're going to sit there and not ask me."

"I am going to sit here and not ask you," John agrees, "because if I ask you then you're answering me instead of answering the question, and those are different, and I've thought about it a lot and that's where I've got to."

"That's insufferable."

"Yes," says John. "Jean says I'm unbearable about light."

She laughs and then stops laughing, because he has turned his head and is looking at her in the particular way he has, which she first noticed on the fourth of July and had no name for and now does: it is the way he looks at a thing he is about to draw and is not going to draw.

"Come here," she says.

---

He is careful at first, which she has expected, and then he is not careful, which she has not.

That is the discovery of the evening and she takes a while to get her head round it: that a man who cannot begin a sentence in a drawing room turns out to have no hesitation at all once there is nothing to say. His hands know exactly what they are doing. Of course they do — she has watched them for six weeks doing fine work at a bench, and it has simply never once occurred to her to put the two facts side by side.

"Oh," she says, against his jaw, and then, "*oh*, that's —"

"Is that all right?"

"Don't — don't ask me things in words, I'm no good at it."

"All right."

"I mean it, John, I haven't got the — there aren't the words for it in my head, I've never had to say any of this out loud to anybody and I shan't do it well."

"Then don't say it," says John Weald, with his mouth at the side of her throat and his hand flat and slow at the small of her back, "and I'll watch, because that's the thing I can do."

And he does, and he is extremely good at it, and she stops worrying about vocabulary some minutes afterwards.

---

They get to the edge of it and she is the one who stops, with both hands in his shirt and her forehead against his collarbone, and she is not embarrassed, which surprises her most of all.

"Not tonight."

"No. All right." He has not moved his hand. "Is that a not-tonight or a not-yet?"

"It's a practical objection and I'd like to say it plainly and then I want you to put your arm back."

"It's back."

"Thank you." She gets her breath. "I'm thirty-one. If it goes wrong it goes wrong quickly at thirty-one, and I'm not married to you yet, and the only doctor on this establishment is a man I have to work beside for the next twenty years."

There is a pause of about two seconds and then John Weald starts to laugh, helplessly, into her hair.

"*Don't.*"

"I'm sorry. I'm sorry. It's only that I have thought of that at three in the morning on four separate occasions and have never once been able to say it to anybody —"

"Four separate occasions."

"At least four," says John. "Usually about the histology."

She hits him in the shoulder and he catches her hand and holds it there, flat against his chest, where she can feel exactly what his heart is doing, which is the trick she taught him on a bench in July and which he has plainly been saving.

"Then we'll be married," she says, "and I should like it to be soon, and that is me answering the question and not you."

He goes very still.

"You're sure."

"I made up my mind on the eighteenth of October at about four in the afternoon."

"That's a fortnight ago."

"It is."

"Nora — that's a *fortnight* —"

"I know how long a fortnight is," says Nora Benfield. "I signed the foot of the register on the eighteenth of October and I put my own name on it and nobody in this building thought it was worth remarking on, because out here that is simply what is done. And I sat and looked at my own name in my own hand for about ten minutes." She takes hold of the front of his shirt. "And then I thought: there is nowhere on God's earth I can go back to where that happens. So no, I'm not deciding about you tonight. I decided about you on a bench in July and I've been deciding about the *place*, which is what I said I'd do, and I have, and the answer is yes, and now will you please stop talking."

---

## The sixteenth

They are married at the Bay by the district registrar on a Saturday in the middle of November, because the parish church wants three weeks it does not have and Mrs Bevan wants the thing done before the inspector's season.

Jean stands up with her. Dr Pobil produces, from somewhere, a bottle that has been in the office cupboard since 1954 and about which the Colonel has opinions. The Colonel makes a speech of four sentences, one of which is about the generator, and Mrs Bevan writes the date in the minute book on the Monday as an item of station business under *Staff*, immediately below an item about lamp oil.

And on the Sunday morning, before the boat back, John gives her a half-sheet in soft pencil, without any ceremony at all, in the way one hands somebody a spare pencil.

It is the long room with the shutters back and the light coming off the water onto the ceiling, and the four boxes open on the floor, and a woman at the far bench with a loupe pushed up into her hair, finishing a stroke.

"That's yours," he says. "I've got two."

It is not numbered. It is not in the book.

She looks at it for rather a long time.
