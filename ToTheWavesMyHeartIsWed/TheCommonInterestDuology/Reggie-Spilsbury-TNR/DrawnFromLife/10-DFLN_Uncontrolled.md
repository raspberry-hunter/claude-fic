# Uncontrolled

## The twelfth

The Order comes up on the boat at about eleven and Hester Bevan reads it standing in the office doorway and then puts her hand out for the doorframe.

Spilsbury is at the end of the long table and does not move, because a man who moves at that moment is a man asking to be thanked.

They get her into the chair. Somebody produces brandy at ten past eleven in the morning. She drinks about a teaspoonful and then says, "Now sit down, all of you, because I want to say who did it and I am going to be accurate," and she is accurate, and it takes four minutes, and it is the best piece of attribution he has ever heard in a room.

And then she gets to the third annex and says it is his, and says she has seven weeks of correspondence proving he tried to keep his name off the submission.

"That is not accurate," he says.

"Which part."

"It was nine weeks."

The room laughs. Reggie laughs. That is what it was for.

---

What he does not say, then or afterwards, is that the annex is finished.

Four hundred and eleven pages. Eleven establishments in four countries. Five years of trade returns at one and threepence a set, read in a rented room above a chandler's, in the evenings, because there were no evenings that had anything else in them.

He started it in the autumn of 1957 with no expectation whatever that it would be used for anything. It has now been used. It is in a schedule with effect from the first of January and there is a paragraph nine in it, and the thing is done and will not need doing again.

He is extremely glad. He is also, standing in an office on the twelfth of July with a teaspoon of the Colonel's brandy in his hand, aware that as of about eleven o'clock this morning he has nothing to do with the other fifty weeks of the year except run a station in Norway, which is not nothing and is not this.

He does not write that down. It is not a finding.

---

## The path, that evening

"You made them keep books," says Reggie, "and then you read the books."

"Mrs Bevan made them keep books."

"And you read them."

They go through it and Spilsbury declines the credit twice, accurately, and then Reggie stops on the path and says: *do you know what Pascual shouted at you across that table in May of 'fifty-six* — *there is no column for it* — *then make one, hombre, you had a pencil.*

Six years. Four hundred and eleven pages.

"I had not connected the two," says Spilsbury, when he can, "and I find that I do not entirely care for the connection."

"Why not?"

"Because it suggests I have been taking instruction from a veterinary surgeon since 1956, and I should rather it were arrived at independently."

And Reggie Tar laughs the whole of the rest of the way up the path — out loud, all the way, at nothing, in the dark, the way he has laughed at Spilsbury's jokes since 1946 and at nobody else's in any building either of them has ever worked in.

That noise, Spilsbury thinks, going up behind him. *That noise is the whole of it, and I get it eleven times a year now, and I have just spent four years earning a thing that will be quoted in journals for forty and I would hand the entire annex back this minute for another hundred of those.*

He does not write that down either.

---

## The thirteenth

Nothing happens on the thirteenth, which is correct, and he spends most of it on the bone series with Codrington, who is going to be very good and is at present about eleven per cent as good as he believes.

In the evening he makes the entry, as usual.

*13.vii.62. Day 3 of 12.*

And then he sits with the pencil for a moment, because he has noticed something, and he does not leave things out.

*N.B. I appear this year to be counting downward. Every previous July I have counted the days elapsed. I do not know when this changed. Recorded without interpretation.*

---

## The fourteenth: the church

He is put in the fourth row on the bride's side by a boy of about twelve with a list, and it is small and hot and packed to the wall.

Jean Leslie is not there and has sent, at what must have been ruinous expense, a floral arrangement of dimensions requiring a second crossing, which stands at the chancel step looking like something that ought to be held under licence. Mr Purdie, who is seventy-six, gives her away and gets through it. The Colonel is in a suit nobody has ever seen. Hester Bevan cries from the second hymn, upright and without a sound, and would knock down any man who mentioned it.

Pascual Pobil stands at the front in a grey suit with his hair flattened and cannot get his face to stop doing what it is doing, and Reggie stands at his shoulder with the ring in his waistcoat pocket.

Spilsbury is very happy.

He wants that stated, because he is going to be accurate about the rest of it and accuracy cuts both ways. He is not managing anything and he is not pretending. He has checked. He sat in the fourth row of a hot church and watched a man he has spent six years being carefully received by stand up and get everything, and what he felt was straightforward and good.

Then they said it out loud, and ninety people said it back at them, and somebody's aunt shouted something in the middle of the vows and the entire church went up.

And then they signed the register.

---

He did not budget for the register.

It takes about ninety seconds and nobody looks at it except the vicar and the two witnesses and a man in the fourth row who has spent his professional life on what gets written down and what does not.

Two names. In ink. In a bound book with a number on the spine, kept by a parish and copied to a registrar and held by a government, permanently, in duplicate, where anybody may apply and see it.

A record of a thing that happened, with both names in it.

Alec Spilsbury sits in the fourth row of the Anglican church at the Bay on the fourteenth of July 1962 and thinks about a biscuit tin on a shelf in a rented room above a chandler's in Vigo, which has moved four times and been into three countries, and which holds the only archive of his life, and which one other living soul has ever seen and that only because he was in the room, and which will be put out with the rubbish by a landlord inside a fortnight of his death.

Then he stops thinking about it, because they are coming back down the aisle and he is on his feet with the rest of them and he means it.

---

## The schoolroom

There are two chairs missing either side of him at the end of a row and he cannot account for it and puts it down to somebody having counted badly, which is the first thing he has got wrong all week.

The speech is not what he expects.

Reggie gets up with nothing in his hands — Spilsbury knows there were four drafts, because he watched him write the first one on Thursday morning and be dissatisfied with it out loud — and does not do the rum-and-anecdote thing at all. He does the second night on the island and Alpha. He does 1954 and not listening. He does 1956, and *there is a paragraph nine in a Colonial Office Order because of it*, and half the room has no idea what that means.

Then: *I have never once seen him do a thing that was easy instead of the thing that was correct.*

Then: *I have had the best of it, and I've had it for nine years, and I'm handing it over today with extremely bad grace* — and stops dead in the middle of it, for about four seconds, in front of ninety people.

Spilsbury does not laugh at that part.

He sits at the end of a row with his hat on his knee and watches a man he has known for sixteen years arrive, publicly and without warning, at something he has plainly never once said to himself; and he puts his hand flat on the crown of the hat and leaves it there, because the alternative is to do something with it.

---

And then Codrington, three rums in and a week deep in a Colonial Office Order, shouts PARAGRAPH NINE, and is sat on by Hendy.

About six people in that room understand it, and one of them wrote the annex, and Spilsbury laughs until he has to take his glasses off and is the last person in the room to stop.

*No animal shall be held otherwise than in the company of at least one other of its own kind, save upon the written certificate of a veterinary surgeon, and then for a period not exceeding fourteen days.*

*Fourteen days,* thinks Alec Spilsbury, with his glasses in his hand. *And I have twelve.*

And then, almost immediately: *no.*

That is a false analogy. It is a false analogy of the cheapest available kind, the sort of thing a man produces at a wedding with a glass in his hand; the provision concerns a wild animal in a licensed pen, the number is derived from a mortality curve and not from anything else in the world, and to put the two figures beside each other is to do exactly what he spent four hundred and eleven pages refusing to do, which is to let a number mean something it does not mean.

It takes him about four seconds to get rid of it.

It is the only observation he strikes out all fortnight.

---

## The step

They get back to the cay at about ten. He takes his jacket off and sits down on the step of the hut at the top of the row and Reggie comes and sits down six inches away, which is what is available.

"You did that well."

"I meant all of it. I especially meant the bit about Marguerite."

And here is what happens in the next forty minutes, set down as honestly as he is able, which is to say: entirely honestly about the machinery, and not at all about the reason.

---

It begins, as far as he can tell, with *she is exactly as good as he is*, which is true and which he says because Reggie has got the fourteen months wrong and will go on getting it wrong and somebody ought to put one accurate stake in the ground.

And then, in the gap after it, he finds that he is going to tell him.

He examines the decision, because he examines everything. It takes him about eleven seconds and it comes out clean.

*One.* It is finished. It finished in the spring of 1957 and it finished well, and a fact that can no longer hurt anybody is a fact and not a confidence.

*Two.* It finished in a marriage, this afternoon, in front of ninety people and a register, and there is now nothing in it that can be turned against Pobil by anybody, including by Pobil.

*Three.* Reggie Tar has just stood up in a schoolroom and been publicly, accurately and generously grateful to that man for nine and a half years — and the account he gave was missing a term. He does not know what he was being given or what it cost to give it. He is walking about with an incomplete record of his own life and there is exactly one man on earth who holds the missing page.

*Four.* Spilsbury does not strike out observations, and he does not leave the unpleasant ones off, and he has been leaving this one off for six years.

All four of those are true. He has checked them. He would defend any of them in a room.

And not one of them is why.

---

Why is that at four o'clock this afternoon he watched two people be entered in a book by a government; and that in April he will be in Bergen and in July he will be here and between those two dates there will be three hundred and forty days; and that this is day four of twelve, and that he has been counting downward since the eleventh and has written that down and has not asked himself what it means.

Why is that he is fifty-two and has had, since 1946, one standing item of another man's attention and nothing else whatever that is his, and that he has spent the whole of this fortnight in a state he has recorded under the heading *tired* on three separate evenings.

Why is that when a man cannot say *I do not think I can do this for another twenty years at twelve days a year*, because saying it would require an answer and the answer would be Reggie Tar resigning a directorship inside a week — then what he does instead, at half past ten at night, on a step, in the dark, is begin, very carefully and with excellent evidence, to hand the man over to somebody who is there.

He does not see that. He will not see it tonight and he will not see it in Ålesund.

He thinks he is completing a record.

---

"There is one thing you did not know," he says, "and you were the only person in that room who did not; and I have been deciding for six years whether it was mine to say, and I have decided this evening that it is, because it is finished and because it finished well."

Reggie goes still.

"Go on."

"You know that he was in love with you."

---

## The fifteenth

He makes the entry in the morning, at the table in the hut, with the shutter up and the whole thing an hour old.

*14.vii.62. Married 11 a.m., Anglican church, the Bay. Ninety people. Excellent.*

*Told R. about P. on the step at c. 10.30 p.m. Reasons: (i) finished; (ii) finished in a marriage and cannot now be used against him; (iii) R.'s account of P. this afternoon was missing a term and he is entitled to the whole of it; (iv) six years is long enough. Stand by all four.*

*He then told me the date of the wedding was moved so that I should be in the room. P. asked M.S. in March. I did not know and had noted the coincidence in this book in April as carrying no weight. Strike that out. The reading was wrong and it is the only one I have got wrong this year.*

*I also advanced the proposition that he would have been better with P. He took it apart in about four sentences — no control, no second arm, single observation, and the design wrong in any case. He was correct on every count and I conceded it on the step, and he then went and got the wrong year for the brake as well, which I did not concede and which I am still turning over.*

*The above is not envy. I have checked it twice and it is not envy.*

And then, four lines further down the same page, under nothing, connected to nothing:

*Separate matter. The fortnight arrangement is going to be harder than I estimated in February. Recorded so that I have it in writing and can look at it again in the spring, when I shall no doubt find I have been making too much of it.*
