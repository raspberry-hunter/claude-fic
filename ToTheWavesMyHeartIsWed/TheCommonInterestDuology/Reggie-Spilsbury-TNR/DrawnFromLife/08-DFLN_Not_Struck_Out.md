# Not Struck Out

## Twelve days

He has costed the fortnight, as usual, on the ship, which is what a man does with nine days and nothing to read.

Twelve days on the island. Of those, four and a half go on the material, properly, with the record open, because that is what the application says and because he will not put a false hour into a form that Hester Bevan keeps in a cabinet. Two go on Pobil's protocol for the old female, which will be a fight and which he is looking forward to. One goes on being polite to the Colonel.

That leaves the evenings, and there are eleven of them, and in eleven evenings there will be perhaps three hours in total in which two men are alone in a room with the door shut and no reason for anybody to come up the path; and he has done that arithmetic every July since 1958 and has never once got a different answer and has never once stopped doing it.

The launch comes round the head at four and the whole station is on the jetty.

Brown. Thinner than last July and greyer than last July, and it is the eleventh of the month and he is fifty-two years old and his whole chest does the thing it has done at eighty feet since 1946.

"*Tar!*" he says, across ten feet of water, because there is a boatman behind him and eight people in front of him and the public register is the only one available.

---

## The reading

He gets it on the fourth day and it is not a difficult reading. He has taken harder ones off a dead animal.

The material is as follows.

There is a second chair at the far bench which has not been put back under it since, he would estimate from the wear on the boards, the winter. There is a second lamp of a better make than anything else in the room and it is not in the Institute's inventory, which he knows because he asked Mrs Bevan for the inventory on the Tuesday about something else entirely and read the whole of it in four minutes, as one does.

Pobil looks at the clock at breakfast on a Wednesday in a manner that has nothing to do with the tide.

Pobil's English deteriorates on a Thursday.

And on the Thursday morning Spilsbury walks into the long room at about eleven, and there are two people at one microscope with their heads eleven inches apart arguing about a field, and neither of them looks up, and he walks straight back out again with his hands behind his back and stands on the path for a moment.

*Ah,* he thinks.

And then, with more feeling than he expects: *oh, thank God.*

---

## Concerning Dr R. Tar

Reggie has not the faintest idea.

Spilsbury tests this on the Friday, without much finesse, by remarking at the bench that Dr Sealy appears to be a considerable woman, and Reggie says, "She is, she's extremely sound, it's a great pity about the other business," and goes back to the tide gauge.

He tests it again on the Sunday and gets the licence.

On the Monday he tests it a third time, which is unscientific and he does it anyway, and gets: "Sixteen months, Alec. Sixteen months on a boat and she's never given him an inch. I told him in November and he took it very well."

And Spilsbury says, "Did he," and goes away and sits down.

Here is the thing he has known since about 1947 and has never once found a way to say out loud without it sounding like a complaint.

It is not that Reggie Tar cannot read people. That is what the whole of that building believed for thirteen years, and it is wrong, and Spilsbury has spent fifteen years unable to correct it without explaining how he comes to know.

The man read Nicholson in a lift in 1954 in about four minutes — found the one thing on earth that would move him, which was being the sort of Director who gets asked — and came away with four days and then with four animals, and with no apparent sense of having done anything at all. He had Jean Leslie wrong for four years, and then was told, and had the entire shape of it in nine seconds, and went and put it in a minute book with the number of instances counted out. He will look at a table for nine seconds and tell you which column is a lie, and a column is only a man being careful.

The faculty is superb. It is simply not running.

Nothing about a face suggests to him that there is anything inside it; so he does not look; so he does not see. Aim him — say *what is going on with that one* — and he will tell you inside a minute and be right. Nobody ever aims him. He is surrounded by people who spent a decade concluding that he was cold and then stopped expecting anything.

And there is exactly one standing exception on the face of the earth, and Spilsbury is it.

He has been read, continuously, without ever having asked to be, since November of 1946. Reggie knows inside four seconds of a door opening what sort of evening this is going to be, and adjusts, and does not appear to have done anything. He knew about Meikle before Vigo and said so on a path. He knew in 1949 what Spilsbury actually minded about that building before Spilsbury had admitted it to himself, and wrote it down in the middle of a letter about mortality tables, offhand, in one sentence, and has never once referred to it since.

It is the most valuable thing Spilsbury owns. He is aware of what it means that he would not trade it for the rest of his life put together, and he has written that sentence down twice and both times has let it stand.

Because he has now had two separate occasions on which he has sat down and worked out honestly, with a pencil, that Reggie Tar would be better off without him. February 1956 was one. March 1957 was the second, and he got as far as the wording. Both times the arithmetic came out the same way right up until it reached this item, and then it stopped; because he cannot get round the fact that there is one man alive who looks at him without being asked to, and that if he goes there will not be another one, and he is fifty-two.

That is not a reason. He is entirely clear that it is not a reason. It is an appetite, and he has never in sixteen years managed to argue himself out of it, and he is not going to pretend in his own notebook that he has.

So: no, he is not going to say anything about Pobil. And not only for the reason he will give Mrs Weald on the fifteenth, which will be true and will not be the whole of it.

The whole of it is that telling Reggie Tar a thing about Pascual Pobil would not be telling him a fact. It would be aiming him.

And that man, aimed at that man, would have the doctor and the early boat and the second lamp inside a minute — and then, four seconds later and without the smallest effort, 1953 to 1957, and every single thing that was standing in the long room for ten months in 1956 while he was standing in it.

He is not going to do that to Pobil. He is not going to do it to Reggie either.

The licence will do.

---

## Concerning Mrs Weald

She is the other thing he does not budget for, every year, and ought to by now.

In 1958 she declined to answer him on a verandah and he has thought about her declining it a good deal more than he has thought about the answer. In 1959 she had the collection in two teak cases on a fixed bench forty-two inches off the floor. In 1960 she had written to Oporto herself. This year she has a new pair of spectacles, made in Kingston to nine and a quarter inches, and she tells him about the man locking the shop door in a manner that suggests she has told no one else about it at all.

What he notices this July, and writes down that evening because he does not carry a thing like this in his head, is this:

Nora Weald arrived on that jetty in October 1957 as the first person in ten years to come into that building without already being inside the arrangement. She worked the whole of it out, unaided, inside four months. He would put her arrival at correctness on Pobil at February 1958, and on Tar at rather earlier than that.

And in three and a half years she has not used one syllable of any of it.

Not to make herself necessary, which is the commonest use. Not to be kind, which is the second commonest and is worse. Not for leverage with Mrs Bevan, not for a better bench, not on a bad night to her own husband.

He has been in this field twenty-nine years and among people rather longer, and he has met perhaps four.

He does not tell her this. He tells her, instead, about a firm in Oporto that has changed hands and a man there who will still do unbuffered stock in quantity, and she writes the address on the back of something, and that is the correct currency and both of them know it.

---

## The fifteenth

She lets him off it, on the verandah, which she need not have done.

He goes down there at half past nine intending to be decent about 1958 and manages about sixty per cent of it. He tells her the reading instead of asking for it, which is the least he can do and which he has been planning since Antigua. She gives him the thing about Reggie not revising, because nothing has come in that he counts as data, and he laughs for eight seconds, which is the most he has laughed in a year.

And then he says more than he means to, as he has done at that woman every July since 1958, and she does not make anything of it, which is why he does it.

What he says is that Pobil has been decent to him for five years without a single lapse in circumstances where nobody on earth would have blamed him for one; that he has never found a way of saying so that would not be an insult; and that the man is now going to be extremely happy entirely without his assistance and that he finds he is glad about it in a way that is nearly unmanageable and has nobody to say so to.

"You've just said it," she says.

Which is true, and is a good deal cheaper than what he actually has, and he goes up the path at ten past ten and stands outside his own door for a moment before he opens it.

Because here is the whole of the item, and he is not going to hand it to a woman on a verandah, and he is not going to hand it to Reggie either, and he has now been carrying it for five years.

He knows what it is to want a particular man across a room for years without a form of words available. He has the complete series: 1946 to 1950 in a building where a wrong word cost you your clearance and then your name; 1950 to 1956 in letters that could not say anything; and 1957 to now at four thousand miles and twelve days a year, with a date written in a notebook because an open interval would kill him.

And the man Pobil wanted was his. Was, is, has been since a November evening about a coat. And for ten months in 1956 Pobil watched the whole of it at four feet — in a building of six people, over a long table, on a path at night — and never once in five years has he let it near the work, or near Reggie, or near Spilsbury himself in a single sentence.

There is no way to acknowledge that. *I know what you wanted and I know you did not get it and I know you were decent about it* is not a kindness; it is a man being told that his privacy was never real.

So the item is not struck out and it is not entered anywhere, and it has been sitting in the notebook, unheaded, since February 1957.

---

## The twentieth, at the north rail

He does it on the twentieth because he is leaving on the twenty-third and because a thing not done in a fortnight is a thing not done for a year.

They have been at the protocol for three days and have disagreed about every line of it and it is now, in consequence, a good protocol. Pobil has a bucket. Spilsbury has his hat in his hand.

"I am going to say something and it is not about the animal."

"*Bueno*," says Pascual Pobil, and goes on looking at the water.

"You are going to marry that woman before next summer. I am not asking you to confirm it and I should prefer you did not." He turns the hat round once. "I have costed what it will be worth to you and I am not going to pretend to you that I have not, because we have both spent our lives estimating things and I would rather be accurate than tactful."

"That is the first true thing you have said to me in five years that was not about tissue."

"Yes. I am coming to the rest of it." He stops, and starts again, because he has had it written since about Antigua and it will not come out in the order he wrote it. "You have been correct with me since May of 1956. In circumstances where nobody would have said a word against you for being otherwise. Not once, not in ten months in one building, not in five years of twelve days, not when you were entitled to, and I have looked for it, Pobil, because I look for everything."

"I know you do."

"I have never found a way of saying so that would not also be an insult, and I have had five years. This is the least insulting version I have got and it is still not good, and I am going to say it and then I am not going to mention it again in either of our lifetimes."

"Say it, then."

"Thank you," says Spilsbury. "That is the whole of it. Thank you, and I know what it cost, and I am not going to say how I know."

The water moves. Somebody up at the huts is putting a bucket away.

Pascual Pobil does not answer for a while.

Then he puts the bucket down on the concrete, which Spilsbury has not seen him do in front of anybody in five years, and turns round.

"You are not forgiven," he says. "You are not going to be. I have been perfectly clear about it since 1956 and I am not going to become vague about it now because you have made a good speech at a rail."

"No. I should think less of you."

"*Ay,* do not say what you would think of me, you are not entitled." Pobil puts his hand out. "Shake my hand, *hombre*."

So they shake hands at a rail at six o'clock in the evening over a bucket, which they have not managed at any greeting or parting in five years of greetings and partings.

Then Pobil picks the protocol back up and says, "Line four is still wrong," and they go on for another forty minutes, and Spilsbury walks back up the path afterwards and finds that he has to stop halfway and stand for a bit.

---

## Vigo

He writes it in the notebook on the ship, under the date, in the ordinary way.

*23.vii.61. Reading taken on the 15th, confirmed 20th. P. will marry M.S. inside twelve months. Spoke to him at the rail on the 20th; said the thing. He refused the forgiveness and took the hand, which is correct and is more use than the other, and I did not deserve the hand.*

And underneath, because he does not strike out observations and does not leave the unpleasant ones off either:

*I am fifty-two. I have eleven evenings a year and a date in a book. The above is not envy. I have checked it twice and it is not envy. It is that I should like there to be somebody who is glad about mine, and the only one available for the purpose is four thousand miles away and is the man in question.*

---

## The correspondence

The letters come to a chandler's on the waterfront at Vigo, where a man named Ferreiro holds them in a drawer and hands them over without comment, and there are, between August 1961 and March 1962, eleven of them, and they run to about nine pages each because Reggie Tar has never in his life written a short letter to anybody except an editor.

Spilsbury keeps all of them, as he keeps all of them.

The material relevant to this matter appears as follows.

*14.ix.61.* Four pages on the surfacing intervals, and then: *Pobil has been in better spirits this fortnight than I have seen him in two years and I cannot account for it. The licence is not up until March.*

*2.xi.61.* Six pages on the old female, who is failing, and then: *I have it. It's Gamma. He has been at that rail since the autumn and she has come up four pounds and he will not say so out loud in case he jinxes it. You would not credit how superstitious that man is about a weight.*

*19.xii.61.* Three lines at the bottom: *Mrs Bevan has put a second bed into the bungalow spare room against an inventory and would not tell me what for. I suspect an inspector in the new year and nobody has told me.*

Spilsbury reads that one twice, in a rented room above a chandler's, with the rain going sideways off the Atlantic, and laughs until he has to put it down on the table.

*8.ii.62.* *She has come through the February. Four pounds up and her rate is back and Pobil sat down on the concrete. I wrote to you in the autumn that I could not account for him and I want it on record that I accounted for him in November and was right.*

He writes back, on all eleven, about the fixation.

---

## Ålesund

The letter from Norway comes on the seventh of February and he reads it standing up in Ferreiro's doorway and then goes out and walks to the end of the mole in the rain and stands there for about half an hour.

A state post. A station, a budget, four people under him, and the cold-water holding work, which is the only work in this field he has ever actually wanted to do, and which is being offered to a man of fifty-two who was dismissed by a commercial house in 1956 for writing three letters about controls.

He does the arithmetic on the mole in the rain, because he does the arithmetic everywhere.

Vigo to the cay is a boat and another boat and about eleven days. Ålesund to the cay is a boat, a train, another boat and about nineteen, and it is a post with a season in it, and the season is the summer.

He stands at the end of the mole and understands, with total clarity, that the best thing that has happened to him since 1946 is going to cost him the only thing he has.

Then he does it again, because he does everything twice, and gets a smaller answer and a worse one.

He does not lose July. July is an application in a cabinet and it will go in every year and it will be granted every year and he will be on that jetty on the eleventh. What he loses is everything round the edge of July.

From Vigo a thing could be got up. Vigo is on a route; boats touch it; a letter is eleven days; and a man could, at need, be somewhere both boats touched — has been, once, at Funchal, for a fortnight in October 1958, which was not an application and was not in anybody's cabinet and is the best fourteen days of his life. From Ålesund there is no getting up. There is no halfway. There is the fortnight, and there are the other fifty weeks, and nothing whatever in between them; and the fortnight will cost him fifty days out of a working season with four people under him, every year, asked for out loud, of a board that will grant it and will notice that it has granted it.

That is the item, and it is not the one he had a minute ago. Not that he will see him less. That there will be one visit a year with no margin round it, and that a man who has spent thirty years not being remarked upon will now be a director with an annual unexplained absence to the same island, entered in a minute book by people he has not met.

He puts his hat back on.

Then he goes back and writes the acceptance, because the animals in the northern holding stations do not care in the slightest about his arrangements, and because there are six hundred men in the water off Norway in his head and have been since 1944, and because Reggie Tar would resign in the morning if he thought a post had been refused on his account and would never once say so.

He writes to the cay the same evening. Nine pages about the fixation, and at the bottom:

*I have been offered Ålesund and I am taking it. It is nineteen days from you and not eleven. The application will go in for July as usual and every July after it, and I shall be on the jetty on the eleventh, and I want you to write the number down tonight as you made me do on a jetty in 1957, because you are no better at an open interval than I am and you have simply never admitted it.*

The reply is four words long and arrives at Ferreiro's on the fourth of March.

*ENTERED. ELEVENTH OF JULY. — R.T.*

---

## The fourteenth of April

The letter with the news in it comes on the ninth of March and he reads it on the stairs.

Six pages of nothing whatever, and then two and a half about Pobil being married on the fourteenth of July at the Bay, and then — and this is the part Spilsbury takes upstairs and sits down with — a full page in which Dr R. Tar sets out the arithmetic.

*Two years and four months, Alec. March of 1960 to July of this year. She never gave him one single inch in the whole of it and he never said a word to a living soul and just kept getting on the boat, and I want it said somewhere that I always thought so.*

He reads it three times.

Then he gets the tin down off the shelf, because the item requires it.

---

It is a biscuit tin and it has been a biscuit tin since 1947 and it has moved four times and gone into three countries, and it holds everything Reggie Tar has ever sent him, in order, and there is nothing whatever remarkable about that except that Reggie does not know it exists.

The one from 1949 is near the bottom and is two sheets and he does not have to look for it.

It was written four days after the night at the fire in the cold flat — the night Spilsbury sat on the hearthrug with his back against the man's shins and costed the whole thing out and said *if you want to stop, stop*, and said that the expensive part was Hollis in his room, and was told *don't be absurd*. Reggie could not say anything in the room. He said it on paper on the Tuesday, in about nine pages about mortality tables, and then, at the bottom, in the middle of a line, with total confidence and no evident effort at all:

*You costed the programme and Hollis and the spring allocation and you did it very well. You did not cost the part where you would have had to watch me do it from the other side of a corridor for two years, which is the only item on the sheet you actually minded, and which you have not admitted to yourself yet. I am not arguing with you about it. I am writing it down so that you cannot tell me afterwards that nobody said.*

He was thirty. He was right. He has never mentioned it again and, Spilsbury is entirely certain, does not remember writing it.

---

He sits with the two letters on the table for some while, the 1949 and the 1962, thirteen years apart, both of them containing the sentence *I always thought so* in one form or another, and only one of them correct.

Then he writes the reply.

Four pages on the fixation, which is genuine and which Pobil will want. And at the bottom:

*As to the other matter: I congratulate them both without reservation and I note that you always thought so. You have said so to me at some length. I have put your letter in the tin with the 1949 one.*

He reads it over.

It will be read aloud at a breakfast table in front of eight people, because that is what Reggie does with his letters, and not one person in that room will understand a syllable of it, and Reggie will say *what tin* and be annoyed for about four minutes and then forget it.

There is another version available. He can see the whole of it: three sentences, and the man would be sitting on the edge of the bed in the hut at the top of the row at midnight on the eleventh of July with the letter in his hand and the whole of 1949 opened up.

He considers it for about four seconds.

Then he folds the one he has written, and seals it, and takes it down to Ferreiro's before he can do anything else about it; and on the way back, at the corner, he takes out the notebook and puts in the last line of the entry, which he will have occasion to strike out in exactly ninety days and which is, in the event, the only reading he gets wrong all year:

*N.B. the wedding falls inside the fortnight. Coincidence — the applications have gone in for July since 1958 and the date will have been settled on the church and the district. No weight to be placed on it.*
