# Per Pro

Tuesdays start in October, and nobody tells them why.

Dacosta finds a note on her bench in Tar's small neat hand. *North pen, 2 p.m. Bring Whitlock. Wear something you don't mind.* That's all. She shows it to Whitlock at lunch, and he reads it three times, as if it might be a trick, and says, "What've we done?"

"Nothing," says Dacosta. "I don't think it's that."

"It's always that."

It isn't always that. It was, in 1969, their first term. There was a stranding in November on the reef off the second head, in daylight, thank God, and Tar took them because there was nobody else. They stood on the rocks with their mouths open while he did it. When he told them to get the sling under her they got it wrong, twice. The second time he turned round in the water and shouted at them with a face she'd never seen on anybody. Whitlock went white and dropped the line. Dacosta didn't cry until she was back in the long room, and then only in the lavatory.

He was grey all that winter. He was grey the year after. She remembers him at the long table in 1970, not eating, and the dinner with the man from Canada when he went out of the door with his dinner half eaten and somebody said *heart*. After that he stood on the jetty with a lantern when the boat went out and shouted at whoever was rowing, and never got in.

And last year was worse. Last spring was the worst she's seen anybody. There was a letter from Norway in March, and then nothing in his face at all, for months. He was the most polite man on the island and it was like being corrected by a wall.

Then something happened in the summer. Nobody knew what. He wrote an obituary for the Linnean, of the man whose specimen is in the deposit. Dr Leslie put the *Proceedings* in the library in October, and Dacosta read it standing up at the shelf and had to sit down, because of a paragraph about a torch.

And then the note on her bench.

The first Tuesday, she and Whitlock stand at the rail of the north pen in their oldest clothes, and Tar comes down the path with the long sling over his shoulder. It's the canvas one from the boathouse. He's carrying it himself, which he's not supposed to. The calf's mother is lying in the shallows at the near end, huge and placid, blowing every forty seconds.

"Right," says Tar. "You were both on the reef in 'sixty-nine."

"Yes, sir," says Whitlock, very carefully.

"You got the sling wrong twice."

"Yes, sir."

"That was my fault," says Tar. "I told you where to put it. I didn't tell you why. So when she moved you didn't know what to do, and I shouted at you, which doesn't teach anybody anything. I should've known that a long time before 'sixty-nine. Get in."

They look at each other.

"In the pen, sir?"

"In the pen. She won't mind. She's done this more times than you have. She'll lie there like a sofa." He drops the sling on the wall. "I'm going to tell you why, this time. And then you're going to do it until you're bored. And then you're going to do it some more."

---

They do it until they're bored. They do it every Tuesday from October to Christmas.

He doesn't shout once. That's the first thing, and it takes them a month to believe it. He stands at the rail in his shirtsleeves with his hands in his pockets. He tells them why the line goes to the shoulder side and not the tail side, and why you wait. He makes them explain it back to him in their own words, and when they get it wrong he says *no, try again*, and waits, as though he had all afternoon, which it turns out he has.

"If she rolls," he says, "don't fight her."

"She's six hundred pounds, sir. We couldn't fight her."

"You'll try. Everybody tries. You'll grab the line and hang on, because it's the only thing in your hands. And she'll roll further, because you've just given her something to pull against."

"So what do we do?"

Tar leans on the rail. "Let go. Wait for the roll to finish. She'll stop. They always stop. And then go again, from the shoulder side, as if nothing happened."

"As if nothing happened," says Whitlock doubtfully.

"As if you'd planned it," says Tar. "It helps. She can tell."

He's different. Dacosta keeps trying to say how, and can't. He's still Tar. He's still sharp, and he still makes you do your labels again. He still says *that's wrong* in a clear carrying voice in front of the whole long room. But he says it now as if it were interesting that it was wrong, and he waits for you to see why.

And he talks. Not about himself. But on the third Tuesday, when the sling's gone under the calf's mother clean for the first time and Whitlock whoops, Tar says, "Spilsbury could get a sling under a grey seal in a force six with one hand. He used to say it was a matter of geometry." Then he laughs, short, from the chest. Dacosta has been on the island two years and has never heard him say that name, or laugh like that, and she stands in the water to her waist and stares at him.

In December he makes them do it with their eyes shut.

"Why, sir?"

"Because it'll be dark," says Tar. "It's always dark. They never come up in the daytime when there's somebody to see. They come up at two in the morning in a norther." He folds his arms on the rail. "The lantern's no use past six feet. You'll find her by the sound of her breathing. And then you'll have to do this by feel. So shut your eyes."

They shut their eyes. Dacosta stands in the warm shallows with her hands on the calf's mother's great smooth side, feeling for the shoulder. She can hear Tar at the rail saying *left a bit, Dacosta, no, your left, there*, perfectly calm. She thinks that she's never been taught anything in her life by anybody who wanted so badly for her to know it.

---

The norther comes through on the eleventh of February, a Friday.

Dacosta's on tide watch from midnight. That means the tide hut at the end of the jetty with a lantern and the gauge and a flask of Mrs Joseph's tea, reading the level every quarter of an hour into the book. In a norther it means sitting in a wooden box on stilts that shakes in every gust and wondering how long it's going to stand. She doesn't mind it. She's done it for two years. She likes the book.

At twenty to two, between gusts, she hears it.

It's a long harsh sound out in the dark past the second head, too fast, and she knows it at once. She's heard it before, once, in daylight, in November 1969, from the rocks. She's heard it every Tuesday since October from the calf's mother at the other end of the scale, slow and easy.

This one isn't slow.

She stands in the door of the tide hut with the wind trying to take the lantern out of her hand and listens to it twice more, to be sure. Then she puts the lantern down and runs.

She wakes Whitlock by hammering on his door. He comes out pulling a jersey over his head, and she tells him, and he goes white, the way he goes white. She watches him think *Dr Leslie*, and remember that Dr Leslie's in Kingston for the Board till Sunday. She watches him think *Pobil*, and look out at the channel, where you can't see the Bay at all for spray, and there won't be a boat across till it's light.

"Tar," he says.

He turns, and so does she, and they both look along the row.

There are two lamps lit on the cay. There's the one in the tide hut, which is hers. There's the one on the verandah of the long room, which Mrs Joseph leaves on all night for anybody who needs it. At the far end of the row, the last hut, there's nothing.

It's dark.

It's been dark since November. Dacosta noticed it the first night, because everybody noticed. In two years she'd never once looked along the row at night and not seen it. You could see it from the Bay. If you were up at three with a sick animal or a bad set of figures, you looked along the row and there it was, and you knew he was up. And you could go and knock, and he'd come.

Then in November it went out, and stayed out. And he started coming in to breakfast looking as if he'd slept.

"We have to get him," says Whitlock.

"No."

"Yvonne, it's a *stranding*, we have to —"

"His light's out."

"So we *knock*."

"He's asleep, Tom." She's shouting over the wind, and she doesn't care. "He's *asleep*. He's been asleep since November. Have you seen him? He eats his breakfast. He laughed at the Christmas dinner, properly, I heard him. He's —" She doesn't know how to say it. "He's getting better. He's getting better and you want to go and bang on his door at two in the morning and tell him there's one on the reef."

"He'd want to know."

"*Course* he'd want to know. And then what? You know what he'd do." She points at the dark past the second head, where the breathing is. "Dr Sealy said not one. Not for any animal. I heard her say it to Pobil on the jetty. And he'd say he'd stay in the boat, and he'd mean it, and then she'd roll, and he'd be over the side. You *know* he would. You've seen him on the jetty with his hands in his pockets when we go out, like he's holding them down."

Whitlock stands in the wind with his jersey half on.

"If we lose her," he says, "and we didn't wake him —"

"Then we lost her," says Dacosta. "And it's on us. Not him." She takes a breath. "Tom. What were Tuesdays *for*?"

He looks at her.

"He taught us," she says. "Since October. He had us in the pen every week with our eyes shut. *It'll be dark. It's always dark. They come up at two in the morning in a norther.*" Her voice has gone odd, and she lets it. "He *knew*. He knew he wouldn't be allowed to go. He knew one would come up in the night and it'd be us in the boat. That's why. That's what Tuesdays were for."

Whitlock looks along the row, at the dark hut at the end, for a long moment.

Then he gets the other arm into his jersey.

"Boathouse," he says. "Get the long sling. I'll get the lantern."

---

The reef is a horror.

Whitlock has thought about it, on and off, since November 1969. He's thought about it lying awake, and in the pen on Tuesdays with his eyes shut and the calf's mother warm and patient under his hands. He'd thought he knew. He didn't. It's black and loud, and the wind's coming across it flat and hard, full of spray that stings. The lantern in the bow's no use at all past about six feet, exactly as Tar said. He can see the water in the circle of light, and the coral coming up out of it like teeth, and nothing else.

They find her by the sound of her breathing.

Dacosta's at the oars. She's the better rower and she knows the reef. He's in the bow with the lantern held out over the side, listening, and he hears it before he sees anything, over to the left. It's long and harsh and too fast. And then the light finds her, a great grey side high up on the coral, rising and falling, rising and falling, with the water draining out from under her in the dark.

"Left side down," says Dacosta behind him. She's panting. "Tom. She's on her left side. Shoulder's up."

"I can see."

He can't see. He can see a grey side, and a flipper, and the light running wet over her. He gets out onto the coral, into the water to his knees, and it's cold, colder than he's ever known the sea here. He puts his hands on her, and she's hot and shaking, and her whole side heaves under his palms. Her breath comes out of her with a noise like tearing.

He counts it. He's supposed to count it. He gets to twelve in a minute, or what he thinks is a minute. He's counting under his breath with the spray in his face and his heart going like a hammer. Somewhere around eight he loses the watch in the dark and has to guess. Twelve. He'll put twelve.

"Water," he says, stupidly, because it's the next thing on the drill. "The temperature."

"*Tom*."

"It's on the *list*."

Dacosta makes a noise that might be a laugh, and puts the thermometer over the side on its string. He hears her count under her breath to a hundred and eighty in the dark, and then she reads it by the lantern.

"Twenty-four," she says. "Now can we get the sling on her?"

They get the sling on her.

It takes twenty minutes, and it's the worst twenty minutes of his life. The line keeps slipping in the wet, and the coral takes the skin off his shins, and he can't see his own hands. But he's done it forty times in the pen with his eyes shut. His hands know where to go even if he doesn't. Shoulder side. Find the flipper, go behind it, under. Feed the canvas through. He can hear Tar's voice, quite calm, at an imaginary rail somewhere above his head in the wind. *Left a bit. No, your left. There.* And it goes under.

"Got it," he shouts. "It's under, it's —"

And she rolls.

He feels her start to go before he sees it. The whole mass of her shifts under his hands and heaves, and the line jerks in his fist, and she's rolling away from him, further up the coral, in the dark, with the sling going slack. And he grabs it. Of course he grabs it. It's the only thing in his hands. He hangs on with everything he's got, and he's being pulled over into the water, and she's rolling further, and he can hear her breath change —

*Let go.*

He lets go.

He doesn't know how. His hands open. He goes backwards into the water to his chest, and gets up spluttering. He stands there, in the dark, in the cold, with his hands empty, and does the hardest thing he's ever done, which is nothing.

*Wait for the roll to finish. She'll stop. They always stop.*

She stops.

She's further up than she was, and her breathing's terrible. But she's stopped, and she's still, and she's on her left side again with the shoulder up, and Dacosta's beside him in the water now with the lantern held high.

"Again," says Dacosta. Her voice is shaking. "From the shoulder side. Tom. As if we'd planned it."

"As if we'd planned it," he says.

They go again.

---

The tide turns at a quarter past four.

They've had her in the sling for an hour and a half by then, with the sling's poles braced on the coral, holding her upright and off her lungs while the water goes out from under her and there's nothing left to hold her up but canvas and two people. Whitlock's arms have stopped feeling like arms. Dacosta's cut both of hers on the coral, badly, and hasn't said anything about it. He only sees it when the lantern swings, black in the lamplight, running down to her elbows.

Then the water starts coming back.

He feels it first round his shins, a pull the other way. Then it's round his knees. Then it's lifting her, very slowly, the weight going off the sling a little at a time. She feels it too. She stops shaking. Her breathing slows, and slows again. Ten minutes after the turn she's floating, just, in the sling, in the black water on the edge of the reef, blowing.

"Now?" says Dacosta.

"Not yet." He doesn't know how he knows. "Let her get the feel of it."

They wait. At twenty to five, when she's been floating on her own for a quarter of an hour and has started, very slightly, to pull against the canvas, towards the dark and the open water, Whitlock says *now*. They slip the poles and let the sling go slack. She takes it with her, the whole canvas sling, off the edge of the reef into the dark, and they don't care.

They hear her blow once, out past the edge. A long, deep sound, slow.

Then, a long way off, again.

Whitlock sits down in the water on the coral. He doesn't mean to. His legs just stop. He sits in the sea to his chest in a norther at a quarter to five in the morning, and puts his hands over his face, and cries like a child. Dacosta stands over him with the lantern and doesn't say anything, and after a while he realises she's crying too.

---

He writes up the log in the long room at half past five.

He sits at the long table in his wet clothes, with Mrs Joseph's tea things round him. He doesn't know when Mrs Joseph came. She's put a pot down and a blanket round each of them without a word, and gone back to her kitchen. His hand's shaking so badly he can hardly hold the pen. He does it anyway, because it's in the drill too. The log first. Everything else after.

The stranding log has columns. Date. Time. Animal. Location. Staff present. *Staff called.*

He stops at that one.

"What do I put?" he says. "For *called*."

Dacosta's sitting across the table with her cut arms held out in front of her on a towel. Mrs Joseph cleaned them and wanted to dress them, and Dacosta said later. She looks at the column for a long time.

"Put what happened," she says.

"He'll read it."

"Yes. He'll read it. He reads everything." She puts her head down on her arms on the towel. "Put what happened, Tom. It's a log."

So he writes it. Dr Leslie at Kingston. Dr Pobil at the Bay, no crossing. And then, because it's what happened, and because it's a log:

*Dr Tar not called (lamp out).*

He looks at it. His handwriting's dreadful. He can't make it any better. He finishes the entry, *05.30 boat in*, and puts the pen down, and puts his head down on his arms beside the log. He means to rest his eyes for a minute before he goes up.

---

Dacosta wakes because somebody's standing over them.

It's broad daylight. The long room's full of sun, and the wind's dropped, and there's a smell of cold tea. At the end of the table, with the stranding log in his hands, is Tar.

He's been up a while. She can tell. He's dressed, and he's shaved, and he's holding the log open. Dr Pobil is standing a little way behind him, with his hair on end, as though he's come over on the first boat in a hurry. Dr Pobil's face, as everybody on the island knows, shows everything. It's showing so many things that she can't read any of them.

She lies very still with her head on her arms and waits.

Across the table, Whitlock lifts his head. She watches him see Tar, and see the log, and go the colour of the tea things.

"Sir," he says. "Dr Tar. Sir, I'm sorry, we should've — your light was out, sir, and Dacosta said —"

She shuts her eyes. *Oh, Tom.*

"Your fourth figure's wrong," says Tar.

Dacosta opens her eyes.

Whitlock's staring at him. Tar's turned the log round, and he's looking at it, not at them, the way he looks at a set of figures on the bench.

"Resp. twelve a minute," says Tar. "It'd have been nearer fourteen, with her on her left side on coral in that wind. You were frightened and you counted slow. Everybody does."

"Yes, sir," says Whitlock faintly.

"The rest of it's right." He runs his finger down the column. "Turn at four-fifteen, away at four-forty. She'll have floated inside ten minutes of the turn. So you held her a quarter of an hour after she was floating before you slipped her."

"She wasn't ready, sir," says Whitlock. "She wasn't pulling yet."

"No. She wasn't." Tar looks at the entry a moment longer. "I'd have let her go at five minutes. In that wind, in a hurry, I would have, and she'd have gone straight back up on the coral and we'd have started again." He looks at the log. "That was very well done. Both of you. I couldn't have done it better."

Dacosta stares at him.

In two and a half years on this island she has heard him say *that's wrong* perhaps a thousand times, and *again*, and *no, try again*. She's heard him say *better* this autumn, in the pen, once or twice. She has not ever, once, heard him say *well done*. Across the table Whitlock has gone slowly red from the collar up, and is looking at the log as if it had spoken.

Tar puts the log down in front of him, and takes a pen out of his own pocket and puts it down beside it.

"Correct it. Initial the correction. Then go to bed, both of you. Mrs Joseph will bring your breakfast up." He looks across at Dacosta, at her arms on the towel. "And put something on those arms, Dacosta, I'm not having you septic."

She sits up. Her arms hurt, and her head hurts, and she has no idea what's happening.

"Sir," she says. "Aren't you — we thought you'd be —"

She doesn't know how to finish it. *Angry. Furious. Out of the door. At the jetty with a lantern.* She doesn't know which one she means.

Tar looks at her.

"I am," he says. "Go to bed."

---

They go. They get up stiffly with their blankets round them, and go out of the long room towards the stairs, and she looks back from the door because she can't help it. Tar's still standing at the end of the table, looking at the two chairs they've left. He doesn't look at them. He isn't going to say anything else.

On the stairs Whitlock says, "He's *what*?"

"I don't know."

"He said we did well. He said he couldn't have done it *better*." Whitlock's voice cracks on it. "And then he said *I am*. Am what? Angry? He didn't look angry. He looked —" Whitlock stops on the landing. "I don't know what he looked."

"Both," says Dacosta. "I think. I think he's both."

There's a window on the landing that looks down into the long room through the high inside panes. She doesn't mean to look through it. She does.

Tar's sitting down at the long table, in Whitlock's chair. He's taken his glasses off, and he's got his hand over his eyes. Dr Pobil's sitting beside him, not saying anything, and as she watches, Dr Pobil puts his hand on the back of Tar's neck, just for a moment, and takes it away. Then he pours two cups of tea out of Mrs Joseph's pot, which must be stone cold by now, and pushes one across the table.

Tar drinks it. All of it.

Dacosta stands on the landing with her blanket round her shoulders and her arms throbbing, and watches him drink a cup of cold tea with his hand over his eyes. She understands, quite suddenly and completely, that they did the right thing. That he knows they did. That he isn't going to say *that* part, not today, and perhaps not for a long time. He's said the other part instead, the part he could, out loud, in front of Dr Pobil. *I couldn't have done it better.* She thinks that for today it's enough, and that it's a great deal more than she was expecting.

"Come on," says Whitlock gently, behind her. He's seen too.

"Yes," she says, and goes up.

---

She sleeps till four. When she wakes the room's full of gold light, and her arms are dressed. She doesn't remember that. Mrs Joseph, probably, or Dr Sealy, if she came over.

She gets up and goes to the window.

The channel's flat as glass. There's a launch coming back round the second head from the reef, slowly, with the engine putting. Dr Pobil's at the tiller, and Tar's in the bow, sitting sideways on the thwart with one arm along the gunwale. He isn't looking at the cay, or the jetty, or anything in front of him. He's looking back the way they came, out past the edge of the reef, at open water, as though there was something out there he'd been to see, and had seen, and wanted to keep looking at a little longer.

Dacosta stands at the window in her nightdress with her bandaged arms folded, and watches the launch come in.

Then she goes and finds Whitlock, who's awake and sitting on his bed staring at nothing. She takes him down to the long room, and they sit at the long table in the late sun, and correct the fourth figure. They initial it, both of them, as they were told.
