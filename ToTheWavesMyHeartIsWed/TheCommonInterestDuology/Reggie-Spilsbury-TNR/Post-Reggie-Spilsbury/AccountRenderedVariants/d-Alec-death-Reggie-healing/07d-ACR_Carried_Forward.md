# Carried Forward

The March letter's late.

Reggie doesn't know he's been waiting for it until the first week goes by and it hasn't come. It comes twice a year, March and September, and it's come in the first week of the month since 1957, in the form, three lines. Hester used to bring it through to the long room and put it on his bench without a word, and since 'sixty-eight Jean has. It's the depositor's report, *in accordance with the terms of deposit*. He reads it once and hands it back, and Jean files it, and neither of them says anything, and it's the only thing in the post all year he'd walk across the cay to meet.

By the fourth of March it hasn't come. He tells himself it's the Bergen boat, or the weather in the North Sea, and doesn't believe either.

Jean comes out to the north pen at half past ten on the Thursday with a letter in her hand.

It isn't the letter. He can see that from the rail. It's the wrong envelope, long and white instead of square and buff, and the stamp's right and the hand's wrong, round and careful, a hand that learned its English from a book. And Jean's walking the wrong way. She doesn't walk to the pen, she walks towards him, straight down the path from the office, without looking at anything else. Jean looks at everything.

She stops at the rail beside him and holds it out.

"It's addressed to the Director," she says. "I've read it. You'd better."

He takes it.

*Statens Havforskningsstasjon, Ålesund. 24th February 1971.*

*Dear Dr Leslie,*

*It is my sad duty to inform you, as the Director of the Institute at which Dr A. Spilsbury's specimen is held on deposit, that Dr Spilsbury died on the evening of Wednesday 17th February, at Ålesund.*

He reads that sentence three times. It doesn't change.

*He was taking the evening temperature from the station quay, as he did each day in all weathers, and was found at the foot of the quay steps by the harbour watchman at about seven o'clock. The doctor believes it was his heart and that it was very quick. He was in his sixty-first year.*

*I write also because I think it would have been his wish that it be recorded. The watchman, who is a careful man, took the thermometer from his hand and read it before he did anything else, as he had often seen Dr Spilsbury do. It read four degrees.*

*My wife and I knew him for fourteen years. He was a very private man and we did not ask him things. He would not keep a notebook, which we always found strange in so exact a man. He was a long time here with something the matter, and we never knew what it was. I tell you this only because you are, I think, the nearest thing to his colleagues that remain, and someone there may wish to know that he was respected here and will be missed.*

*His solicitor in London will write to you concerning the deposit.*

*Yours faithfully,*
*H. Rasmussen, Director*

Reggie folds it up again along the creases. He does it carefully. He gives it back to Jean.

"Thank you," he says.

Jean looks at him. He doesn't know what his face is doing. He'd have thought, if anybody had asked, that it'd do something. He'd have put money on the weather, on the voice coming on, on the shoulders coming down half an inch. None of it comes. There's nothing for it to come for. He can feel the place where it would be, the way you'd feel a tooth that's been taken out, with your tongue.

"Reggie," says Jean.

"I'm all right."

"You're not."

"No," he says. "But I'm going to do the weights. Would you tell Pascual? Not now. After lunch. I'd rather he heard it from you than from me, I'll do it wrong."

Jean stands there for a long moment. Then she puts her hand on his arm, which she hasn't done in seventeen years, and takes it away, and goes back up the path to the office.

He does the weights. He does all of them. He gets every figure right, because he checks them twice. The calf's mother comes up every forty seconds, blowing, and he counts that too, from habit, and gets it right as well.

*Four degrees.*

His hand has gone to his inside pocket, on the left. It's been going there since April, since the letter. *I should like to see you. That is the whole of it.* Jean put it down by his elbow in April and said *that one's not mine*, and he read it standing up, and put it in the pocket, and it hasn't come out since. He's had eleven months to answer it and he knew every day of them exactly why he wasn't going to.

He isn't going to now, either. There's nowhere to send it.

He stands at the rail of the north pen with his hand flat on his chest, over the pocket, over the letter, and understands that this is what the rest of it is going to be. Not a door he's holding shut. There isn't any door. There's a wall, and a letter in his pocket from the other side of it, and no reply possible, ever, because he left it too long.

He doesn't cry. He keeps waiting to, and it doesn't happen.

Pascual finds him there at a quarter to two. Pascual's face is wet and blotched and he isn't trying to hide it, and he stands at the rail next to Reggie and doesn't say anything at all. After a while he puts his arm round Reggie's shoulders, hard, and holds on.

"I'm sorry," Pascual says. "*Hombre*. I'm so sorry."

"I know."

"I liked him. God help me, I'd liked him from the first day. He'd a face like the ash."

"He did," says Reggie. "He always did."

They stand at the rail. The calf's mother comes up. Reggie counts it. He goes on counting it, and Pascual goes on holding on, and neither of them says anything else, and it goes on being true.

---

The lamp's lit every night.

Marguerite can see it from her verandah. She's been able to see it since 1957, when she first sat out here with Pascual and asked him about the man at the end of the row. There are lamps along the cay from about eight, three or four of them, and one at the far end by itself, a little brighter than the rest. Pascual fills it on Saturdays and has done for thirteen years, and has never once said why, and she has never once asked.

It used to go out at some point in the night, when the oil burnt down. Now Pascual says it doesn't. He fills it on Wednesdays as well now, because it's burning dry by the middle of the week. It's lit when she goes to bed and it's lit when she gets up, and it's lit on the Sunday nights he sleeps in her spare room, where there's no lamp at the end of anything. On those nights he sits up in the cane chair on the back verandah until two or three with the light off, looking across the channel at it.

She isn't a fool, and she doesn't think it's the dark he's afraid of.

He's a better patient than he was at Christmas, which frightens her more than anything. He takes his tablets where Pascual can see him. He goes to bed at ten, and she knows perfectly well from Pascual that he doesn't sleep. He eats what's put in front of him, all of it, without noticing what it was. He's no trouble at all. He does everything she tells him, the way you'd do what you were told by a nurse in a waiting room, because it's easier than not.

His pulse, on her verandah on the second Sunday in April, is ninety-two.

"You're not doing anything," she says.

"I'm doing everything you said."

"Yes. And you're not doing anything." She keeps two fingers on his wrist. "You're taking the tablets and eating your dinner and going to bed at ten and lying there till six. It's the most thorough nothing I've ever seen, and I've seen a great many men do nothing."

He looks at her, and there's nothing in his face at all. That's what frightens her. She has known Reggie Tar's face since 1952, and in every one of its states it has been *doing* something. Delighted, or furious, or wrong, or charming, or pretending not to be in pain on a set of steps. It has never once in nineteen years been blank.

"I don't know what you want me to do," he says.

She doesn't know either. It's the first time in thirty years of doctoring she hasn't.

There's a dinner at the long table in the middle of April, with a man from Plymouth and his wife, and Marguerite is there because Jean asked her to be. Halfway through, the man from Plymouth puts down his fork and says, with real kindness, that he was so very sorry to hear about Spilsbury. He's heard they were at the Labs together, after the war. It must have been a shock.

The whole table goes still. Marguerite watches Jean's hand stop on the way to her glass, and Pascual turn to stone, and old Hester Bevan at the far end, who still comes over on Tuesdays, put her napkin down very slowly.

And Reggie says, "Yes."

That's all. He doesn't lean back. His shoulders don't come down. Pascual has told her about that, about what used to happen to his face when anybody said that name, the charm coming on like a lamp turned up. She's seen it herself, once or twice, across a table, and thought it the oddest and saddest thing about him. It doesn't happen. He sits at the head of the table with his hands either side of his plate and looks at the man from Plymouth, and says *yes*.

"Yes," he says again. "We were. It was. Thank you."

Then he says, quite clearly, in an ordinary voice, "He was the best man I ever worked with."

Nobody says anything. The man from Plymouth looks as though he's put his foot through a floor he didn't know was there, and Reggie gets up and says *excuse me*, and goes out onto the verandah. Marguerite hears him go down the steps, not one at a time. He takes them at his usual pace, which he's not supposed to, and she lets him.

"I didn't mean —" says the man from Plymouth.

"You didn't do anything," says Hester Bevan from the far end of the table, in the voice of a woman who has run the Institute's accounts for twenty years. "You said a true thing. It's allowed now." She picks up her napkin again. "Eat your fish."

---

The parcel comes in the second week of May.

Jean brings it over to the Bay on the Sunday, because it's addressed to the Director and it's got the Ålesund stamps on it, and she's opened the outer wrapping. Inside there's a letter from Rasmussen and something flat, in brown paper, which she hasn't opened. She puts both on Marguerite's table after dinner, in front of Reggie, and says *it's yours, I think*, and goes out to the verandah with Pascual and shuts the door.

Marguerite stays. She isn't asked to, and she isn't asked to go, so she stays, at the other end of the table, with her hands in her lap.

He reads the letter first. It's short. He reads it aloud, which she doesn't expect.

"*Dear Dr Leslie, in clearing Dr Spilsbury's rooms we found almost no papers, which did not surprise us. We found one photograph, propped on the table by his bed, which his landlady tells me he had kept there since the autumn. As it appears to have been taken at your Institute, I return it to you, in case it is of some value to the station. I hope I do right. Yours faithfully.*"

He puts the letter down.

He doesn't open the parcel for a long time. Marguerite watches him look at it, and she knows what it is. She's heard about John Weald's photograph, from Pascual, from Jean. She knows what it looks like, and that there's a man in Norway who kept it on his table, and that Reggie has known that since October.

He unwraps it. The brown paper comes off square.

It's the north pen, from the steps, with the light behind. There's a man at the rail, side-on, in his shirtsleeves, with his glasses pushed up and his hand flat on his chest. The print has gone slightly soft at one edge, the left, where a thumb has held it. It's been held in the same place so many times that the gloss has worn off in an oval the size of a fingertip, just above the top of the nearest post.

Reggie looks at it for a very long time.

Then he turns it over.

There's pencil on the back. Marguerite can see it from the end of the table, but not read it. It's a single line, small, in a hand like print.

Reggie makes a sound.

She's heard men make every kind of sound there is. She's heard the sound people make when they're told, and the sound they make when it's over, and the sound in between. This is the in-between one. It comes out of him as if something's been pulled, and he puts both hands flat on the table either side of the photograph and bends his head over it, and his shoulders go.

She gets up. She goes round the table and sits beside him, and puts her arm across his back. She doesn't say anything, because there isn't anything. After a moment the door to the verandah opens and Pascual comes in, and sees, and comes round the other side and kneels on the floor by the chair. He puts his forehead against Reggie's arm. Jean stands in the doorway with her hand on the frame and doesn't come in, and doesn't go.

Marguerite reads it, over his bent head, because she's a doctor and she reads what's in front of her.

*10.x.70. 1½ st.*

It's a date and a figure. That's all. The measurement a man in Norway took off the posts of the north pen rail with his thumb, eight months ago, and wrote down on the back of the only thing he had.

"He hadn't anything to write it in," Reggie says, into the table. His voice is wrecked. "He hadn't a book. He burnt them. He told me. He'd nothing to write in, and I thought — I told John he wouldn't have written it down. I *told* him. And he wrote it on *me*."

"Yes," says Marguerite.

"He measured me. And he couldn't say. And I *knew* he'd measured me, I knew in October, and I was good for a *week*, and then I —" He stops.

Marguerite waits.

"And he was sitting in Norway with this," Reggie says. "And he couldn't do anything. And I let him."

"Yes," says Marguerite. There's nothing else that's true.

He cries for a long time. It's the first time since the fourth of March, and she thinks, from the sound of it, that it may be the first time for much longer than that. Pascual stays on the floor with his head against Reggie's arm, and Marguerite keeps her hand flat between his shoulder blades. She can feel every one of his vertebrae, which is her fault and his, and she counts his breathing because she can't help it. At some point she stops counting.

When he's done, he sits up. His face is wrecked. His glasses are on the table, and she doesn't remember him taking them off.

He looks at the photograph again. At the man at the rail.

"A stone and a half," he says. "He was right. He was always right about weights. It was a stone and a half and four pounds, Marguerite, you weighed me the next week."

"I remember."

"He'd have wanted the four pounds."

And something happens at the corner of his mouth. It's small and wet and terrible, and it's the first thing his face has done on purpose since March.

She sees it and thinks: *there*.

The Linnean writes in June.

It's the Secretary. It's a very proper letter, typed, on the Society's paper, and it asks whether Dr Tar would consider contributing the notice of the late Dr A. Spilsbury for the *Proceedings*. He's understood to have been Dr Spilsbury's principal colleague at the Labs in the years after the war, and the Society is conscious that Dr Spilsbury's later work was done abroad, and that there may be few in this country now who knew him well.

Reggie reads it on the verandah of the long room and puts it in his pocket, the right-hand one, and doesn't answer it for a week.

It isn't that he can't write it. He could write it in an afternoon. He knows the dates. He knows the work, every paper, better than anybody living. That's the trouble.

In March 1957 he sat in an office and said a sentence about that man, in the voice, leaning back, and it went into a file in an Ulsterman's hand. It's still there. *There has always been something about Dr S.* It's the only thing he has ever said about Alec Spilsbury in public, and it was the worst thing he's ever said about anyone. He said it to keep a man at a window out of a file. It's the only thing the Crown has on record, and it'll be there when they're all dead.

He's frightened, he finds, that if he sits down to write the notice, the voice will come. That he'll lean back, on paper, and be charming about the decline curve, and the notice will be the minute over again, only longer.

He says so to Pascual, on the step, on the Tuesday. He doesn't say the part about the file. He says the part about the voice.

"So don't use it," says Pascual.

"It isn't a thing I use. It comes."

"It didn't come at dinner." Pascual's looking at him sidelong, from the step below. "With the man from Plymouth. Marguerite said. You said he was the best man you ever worked with, just like that. No lamp. Nothing."

"That was different."

"How?"

Reggie sits with that.

"There was nothing to put it on for," he says slowly. "It came on so nobody could see. There was something there not to be seen, and if they'd seen it, it'd have cost him. Or you. Or Jean."

"And now?"

He looks at his hands. "Nobody's going to take the licence off Jean because I miss a dead man. There's nothing left it can cost anyone."

"Then there's nothing to put it on for," says Pascual. "Is there. Write the thing."

He writes it.

---

It takes him all summer. That surprises him. He'd thought an afternoon.

He writes it at the table in the hut at the end of the row, at night, with the lamp. He writes it in his own hand, not the typewriter, because he wants to see what the hand does.

The hand's all right. That's the first thing he finds out. It's slow, and it goes back over things, and it crosses out whole pages. But it doesn't do the thing he was afraid of. It doesn't lean back.

He writes the dates first, because they're easy. Born 1910. The Labs, before the war and after it. The decline curve, which is still the best thing of its kind anybody's done. Ålesund, 1957 to the end, and the hooded seals of the West Ice, a series of fourteen years that nobody else on earth could have kept without missing a season. The fifty-one days on the wall of the north pen in 1956. The specimen, which is in the deposit, which is the reason anybody applies to the Martin at all.

Then he writes the other part, which isn't dates.

He writes about 1946, and a fuse box, and a man with a torch in his teeth talking him through the circuit in a perfectly level voice while the whole building went dark. He writes about a corridor, and being told his curve was wrong, which it was, by a man he'd never met, in front of four people, with no warmth at all and complete accuracy. He writes that he was furious for a week, and then went back and found the error, and it was exactly where the man had said. He writes that Alec Spilsbury could not bear to be approximately right, and made everybody near him unable to bear it either. He writes that this was the greatest kindness he ever did anybody, and that he'd have denied it was kindness and produced figures to show that it wasn't.

He doesn't write *I*. Not about anything that matters. He writes *his colleagues* and *those who worked with him*, and the forms hold it, the way a form holds anything you put in it.

But somewhere in August he finds that he's crying at the table in the hut at two in the morning over a paragraph about a torch. And he finds that he doesn't mind, and that the paragraph's good, and that he's going to keep it.

---

The solicitor's letter comes in July, and Jean brings it out to the pen, the way she brought the first one.

"You'll want to see this too," she says.

It's about the will. There isn't much. There's the deposit, which is the Institute's already. There are his books and reprints, left to the Martin's library, *to be kept together or dispersed as the Director sees fit*. There's a sum, not large, left to the Institute for a studentship, *to be named or not named at the Director's discretion*.

And there's a deposit account at a bank in London. It was opened in May 1957, and it's had fifteen pounds paid into it every quarter day since. The will leaves the whole of it to Frank Nicholson, formerly a technician at the Labs, *whose present address is not known to me*. It directs the executors to make every reasonable effort to trace him.

Reggie reads it twice.

Fifteen pounds every quarter day. Since midsummer 1957. Fourteen years. Sixty pounds a year, into an account for a man who'd gone away, because Alec hadn't known how to stop.

"Did you know?" says Jean.

"No." He hands it back. "Of course he did. Of course he did that."

"The solicitor says they've had a man looking since March. There's no trace after 'fifty-seven."

Reggie looks at the calf's mother coming up.

It isn't square. It'll never be square. He knows that the way he knows the weights. Nothing anybody pays now will give Frank back what he had in 1957, and there's nothing Reggie can say to him. Nobody should be asking Frank for anything. Frank has been asked for enough.

But the searching costs money. The solicitor's letter says so, apologetically, in the third paragraph.

"Write back," says Reggie. "Tell them to go on looking. Tell them to send the bill for it to me. Not the Institute. Me."

Jean looks at him for a long moment.

"You're sure."

"Yes," says Reggie. "It's mine to pay. It always was. He just got there first."

Jean nods, once, and goes back up the path. She calls the studentship the Spilsbury Studentship in the minutes of the Board in September. Nobody on the Board asks why, and nobody on the Board would have known to.

---

Pascual reads the proof on the step, at the end of August.

He doesn't have to. Reggie didn't ask him to. But it came back from London on the Tuesday boat, the galley in long strips, and Pascual picked it up off the table in the hut. He read it standing up in the doorway, and then he sat down on the step and read it again, and Reggie sat down beside him and let him.

He's slow. He reads English slowly, the way he always has, with his lips moving slightly on the long words. When he gets to the torch he stops, and puts his hand over his mouth, and goes on.

He gets to the last paragraph.

Reggie watches him get to the last sentence. He knows exactly where it is on the strip. He wrote it in the first week of July, before anything else in the paragraph was there, and he's crossed out every sentence round it since, and not that one.

*There was always something about Alec Spilsbury; those of us who worked beside him knew exactly what it was, and were the better for knowing.*

Pascual stops.

Reggie watches him stop. He watches him go back, and read it again, and go very still.

"You said that," says Pascual.

"Yes."

"In the office. In 'fifty-seven. With Halloran. You said —" Pascual's voice goes, and he gets it back. "You said that. *There's always been something about him.* I was by the window. I heard you. It was the worst thing I ever heard you say. You said it like —"

"I know how I said it."

"And you've put it *here*." Pascual holds up the strip. His hand is shaking. "In his — in the thing about him. For the Linnean. For *ever*."

"Yes," says Reggie. "It's in a file. It's still in the file, in Halloran's writing, and it'll be there till they burn the building down."

"So why —"

"I wanted it somewhere else as well. In the right voice. Signed." He looks at the proof in Pascual's hand. "He knew about the other one. I told him myself, in 'fifty-seven, how I'd said it. In the voice, leaning back. He carried that off to Norway. I wanted there to be one on the record in the right voice. Even if nobody knows but me."

Pascual's looking at him.

"And me," Pascual says. "I know."

"And you."

"*Why* did you say it?" Pascual says. "In the office. You told me it was the licence, and it was the licence, but it wasn't all of it. I've known that for fourteen years, *hombre*. You asked me not to ask, so I haven't."

"Pascual."

"I was in that room. I was by the window. And I don't know why you said the worst thing you ever said, in that voice, when you didn't have to."

Reggie looks across at the channel, at the Bay, at the house above the harbour where there's a light on in the kitchen.

He's kept this for fourteen years. He kept it because it would have cost. If Pascual had known, in 1957, he'd have gone into the office and told Halloran to put *his* name in the file instead. If he'd known in 'sixty, or 'sixty-five, or last year, he'd have wanted to put it right. He'd have wanted to write to Norway, or go there, or do something enormous and Pascual-shaped. And it couldn't be put right, and every attempt would have cost somebody again, the same somebodies, and so he kept it.

There's nothing now that it can cost. There's no one in Norway for Pascual to write to. There's nothing to put right. It's a closed account. You can show a man a closed account.

"I'll tell you," says Reggie. "I'll tell you the first bit first, because it's the most important. It was worth it. I'd do it again tomorrow. Whatever you think in a minute, it was worth it. Have you got that?"

Pascual stares at him.

"Have you got that, Pascual?"

"I've got it."

So he tells him. The inspector's question, whether he'd known about Alec when he brought the new man onto the station. Halloran looking, while he asked it, at the window. At Pascual, in the chair by the window, who'd come in 1952 and been Reggie's friend from the first week and had a face that showed everything. Halloran was making a note, and there were three answers Reggie could have given. And there was one that would make him stop looking at the window. So Reggie said it, the true thing, in the voice. And Halloran stopped looking at the window, and wrote it down, and never looked at Pascual again.

He tells it quite plainly, in order, the way he'd tell a result.

Pascual doesn't say anything for a long time.

Then he puts the proof down on the step, very carefully, square, the way Alec would have. And he puts his face in his hands, and sits there on the step of the hut at the end of the row, and Reggie puts his arm round him. It's the other way round, for once. It's been the other way round for fourteen years, and it's the other way round now.

"You should've told me," says Pascual, into his hands. "You should've told me, you *bastard*. You'd no right."

"I couldn't. You'd have done something about it."

"I'd have —" Pascual stops. "Yes. I would've. I'd have done something stupid."

"I know. That's why."

"And now there's nothing I can do."

"No," says Reggie. "Now you can't. That's why I can tell you."

Pascual lifts his head. His face is wrecked and wet and furious, and full of everything, the way it's always been. He looks at Reggie for a long time.

"It *was* worth it," he says finally. "Wasn't it. You said so first so I'd — you told me that first on purpose."

"Yes."

"You're a manipulative bastard, Reggie Tar."

"Yes," says Reggie. "I am. It's in the file."

And Pascual laughs. It's awful, and wet, and it breaks in the middle. Then Reggie's laughing too, on the step, with his arm round his friend, at two o'clock on an August afternoon with the proof of a dead man's obituary square on the step between their feet.

The *Proceedings* comes out in October. Jean puts the Institute's copy in the library, and a second copy on his bench, and doesn't say anything.

He reads it once. It's all right. It's better than all right, and it's in print, and it can't be taken back now, and his name's at the bottom.

He finds that he's taken the letter out of his pocket.

He doesn't remember doing it. He's standing in the hut at the end of the row, at the table, with the April letter in one hand and the *Proceedings* in the other, and the photograph propped against the wall where he put it in May. *I should like to see you. That is the whole of it. I have no other reason, and I shall not pretend to one.* He's read it so many times the fold's gone brown and soft as cloth.

He can't answer it. He knows that. He's known that since March.

But he finds, standing there, that he doesn't have to carry it about any more. He's carried it on the left, over his heart, for eighteen months, the way you'd carry a letter you meant to answer. He isn't going to answer it. That door isn't there. He can put it down.

He opens the drawer of the table. He puts the letter in, flat, and the *Proceedings* on top of it, open at the page. Then, after a moment, he takes the photograph from against the wall and lays it face down on top of both, so that the pencil shows. *10.x.70. 1½ st.*

He shuts the drawer.

His hand goes to his pocket about forty times over the next week. By the end of the month it's about ten. He notices every one, and doesn't mind, and doesn't try to stop it. He lets it go on and come down by itself, the way you'd let a fever come down.

He forgets the lamp on a Thursday in November.

He doesn't decide to. He comes up from the pens at ten after a late feed, dead tired, in the good way, the way he used to be tired in 1953 before there was anything to be tired about. He lies down on the bed in his shirt meaning to read, with the book on his chest and the lamp on the table unlit, because he hasn't got round to it.

He wakes at six with the book on the floor and the grey coming in at the shutters.

The lamp's cold. There's oil in it; Pascual filled it on Wednesday. It hasn't burnt at all. He's slept eight hours in the dark, and he lies there and looks at the ceiling and understands that he doesn't need it. He hasn't been waiting for anything. There's nothing to wait for. The post isn't coming, and nobody's on their way up the path, and there's no door at the end of the row with anybody on the other side of it. He can sleep.

He hasn't slept in the dark since 1957.

He lies there for some time. Then he gets up and goes down to the jetty with the thermometer and lets it down into the water. He waits three minutes and brings it up, and reads it.

It's twenty-six. November, and still twenty-six.

He's got nowhere to send it. He finds that he doesn't mind that either. He knows where it would have gone. He stands on the end of the jetty in the grey light, reading it, and says it aloud, once, to nobody.

"Twenty-six."

Then he goes back up to the long room, and has his breakfast, and eats all of it.

---

The norther comes through on the eleventh of February, a Friday. It blows all night, and he doesn't hear it.

He wakes at six on the Saturday with the book on the floor and the shutters rattling, and it's gone quiet outside, the way it goes when a wind's blown itself out. He lies there for a moment feeling rested, which he's still not used to. Then he gets up and puts his shirt on and goes down to the jetty with the thermometer, and stops at the top of the steps.

The big boat's been pulled up wrong. It's half across the slip, not square, with the oars thrown in anyhow, and there's weed round the rowlocks. The sling's not on its hooks in the boathouse. The long sling, the canvas one, is gone.

He stands there.

He knows what that is. He's known what that is since 1952. There's an animal on a reef, in a norther, at night, and somebody's gone out to it, and it wasn't him.

He goes up to the long room faster than Marguerite would like.

Whitlock and Dacosta are asleep at the long table, both of them, with their heads on their arms among the tea things and a blanket over each. Somebody's put the blankets on them. Mrs Joseph, from the look of the tea things. Their clothes are still wet at the cuffs. Dacosta's got coral cuts on both forearms, cleaned and not dressed, and Whitlock's got the stranding log open under his elbow.

Reggie turns it round very quietly and reads it.

It's in Whitlock's hand, which is bad, and gets worse towards the bottom, and every figure in it's right.

*12.ii.72. 01.40. Young female reported on reef off second head by Dacosta (on tide watch). Wind N, strong. Dr Leslie at Kingston. Dr Pobil at the Bay, no crossing. Dr Tar not called (lamp out). 02.05 boat launched, Whitlock and Dacosta. 02.30 animal located by breathing. High on coral, L side down. Resp. fast, approx 12/min. Water temp 24°. 02.50 sling placed. Animal rolled. Sling replaced (per drill, line to shoulder side). 04.15 tide turned. 04.40 animal refloated and away, E of reef edge. Heard to blow twice. Sling lost. 05.30 boat in.*

*Dr Tar not called (lamp out).*

He reads it several times.

He wants, very badly, to be angry. He can feel the shape of it ready to hand. *You should have woken me. Two of you, in a norther, in the dark, on that reef.* He's got the whole of it ready. He'd shout, and they'd deserve it, a bit. And under the anger, where he can feel it too, there's the other thing: an animal, a bad one, and he slept through it.

He hasn't slept through a stranding in twenty years.

He stands at the end of the long table with the log in his hands and looks at the two of them asleep. Whitlock's a third-year from Norwich, and Dacosta's a third-year from Bridgetown. They were first-years in 'sixty-nine, the last bad one, when the calf's mother's sister came up on the same reef in daylight. They'd stood on the rocks with their mouths open while he did it, and got the sling wrong twice, and he'd shouted at them. He can remember doing it. He can remember, more than that, thinking afterwards that shouting was how you taught it, because that was how he'd been taught it himself.

He'd been a very bad teacher in 1954. The worst there's ever been on this island, to a boy called Charles, who deserved better than anything Reggie gave him. He's been getting better at it since, slowly, a student at a time, and not fast enough. But this autumn, after the *Proceedings*, he'd found he wanted to do it properly. He can't have said why. So he'd had these two in the north pen every Tuesday afternoon from October with the long sling and the calf's mother, who's patient and huge and doesn't mind. He made them do it again and again, till they could do it with their eyes shut. Then, in December, he made them do it with their eyes shut. *Line to the shoulder side. If she rolls, don't fight her. Wait for the roll to finish and then go again.*

*Sling replaced (per drill, line to shoulder side).*

He puts the log down very gently, open where it was, under Whitlock's elbow.

Pascual comes over on the first boat after the wind drops, at about nine, with his hair on end. He's heard about it at the Bay from Alleyne's boy, who heard it from God knows where. He comes up the jetty steps two at a time, and into the long room, and stops dead, and looks at Reggie at the end of the table with the students asleep in front of him.

"You're here," says Pascual.

"Where else would I be?"

"I thought —" Pascual stops. His face does everything. "Alleyne's boy said there was a stranding, off the second head, in the night. In *that*. I thought you'd have —" He doesn't finish that either.

"I didn't," says Reggie. "I was asleep. They didn't wake me."

"Why not?"

Reggie picks up the log and holds it out to him, open. Pascual reads it with his lips moving slightly on the long words. He gets to the bracket and stops.

"Your light was out," Pascual says.

"Apparently."

"They looked along the row," says Pascual slowly. He's working it out as he says it. "They came out of the tide hut in the wind, and they looked along the row for a light. And there wasn't one at the end. So they didn't knock." He looks up. "*Hombre*. They'd have knocked. Every other night for fourteen years. You were always up. Everybody knew you were always up. It's why they'd come to you."

"I know."

"And they let you sleep."

"Yes."

Pascual looks at the two students asleep at the table with their cut arms and their wet cuffs. He looks at the log, and then at Reggie. Reggie can see him think of the reef in the dark, the water, the steps, Marguerite's *not one, not for any animal*, and not say any of it.

"They got her off," Pascual says instead.

"They got her off. By the drill." Reggie hears his own voice, and it isn't steady, and he lets it not be. "Line to the shoulder side. I taught them that in the north pen. On Tuesdays."

"I know you did. I watched you. I thought you'd gone soft."

"I had," says Reggie.

Whitlock stirs at the table, and lifts his head, and sees Reggie standing over him with the log. He goes the colour of the tea things.

"Sir. Dr Tar. Sir, I'm sorry, we should've — your light was out, sir, and Dacosta said —"

"Your fourth figure's wrong," says Reggie.

Whitlock stares at him.

"Resp. twelve a minute. It'd have been nearer fourteen, with her on her left side on coral in that wind. You were frightened and you counted slow. Everybody does."

"Yes, sir," says Whitlock faintly.

"The rest of it's right." He runs his finger down the column. "Turn at four-fifteen, away at four-forty. She'll have floated inside ten minutes of the turn. So you held her a quarter of an hour after she was floating before you slipped her."

"She wasn't ready, sir," says Whitlock. "She wasn't pulling yet."

"No. She wasn't." "I'd have let her go at five minutes. In that wind, in a hurry, I would have, and she'd have gone straight back up on the coral and we'd have started again." He looks at the log. "That was very well done. Both of you. I couldn't have done it better."

He says it because it's true. He says it, too, because nobody said it to him when he was twenty-three. He'd have given a great deal to hear it, and it costs him nothing at all to say.

Whitlock goes slowly red from the collar up.

He turns the log round and puts it down in front of the boy, and takes the pen out of his own pocket and puts that down beside it. "Correct it. Initial the correction. Then go to bed, both of you. Mrs Joseph will bring your breakfast up. And put something on those arms, Dacosta, I'm not having you septic."

Dacosta's awake too now, blinking.

"Sir," she says. "Aren't you — we thought you'd be —"

"I am," says Reggie. "Go to bed."

They go, stiffly, with their blankets round their shoulders, looking back at him from the door as though they're waiting for the rest of it. There isn't any rest of it. He watches them out.

Then he sits down at the long table, in Whitlock's chair, which is still warm. After a moment he takes his glasses off and puts his hand over his eyes.

Pascual sits down beside him and doesn't say anything at all. He doesn't need to. He puts his hand on the back of Reggie's neck, briefly, and takes it away, and pours two cups of cold tea from Mrs Joseph's pot, and pushes one across.

Reggie drinks it. It's terrible. He drinks all of it.

In the afternoon he goes out in the launch in the flat calm with Pascual at the tiller, round the second head, and they sit off the reef with the engine cut for an hour. At about three she comes up, a long way out past the edge, and blows, and goes down again. She's young and whole and moving easily, and she's no idea who went out to her in the night, and doesn't need to.

He watches her go.

The first Spilsbury Student comes on the Kingston boat in October 1972. She's late, because the boat is always late.

Reggie meets her on the jetty. He's the Consultant, and it's not his job, and Jean told him so. He met the boat anyway, because it's the first one, and because somebody should, and because the Board minutes say *Spilsbury Studentship* in Jean's hand and he wants to see who it's for.

She comes down the gangway with one suitcase and a notebook in her other hand, a young woman in her early twenties, very upright. She's from Port of Spain by way of the university at St Augustine, and her name on the Board papers is Baptiste. She looks at the jetty and the row and the long room and the north pen, all of it, from the top down. Then she looks at him, and he can see her deciding who he is.

"Dr Tar?"

"Yes. Welcome to the Martin."

"Thank you." She shakes his hand firmly. "I've read your papers, sir. And the other one. The obituary. In the *Proceedings*."

"Have you."

"I thought I ought to, as it's his money." She tucks the notebook under her arm. "I didn't know anything about him before, except the decline curve. And the seals. I've been told he was very difficult."

Reggie stands on the jetty in the sun, and waits for the weather.

It doesn't come. It hasn't come for a year and a half now. There's just the name, said by a stranger, in an ordinary voice, and the sun, and the calf's mother blowing in the north pen. The water's twenty-seven today. He took it at six.

"He was," says Reggie. "He was the most difficult man I ever knew. He told me my curve was wrong in a corridor in front of four people the first day I met him, and it was." He picks up her suitcase before she can stop him. It's heavy, and he doesn't care. "Come up to the verandah. There's tea. I'll tell you about him."

"About the work?"

"About him," says Reggie. "The work's in the *Proceedings*. You've read that. I'll tell you the rest."

She looks at him. She has a good look, careful, taking things in. He thinks she's the kind who writes down the time of everything. He's been on this island long enough to know one when he sees one.

"All right," she says.

They go up the path together, at his pace, which she notices and matches without saying anything. On the verandah Pascual is pouring out, and looks up, and sees who it is. He looks at Reggie with every single thing he feels going across his face, and says *sit down, sit down, you'll want tea*, and goes to find another cup.

Reggie sits down in the cane chair with the rail at his back, where he can see the pens and the row and the far end of the row, the last hut, where there's no lamp in the daytime and none, now, at night.

"It was 1946," he says. "At the Labs. There was a fuse box."

And he tells her. He tells her all of it that can be told, in the right voice, in the sun. She listens, with her notebook shut on her knee, and doesn't write anything down.
