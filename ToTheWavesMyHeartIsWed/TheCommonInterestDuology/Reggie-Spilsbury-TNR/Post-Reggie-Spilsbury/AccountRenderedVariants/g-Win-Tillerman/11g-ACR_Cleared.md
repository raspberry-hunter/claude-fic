# Cleared

By one o'clock he's decided to be useful.

It's the only thing for it. He's been in the house for an hour and a half. In that time he's read the *Gleaner* twice without taking in a word, and walked round the yard, and looked at the generator. He's stood in the kitchen doorway until the kitchen boy asked him if he wanted something, in a tone that suggested the kitchen boy had a fair idea what. Win's in the laundry at the back with the girls and the copper. He can hear her voice through two walls, telling somebody about starch.

He can't hear what she's saying. He finds he doesn't need to. The sound of her voice is quite enough to be going on with.

She's come through the kitchen three times since noon. She'd no call to come through the kitchen at all; the laundry has its own door to the yard. Each time he's been in the doorway to the passage, and each time she's come through it past him instead of round by the pantry, which is wider. And each time she's turned sideways to do it. She's done it slowly, facing him, so that the whole length of her goes by the whole length of him. Her hip, and her side, and her breasts, which go across his chest with nothing between them but her blouse and his shirt, so close he can feel the warmth of her through both. *Excuse me, Dr Tar*, she says, perfectly pleasantly, looking up at him from about four inches off. And goes on to the pantry for something she doesn't need, and comes back the same way.

He has, since May, kept up a reasonable discipline about not thinking of Win's breasts in the hours of daylight. It's a matter of attending to other things. It's worked tolerably well. Since noon he's had no discipline at all, and nothing whatever else to attend to.

The third time, he stood quite still in the doorway with his hands in his pockets and said *Win*, and she said *Mm?* over her shoulder, all innocence. There's nothing whatever innocent about Win Carew's shoulder. He's known that since May.

So he'll be useful. He's a grown man. He's fifty-four. He's got a doctorate and a Fellowship and a chapter in the *Proceedings*, and he'll not stand about a woman's house like a boy outside a sweetshop because of something she said on a road at twenty-five past eleven.

He'll find the leak.

He's been meaning to since November. He's got the figures in the back of his notebook, both ends of the joist and the time between, from two south winds and a north-easter. He's worked out the fall and the point it's got to be coming in, three feet left of the chimney, in the valley. He's only wanted an afternoon.

He gets the torch from the hall drawer. He gets the steps from the shed. He goes up to the landing outside room four, where there's a trap into the roof, and stands at the bottom of the steps, and thinks about Marguerite.

He goes up anyway. Slowly. One tread at a time, with his hand on the frame, as though Marguerite were standing at the bottom counting. He's allowed a stair. It's only a very tall stair.

It's a hundred and ten degrees in the roof and full of dust and a dead lizard. He crawls along the joist on his elbows with the torch in his teeth and his glasses sliding. Three feet left of the chimney, in the valley, exactly where he said, there's a place where the felt's gone and the light comes through in a thin bright crack. It's so exactly where he said that he lies there on the joist in the heat and laughs, with the torch in his mouth, until he has to stop and breathe.

He'll tell her. He'll show her the figures, and the crack, and she'll put it in the book.

He comes back down the steps covered from head to foot in eighty years of roof. He's got cobweb in his hair and a black streak down one side of his face, and he's grinning. And Win comes up the front stairs with a basket of clean sheets on her hip, and stops on the top step, and looks at him.

She doesn't say anything. She just looks. She looks at the dust, and the cobwebs, and his face. Then she looks, quite slowly, at the rest of him, all the way down to his shoes, and all the way up again, and she takes her time about the middle. Her face doesn't change at all, except that one corner of her mouth goes in.

He can't remember what he was going to tell her.

He knows it was something. He knows it was good. He stands on the landing with the torch in his hand and his mouth open, and it's gone, the whole thing, the joist and the fall and the crack of light.

"You'll want a wash," says Win. She goes past him into room four with the sheets. The landing's six feet wide, and she goes past him so close that her hip slides across his, slow, from one side to the other. "All over," she adds, from the doorway of room four, without turning round.

He hears her start making the bed. After a while he goes and has a wash.

---

At half past two he offers to help with the Ministry's bill.

She's at the desk in the little office by the fuse board, with the adding machine that doesn't work and the pencil that does. He sits across from her and says he'll check her columns, as he does sometimes, because he likes to. She gives him the sheet without looking up.

It's thirty lines. He goes down them once, and it's all quite straightforward.

Then she gets up. She comes round the desk, behind his chair, to point at something. He feels her before he sees her hand. She's leaning over his shoulder, with the front of her against his back and her arm past his ear. And it takes him perhaps a second and a half to establish, with the part of his mind that has never once in his life stopped establishing things, that at twenty past eleven on the harbour wall she had a brassiere on under that blouse, and she hasn't now.

He knows it the way he'd know a change in the weight of a sample. Her breasts are against his shoulder blades, soft and full and quite unconfined, through two thicknesses of cotton. When she breathes he feels them move. Her cheek is very nearly against his, and her breath's warm on the side of his neck. Her finger comes down on line nine and rests there.

"Mind that one," she says. Very low. Right in his ear. "Laundry. It's a big figure." She doesn't move. She leans, if anything, a fraction further. "Take your time over it, Reggie. I like a man who takes his time."

And her other hand comes up, quite lightly, to the back of his neck. Her thumb goes up into the short hair behind his ear, once, slowly, the way she did it last night against the door. Then it's gone, and so is she, round to her own side of the desk. She sits down and folds her hands on the blotter and looks at him with polite interest.

He looks at the sheet.

There are thirty figures on it. He knows that. He could have told you so a moment ago. He goes down them again, and every one of them is perfectly clear and perfectly meaningless, as though they were in Greek. All he can think is that at some point between the laundry and the bill she went upstairs, and shut a door, and took it off. On purpose. For this. He gets to the bottom somehow and writes a total in pencil. She takes the sheet back and looks at it.

"That's forty-two, Reggie."

"What is?"

"Line nine. Laundry. You've put it down as twenty-four." She turns the sheet round so he can see. "And you've carried a seven that isn't there."

He looks at it. She's right. He's added a column of thirty figures and got it wrong in two places. He's never — he can't remember when. 1938, perhaps. School.

"I'll do it again."

"You'll not," says Win, very kindly. She takes the pencil out of his hand. Her fingers touch his, and she lets them stay a moment longer than she needs to. She runs one fingertip down the length of the pencil as she draws it out of his grip, slowly, all the way to the end. Then she bends over the sheet. "Go and sit on the verandah. You're a danger to the accounts."

He looks at her. He looks, God help him, at the front of her blouse, and then back at her face, which is the wrong order, and she sees him do it.

"You've —" he says, and stops.

"I've what, Dr Tar?" says Win, correcting line nine.

He goes and sits on the verandah.

---

At four o'clock, Marguerite comes down the hill.

He hears her on the steps before he sees her. He knows her tread; he's heard it come up his own steps on the cay, at speed, more than once. She says something to the kitchen boy in the passage about Mrs Carew, and the kitchen boy says Mrs Carew's in the laundry, ma'am, with her arms in the copper. Then Marguerite comes out on to the verandah with her bag, and sees him, and stops.

"Reggie," says Marguerite.

"Marguerite."

"It's Tuesday."

"I know what day it is."

"Do you." She doesn't sit down. She stands there in the doorway with her bag in her hand and looks at him in the way she looks at a wound somebody's told her is nothing. "You're on the cay by Tuesday. You've been on the cay by Tuesday every week since January. Alleyne's boy has taken the first boat across this morning and the noon boat, and I watched them both go from my front window, and you weren't on either."

"No."

"So what are you doing at the Bay on a Tuesday afternoon?"

He's had four hours to think of an answer to that. He's had a doctorate's worth of training in the construction of a plausible account. And he finds, sitting there in the chair with Marguerite in the doorway, that he hasn't got one.

"There's a leak," he says.

"A leak."

"In room four. In the roof. I've been meaning to see to it since November. I've got the figures —" He reaches for his notebook. She doesn't look at it. He puts it back. "It's in the valley. Three feet left of the chimney. I found it."

"You stayed over a working day," says Marguerite, "for a leak."

"And the Ministry's bill. Win asked me to check her columns."

"Win's done the Ministry's bill unaided since 1958."

"The adding machine's broken."

"The adding machine has been broken since 1964. The whole Bay knows it's broken. She keeps it for show." Marguerite's face is doing nothing at all. "What else?"

He oughtn't to say anything else. He knows that perfectly well. He's been interrogated by better than Marguerite — by Jean, by a Board, once by a man from the Ministry of Defence. He knows the first rule is that you stop.

"I wanted to be sure he'd gone," he says.

"He went round the head at twenty past eleven," says Marguerite. "With a parcel. I watched that too."

"He might come back."

There's a pause.

"For something he'd forgotten," says Reggie. "He might have — he could have left something. In room nine."

Marguerite looks at him for a long moment. Then she puts her bag down on the table, and sits in the other chair, and holds out her hand.

"Give it here."

"It's not Sunday."

"I know very well it's not Sunday. Give it here."

He gives it to her. She puts her fingers on his wrist and looks at her watch, and her face does nothing whatsoever.

She's thirty seconds in when Win comes out with the tea.

She's come straight from the copper. Her sleeves are pushed up past the elbow and there's a damp patch down the front of her skirt, and her face is shining with the steam. She puts the tray down on the table between them, and says, "Dr Sealy. I'll not stop, I've the sheets in. There's lemon cake." And on her way back to the door she passes behind his chair, and her hand goes along the top of his shoulder as she passes, and across the back of his neck. Her fingers slide up into his hair at the nape, quite slowly, and out again.

"Drink your tea, Reggie," says Win, low, and goes back in.

Marguerite doesn't look up from her watch.

"That's spoiled it," she says. "Start again."

She starts again. He sits there with his wrist in her fingers, looking at the harbour, and does not look at the door Win went through, and does not think about the back of his neck, and fails at both. The minute goes on for some time.

Then she lets go and writes in the little black book.

"Well?"

"A hundred and four," says Marguerite. "At four o'clock in the afternoon. Sitting down." She shuts the book. "On a verandah. Looking at the harbour. And that was the *second* minute."

"It's the heat."

"It's eighty-six degrees, Reggie. You've been in the Caribbean twenty-five years."

"I went up in the roof."

"In the *roof*." She looks at him, and there's nothing in her face at all. It's her professional face, the one she uses on fishermen who've tried to set their own arms. "You went up a ladder. Into a roof. In August."

"I found the leak."

"I'm sure you did." She puts the book in her bag. She stands up. She looks down at him for a long moment. Then she looks through the verandah door, down the passage, to where Win has stopped in the kitchen on her way back to the laundry. She's standing at the stove with her back to them, lifting the lid on something, with her hip against the rail. Then Marguerite looks back at him.

He sees her work it out. He watches it happen, the way he's watched her read an X-ray: once, all at once, and then quite still. The leak. The adding machine. The man who might come back for something he'd left in room nine.

"I'll not ask," says Marguerite.

"Thank you."

"I'll not tell Pascual."

"*Thank* you."

"I'll tell Pascual," she amends. She picks up her bag. "Take one at nine. Before, not after. And, Reggie —"

"What?"

"Stay off the ladder," says Marguerite, and goes down the verandah steps and up the hill without looking back. He watches her go. Her shoulders are perfectly straight, and they're shaking.

---

Dinner's at seven, in the kitchen, the two of them. The girls have gone home and the kitchen boy's gone to his mother's, and there isn't a guest in the house. The Ministry doesn't come till Thursday. The house is so quiet he can hear the clock in the hall.

She gives him fish. He eats it. She puts a second piece on his plate while she's telling him about the laundry contract, without looking, the way she did at Marguerite's in 1971, and he eats that too. He doesn't taste either. He's watching her hands. They're square and brown and very capable, with the nails cut short and a burn across the back of the left from the copper, years old. He's known those hands since the fuse board. He's watched them do the day book two hundred times. He's never seen anything so beautiful in his life, and he can't stop looking, and he's sure she knows.

She knows. When she puts the second piece of fish on his plate she leans across him to do it, much further than the table needs, and the soft weight of her breast comes against his shoulder and stays there while she arranges the fish. "Eat up," she says, low, by his ear. "You'll want your strength." Then she sits down again, and takes up her fork, and goes on about the laundry contract as if she'd said nothing at all.

He eats the fish. He's got as far as thinking he's safe when something touches his ankle under the table.

It's her foot. She's slipped her shoe off. Her bare foot goes up the inside of his calf, very slowly, under his trouser leg, all the way to the back of his knee, and stays there. Above the table her face is perfectly composed. She's cutting her fish into small pieces. She doesn't look at him at all.

"Pass the salt, Reggie," says Win.

He passes her the pepper.

She takes it. She looks at it. She looks at him over it.

"That's the pepper," says Win.

"I know it is."

"I asked for the salt."

"I *know*, Win."

And she laughs. She puts the pepper down and laughs, with her hand over her mouth, for a long time, while he sits across the kitchen table from her with his ears going red and his heart going like the Ministry's adding machine, if it worked. He thinks she's the cruellest woman on the north coast and he's never been so happy.

---

At nine she does the day book on the top verandah.

She does it every night. She's not going to not do it for a little thing like this, she says, and sits down with it in the lamplight. He sits in the other chair and watches her write. *Laundry, 14 sheets, 22 pillowslips, towels.* *D. Carew departed, Kgn boat.* She writes that with no change of face. *Rm 9 vacated.* *Rm 4 roof: leak found (Dr T.). Valley, 3 ft L of chimney.* She's put the leak in the book. He hadn't told her. She must have got it from the kitchen boy, who'll have got it from the dust.

He watches her write his name, *Dr T.*, in her small neat hand.

The lamp's on the table between them. The harbour's dark. Across the channel there's the long low shape of the cay, and the long-room lamp at the near end, and nothing at the far one.

He takes one out of the tin in his waistcoat pocket and puts it under his tongue. Before, not after. She sees him do it. She doesn't look up from the book, but he sees the corner of her mouth go in.

She writes the last line. She blots it. She shuts the book and puts her pencil down on top of it, square.

"Go up," says Win.

He looks at her.

"Go up," she says again, and now she does look at him, across the lamp. Her face is quite calm, and her eyes aren't calm at all. "I'll be two minutes. I'm going to lock the back."

He goes up.

He goes up the stairs at the pace Marguerite allows, which is the hardest thing he's done all day. He goes into her room, and doesn't light the lamp, and stands in the dark by the door with his back to the wall. He hears the back door go, and the bolt. He hears her in the kitchen, and the lamp in the bar turned down. He hears the hall clock strike, and her foot on the bottom stair.

He counts her up them. There are nineteen. He's known that since November.

---

She stops outside her own door with her hand on the knob.

She's forty-eight years old. She's run this house fifteen years. She's buried her mother and brought up two children and paid a man's debts for twenty-one years and stopped. And she stands on her own landing in the dark with her hand on the brass, and her heart's going so hard she can hear it. She has to wait a moment before she can open the door.

She's been thinking about it all day. Over the copper. Over the sheets for room four, with him standing on the landing covered in roof like a chimney sweep, looking at her as though she'd hit him with a plank. In the linen cupboard at two o'clock, with the door shut and her blouse open and her heart in her mouth, undoing the hooks with her back to the shelves. She's never done such a thing in her life. She'd stood there afterwards with the brassiere in her hand, and thought *Winifred Carew, you're forty-eight*, and laughed out loud among the pillowslips, and put it on the top shelf behind the bolster covers.

Then the Ministry's bill, where he got line nine wrong. She'd have given a year's takings to see his face again when she told him. And Marguerite, and the tea, with Marguerite's fingers on his wrist, which was wicked, and she knew it was wicked while she did it, and did it anyway. And the fish. And the pepper.

She's done it on purpose, all afternoon, every bit of it. She's not ashamed. She's never in her life had a man to do it to, who'd come apart like that, in front of her, a little at a time, all day. It's the most fun she's had since the storm.

And now she's outside the door, and her mouth's dry.

She opens it.

He's there. He's right there, beside the door, against the wall in the dark, and he doesn't wait for her to shut it. He shuts it himself, past her, with his hand flat on the wood. Then his hand's on her waist, and he turns her round and puts her back against the door, and it's last night.

It isn't last night.

She knows it isn't in the first second, before he's even kissed her. He's got her against the door just the same, and his body's against hers from knee to shoulder just the same. But he's looking at her. It's too dark to see much, and he's got no glasses on, and he's looking at her anyway, close, with his whole face. And he isn't shaking.

"Do you know," he says, "what you've done to me today?"

His voice has gone right down. It's quite level. He might be telling her the time of the tide, if it weren't for what's underneath it.

She does know. She wants to hear it.

"Tell me," says Win.

"Three times through the kitchen." His hand moves on her waist, very slightly, his thumb along the bottom of her ribs. "Sideways. *Excuse me, Dr Tar.* I could feel every inch of you go by."

"You were in the way."

"I was in the doorway. You'd the whole pantry." His other hand comes up into her hair and finds a pin and draws it out, slowly, and drops it. She hears it hit the boards. "Then the landing. I'd found the leak, Win. I'd found it to the *foot*. I lay up there on the joist and laughed. And I came down, and you looked at me —" Another pin. "You looked at me as if you were pricing me by the pound, and I couldn't have told you where the chimney *was*."

"I was pricing you."

"I know you were." Another pin, and her hair comes half down on one side. "And then you went upstairs and took it off."

Her face goes hot in the dark. She doesn't say anything.

"Don't," he says. "You did. You'd one on at the harbour at twenty past eleven. You hadn't at half past two." His mouth's at her temple now. His voice is getting less level. "I've been adding up columns for forty years. I've not got one wrong since I was at school. And you leant on me, with nothing on under your blouse, and I couldn't feel anything in the *world*, Win, except you against my back. I couldn't have told you what nine *was*."

"I know," says Win. Her own voice has gone somewhere she doesn't recognise. "I was there."

"The pepper."

"I was there for the pepper as well."

"Marguerite's put me in her book at a hundred and four. Sitting down. On the *second* minute." The last pin. Her hair comes down all round her shoulders, and he puts both hands in it. "You put your hand on my neck *in front of Marguerite*. She's gone up the hill laughing. She'll tell Pascual. I've been out of my mind since noon. I've walked round your house all afternoon like a man with a fever. I've read the *Gleaner* twice and I couldn't tell you one word of it, I went up a ladder to get away from you —"

"Good," says Win.

He stops.

"Good," she says again, and takes hold of the front of his shirt in both fists. "I meant you to be. All of it. Every bit."

He laughs. It goes right through her; she can feel it where he's pressed against her. Then he kisses her.

It's hard. It's hard and hungry and it doesn't ask, and she opens her mouth under his and pulls him in by the shirt. He makes a sound against her mouth she's never heard him make. His hand's on her jaw, tipping her head back, and then his mouth's on her throat. Not the soft place under her ear, where he always goes first and waits. The front of her throat, where the pulse is, with his teeth. She hears herself make a noise, high and startled. It isn't a noise she'd have let out last night, with Desmond at the back of the house.

There's nobody in the house.

She thinks it all at once, with his mouth on her throat. The girls have gone home. The kitchen boy's at his mother's. The Ministry's in Kingston till Thursday. Desmond's on the Kingston boat, or in Kingston by now, in a bar, telling it. The house is empty from the cellar to the roof. For the first time since she bought it in 1958, there is nobody in Harbour House but her.

And him.

"Reggie," she says. "*Reggie*. There's nobody in the house."

He lifts his head.

"I know," he says, against her jaw. "I've known since seven. I counted them out."

"You *counted* —"

"The girls at six. The boy at twenty to seven. I heard you lock the back." He kisses the corner of her mouth. "I'd nothing else I could count, Win. You'd seen to that."

She gets his shirt open. She doesn't bother with the buttons; one of them goes somewhere in the dark and rattles across the boards, and he laughs into her mouth. He doesn't bother with hers either, not properly. He gets two undone and loses patience, and pulls the blouse open and down off her shoulders so it catches at her elbows. And then he stops.

He doesn't say anything. He just looks. There's a little light from the harbour, through the verandah door, not much. She can feel him looking all the same.

She's never thought much of them. They've been in the way since she was thirteen. They were the reason for the green dress, and the reason men at the Myrtle Bank looked at the front of her instead of listening, and after Joy they were never what they'd been. She stands against her own door with her blouse round her elbows and lets him look, and feels her heart going under them.

"You did," he says, very low. "You took it off."

"At two o'clock," says Win. "In the linen cupboard. It's behind the bolster covers." Her voice shakes. "I've never done such a thing in my life."

"Win." He puts his hands on her. His palms are hot. He's never had hot hands. He's always had cool ones, precise, like a man doing a delicate thing. Tonight they aren't precise at all. He holds her breasts the way he'd hold something he'd been trying all day not to think about, and his thumbs move, and she has to put her head back against the door. "I've been thinking about these since noon."

"Since *noon*?"

"Since May," says Reggie. "Since noon I've not been thinking about anything else."

She laughs, and it comes out as a gasp, because he's bent his head and put his mouth to her. He isn't slow about it, or careful. She puts her hand in his hair and holds him there and hears herself say *oh*, out loud, to the dark room. She's never in her life said *oh* out loud to anything.

He gets her skirt up. Not off, just up, rucked round her hips, with his hands on her thighs under it and the door hard at her back. He's holding her as though she might go somewhere.

"Tell me," he says, into her neck. "Tell me what you want. I want to *hear* you. There's nobody in the house, Win. I want to hear you say it."

"*There*," says Win. "There. *Harder*. Oh — yes —"

She hears her own voice go out through the dark room and doesn't care. She's never been loud in her life. Not with Desmond, not in the flat in Kingston with the walls like paper and the neighbours. Not here, in fifteen years of guests. And now she is. She says his name so it goes out through the verandah door and across the yard. It's the most astonishing thing. She keeps doing it just to hear it.

"Mine," he says, against her throat.

She goes still.

He feels her go still. He lifts his head at once, and she can feel him looking at her in the dark.

"I'm sorry," he says. His voice has gone uncertain for the first time. "I've no right to that. I know I've not. I said it last night, about him, and it didn't do me credit. It just —"

"Say it again."

He's quiet.

"Say it again," says Win. She puts her hand flat on his bare chest, over his heart, which is going like a hammer. "Not about him. About me. Nobody's ever said it to me like that. Not ever. Not once. Say it again."

"Mine," says Reggie.

"*Yes*." She pulls his mouth down to hers. "Yes. And you're mine. Do you hear? You're *mine*, Reggie Tar. Every bit of you. The Institute can have you nine to five and Jean can have you on Tuesdays, and Marguerite can have your pulse. But the rest's mine."

He makes a sound she's going to remember for the rest of her life. Then he gets his arms round her, and lifts her. He actually *lifts* her, all of her, and she's no small woman, a man with a heart Marguerite won't insure, off her feet and away from the door —

"Reggie! Your *chest* —"

"Marguerite said a hundred and ten."

"You're *over* a hundred and ten!"

"I know," he says, "I'm not counting," and he puts her down on the bed so hard the springs shout. She laughs so much she can't breathe, and pulls him down on top of her, laughing.

---

It isn't slow.

She'd wanted it not to be, and it isn't. She gets his belt undone while he's still kicking his shoes off, and her hands aren't steady, and she doesn't care. When she gets hold of him he says her name in a voice like a man who's been hit, and puts his forehead down against hers.

"Win. *Win*. If you do that I'll not —"

"Then don't," says Win, and lets go, and pulls him to her by the hips.

He comes into her all at once, and she arches up off the counterpane to meet him, and cries out, and doesn't stop. There's nothing careful in it at all. Not in him, and, she finds, not in her. She's got her heels hooked round the backs of his legs and her nails in his shoulders. His mouth is on hers, and then on her neck, and then lower, on her breast again, and she can't keep still. She doesn't try. The bed goes against the wall with every stroke, *thump*, *thump*, like somebody at a door, and she thinks wildly that the whole of the Bay must be able to hear it. And she thinks, *good*.

"Look at you," he's saying. "God, *look* at you. I've been watching you all day. You've no idea. In the laundry, with your sleeves up. With the *tea*. You put your hand on my neck and I'd have —"

"Tell me," says Win. "Tell me what you'd have —"

"Anything. Anything you liked. On the verandah. In front of Marguerite. I'd not have cared —"

"*Reggie* —"

"Win. Win, Win, God, *Win* —"

"I'm here. I've got you. Don't stop —"

"I'm not —"

"*Don't* —"

She's watching his face. She can see it now, just, in the little light from the harbour through the verandah door. It's nothing like it was last night, when he wouldn't look at her. It's nothing like the voice. There's nothing in front of it at all. His mouth's open and his hair's in his eyes and he's looking at her as if she were the last figure on the last line of the last page. As if he'd added it up, all of it, the whole column, and she was what it came to.

That's what does it for her. Not his hands, or his mouth. His face.

It takes her like a wave over the harbour wall in a south wind. It comes up under her and goes right over her head, and she hears herself shout. Actually shout. His name, or something like it, out of the open verandah door into the dark over the harbour. And he follows her a moment later with his face in her neck and his whole body going rigid, and he doesn't bite anything back at all. He says her name against her throat like a man who's been holding it in his mouth all day.

---

Afterwards they lie in a heap on the counterpane with the pillows on the floor and her skirt round her waist and his shirt still on, one arm, open. She can't stop laughing. She doesn't know why. It keeps coming up out of her in little waves, like the end of something.

His head's on her breast. She's got her hand in his hair. She can feel his heart through both of them, going hard, and then less hard, and then coming down, beat by beat. She lies there and feels it come down and doesn't count, not once.

"Well," he says eventually, into her skin. He sounds hoarse. "That's the *Gleaner* settled."

She laughs so hard the bed creaks.

"They'll have heard it in the fishmarket."

"They'll have heard it on the *cay*," says Reggie. "Pascual'll have heard it. Marguerite'll have it in her book."

"*A hundred and ten*," says Win, in Marguerite's voice, and he laughs against her until they're both helpless.

"She knows," he says, when he can. "She worked it out on the verandah. I told her there was a leak."

"You *never*."

"And the Ministry's bill. And the adding machine." He hides his face against her. "And then I told her Desmond might come back for something he'd left in room nine."

Win puts both hands over her face and makes a noise into them that frightens the frogs.

When they've stopped, he lifts his head and looks at her. He can't see her much. She can see him looking all the same.

"Win."

"Mm."

"Thank you. For last night. For making me wait." He puts his hand on her cheek. "I'd not have wanted it to be about him. I didn't know that till you said. You were right."

"I know I was."

"You usually are."

"I *always* am," says Win comfortably. "It's in the book."

He puts his head back down on her breast. After a while his breathing goes long and slow, and he's asleep, sprawled half across her with one arm over her waist. She lies there under him and doesn't move. She looks at the ceiling, and then at the verandah door, where the harbour's dark and the cay is a long low shape against the stars.

And then, because she can reach it without moving him, she stretches out her arm to the chest of drawers. She finds the photograph of the children in the dark by feel. She turns it round, so it faces the room again.

It's been facing the wall since May. She doesn't know why she turns it now. Only that there's nobody in the house who needs not to be seen, and nothing in her room she'd want to hide from anybody. Not tonight. Not ever again.

In the morning Reggie wakes before her, at six, the way he always does. He lies there curled round her in the grey light and sees Lloyd and Joy looking at him from the chest of drawers. And Win, who's been awake since a quarter to and has been pretending not to be, feels him go quite still behind her. Then he laughs, very quietly, into the back of her neck.

"Good morning," he says, to the photograph. "I'm Reggie. I expect you've heard."
