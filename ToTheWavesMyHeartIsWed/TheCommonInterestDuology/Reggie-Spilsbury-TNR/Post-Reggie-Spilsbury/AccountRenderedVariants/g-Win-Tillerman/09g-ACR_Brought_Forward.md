# Brought Forward

The lamp doesn't come on.

Win Carew notices at half past ten, because that's when she looks. She's on the top verandah of Harbour House with the day book open on her knee and the last of the coffee gone cold, doing the laundry figures, and at half past ten she looks up across the channel the way she has every night since 1958. There's the long low shape of the cay against the stars. There are the students' lights, going out one by one. There's the lamp on the long-room verandah that Mrs Joseph leaves burning all night for anybody who needs it.

At the far end of the row, the last hut, there's nothing.

She puts her pencil down.

The fishermen call it the Doctor's Light. Alleyne's boy steers home on it when he's been out late past the second head, and so did his father, and so do the Kilburns. You put the Doctor's Light over the church roof and come straight in, and you'll clear the bar with a fathom to spare. It's been there so long that nobody at the Bay remembers it's a man. It's a mark. It's on the chart in the harbour office, in pencil, in old Mr Alleyne's hand.

Win has always remembered it's a man.

She came home to the Bay in the autumn of 1958 with two children and four suitcases and a biscuit tin full of her husband's debts, and took the old harbourmaster's house on a loan from her mother. She made it into rooms, because there was nothing else she knew how to do that paid. The first night, she sat out on this verandah because she couldn't sleep, and looked across the water, and there it was. One light, by itself, a little brighter than the rest, burning at two in the morning when there was nobody on earth awake to see it but her.

She'd thought, that first night, *there's somebody else can't sleep*. And she'd felt, God help her, less alone.

She knows whose it is. She's known since the fourteenth of July 1962. She was at the Sealy wedding, in the schoolroom, halfway down the long table, and she saw old Mr Purdie lean across and ask the best man about somebody called Spilsbury. She saw what happened to the best man's face. It was the charmingest thing she'd seen in her life, and she'd been married to Desmond Carew for eleven years, so she knew charm when it came at her. It came on like a lamp turned up. And behind it, for about a second, before it got going, there was a look she knew too, because she'd seen it in her own glass in Kingston most mornings of 1957.

That night the students' boat went back across at ten. At twenty to eleven the light at the end of the row came on.

She asked her mother about it once, the next Monday, in her mother's kitchen, as a thing of no importance. Her mother had done the linen for the row since 1938, and knew every bed on the cay by the way it was slept in. Her mother didn't look up from the ironing.

"That's the doctor's business," said Iris Greaves. "I do the sheets."

And that was answer enough, from her mother. It was the most anybody at the Bay ever got.

---

She sits with the day book shut on her knee and watches the dark place at the end of the row.

She tells herself it's nothing. He's gone to bed early. He's run out of oil. But she's known that light thirteen years, and it hasn't run out of oil once. Since the spring it's burnt all night, every night. Everybody at the Bay said that was when the man in Norway died, the one whose specimen is in the deposit. Mr Purdie told the fishmarket so, because he'd drawn the deed. The light burnt all night after that, and Marguerite Sealy got a look on her face on Sundays that Win didn't like, and people at the Bay said *heart* about the doctor at the end of the row, the way they'd said it about her own father before he went.

So she sits and watches, and it doesn't come on.

At midnight she makes herself think it through, the way she'd think through a bill. If he'd been taken bad, there'd be lanterns moving. Somebody would've found him. There'd be the radio-telephone going in the office, and a boat, and Marguerite called out with her bag. There's nothing on the cay at all, not a lantern, not a sound across the water but the frogs. Nobody's running.

That doesn't mean anything. Nobody would be running if nobody had found him.

She goes in and gets the field glasses out of the hall drawer, the ones the bird men from Cambridge left behind in 'sixty-six. She sits down again with them in her lap. She isn't going to wake Marguerite for a lamp. She isn't going to be the woman who rang the doctor at one in the morning because a man she's spoken to perhaps a dozen times, across Marguerite's dinner table, didn't light his lamp. But she's not going to bed, either.

At some point after three, she sleeps in the chair.

She wakes at six, stiff, with the glasses on the boards by her foot and the grey coming up over the second head. She picks them up before she's properly awake and puts them to her eyes and finds the jetty.

There's a man on the end of it.

He's small, in his shirtsleeves, with no hat. He's got a string over the side of the jetty and he's standing very still, waiting, with his other hand in his pocket. She watches him for three minutes by the church clock. Then he draws the string up hand over hand and holds something up to the light and reads it.

His mouth moves. She can't hear it. He's said something, to nobody, to the water.

Then he winds the string round the thing and puts it in his shirt pocket, and walks back up the jetty and up the path towards the long room. Not fast. Not slow. Like a man going up to his breakfast.

Win sits on the top verandah of Harbour House with the glasses in her lap and finds she's crying, which is ridiculous. She's forty-six years old and she has nine guests coming down in an hour wanting eggs. She's never said anything to the man but *pass the salt*. She wipes her face with the heel of her hand and goes down to start the breakfasts.

She doesn't tell anybody. Not Marguerite. Not anybody. It isn't hers to tell. It's the doctor's business.

But that night at half past ten she looks up, the way she always does, and the end of the row is dark again. And the night after that. And the fishermen grumble all that winter, coming in late past the second head, because they've got to use the church lamp and the old leading mark on the hill. It isn't half as good, and you've got to know the bar.

---

The Board comes out every November, and every November it stays at Harbour House, because the spare hut on the cay has two beds and a roof Hester still doesn't trust. So on the second Saturday of November 1972 Reggie is sitting at the long table in Mrs Carew's dining room at the Bay. He's between Sir Hugh Mallory, who is a trustee and a banker and deaf in the left ear, and Lady Mallory, who isn't deaf in either, eating a very good fish.

He's there because Jean made him come. He's the Consultant. He's supposed to be consulted. He's spent the fish explaining the calf's mother's weights to Lady Mallory, who asked, and who's been listening, and he's enjoying himself more than he expected to.

It's been like that for a year, more or less. He keeps being surprised by it.

The wind gets up during the pudding.

Jean hears it first. She puts her spoon down and listens and says *excuse me* and goes out onto the verandah. When she comes back she has the face she has for weather. The glass is going down like a stone, she says. It's come up out of the south, late, the way they do in November. She's taking the last boat across at nine with Pascual, because there's a pen full of animals and a Director's place is on the cay. The Board will be perfectly comfortable where it is. Reggie is sleeping at Marguerite's, as he does, and will come over on the first boat when it's calm.

"Marguerite's been called up the coast," says Pascual, coming in behind her with the rain on his shoulders. "A confinement out at the point. Kilburn's girl. She'll be all night." He looks at Reggie. "The house is empty, *hombre*. You'll be on your own up there."

"Then I'll be on my own up there. It's a very good house."

"It's a very good house up a very steep hill in *that*."

"He's not going up any hill in that," says Mrs Carew.

She's in the doorway to the kitchen with a tray, and she says it in the voice of a woman who's run a guest house for fourteen years and has never lost an argument about the weather. Reggie has sat across Marguerite's table from her a dozen times, perhaps twenty, since last spring. She's Iris Greaves's daughter; Iris did his sheets for twenty years. He knows she keeps Harbour House, and that the Board likes it, and that her bills are reasonable and come in on time, because Jean says so. He couldn't tell you the colour of her eyes. He has a vague idea that she's put a great deal of food on his plate, one way and another, over the last eighteen months, and that he's eaten it.

"There's room seven," she says. "It's small and it's at the back and it doesn't leak. Go on, Dr Pobil, you'll miss your boat. He'll be here in the morning, I'll not lose him."

Pascual looks at her, and at Reggie, and goes. Reggie watches the launch's light go out past the harbour wall into the rain with Jean in the bow. He feels the usual thing, the pull in his hands, and puts them in his pockets.

The lights go at a quarter to ten.

---

They go all at once, with a bang somewhere at the back of the house. Lady Mallory says *oh* in the dark, and somebody drops a glass. Sir Hugh says, very loudly, because of the left ear, "Isn't there a man?"

"There's me," says Mrs Carew's voice from the door. "Sit still, everybody. There's candles on the sideboard, Mr Farquharson, you're nearest. I'll be two minutes."

Reggie hears her go through the kitchen. He hears the back door open, and the whole noise of the storm come in, and the door bang shut.

He gets up.

He doesn't think about it. He finds his way through the kitchen by the stove light and out of the back door into the rain, which hits him like a thrown bucket. There's a torch bobbing across the yard towards a shed. He goes after it.

"Mrs Carew. You can't —"

"I can." She doesn't turn round. She's got the shed door open against the wind with her shoulder. "Hold this."

She gives him the torch.

The generator's in the back of the shed, a squat green thing with a fuel tank on top. It's stopped. He can smell the petrol. He points the torch at it and opens his mouth to say something about the float chamber, because it'll be the float chamber, it's always the float chamber, and he's got the whole of it ready —

She hits it. She hits the side of the carburettor with the heel of her hand, hard, twice, in a particular place, like a woman slapping a horse. Then she pulls the cord.

It starts.

Reggie stands in the shed with the rain coming in behind him and the torch in his hand, and laughs out loud.

"It's the float," she says, over the engine. "It sticks. Desmond's band had a van that did the same. I've been hitting this one since 'fifty-nine." She's looking at the house. "Lights aren't on."

"No." He's already looking. "That bang. It'll be the fuse board. Where is it?"

"Back passage, by the pantry."

"Show me."

It is the fuse board, as it turns out. The main fuse has gone, the old porcelain kind, with the wire burnt through. There's a card of fuse wire on a nail beside the board, which he could have kissed her for. He takes his glasses off, because they're streaming, and gives them to her, and holds the torch in his teeth while he does it. He talks round it, because he's always talked while he does a thing with his hands.

"*Higher.* No — your left. There."

"You can't talk with a torch in your mouth, Dr Tar."

"*I'm managing.*"

"You're dripping on my fuses."

He gets the wire round and screws it down and pushes the carrier home, and every light in Harbour House comes on at once. There's a cheer from the dining room like a football crowd. She's standing beside him in the back passage, soaked to the skin, holding his glasses in one hand. She looks at the lights, and then at him, and something happens at the corner of her mouth.

"Well," she says. "We'll do."

He takes his glasses back and puts them on, and sees her properly for the first time. That's what it feels like, later, when he thinks about it. As though he's been looking at her through wet lenses for eighteen months and somebody's handed him the dry ones.

Her eyes are brown. Very dark. There's a pencil behind her ear, which has survived all this.

---

The roof goes at half past ten, in Sir Hugh's room.

Not all of it. There's a corner at the front of the house where the flashing lifts in a south wind, and the water comes through the ceiling of room four in a steady stream, straight onto the washstand. Sir Hugh stands in the doorway in his dressing gown and says it's a disgrace, and he'll speak to the Institute about it, and he doesn't know what the Institute's thinking of, lodging its Board in a place like this.

Reggie sees Mrs Carew's face not change at all. Then she goes to the linen cupboard on the landing, and opens it, and there's a list pinned to the inside of the door.

He reads it over her shoulder, because he can't not.

*S. WIND. Rm 4: bucket 2 under corner by window (fast), bucket 5 on washstand (slow, moves N after 1 hr). Rm 6: bowl under lamp hook. Landing: cloth at skirting by 7. Rm 9 dry. Rm 3 dry unless SE.*

And under that, in a different pencil, *N. WIND*, and another list. And under that, *E.*

"You've mapped it," he says.

"Every storm since 'fifty-nine." She's getting buckets out of the bottom of the cupboard. They're numbered in paint on the side. "It moves. It comes in by the window first and then it runs along the joist and comes down over the washstand, about an hour later. I've tried telling the roofer. He goes up and says there's nothing wrong with it."

"How much?"

"How much what?"

"Water. How much comes in. In an hour."

She looks at him, with bucket two in her hand.

"About a bucket and a half by the window," she says slowly. "In a south wind. Less over the washstand. Why?"

"Because if you'd a rate at each end, and the time between, you could work out the fall along the joist, and then you'd know where it's getting in." He can feel it happening. He can feel the thing come on in the back of his head, the way it does, where everything else goes quiet. "It won't be where he's looking. He's looking at the window because that's where it comes in first. But if it moves north along the joist it's coming in above that and *running*. It'll be the ridge, or the valley behind the chimney —"

"Dr Tar."

"— and you'd find it in an afternoon, if you'd —"

"Dr Tar." She's holding out the bucket. "Sir Hugh's getting wet."

He takes the bucket.

---

He carries buckets for an hour. It's the most enjoyable hour he's had in years, and he's aware as he does it that this is absurd. Bucket two fills, and he empties it out of the landing window into the storm, and brings it back, and bucket five fills slower, exactly as the list says. He finds he's timing them without meaning to, by his watch. She's timing them too. He catches her doing it at the landing window, and she catches him catching her, and neither of them says anything.

Halfway up the stairs with bucket two full, the third time, he feels it: the old tightness, the band. Not bad. A warning.

He stops, one foot on the next tread. He's reaching for his pocket when she comes down past him on the stairs and takes the bucket out of his hand.

She doesn't say anything. She doesn't look at his face. She just takes it, the way you'd take a bucket off anybody on a stair, and carries it on up, and empties it out of the landing window. Then she goes into room four and puts it back under the corner. He stands on the stair with his hand on his chest over the pocket and the tablet under his tongue, and waits for it to ease, and it does.

Nobody has done that. Marguerite would have taken his wrist and told him the figure. Pascual would have shouted at him in two languages and then carried him. Jean would have looked at him until he put it down himself. Mrs Carew took the bucket and went on up the stairs as if nothing had happened, and let him have it that nothing had.

When he gets to the landing she hands him bucket five, which is half the weight.

"That one's slow," she says. "It's only on the washstand."

"I know. It's on your list."

---

At about one, Sir Hugh stops being frightened and starts being difficult, which Reggie has seen men do in a storm before.

He's come down to the kitchen in his dressing gown to say that he can't possibly sleep in room four with buckets going, and that he wishes it put on record that the Institute has lodged its Board in a house with a roof like a colander. He'll raise it at the meeting. He'd be obliged if somebody would find him another room. And Reggie sees Mrs Carew, at the stove with the kettle, go very straight in the back.

Reggie gets up.

"Sir Hugh," he says. "You're perfectly right, and you'll have room nine, it's the only dry one on that side — Mrs Carew has it on a list, she's got the whole house mapped, I've been reading it for an hour, it's the most exact piece of work I've seen in years. Come up. I'll carry your bag. And I'll tell you a thing about that roof that'll amuse you, because you'll know about this, you know about everything with a mortgage on it."

He hears his own voice do it. It comes on as easy as anything. He leans on the doorframe and raises his eyebrows, and the warmth comes into it. Sir Hugh stops halfway through a word, and goes pink, and says, "Well — I dare say —"

Ten minutes later Sir Hugh is in room nine, delighted, having explained to Reggie at length the principle of the repairing lease. Reggie comes back down to the kitchen and finds Mrs Carew sitting at the table with her hand over her mouth.

She's laughing. Silently, with her shoulders going.

"What?"

She takes her hand away. "*You're perfectly right, Sir Hugh.*" She leans back in her chair and raises both eyebrows at him, and drops her voice about half an octave, and it's so exactly the thing that he stops dead in the doorway. "*You'll know about this, you know about everything —*"

"I don't sound like that."

"You sound *exactly* like that. You went like — *so* —" She does the shoulders. It's very good. "Like a man selling a car."

He stands there.

Pascual has shouted at him about the voice. Pascual shouted at him about it in 1957 so the whole station heard, and on a boat in 1960, and on the step of the hut last year, not shouting, crying. Marguerite has measured it. She's taken his pulse through it and told him the figure. Jean rolls her eyes at it. Charles, a long time ago, was frightened of it. Alec — he doesn't think about what Alec did about it.

Nobody has ever laughed.

"Does it work on you?" he says.

"Dr Tar." She wipes her eyes. "I've kept a guest house fourteen years. Every man who's ever left without paying his bill has done that voice at me on the way out. I know it better than my own name. It doesn't work on me at all." She gets up and pours the kettle. "It worked on him, mind. And it saved my roof going in the minutes, so I'll not complain. You'll have tea."

He sits down at her kitchen table, because his knees want him to. And he laughs. It starts in his chest and he can't stop it, and it goes on until the band comes round his ribs again, a little. She looks round from the stove and sees him put his hand flat on his chest. Without any fuss at all she pushes a chair out with her foot so he can put his feet up on it, and puts the tea in front of him with three sugars, and turns back to the stove.

---

The storm goes through at about three. They sit in the kitchen and eat the cold chicken from the Board's dinner, with their fingers, off the dish, and she does the day book.

She does it at the kitchen table by the lamp, because she does it every night. She's not going to not do it because of a little weather, she says. He watches her do it. There's a column for rooms and a column for meals and a column for laundry. Tonight there's a new line at the bottom, in the same small neat hand: *Storm S. Rm 4 roof. Main fuse (Dr T.). 3 hrs generator.*

"You've put me in the book."

"You mended my fuse. It goes in the book." She turns the page back. "Everything goes in the book."

"Can I see?"

She looks at him for a moment. Then she turns it round and pushes it across.

He reads it. He reads the whole of that week, and then the week before, and then he asks if there's more. There's a shelf of them in the pantry, one a year, back to 1959. She fetches three, because he asks, and he reads them at her kitchen table at half past three in the morning with his glasses pushed up, like a paper. They're beautiful. The occupancy goes up every year but two, and he can see which two, because the tourist boats stopped calling. He can see the Institute's visitors come in as a line in 1960, and go from a line to a column. He can see her put the prices up in 1965, a shilling at a time, very carefully, watching whether they'd stand it. They stood it.

He asks her about the shilling. He asks her about the two bad years, and the laundry contract, and why the meals column drops every August, and whether she's thought of a second cistern. She answers, every time, straight out, with the figure. He asks another. He can't stop. He's aware, somewhere at the back, that he's been asking questions for forty minutes and that it's nearly four in the morning. He's aware that she's been up since six and that he hasn't asked her a single question about anything but her ledgers. He asks another one anyway.

She puts her chin on her hand and looks at him across the table.

"Dr Tar," she says, "you've asked me more questions tonight than my husband did the whole of the time I was married to him."

He stops.

"Where is he?"

"Miami, last I heard." She says it perfectly easily. "We've not lived together since 'fifty-eight. I came home. He didn't."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be." She takes the ledger back and shuts it. "I'm not. Not about that." She stands up. "Go to bed. Room seven. There's a towel on the chair."

He goes to bed.

He lies in room seven with the shutters ticking as the wind drops, and doesn't sleep for a long time. It isn't the old kind of not sleeping. It's the other kind, the kind he had at the Labs when a set of figures came out right at midnight and he couldn't put them down. He lies there and thinks about a list on the back of a linen-cupboard door. Two leaks, a rate at each end, and the time between. You could find it in an afternoon.

He knows the feeling. He's had it about a curve, and about an animal or two, and once, in a corridor in 1946, about a man who told him in front of four people that he was wrong.

It's the list, he tells himself. It's a very good list.

He falls asleep working out the fall along the joist.

---

Marguerite starts the Sundays in April 1971, and she starts them for exactly the reason she'd give if anybody asked. The man needs company, and she's a doctor, and company is a thing she can prescribe.

He's been sleeping in her spare room on Sunday nights since Christmas, so the dinner's there already. She only adds people to it. She has the new schoolmaster and his wife, and the Methodist minister, who's a great talker and needs no help. She has Hester Bevan, when Hester can get across. She has Mr Purdie while he's still able, who's eighty-six and deafer than Sir Hugh and tells the story of the sugar wharf every time as though for the first time. She has the Alleynes, and a visiting ichthyologist from Miami who turns out to have been at school with Jean, and Kilburn's eldest, who wants to be a doctor and needs to be told how hard it is. She has men and women, old and young, clever and not. She has anybody she can think of who'll talk, so that he doesn't have to, and who won't ask him anything, and who'll make a noise at her table that isn't the sound of a man doing everything he's told.

She has Win Carew, because Win is her friend and cooks better than Marguerite does and brings a dish. And because Win, of everybody at the Bay, is the one she'd trust to sit next to a man in that state and neither stare nor chatter.

Win sits next to him in May 1971, and in June, and several times over the summer. Marguerite watches from the other end of the table. Win doesn't talk to him much. She passes him things. She puts a second piece of fish on his plate without asking and without looking at him, while she's talking to the minister about the harbour dues. He eats it. He doesn't notice he's eaten it. On the Sundays Win comes, Marguerite has observed by September 1971, he eats about a third more. She puts it down to the cooking.

Then, in the winter, he gets better. The lamp goes out, and he sleeps, and he eats his breakfast. He laughs at the Christmas dinner, properly. Pascual comes home from the cay that night and sits on the end of the bed and cries for twenty minutes, from sheer relief.

She keeps the Sundays anyway. By then they're good dinners. People want to come.

And she keeps taking his pulse.

She's taken it every Sunday since December 1970, on the verandah, before dinner and after. She writes it in a small black book she keeps in her bag with the others. It isn't a thing she thinks about. It's a thing she does, the way she'd take the temperature of anybody she had reason to watch. He puts his wrist out without being asked, now, and goes on talking while she counts.

In January 1973, on a quiet evening, she goes through the book, because she's thinking of cutting his tablets back, and she wants a proper series.

The figures before dinner are very good. They've been very good for a year. Seventy, seventy-two, sixty-eight. The figures after dinner are mostly the same, a few beats up, which is dinner.

Except that some of them aren't. Some of them, since about November, are eighty. Eighty-two. Seventy-eight on a night when the before was sixty-six.

She's a doctor. She doesn't draw conclusions from four figures. She turns to the back of the book, where she keeps the dinner lists because it's the only book she ever has with her, and she lays the two side by side.

She draws them from eight.

---

"*Win?*" says Pascual. "Win *Carew*?"

He's sitting bolt upright in bed. She'd waited until the light was out, because she knows his face, and it hasn't helped at all. She can hear his face in the dark.

"Keep your voice down."

"We're in our own bed, *querida*, who's going to —" He grabs her arm. "Win. *Win*. Oh my God. Oh my *God*. The *Mondays*."

"What?"

"He stays over. Since — since before Christmas. He used to go back to the cay first thing Monday, and now he stays the Monday and goes back on the last boat. I asked him, and he said he was seeing about the Board's account."

"The Board's account."

"The *Board's account*. Jean pays the Board's account, Jean's paid it since 'sixty-eight, it comes in one envelope in December." He's laughing now, and trying not to, and failing, into the pillow. "Jean knows. Jean *must* know. Jean looked at him the Monday before Christmas like — I thought she had indigestion —"

"Pascual."

"Win Carew. The *storm*. He stayed at Harbour House the night of the storm, the Board was there —" He sits up again. "He came back the next morning and talked about a roof for an hour. I thought he'd gone mad. He drew it for me. He drew me the *joists* —"

"Pascual. Listen to me."

He listens. He's shaking with it, beside her, and he's got hold of her hand.

"You're not to say a word to him," says Marguerite. "Not one. Not with your mouth and not with your face."

"I can't do anything about my face."

"Then stay on the cay till you can. If he sees it on you he'll stop going. You know he will. He's fifty-four and he's been through what he's been through, and he's got a heart I wouldn't insure, and if he thinks the whole Bay's watching him go down the hill on Mondays he'll stop, and tell himself it was the Board's account."

Pascual is quiet for a while.

"Does she know?" he says. "What it was. Before."

"I don't know. I'll find out."

"And if she doesn't —"

"Then I'll tell her. It's allowed now." She lies back. "And you'll leave it to me, and you'll keep your face on the cay, and I'll see to the seating."

"The *seating*," says Pascual, in the dark, with enormous satisfaction.

---

She sees to the seating. She puts Win beside him every Sunday in January, and on the last Sunday she asks Win to walk him down to the boat because her knee's bad. Her knee isn't bad. She watches them go down the hill together in the dark, very slowly, at his pace, not touching, talking, and she thinks, *there*.

Then she goes across to Harbour House on the Wednesday, in the slack of the afternoon, to tell Win about the lamp.

She's rehearsed it. She's thought hard about how much is hers to say and how much isn't, and she's decided that the fact of it is hers, now, and nothing else. He loved a man for twenty-five years. It couldn't be. The man died, and it broke him, and he came back. She'll give Win that much, plainly, so that whatever Win decides she'll decide it knowing. Anything more is his.

Win gives her coffee in the kitchen. Marguerite sits down at the table and puts her cup down and begins.

"Win. If anything's going to happen — and I'm not saying it is, and I'm not asking — there's something about him you ought to know. And I'd rather you heard it from somebody who —"

"Is it the lamp?" says Win.

Marguerite stops.

Win's at the stove with her back to her. She doesn't turn round. "Marguerite, the whole Bay came in on that light for fourteen years. It's on the chart in the harbour office. I know about the lamp."

"Do you know who it was for?"

Win turns round. She looks at Marguerite for quite a long time with no expression at all, which Marguerite has not seen her do before. Then she looks down into her coffee.

"I know it's not mine to say," she says. "And it isn't yours either, love, is it? If he wants me to know, he'll tell me."

And Marguerite Sealy, who has been a doctor for thirty years and has sat across a great many kitchen tables from people who didn't want to tell her things, understands that she has just been told a very great deal. She picks her cup up.

"Is it going that way?" she says instead. "With him."

"Marguerite," says Win. "He's been asking me the price of eggs off Kilburn's boat every Monday for six weeks. And writing it *down*. He's got a little notebook in his inside pocket and he writes down my *egg prices*. With the date." She puts her hands over her face. "He says there's a seasonal curve. He's drawing it for me. He showed me on Monday. It's got *error bars*."

Marguerite puts her cup down, because she's going to spill it.

"Oh, Win," she says. "Oh, my dear. Yes. That's how it goes, with him. That's exactly how it goes."

He tells her on the nineteenth of February, which is a Monday, two years and two days after the quay steps at Ålesund.

He doesn't choose the date for that. He doesn't notice until he's halfway down Marguerite's hill. He's decided on the Saturday, in the north pen, watching the calf's mother blow, that it's got to be done now or not at all. He's been staying over at the Bay on Mondays since November, and on the last three he's sat on her top verandah after the guests have gone in, and held her hand, and she's let him. He's fifty-four. He knows what that is. And there's a thing she doesn't know, and it's not fair to hold her hand across it.

So on the Monday he sits on her verandah at ten o'clock with the day book done and the coffee cold, facing the channel. He tells her.

What it cost, and who paid, which wasn't him, mostly, and that's the part he minds. The letter in April 1970 that he didn't answer. The fourth of March. He doesn't tell her about the file. He doesn't tell her about Frank Nicholson, because that's Frank's. He tells her the rest, all of it that's his, and it takes about twenty minutes. She doesn't say anything the whole time. He turns his head.

He can't say anything.

"I asked my mother," Win says. "She did your sheets. She said it was the doctor's business and she did the sheets. That's all she ever said."

"Iris knew."

He puts his other hand over his eyes.

He'd thought — he doesn't know what he'd thought. That it was his, and Pascual's, and Marguerite's, in pieces. That it was in a file in Halloran's hand and in the *Proceedings* in his own, and nowhere else. That nobody had seen the lamp but the people he'd let see it.

And there'd been a woman on a verandah at the Bay the whole time, looking across the water, not saying.

"There's something else," she says. "I'll tell you, and then you can decide if you want it. It's yours, not mine. I've just been keeping it."

"Go on."

"The eighteenth of November, 'seventy-one. A Thursday. Your lamp didn't come on." Her hand's tight on his. "It'd come on every night for thirteen years. I sat up. I thought you'd died."

"Win —"

"I thought you'd died in that hut on your own with nobody knowing. And I'd not wake Marguerite for a lamp, so I sat up with the glasses the bird men left, and I went to sleep in my chair. And at six I woke up, and I looked, and you were on the end of the jetty with a string over the side."

He takes his hand away from his eyes.

"You took the temperature," says Win. "You read it. You said something. I couldn't hear what. And then you went up to your breakfast." Her voice goes, a little, and comes back. "I sat there and cried like a fool. I'd never said a word to you but *pass the salt*."

He looks at her. He can't see her very well. It's dark, and his glasses have gone, somehow.

"Twenty-six," he says.

"What?"

"That's what I said. Twenty-six. It was twenty-six. I'd nowhere to send it." He hears his own voice, and it isn't steady, and he lets it not be. "I said it to nobody. I thought I was saying it to nobody."

Win doesn't say anything for a while. She sits beside him on the verandah and holds his hand and looks across at the cay, where there's no lamp at the end of the row.

"Well," she says eventually. "You weren't."

"Not the doctors. Not the Colonel. Not Desmond when he was courting me and wanted to make an impression." She shakes her head. "If I may."

The Founders' Dinner is on the fourteenth of April, on the cay, in the Director's house. He asks her to it on the Monday before, in the kitchen, with his hands in his pockets, as though he were asking her to pass the salt.

Reggie's on the jetty to meet the boat, in a dark suit she hasn't seen, with his hair flattened. It's a lovely evening. That's the trouble, afterwards. It's one of the loveliest evenings of her life.

She sits at the top table beside him, between him and Hester Bevan, who's eighty and upright and says *so you're Iris's girl* in a tone of the deepest approval. Across the table Pascual can't keep his face still. He's given up trying by the soup. "Monday," he says.

"Monday," says Win.

Alleyne's boy takes her back across with the Mallorys. Lady Mallory says what a charming man Dr Tar is, and how nice for him, after everything. Win says yes, and looks back at the cay, where he's standing at the end of the jetty with his hands in his pockets, watching the boat go.

And then he turns round and goes back up the path. And she watches the row, the way she's watched it for fifteen years, and the end of it stays dark.

It's a man called Wilmot who does it, on the Tuesday.

He's from the Ministry in Kingston, and he's stayed at Harbour House two or three times a year since 1964, always with a colleague. It's a different colleague some years, and the same one lately, a younger man called Ferris. They take rooms three and five, which have the door between. Mr Wilmot has a photograph of his wife and daughters in a leather frame, and he sets it up on the washstand in room three the moment he arrives, every time, facing the bed.

Win has known what she was looking at since about 1965. She's never said a word. She's her mother's daughter; it's their business, and she does the sheets. She does room three's and room five's, and one of them's always been slept in and the other one hasn't. She makes up both.

On the Tuesday after the Founders' Dinner she goes into room three with the clean linen. The photograph's on the washstand, facing the bed. The wife is a pleasant-looking woman in a church hat. The bed hasn't been slept in.

Win stands there with the sheets in her arms and looks at the woman in the church hat for a long time.

She thinks about a dark suit and a hand held at the top of the jetty steps, in front of the Board. She thinks about a shoulder against hers through the whole of Jean's speech, where Sir Hugh could see it, and Lady Mallory, and every student on the island. She thinks about a kiss on the cheek on a jetty, very correct, in the lantern light, in front of everybody.

And she thinks about her own verandah, where nobody sees. Where he's sat every Monday for two months since February, and kissed her, and held her face, and said her name in a way that goes right through her. And at a quarter to ten, every Monday, he's looked at his watch and gone down the hill to the last boat.

He's never once asked to stay.

She's forty-eight. She was married to Desmond. She's not a girl, and she isn't a fool. She knows what a man looks like who wants to stay, and she knows what a man looks like who's going home on the last boat because home's where he wants to be.

She sees Iris's face over the ironing board. *That's the doctor's business.*

She makes up room three, square, and room five. She goes down to the office and writes a note on the Harbour House card, and on the Monday morning she sends the kitchen boy up the hill to Dr Sealy's with it. *Full house this week, I'm afraid. W.C.* The Monday after, she sends another one the same.

Neither of them is true.

"Win," says Marguerite. "Have you gone mad?"

It's the Thursday after the second note. Marguerite has come down the hill in the heat of the afternoon without her bag, which means it isn't a visit. She's sitting at the kitchen table, and Win has told her. Not about Mr Wilmot. That's not hers. About the rest of it.

"I don't know," says Win. "Very likely."

"You think you're his *cover*."

"I think a woman on his arm at the top table in front of the Board is a very useful thing for a man to have." Win's at the sink, with her back to her, because it's easier. "I think he's been through a great deal and he's getting on, and he's lonely, and he's decent, and he likes me. I think he'd like to be seen with a respectable woman. "Marguerite —"

Win laughs until she has to sit down. Marguerite watches her do it with the look she uses on patients who've been told the X-ray's clear.

"Have you asked him?"

"I'm not asking a man *that*."

"Then he can say so."

Marguerite looks at her across the kitchen table for a long time. Win can see her deciding something.

"Yes," Marguerite says at last. "He can."

Win doesn't come on the Sunday.

She's come every Sunday since January. Marguerite's set her place and then taken it away again, very quietly, before anybody sat down. Reggie watches her do it.

"Where's Win?"

"Not coming," says Marguerite. "She sent up a note."

"Saying what?"

"Saying she's not coming."

He sits down. He doesn't eat much. "*Mm*."

Pascual is sitting in the cane chair in the dark with his hands on the arms, and he's trying very hard, and it's no good. It's never been any good. It's the face that told Halloran everything in 1957 from the chair by the window. It's doing about six things at once.

"I don't know anything."

"Pascual."

"She thinks you want him," he says.

"Not — not *want* him. She knows he's dead. She's not stupid." Pascual leans forward with his elbows on his knees. "She thinks he's what you'd have. If you could. On your arm at the top table in front of the Board, where Sir Hugh can see. Kissed on the cheek on the jetty. And then you go home on the last boat."

Reggie doesn't say anything at all.

Pascual watches him. Reggie feels it happen and can't stop it. It comes up from somewhere under his ribs and goes out through his face and leaves it. He feels his hands go cold on his knees.

He's gone white. He knows he has. He's done it once in Pascual's sight before, on a verandah in 1954.

"Cover," says Reggie.

"That's the word she used. Marguerite said."

"I did that." He hears his own voice, and it's quite flat. "In 'fifty-seven. In the office. "She's wrong."

"Yes. She's wrong. And she's right to wonder." He puts his hand flat on his chest, over the pocket, though there's nothing in it now. "She doesn't know about the file. She doesn't need to know about the file. She's worked it out from the *shape* of me, Pascual. She's read me off a jetty."

"She's read you off *half* a jetty," says Pascual. "*Why* do you go home on the last boat?"

Reggie laughs. It isn't much of one.

"Because I'm fifty-four," he says. "Because Marguerite won't insure me. Because she's been married, and it was bad, and she's run that house on her own for fifteen years, and I thought — I thought it ought to be when *she* wanted it. Not when I did. I thought if I sat on her verandah long enough and didn't push, she'd say. I was waiting to be asked."

Pascual puts his face in his hands.

"*Ay*, Dios," he says, through his fingers. "*Reggie*. She's waiting to be asked."

"She said that?"

"She told Marguerite she wasn't asking a man *that*." Pascual lifts his head. "You're a pair of — you're two of the cleverest people on this coast, and you've sat on a verandah every Monday for two months each waiting for the other to — *hombre*, I've seen animals court with more sense. I've seen *sea cucumbers* —"

"All right."

"— the calf's mother has more *idea* —"

"All *right*, Pascual."

Pascual stops. He's laughing, and his eyes are wet, and he's furious, all at once, the way he always is.

And then Reggie watches him get up out of the cane chair with the whole of it in his face. It's a decision, and it's enormous, and it's entirely Pascual-shaped.

"I'll go down," says Pascual. "In the morning. First thing. I'll tell her. I'll tell her about the eggs, and the joists, and how you talked about that roof for an hour and drew it for me, and how you look at her across the table — *hombre*, the way you look at her, like she was an animal coming up off the reef, I've seen you do it twice in twenty years —"

"No."

"She'll believe me. She'll believe me in five minutes. I'll —"

"*No*, Pascual." Reggie gets up off the step. He does it slowly, because Marguerite's in the house and would hear him do it fast. "Sit down."

"Reggie —"

"You'd say it better than me. I know you would. You'd go down there and say it with your face, and she'd believe every word, and it'd be worth nothing to her. Because it'd be *you* saying it." He puts his hand on Pascual's arm. "She thinks I'm a man who'd let somebody else do the costly thing for him. She's got reason. If I send you down there to do it, I've proved it."

Pascual stands there in the dark with Reggie's hand on his arm.

Pascual looks at him for a long time. Then he sits back down in the cane chair, heavily, and puts his hands over his face again.

"I know."

"You would. And she'd have made you tea."

Pascual laughs, wet, behind his hands.

She looks at him across her kitchen for a long time.

"Then why," she says, "do you go home on the last boat?"

And he laughs. He can't help it. It's the thing he's been dreading since Pascual said it, and when it comes it's so plainly ridiculous that he has to put his hand on the dresser.

"Because I'm fifty-four," he says. "And I've got a heart Marguerite won't insure. And you were married to a man who never asked you anything, and you've run this house on your own for fifteen years without anybody's say-so, and I thought — God help me, I thought it ought to be when you wanted. Not me. So I sat on your verandah. And waited to be asked."

Win stares at him.

"*You* were waiting to be asked," she says.

"Yes."

"For two months."

"Nine weeks. And a day."

"*Reggie Tar*." She's laughing now, with her arms still folded, and crying a bit as well, and furious, all at once. "I've been *waiting to be asked*. I've been sat on that verandah every Monday with my heart in my mouth waiting for you to — and you looked at your *watch* —"

"I was looking at the time. Marguerite has me in bed by ten. She's had me in bed by ten since 1971. It's on a card by my washstand."

"*In bed by ten*." She puts both hands over her face. "Oh, God. Oh, Marguerite. She knew. She sat in my kitchen on Thursday and *knew*. She said *he can say so*."

"She said it to me as well," says Reggie. "Last night. On her verandah. In her dressing gown. She said you'd been up to the surgery in February. She said you'd written it down."

Win takes her hands away from her face, slowly.

She's gone the colour of the day book's cover. He didn't know she could.

"She *told* you that?"

"She told me you'd know what to do."

"I do know what to do." She says it with enormous dignity, scarlet to the hair. "I asked her properly. As a practical matter. It's in my book."

"I know. Pascual made a noise like a kettle."

And Win Carew sits down at her own kitchen table, very suddenly, and laughs until she can't breathe. He sits down across from her, because his knees want him to, and laughs with her. The sauce catches on the stove behind them, and neither of them gets up.

---

When they've stopped, she's quiet for a while. She's looking at her hands on the table.

"I'll tell you mine," she says. "Since we're doing it. Then you'll have the whole of both."

"You don't have to."

"I know I don't." She looks up. "Desmond had a band. In Kingston. The best dance band on the north coast, he'd tell you, and some years it was. He was the loveliest man. He could make a whole room get up off its chairs."

"I can imagine."

"You can't. You'd have to hear him." She smiles, not much. "I married him at twenty-two and I kept his books, because he couldn't, and I paid what he owed, because he wouldn't. Every month. And then every week." She turns her wedding ring round. It's still on her hand. He's never looked at it before. "I paid from the week I married him. The band's debts, and his, and the other thing's."

"What other thing?"

"Cards. Mostly. Horses when there were horses." She says it quite plainly. "I paid, and he was sorry. He was always sorry, and he always meant it. And I loved him, and I paid. I came home in 'fifty-eight because the children were old enough to see what I was doing. And I kept on paying from here, every month, in an envelope, because he'd write and ask. Till 'sixty-eight."

"What happened in 'sixty-eight?"

"He wrote and asked. And I didn't answer." She looks at the ring. "It took me a month not to. I had it in my apron pocket every day for a month. It's the hardest thing I've done, not writing a cheque."

Reggie sits across from her at the kitchen table. He thinks about a letter in his inside pocket, on the left, for the best part of a year, till the fold went soft as cloth.

"I had one in my pocket," he says. "From him. I couldn't answer it and I couldn't not."

"I know." She reaches across the table, and takes his hand. "Marguerite didn't tell me that. I worked it out. You put your hand there. On your chest, on the left, when you're thinking. You've done it since November. There's nothing in the pocket."

"No. There's nothing in it now."

"Then we know the same thing," says Win. "Don't we. What it is to love somebody and know it's no good, and stop."

"Yes."

"I still love him a bit," she says. "Desmond. I'll not lie to you. You don't stop. You just stop paying."

"I know," says Reggie.

She holds his hand across the table among the pans, and looks at him. He looks back. He knows what his face is doing. He hasn't the faintest idea how to stop it, and doesn't try.

"Well," says Win. "You'd better ask, then. Hadn't you. Since we've established it's you."

He gets up and comes round the table. She stands up to meet him. He puts his hands either side of her face, the way she did on the verandah in February, and he kisses her. It isn't gentle this time, and it isn't short. She makes a small sound into his mouth and takes hold of his shirt in both fists. He can feel her heart going against his chest, and his own going back at it, hard, and he doesn't care.

"Win," he says, against her mouth. "Will you have me upstairs?"

"*Yes*," she says. "*Yes*, you — yes. Not room seven."

"Not room seven?"

"Room seven's for guests," says Win, and takes his hand, and turns the gas off under the sauce.

On the half-landing, by the window where he emptied bucket two in November, he stops. He takes the little tin out of his waistcoat pocket and puts one under his tongue, before, not after, the way he's been told. She stands a step above him and watches him do it, and doesn't say anything, and doesn't look away.

"Marguerite says before," he says.

"I know," says Win. "It's in my book."

---

Her room is at the front, at the top, with the door onto the verandah where she does the day book. She's slept in it on her own for fifteen years. She has a moment in the doorway, with his hand in hers, of seeing it the way he'll see it. The iron bed and the white counterpane Iris made. The chair with her Sunday stockings over the back. The photograph of the children on the chest. The day book open on the table by the verandah door, where she left it at six.

He doesn't look at any of it. He looks at her.

She'd heard it from Marguerite, in the kitchen, in January, and she'd laughed. *That's how it goes, with him.* She'd thought she knew what Marguerite meant. She'd thought it meant the eggs.

She shuts the door behind them. He stands in the middle of her room in his shirtsleeves, and waits, the way she's seen him wait at the rail of the north pen for an animal to come up. It frightens her a little. Nobody's looked at her like that. Desmond looked at a room, the whole room at once, to see who was watching him.

"I'm forty-eight," she says. It comes out before she can stop it. "I've had two children and I eat my own cooking. I'm not — you'll have to take me as I come, Reggie."

"I'd like to take you as you come," he says. "Would you let me see?"

She has her hand on the lamp. She'd been going to turn it down.

"You want the lamp."

"I'd like to see you." He says it quite simply, as though it were a figure. "I've spent a great deal of time in the dark, Win. I'd rather see what I'm doing."

She leaves it. She turns it down a little, for her own sake, and he lets her.

---

He takes her dress off her as though it were the most interesting thing he's done in years. There are twelve buttons down the back. He does each of them separately, slowly, and after each one he puts his mouth to the place on her spine it uncovers. By the fourth her eyes are shut. By the seventh she has hold of the bedpost. By the last she's laughing, breathless, and saying *Reggie, for God's sake*, and he says *I'm counting*, against the small of her back. She can feel his mouth smile.

She turns round in his hands with her dress open and falling off her shoulders. He looks at her. Just looks, with his glasses on, in the lamplight, as though she were a result he'd waited all night for. She can't bear it, and she can't look away.

"You're very beautiful," he says.

"Don't you do the voice at me."

"I can't. I told you. That's just what I've got."

She takes his glasses off. She folds them and puts them on the chest beside the photograph of the children, and turns the children's faces to the wall, and he laughs. She undoes his shirt. He's thin, much thinner than she'd thought under the good suit. She can count his ribs with her hands, and she does, the way Marguerite would, and stops herself. There's a place on his chest where his heart's going so hard she can see it under the skin. She puts her palm flat over it and looks up at him.

"It's all right," he says. "It's allowed to do that. Marguerite says it's allowed to go to a hundred and ten."

"She told you a *number*?"

"She told me the number in 1971. I've been waiting to use it."

She laughs into his shoulder. He gets his hands under the fallen dress and pushes it down over her hips, and it goes, and she's standing in her slip in the lamplight with her hands on a man's bare chest. Her face is hot, and she doesn't care.

---

He's a scientist. She'd known that. She hadn't known what it would mean.

It means he asks. Nobody has asked her. Desmond knew what he liked and assumed she liked it too, and mostly, in the early years, she had. It was generous enough, and quick, and cheerful, and afterwards he'd go to sleep or go out. She'd lain in the dark in Kingston a great many nights, not unhappy exactly, and not knowing what else there was, and not having any words to ask for it if there were.

Reggie asks. He lies beside her on Iris's counterpane with his mouth at her throat, at the hollow of it where her pulse is, and he asks. Whether she likes that. Whether she'd like it harder, or slower, or not at all. He moves his mouth down to the join of her neck and shoulder and waits for her to say, and she can't.

"I don't —" Her voice has gone somewhere. "Reggie, I don't have the words. For any of it. I've not got the — I can't *say* things."

He lifts his head.

"Then don't say them. Show me." He takes her hand. "Or tell me yes, or no. Or *there*. You told me *higher, your left* in the back passage in November, with the lights out, and I managed."

"*You* said that."

"Did I?" He considers. "I did. Well. You can have it back."

So she shows him. She takes his hand, which is cool and very precise and not at all what she'd expected, and puts it where she wants it, on her hip, under the slip. After a moment she moves it further. He goes where she puts him and then he goes on from there by himself, finding it out. And every time she makes a sound he stops, and waits, and then does it again, exactly, as though he were writing it down.

"*There*," she says, at some point, into the pillow, and hears herself, and doesn't care.

"There," he agrees, quite hoarse, and stays.

She's never been attended to. That's what it is. She lies on her own bed at forty-eight with the lamp low and her slip rucked up round her ribs and a man's mouth on the inside of her knee, and understands it. She's run a house for fifteen years. She's put the second piece of fish on everybody's plate, and taken the bucket on the stair, and paid the envelope in Kingston every month for ten years. She has never once, in all that time, been the person in the room that somebody else couldn't see past.

He can't see past her. She can feel it. It's like standing in a light.

Marguerite's words come back to her, in the kitchen in January. *That's exactly how it goes, with him.* And behind them, the other thing, the thing she'd sat on her own verandah afraid of for three weeks. *He'd have him, if he could.*

There isn't any *him* in this room. There's hardly any room. There's her, and his breath against her skin, and his hand spread on her stomach. She'd been ashamed of it for twenty years, since the children, and he's holding it as if it were the one thing he'd come for.

"Reggie," she says. "Come here. Come up here. I want to see your face."

He comes up. She pulls him down onto her and holds his face in both hands, the way she did on the verandah, and looks at it. His eyes are unfocused without his glasses, very blue, fixed on her as if nothing else existed. There's sweat at his hairline. His mouth is wet. He doesn't look like a man doing anything he's supposed to. He looks like a man who's forgotten there was anything he was supposed to do.

"That," she says. "That's what you look like."

"What do I look like?"

"Like the jetty. That morning. With the thermometer." Her voice has gone again. "Like you'd found the thing, and you were going to read it."

He doesn't say anything. He puts his forehead down against hers, and she feels him breathe out, long and shaking, all the way down.

---

When he comes into her, he's slow about it, and watches her face the whole time, and asks. She says yes. She says it a great many times, after that, because it's the one word she's sure of, and every time she says it he makes a sound against her neck as though she'd handed him something. She holds on to his back. She can feel every one of his vertebrae under her hands, and the shoulder blades going like wings, and his heart. His heart's going so hard she can feel it through both of them.

"Tell me if —" she says.

"I will. I *will*, Win, I promise you. It's all right. It's *good*. It's —" He stops, because she's moved under him, and he loses the rest of it. "*Oh*. Oh, *there*. That's — do that again."

She does it again. She's never done that. She's never been asked to.

He laughs, into her shoulder, helplessly, and she laughs back, and the iron bed creaks like a ship. Somewhere below them the Ministry man and his friend are in rooms three and five with the door between. She thinks, *well, they'll know what that is*, and laughs harder. He wants to know what's funny. She can't tell him. She holds on.

It doesn't take very long, after that, for either of them. She's surprised by it. She'd somehow thought, at forty-eight, with her own wedding ring still on her hand, that she'd had whatever she was going to have of this. It takes her the way a wave takes the steps at the harbour. It comes up under her and lifts, and she hears herself say his name in a voice she's never used in her life. And he's saying hers, into her hair, over and over, like a figure he's checking.

---

Afterwards she lies with her head on his chest and counts his heart.

She doesn't mean to. It's Marguerite's fault. She counts it going down, from somewhere very fast to somewhere reasonable, against her cheek. She keeps a hand flat beside it. At some point she realises she's stopped counting and is only listening.

"Well?" he says, into her hair. "What's the figure?"

"I lost count."

"Marguerite'll want it."

"Marguerite," says Win, "can take her own pulses."

He laughs. It goes through his chest under her ear. And then he's quiet for a long time, stroking her back with one hand, slowly, up and down her spine, over the places where the buttons were.

"Win."

"Mm."

"I'd have married you. If you'd been free. I'd have asked you on the stairs." His hand stops. "I'd like that on the record. I've no right to ask it and I know you can't. But I wanted it said."

She lifts her head and looks at him. He can't see her properly, she knows, without his glasses. But he's looking where she is, all the same.

"It's on the record," she says. "It's in the book."

He falls asleep not long after, quite suddenly, the way she's seen children do after a fever. She lies awake. At some point in his sleep he turns over, and curls round her from behind, and gets an arm over her waist and his face into the back of her neck. He holds on as if she were a spar in a heavy sea. She lies there in the curve of him and doesn't move, because she doesn't want to wake him, and because she's never been held like that.

She can see out of the verandah door from the bed. There's the channel, and the long low shape of the cay against the stars, and the lamp on the long-room verandah that Mrs Joseph leaves for anybody who needs it.

At the far end of the row, the last hut, there's nothing.

She looks at the dark there for a long time, with his breath on her neck. She's glad of it. She's gladder of that dark than of any light she's ever seen.

---

He wakes at six with the grey coming in at the verandah door. He's wrapped round her like a man round a spar, and he has no idea how he got there.

He lies still. She's asleep. Her hair's come down in the night and it's in his mouth, and there's a crease on her cheek from the pillow, and she's snoring very slightly. He finds this so moving that he has to shut his eyes.

Then he gets up, carefully, and finds his glasses on the chest beside the children, who are still facing the wall. He finds his trousers, and his shirt, one sleeve inside out. He goes down the back stairs in his socks, into the kitchen, where the kitchen boy hasn't come in yet and the sauce is still on the stove in its pan, burnt black at the bottom, where they left it.

He opens the dresser drawers, one after the other, until he finds what he's looking for. It's in the second one down, with the skewers. It's the sugar thermometer she uses for the guava jelly, a long glass one in a tin case, marked up to three hundred and something Fahrenheit. It'll do. He can convert.

He goes out of the back door, and down through the yard past the generator shed, and across the road to the harbour. He goes down the harbour steps, the ones Iris came up in 1957 with a tall man carrying her bundle, and sits on the bottom one with his feet just clear of the water. He ties a bit of string from his pocket round the top of the case and lets it down.

He waits three minutes by his watch. The sun comes up over the second head while he does it. Across the channel, on the cay, somebody's already down at the north pen. Pascual's launch is on the slip, and the calf's mother blows, faint, over a mile of flat water.

He brings it up and reads it, and works it out in his head. He does it twice, because he checks things twice.

Then he goes back up the steps, and across the road, and through the yard, and into the kitchen.

She's there. She's got her dressing gown on, and her hair's still down. She's sitting at the kitchen table with the day book open in front of her, at a new page, with the pencil in her hand. She was waiting for him. He can see she was waiting for him.

"You've had my jelly thermometer," she says.

"I've had your jelly thermometer."

"What for?"

He stands in the doorway with it in his hand, wet, with the string still on it.

"Twenty-seven," he says.

Win looks at him for a moment. He can see her working it out, the way she works out a bill. Then something happens at the corner of her mouth, and she bends her head over the day book.

He watches her write it. She writes it at the top of the new page, above the date, in her small neat hand, where you'd carry a balance over from the page before. *Sea 27°.* Then she underlines it. Then she writes the date under it, *Tues. 8.v.73*, and the first line of the day's accounts. *Rm 3 & 5, breakfast, 2.*

"There," she says. "It's in the book."

He comes and sits down across from her at the kitchen table, among the pans, in his socks. He puts the thermometer down between them, square.

He's got somewhere to send it.
