**Pearl Tillerman to Reginald Tar**

*Baltimore, Md., 12 October 1999*

Dear Reggie,

Here it is. Look at the postmark before you read another word. *Baltimore*. You said in July 1997 on the balcony in Barbican that you'd never had one, and you'd like one. So this is the first one, and I've made the man at the post office on Charles Street stamp it twice, clearly, so you can't say it's smudged.

We're in. It's a row house on a street with trees, three storeys and very narrow, like living in a piano stood on end. Sammy carried every box up the front steps himself. He wouldn't let the men do it; he says the men don't know which way up the books go. The club's good. He's teaching children in the mornings and members in the afternoons, and on Saturdays we drive down to the farm. It's two and a half hours. He says it's nothing. It's not nothing, but it's not the Port Maria road either.

I start at the Harborview on Monday. Assistant controller. They were very impressed that I'd reconciled a hotel's books every month for eight years and had never once had them come back. I didn't tell them who taught me. They wouldn't have believed me.

You'll want the tide. I went down to the pilings at the farm on Saturday at high water, with Sammy, like you said. The weed line was just over a foot and a half, springs, as near as I could make it. Sammy was right. I told him I'd checked. He said he knew I would, and he'd have been worried if I hadn't.

Maybeth says to tell you she plays *Stardust* now. In Ike's key. I taught her in August and she's slow at everything but that, and she had it in a day. She plays it in the evenings in the front room at the farm. The whole family stops in the doorway, same as they did for me.

Happy birthday, a bit late. Eighty. You said so on the jetty steps, when I was four, and I've been holding you to it ever since, and you did it. You said I'd be very old, with grey hair, saying *Reggie, please, go away*, and you'd say *not yet, I'm still counting*. Well, I'm twenty-eight. I haven't got a single grey hair, and I'm NOT saying it. I'm not ever going to say it. I hope you're still counting. Ike says you had cake at Harbour House, and Auntie Marguerite let you have a slice, and Uncle Pascual cried.

I miss you. I miss the verandah. I miss Grandma's fish. Baltimore has crabs instead, which Sammy's family eats with hammers, on newspaper. I'll tell you about it when I've stopped laughing.

Your loving

Pearl

P.S. Sammy says to tell you the court at the club faces the wrong way and the afternoon sun's in his eyes on the serve. He says you'd understand, because you'd want to know which way it faces. He's right. It faces west-southwest. I measured it.

**Reginald Tar to Pearl Tillerman**

*Harbour House, the Bay, 30 October 1999*

My dear Pearl,

I have looked at the postmark. I have looked at it with my glass. It says *Baltimore MD*, twice, very clearly, and the man on Charles Street has my thanks. I've put it on the mantelpiece in the office, which your grandmother says is sentimental, and which she dusts every morning very carefully so as not to move it.

A piano stood on end is a very good description of a row house. I've seen photographs. You'll get used to the stairs. I never did get used to ours, but then I'd a better excuse.

West-southwest is a bad aspect for a tennis court. Tell Sammy I've looked it up. The club should have had it laid north-south in the first place, as any fool could tell them, and I'm sorry for his serve.

A foot and a half, springs, off the pilings. I've written it down. I have a page now in the back of the day book, your grandmother lets me have the back page, headed *Tides I Have Been Sent*. It has Kingston Harbour on it, in your hand, from 1977, and the ferry steps. It has Port Royal, which Ike sent from the Pegasus in 1996 when he was supposed to be practising. And now it has the farm on the Chesapeake. It's the best page in the book. I'd not give it for the whole Admiralty.

Eighty. Yes. And I'm still counting. I note, however, that you've not a grey hair and you refuse to say your line, which is a serious departure from the plan. I drew it with arrows, you'll remember, and a plan with arrows is binding. I've decided to allow it. I'll keep to my half. You can keep your hair.

Your aunt Marguerite took my pulse at the party and said *seventy-four*, and then she said *I told you*, and then she had to go and sit down in the kitchen for some time. She's eighty-three. She's not been well this summer; I'll not pretend to you she has. She sleeps a good deal. Pascual is with her all the time. I go up the hill on Tuesdays and sit on the back verandah with him, and we don't say much. We've said most of it by now.

I'm very well. I'm careful. I do everything I'm told, which your aunt says is a miracle, and your grandmother says is a sign of old age. I sit on the verandah in the mornings and watch the boats, and in the afternoons I go across to the cay on the Tuesday boat and look at the calf's calf's calf. I can't count between her breaths any more; my eyes aren't good enough to see her come up at that distance. Baptiste's student counts for me and tells me the figure. It's thirty-four. It was thirty-one, for her grandmother. They're getting better at holding it.

Tell Maybeth I should like very much to hear her play *Stardust*. Tell her I can't carry a tune in a bucket, and your grandmother has forbidden me to try, but I'd listen to the whole of it and not say a word. Tell her that's what I'm good for.

Your loving

Reggie

P.S. Crabs with hammers. I await the full account.

P.P.S. Your grandmother says to tell you that she misses you, and that she's said so to me every day since August, and that I'm to put it in writing because she won't. She's watching me write it. She says I've put it very badly. I've put it exactly as she said it.

**Isaac Carew to Reginald Tar**

*Royal College of Music, London, 8 February 2000*

Dear Reggie,

Thank you for yours of the 20th. I'm sorry this is late. It's the final year and they've got me doing the Brahms second concerto for the spring, and I've been in a practice room in the basement since Christmas with a Steinway that's sharp at the top, if you can believe it. The opposite of home. I keep reaching for the flat and it isn't there.

You asked about the generator in the basement here. It's not a generator, it's a boiler. It's enormous and Victorian and makes a noise at eleven every night like a whale being sick. I asked the caretaker if I could look at it. He was so surprised anybody'd asked that he took me down and showed me the whole thing. It's got a float, Reggie. A float valve, in the feed tank, the same as the one at Harbour House, only the size of a football. It sticks. He hits it. I told him Grandma'd been hitting hers since 1959 and he said *your gran and me'd get on*.

Auntie Joy's well. She's retiring in June and says she'll come home, but she's said that every year since I got here. She makes me Sunday dinner in Hammersmith and asks if I'm eating, and then she watches me eat it, exactly like Grandma. I told her so. She said *where do you think Grandma got it from*, and I didn't know what to say.

The other thing. You'll have heard from Pearl, because Pearl tells you everything. I've been seeing somebody. Not Andrea. Andrea and I still write, but it's — I'll tell you about that another time. This is somebody here. Her name's Ruth, and she's a piano technician. She came to regulate the Steinway in November because the action was sticking in the treble. I stood in the door of the practice room and watched her take the whole thing apart, the way you and I took the front off the bar piano when I was six. And I asked her a question about the hammers, and she answered it, and then she asked me one back.

I don't know yet. It's early. But she tunes pianos, Reggie. She knows why the top goes flat. I told her about the sea air and the Bay and she said *of course it does, the wires stretch*, as if everybody knew. I think you'd like her. I think she'd ask you about the tuning fork.

Tell Grandma the Brahms is going well. Don't tell her about the boiler.

Love,

Ike

P.S. I've still got your tuning fork. The A. You lent it me in 1984 and I never gave it back. I use it every day. Is that all right? I'll bring it back if you want it.

**Andrea Chung to Isaac Carew**

*Toronto, 2 March 2000*

Dear Ike,

This is going to be a hard one to write, so I'm going to write it the way you taught me, which is to say what I saw, and ask a question, and wait. Only I don't think I'll ask the question, because I think we both know the answer, and I don't want to make you write it.

I saw a boy at a piano in the University Chapel who went off in the second verse and came back, and I forgot to turn the page. I've got the programme. It's on the wall of my room here. I've written to you every month for two and a half years and you've written back every month, four pages, about the going-off bits and the boiler and your sister's new house. They've been the best letters I've ever had. I'm going to keep every one.

And I've met somebody. Here, at the conservatory. His name's David and he plays the viola, which you'll laugh at. He's very kind and very quiet, and he doesn't go off anywhere, and he asks me how I am and waits for the answer.

I think you've met somebody too. You haven't said. But your February letter was about a piano technician for a page and a half, and you've never written a page and a half about anybody but your sister.

So I'm not going to ask. I'm just going to say thank you. For the second verse, and the letters, and for teaching me that you're allowed to come in late and it's still music. I'll hear you play one day at the Roy Thomson Hall and I'll be in the front row, and I'll turn round to David and say *I used to turn his pages*, and he'll say *I know, you've told me*.

Write to me when you've something to say. Not every month. Just when it's true.

Love, always, in the way it's going to be,

Andrea

P.S. Tell Reggie I remember him pointing at the vestry door. Tell him he was right. It was a very good summer.

**Samuel Tillerman to Reginald Tar**

*Baltimore, Md., 14 March 2000*

Dr Tar,

I'll telephone the harbour office at a decent hour. Pearl says if I ring at five in the morning Mrs Tar will have my head. But I'll forget half of it on the telephone, I always do, so I'm writing it down now, while it's all still here.

We've got a girl. Born at 3.52 this morning at Johns Hopkins. Seven pounds four ounces. She's got a lot of black hair and Pearl's frown and she's LOUD. She came out loud. The nurse said she's never heard a baby hold a note like that. Pearl said *that's the Myrtle Bank* and then went to sleep in the middle of the next sentence.

We're calling her Iris. Iris Winifred. Pearl says her great-grandmother did the sheets on your row for thirty years and never let anybody carry her bundle except one man. She says you'll know the story. She said you'd like it to be Iris.

Gram came up from the farm in Dicey's truck at six this morning. She's eighty-something and she won't say what. She sat by the bed for an hour holding the baby and not saying anything, which is unusual for Gram. Then she gave her back and said *she'll do*. That's the highest thing Gram says about anybody. She said it about Pearl the first time too.

James has made a table. If we come at Christmas you'll be eighty-one and some months when you hold her. Gram says she's older and she's held her already, so that's settled. Pearl says Mr Pobil's older than both of you and will want a turn. James has put everybody in, in order of age, with dates. He's a doctor and he writes everything down anyway, but Pearl's made it worse. She likes a table. She's started sending him hers.

Maybeth played *Stardust* at the farm last Saturday for the baby, before she was born, with her hand on Pearl's stomach. Pearl said the baby kicked on the second line. That's where Pearl comes in. Maybeth says it's a sign. James says it isn't. I think Maybeth's right.

We'll come at Christmas. All three of us. Pearl says she wants you to hold her the way Mrs Carew taught you to hold Pearl, under the arms, and let her do the rest.

Sammy

P.S. The court still faces west-southwest. I've complained. They've said they'll look at it.

**Reginald Tar to Pearl Tillerman**

*Harbour House, the Bay, 9 January 2001*

My dear Pearl,

You'll be home by now, and back in the piano stood on end, and I expect it's snowing. Your grandmother says it snows in Baltimore in January. I've not seen snow since I left England in 1952, and I didn't much care for it then. I find I'd like to see it on your steps.

I've been trying to write this since you left on the 2nd and I can't get it right. So I'm going to put it down the way it happened and let you do the rest, which is what you always do anyway.

You came up the harbour steps on the 22nd with her in a sling on your front, the way the women at the Bay carry them. Sammy was behind you with four cases and a folding cot and a face like a man who'd brought a boat in through the second head on a bad night. And your grandmother went down the steps. Pearl, your grandmother went down the harbour steps two at a time, at seventy-five, in her apron. I've not seen her go down a stair two at a time since 1972.

I didn't. I waited at the top, on the bench, because I'd been told to, and because I'd taken one and was waiting for it to work. And you came up to the top and knelt down on the paving in front of the bench, in your good travelling skirt, and took her out of the sling, and held her out to me.

I've held you. I've held Ike. I held both of you in the kitchen at Harbour House when you were small, and your mother had to show me how, under the arms. I've held the calf, and the calf's calf, in the sling at the north pen, with Pascual on the other end of the line. I thought I knew how.

She was so small. She was so much smaller than you. And she was asleep, with her fists up by her ears and her mouth open, and your frown. And I put my hands under her arms, the way your mother taught me. And she woke up, and looked at me, and didn't cry.

She looked at me for a very long time. I don't know how long; I didn't count. It's the first time in my life I've forgotten to count. She looked at me all at once, and then quite still, as though there were nothing else on the whole of the harbour wall.

And then she put her hand out and took hold of my glasses by the bridge and pulled them off, and put them in her mouth.

Your grandmother laughed so much she had to sit down on the steps.

I've written it down. It's on the back page of the day book, under *Tides I Have Been Sent*. There's a new heading, below. It says *Things That Have Happened To Me At Eighty-One That I Did Not Expect*. There's only one thing on it. I don't think there'll need to be any more.

Thank you for bringing her. Thank you for kneeling down.

Your loving

Reggie

P.S. Sammy and I went down to the harbour steps on Boxing Day at high water and looked at the weed. He asked me how I'd measured it before there were proper tide tables. I told him about the string and the thermometer. He listened to the whole of it. He's a very good listener, your young man. I'd expected him to be. I hadn't expected him to be quite so good.

P.P.S. Your grandmother has asked me to tell you that the glasses are quite all right and she's had them cleaned in Kingston.

**Pascual Pobil to Pearl Tillerman**

*The house on the hill, the Bay, 22 August 2001*

Querida Pearl,

Your grandmother will have telephoned you. I know she did, because I was in the kitchen when she did it, and I heard her go into the office and shut the door, and I heard her say your name, and then I went out on to the verandah because I couldn't hear any more.

Marguerite died on Sunday morning, at a quarter past six, in our bed, with the window open. I was holding her hand. It was very quiet. She'd said the night before that she'd had enough, and she'd like to go now, please, and I said I'd not stop her. I've never been able to stop her doing anything. Forty years I've been trying.

Your mother will have told you about the funeral. The whole Bay came. The Kilburns carried her, four of Winston's grandsons, and old Mrs Kilburn's great-grandson played the harmonium, badly, and I didn't mind.

I'm writing because of something Reggie did. I want you to know it, because you won't hear it from him.

After the funeral, when everybody'd gone, I was on the back verandah. I didn't want the house. And Reggie came up the hill. He's eighty-two, Pearl, and he's not supposed to do that hill at all; Marguerite said so for thirty years. He came up it anyway, on his own, very slowly, with his stick, stopping every twenty yards. It took him three-quarters of an hour. I watched him the whole way and I couldn't go down to help him because I couldn't move.

He came up the steps and sat down beside me on the bench. He took out his little tin, and took one, and put the tin away. And then he held out his wrist to me.

He held it out, the way he held it out to Marguerite every Sunday for thirty years. And he said, *Somebody's got to count it, hombre. She'd be furious if nobody did.*

So I counted it. It was ninety-two, which is too high, because of the hill. I told him so. He said *I know, I'll sit here till it comes down, and you can count it again*. And we sat there. And I counted it every ten minutes till it was seventy, which took an hour and a half. By the end I'd stopped crying, and I hadn't noticed, and that was what he'd come up the hill for.

He's the best friend I ever had, Pearl. Since 1952. We made each other better. It took us fifty years and a great deal of shouting. You and Sammy will do it quicker, I think. Your generation's cleverer.

Look after him for me, when you come. Make him sit down.

Tu tío que te quiere,

Pascual

P.S. I have his pulse in Marguerite's book now. Win gave it me. I'm to take it on Tuesdays, when he comes up. I don't know the proper way to write it down, so I've been drawing a line. He says it's the best graph he's ever seen.

**Pearl Tillerman to Reginald Tar**

*The farm, Somerset County, Md., 18 April 2002*

Dear Reggie,

Look at the postmark. I'm sorry. It says *Crisfield*. It's not Baltimore any more, and it's never going to be again, and I know you had the other one on the mantelpiece. You can put this one beside it.

We've moved to the farm. We did it in January, in the snow, which I've now seen and don't care for either. The row house was very narrow and Iris had started to climb the stairs, all three storeys of them, and Sammy was driving down every Friday night and back every Monday at five in the morning to help Maybeth. And Gram's slower this winter. And one night in December Sammy sat at the kitchen table and said, *what if we just lived there*, and I got out a pad and did the columns. They came out.

So I've left the Harborview. I've set up on my own, in two rooms over a hardware shop in Salisbury, with a brass plate on the door that Dicey made me: *P. Tillerman, Accounts*. I've got nine clients. Dicey's boats, first, of course, and a marina, and two inns, and a man who grows chickens, fourteen thousand of them, and has kept his books in a feed sack since 1971. I go in four days a week and do the rest at the kitchen table. Grandma would laugh at me. I've got a day book. It's green, not blue. I tick the bottom of every column.

Sammy's coaching at the high school, afternoons, and running clinics for the farm children on Saturdays, on the town courts, which face east-west and are cracked. And he's planning a court of his own. Here, on the point.

We haven't got the money yet. We know exactly how much we haven't got; Sammy's written it on the inside of the pantry door, like Grandma's buckets, and I've checked it. But he's planning it, the way Sammy plans things, which is by walking up and down the field behind the house for an hour every evening, not saying anything, and then knowing. He asked James about the drainage, because the whole point's barely above the tide and James worries about water. James sent eleven pages. He asked Dicey to look at the grade, because she's got an eye for a line from the boats, and she lay down on her stomach in the wet grass with a spirit level and said *two inches, that end*, and was right. He's staked it out with string. You'd like the string.

It runs north–south. He said you said any fool could have told the club, and he wasn't going to be that fool.

Maybeth's giving piano lessons in the front room on Tuesdays and Thursdays, to the children from the farms down the road. Iris sits under the piano through all of them, with her back against the leg, and hums. Maybeth says she hums in tune. I've checked. She does.

And Jeff, Dicey's Jeff, comes on Sundays with his guitar. Everybody sings at the table, the way they always do, and Jeff plays, and Maybeth plays. Last week Gram sang the last verse of something on her own, very thin and very true, and then said *that'll do* and went to bed. Sammy says she hasn't done that in ten years.

You'll want the tide. I go down to the pilings every Saturday at high water now, with Iris on my hip. A foot and a half at springs, same as ever. I've started a page for it. I'll send you the whole of it at Christmas.

I miss the verandah. I miss the fish. But I've got a kitchen table that seats twelve if we squeeze, and a green book, and a court that isn't there yet, and I'm happy, Reggie. I wanted you to know that. I'm as happy as I've ever been.

Your loving

Pearl

P.S. Tell Grandma I've got a client who pays in eggs. She'll know what to do. I don't.

**James Tillerman to Reginald Tar**

*Johns Hopkins Hospital, Baltimore, 3 November 2002*

Dear Dr Tar,

You won't remember me particularly. I'm the one who stood at the back at the wedding at the Bay in 1998 and asked you about the drainage of the cay, and then couldn't stop asking. My brother tells me you remember everybody who asks you a question. So perhaps you do.

I should say first that I've read your papers. Not all of them; there are rather a lot. But the ones in the Hopkins library, which has a surprising run of the *Proceedings*. I'm a physician, a cardiologist, so I went straight for the 1956 one on heart rate in the calf during the dive. The slowing, the bradycardia, which you charted at the rail by hand, every breath, for eleven days. I've read it four times. I have a question about your Figure 3. Why does the rate drop before the animal goes under, and not after? Is it anticipation? Can a heart know what's coming? I've got a patient who seems to, and I can't make it out.

But that's not really why I'm writing.

I'm writing because of something you said at the wedding breakfast, in the yard, under the almond trees. I asked you how you'd known, back in the fifties, that an animal was going to come up before it came up. And you said you didn't know. You said you'd only counted it enough times that you could tell when a figure was about to be wrong.

I've been thinking about that for four years. It's the most useful thing anybody's ever said to me about medicine, and you weren't talking about medicine. Nine-tenths of what I do is counting a thing enough times that I can tell when a figure's about to be wrong. I've said it to every resident I've had since. And I've been writing an essay about it, for a magazine, about listening to hearts. It's in draft; I've enclosed it. You're in it, by name, with the figure, and the rail. I should have asked before I wrote you in, and I didn't. So I'm asking now. May I? If you'd rather not, I'll take you out, and it'll be a worse essay. My editor has already asked who you are. I said *a marine biologist in Jamaica who married my sister-in-law's grandmother*, and she said *ah*, as though that explained it.

Sammy says I think too much and I should write to you and get it out of my system. So I'm writing. I don't want anything, except your permission, if you're willing to give it, and, if you have a moment, Figure 3.

My brother's very happy. You'll know that. He talks about Pearl the way he used to talk about Dicey's boats, and Maybeth's piano, and me, God help us. I used to think it was embarrassing. I've changed my mind.

With great respect,

James Tillerman

P.S. Sammy says I'm the clever one. I'm not. I'm the one who worries. He's the one who turns up. It took me thirty years to work out which is more use.

**Reginald Tar to James Tillerman**

*Harbour House, the Bay, 19 November 2002*

Dear James,

I remember you perfectly. You asked about the drainage of the cay, and then about the cistern, and then about why the Institute's jetty was built on the north side. You were right about the jetty, and I'd been wrong about it for fifty years. I've told Jean. She said she knew.

Figure 3. Yes. I've enclosed the original tracings from 1956, which I kept, and a new chart I've drawn for you with the rate against time to the dive, second by second. I've marked the drop in red. It begins eleven to fourteen seconds before she goes under, every time, in every one of the eleven days. I don't know why. Nobody's known why for forty-six years. I've always thought it was anticipation. I've always thought a heart can know what's coming, if it's done the thing enough times. I've some personal reasons for thinking so. Tell your patient I believe him.

The chart runs to four sheets and I apologise for it. Your sister-in-law will tell you I've been doing charts with arrows for people since 1975 and can't stop.

Thank you for your letter. I've read it three times, and the essay twice. Yes, you may put me in. You may put in the rail and the figure and the eleven days, and anything else you like, and you're not to take me out on any account. Tell your editor I said *ah* back.

I've a question about the essay, since you asked me one. On your fifth page you say you didn't tell your patient what his heart was doing: that it slows before the bad news, as if it knew. Why not? I'd have wanted to be told. I think he might. I'd rather know what my own heart's up to, even when there's nothing to be done about it. I think most men would. Tell me what you decide.

As for your P.S., you're quite wrong. Worrying is a very great deal of use. It's how you count a thing enough times. The one who turns up needs the one who worries. Your brother knows that. It's why he rang you about Baltimore.

But you're right as well. The one who worries needs the one who turns up; rather more, I think. I've been both, at different times in my life, and it's no good being only one.

Yours, with great regard,

Reginald Tar

P.S. You're the second Tillerman to write to me about a thing I said and couldn't remember saying. The first was your sister Maybeth, in 2000, about *Stardust*. You're a very alarming family. I'm delighted to be married into it, at two removes.

**Reginald Tar to Pearl Tillerman**

*Harbour House, the Bay, 20 July 2003*

My dear Pearl,

This is a letter with a thing in it I'd rather not put, and I'm going to put it anyway, because you'd want to know, and because I've been told I should tell people things.

I had a bad night on Tuesday. Not very bad. I'm not in hospital, and I'm writing this on the verandah with my feet on the stool and your grandmother in the other chair pretending to do the day book. But it was a bad night, and I took three, and it took longer to go than it should have. On Wednesday your grandmother had the new man up from Port Maria, Dr Lindo, who's Marguerite's successor and very young and very careful. He listened for a long time, and took a tracing, and sent it to Kingston.

He says it's tired. That's his word. He says it's done a great deal of work for eighty-four years, and a good deal of it the wrong way round, and it's tired. He says there's nothing to be done that would be worth the doing, not at my age. He says I'm to sit more, and not go across to the cay any more on my own, and take one before I so much as get up out of the chair. And he says, very carefully, that he couldn't tell me how long. It might be years. It might not.

I'm telling you because you'd want to be told. I'd have wanted to be told, and I wasn't, about a good many things. I'm not telling you so you'll worry, though I know you will. And I'm not telling you so you'll come, because you won't, not in your condition. Your grandmother says so and I say so, and Sammy will say so if you ask him, and so will Dicey, who I gather has moved into your spare room already with a toolbox. Jeff, I'm told, has been left in charge of their house, and of himself, and has been told he'll manage.

I'm not frightened. I want you to know that too. I've been careful for thirty years. I've been careful for your grandmother, and then for you, and for Ike, and for Iris, who pulled my glasses off. I've had thirty years I'd no right to expect, sitting on this verandah looking across at the cay. I'd not give back one of them. I've had fish every night and your grandmother across the table and four hundred and ninety-one letters in a drawer. That's a very good account. It's in credit.

Your brother-in-law James writes every month now, since November. He's a very good correspondent and a very alarming one. We've got as far as Figure 7 of the 1958 paper, and he's found a mistake in it that I made at thirty-nine and nobody's noticed since. I've told him he's to write it up. Tell him I said so again; he won't believe it from me.

Tell me how you are. Tell me about the baby, and whether it kicks on the second line. Tell me about Iris, and the court, and whether Sammy's got as far as the drainage. North–south. I'm very glad. Tell me the tide at the farm. I want to know everything, the same as always.

**Reginald Tar to Pearl Tillerman**

*To be sent after. Harbour House, the Bay, 24 July 2003*

My dear Pearl,

If you're reading this, your grandmother's sent it, and I'm not on the verandah any more. I'm sorry. I'd have liked to see the baby. I'd have liked to hold it under the arms and have it take my glasses. I'd have liked a good deal more of everything. But I've had thirty years I'd no right to, and I'll not complain about the last few weeks of it.

I'm writing this now because Dr Lindo said *tired*, and I've been careful long enough to know what a word like that means. I've written one for Ike as well, and one for Pascual, and one for your grandmother, which she's not to open till after either, and which she's put in her apron pocket, God help me, where she keeps things she means to carry for a month.

I've not told you what to do. Not once, since you were four. You've told me so, on the verandah, and you were cross about it, and I'm told I was right. I'm going to keep to it. There's nothing in this letter telling you what to do.

But I promised myself, on the verandah in 1989, with three drafts in my lap, that I'd tell people things. That I'd tell them what they didn't know, if it was mine to tell and they'd want it. So I'm going to tell you something. It's mine. And I think you'd want it.

On the verandah in 1997, when you asked for my blessing, I told you there'd been somebody I loved once, on the other side of the North Sea, for fourteen years, with a lamp. I didn't tell you who.

His name was Alec Spilsbury. He was a zoologist. I met him in a corridor in 1946, when he told me in front of four people that I was wrong about a curve, and I was. I loved him for twenty-five years. For most of them we couldn't be together. Partly it was the law, and the times, and what it would have cost other people. But partly it was things I did, and things he did, to keep it hidden. Some of them were cruel, and some of them cost other people a great deal, and in the end they were what broke it. He went to Norway in 1957. I kept a lamp lit at the end of the row, every night, for fourteen years, for somebody who couldn't come, and whom I'd have turned away if he had come. He died in 1971. I let it go out that November, and I took the temperature the next morning and said the figure to nobody.

Some of that I'll not put in a letter. Your grandmother knows it. So does Pascual; he was there for most of it, and shouted at me through the worst of it. Ask either of them, if you want to, and they'll tell you. I've told them both they may. It's because of those things, I think, that I understood your grandmother so well, the first year. Why she'd stopped singing. Why she carried a letter in her apron for a month and didn't answer it. I'd done the same, with a letter in this pocket. We'd both of us paid, and both of us stopped. It's a great deal to have in common with somebody. It was a place to start.

Your grandmother saw that lamp from her verandah for thirteen years before she ever spoke to me. She saw it go out. She's the only one who did, apart from Pascual. She'll tell you, if you ask her properly.

I'm telling you because you told me, on the verandah, that distance was the thing you were afraid of. And I told you distance is a great deal, and it's not everything. I wanted you to know how I knew. I knew because I'd done it, fourteen years of it, with a lamp and the whole North Sea. And it wasn't everything. It was a great deal. And then it ended. And I came up the harbour steps at the Bay, in a storm, and your grandmother hit a generator with the heel of her hand, and I found out there was a great deal more.

I've been very lucky, Pearl. Twice. More than most men are once. I'd like you to know that, whatever you hear about me after.

And I'm telling you because I'd have given a great deal, when I was young, to know that somebody who loved me had loved like that too, and come out the other side, and been all right. Nobody told me. So I'm telling you. And perhaps one day there'll be somebody you'll want to tell. One of yours, or somebody else's, who needs to know it can come out all right. Then it's yours to tell. I'd like that. I'd like it to be of use.

Now the rest.

The letters are yours. Ike will have told you by now. Four hundred and ninety-one. I read every one more than once, and some of them a great many times. The one about the weed on the ferry steps, when you were six. The one about *Stardust* when you were nine, and *I will not grow out of it*, underlined. The one from Baltimore with the postmark. Keep them or burn them, as you like. They're yours. They were always yours. I only had the looking after of them.

And now the thing I most want you to have, which isn't in the drawer.

In June 1974 I was fifty-five years old and I had never held a child. Not properly. I'd held one once, at a christening in 1938, at arm's length, like a specimen jar, and it screamed. I'd been told, more than once, by people who had every right to say it, that I didn't see people. I'd loved very few people in my life. Four, if I'm honest, including your Uncle Pascual and Aunt Marguerite, which seemed to me a great many to be going on with. Of the other two, one I'd had to hide for twenty-five years, and one I'd only just found. I'd have told you, if you'd asked me then, that I knew what love was. I thought I'd had the whole of it.

And then you came up the kitchen at Harbour House to my chair, at three years old, and put your arms up.

You didn't ask. You didn't wait to see whether I'd earned it. You didn't know anything about me at all, except that I'd drawn some boats and told you the sea breathes. You just decided. You put your arms up as though there were no doubt in the world that I'd pick you up, and I didn't know how, and your mother had to show me. And by the Tuesday you'd decided I was yours. Nobody had ever done that to me in my life. Everything I'd ever had, I'd argued for, or earned, or hidden, or waited for. You simply took me, the way you'd take a hand on a stair.

I didn't know what it was to love a child. I didn't know what it was to love somebody and watch them change, year on year, into somebody else, and still be the same person the whole way through. The same frown at four, and at nine with *I will not grow out of it* underlined, and at eighteen under a streetlamp, and at twenty-six on a verandah asking me for a thing I didn't know how to give. I didn't know you could love somebody and want them to go away from you, to Baltimore, to a life you'll not see, and be glad of it. I didn't know you could love somebody without wanting a single thing back from them except the next letter. I learned all of that from you. I'd not have learned it from anybody else. Nobody else would have put their arms up.

Your grandmother changed me. She laughed at me in a kitchen in 1972 and I've not been the same man since. Pascual changed me, by shouting, for fifty years. It's not a competition, and they'd both box my ears if I made it one. But you did a thing neither of them could have done, because they're my age, and they met me as I was. You met me as nothing at all, a grey old man at a kitchen table. You made me into somebody's. I was yours before I was anything else to you. That's a very great thing to be made into, Pearl, at fifty-five. I'd not known it was there to be had.

You'll have noticed I sign *your loving* to nobody else. Not to your grandmother, who has it every day across the fish and doesn't need it in ink. Not to Ike, who'd die of embarrassment, and whom I love more than he knows. I signed it to you the first time in 1977, because you were six and I didn't know how else you ended a letter to a child. And then I found it was simply true, and I couldn't sign it any other way. I've been your loving Reggie for twenty-six years. It's the best thing I've ever been. You made it true long before I put it in ink. I only wrote it down.

You asked me once, on the verandah, what would happen if you were a long way off and I wasn't here any more. I told you then. I'll tell you again, now that it's so.

You'll count between breaths at the rail of something. You'll ask somebody a question and wait for the whole of the answer. You'll look at the weed on a flight of steps and write the figure down. You already do. You were doing it at four, holding your breath with the calf, and you've never stopped. That's me, Pearl. That's where I'll be. It's not a thing I can take back by dying. I gave it you. It's yours.

And you've got the rest from the others. The columns from your grandmother, going up as well as down. The music from your grandfather, which is real, and his own, and which he gave back to your grandmother on her wedding morning, which is the best thing I ever knew him do. The seams from your mother. Your father's standing order, the one he stopped. Ike. Sammy, and his family that sings at the table. Iris, who pulls glasses off. It's a very great deal, Pearl. It's the best account I've ever seen. It's very much in credit. You'll not need me for it.

But I'd like to be on the list. If that's all right. I think I am already. I'd like to stay there.

Tell the baby about the tide. Tell it the sea goes up and down, twice a day, like breathing. Put its hand on your chest so it can feel.

And sing. Not because I'm telling you. I'm not. Only because I'd like to think of it.

Your loving

Reggie
