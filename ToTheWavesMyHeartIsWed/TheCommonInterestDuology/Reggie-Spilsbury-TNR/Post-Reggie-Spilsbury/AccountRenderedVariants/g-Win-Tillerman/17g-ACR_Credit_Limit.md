# Credit Limit

The first thing Ike knows about Julian Rowe is that he's better than Ike at the piano.

He knows it before he's seen him. He's in the church hall on a Saturday in January, at the flat-topped upright, doing his scales for Mrs Beckford. He's ten, and he's been playing six years, and he's the best in Grandad Hinds's church and knows it. Then the door at the back opens, and somebody comes in, and stands at the back, and listens.

Ike doesn't look round. You don't. Mrs Beckford says if you look round you've lost it. He finishes the scale. He starts the Clementi. He gets to the hard bit in the left hand, the bit he always nearly gets, and gets it.

And from the back of the hall somebody says, "Nice."

It's not much of a word. But it's said the way Mrs Beckford says it once a year. And it's said by somebody who knows exactly which bit was the hard bit, because he says it right after, not at the end.

Ike looks round.

There's a young man at the back of the hall, leaning on the door frame, with his hands in his pockets. He's tall and brown and loose, in a white shirt with the sleeves pushed up, and he's got a face that's smiling before anything's happened. And beside him, with her school bag on her shoulder and her face gone pink, there's Pearl.

"Ike," says Pearl, "this is Julian. He plays at the Pegasus on Fridays. He heard me sing at the Christmas service and he's —" She stops. "He asked if he could hear us. Together."

Julian comes down the hall. He doesn't hurry. He stops by the piano and looks at the keys, and at Ike's hands on them, and then at Ike.

"Clementi," he says. "Op. 36. You get the left hand in the second bit better than I did at your age."

"How old were you?"

"Twelve." Julian grins. "You're what, ten? You'll be better than me by the time you're twelve. I'll have to watch out." He puts one long hand on the top of the piano. "Flat at the top."

"*Everybody* says that," says Ike.

"Because it's *true*, man." And Julian laughs, and Ike, who had decided to hate him on principle, laughs too, before he can stop himself.

---

Julian sits down at the piano. Ike gives him the bench, which he's never given anybody but Grandpa Des, and he's not sure afterwards why he did. Julian runs his hands up and down it, once, all the way, the way Grandpa Des does. Ike's stomach goes tight.

"What do you two do?"

"Hymns," says Pearl. "And *Stardust*. And some others. Ike does the chords."

"*Stardust*." Julian looks round at her, quick. "Who taught you *Stardust*? Nobody our age knows *Stardust*."

"My grandfather."

"Your grandfather's got taste." He plays the start of it. Not the way Ike plays it. Not the way Grandpa Des plays it either. Something in between, and newer, with a chord in the second bar that Ike's never heard and wants, immediately, to know how you do. "Come in where you like."

Pearl comes in on the second line.

And Ike, standing at the end of the piano with his hands hanging, hears it.

He's heard Pearl sing his whole life. He's played for her since he was four. He knows her voice the way he knows his own hands, where it goes up and where it holds and where it gets frightened. And he's heard Grandpa Des play for her, twice, in this hall, and been jealous both times, and not known it was jealous till after.

This is that. This is Grandpa Des. Julian's *with* her. Every bar. When she holds, he holds. When she goes up, he's there before she gets there, with the chord already waiting, like a hand held out. He makes room. He listens to her with his whole body, bent over the keys with his head turned towards her, and his face has gone quite still.

And Pearl's face.

Ike looks at his sister's face and his stomach turns over. He's seen it once before. At the Bay, in the bar, three years ago, the night Grandma came in from the kitchen and sang. Pearl looked like that then, like somebody who's been carried.

She's being carried now. By somebody who isn't him.

They get to the end. Julian plays two bars after her, soft, and stops. Exactly like Grandpa Des. Exactly.

Nobody says anything.

Then Julian turns round on the bench, and looks up at Pearl, and his face comes back on, the smile, like somebody turning a lamp up.

"*Pearl*," he says. "Where have you been all my *life*?"

And Ike, at the end of the piano, finds he's gone quite cold all over. He doesn't know why. It's a thing people say. It's in songs.

But he's heard it before. He's heard exactly that, in exactly that voice. He can't think where.

---

Pearl knows exactly what she's doing. She'd like that on the record.

She knows it the first night, at the Pegasus, when she sings two numbers with Julian's quartet in the Starlight Lounge in her mother's good blue dress, altered. He's at the grand piano in a white jacket. There are a hundred people at the tables, tourists and businessmen and a table of Ministry wives. She sings *Stardust* and then *Stormy Weather*, which he taught her that week in the church hall. And he's with her every bar, and she comes off the little stage with her heart going like the generator at Harbour House. He's standing at the bottom of the steps in his white jacket, holding his hand out to help her down, and he says, *you're the real thing, Pearl Carew. You're the real thing.*

And she thinks, very clearly, with his hand in hers and her heart going: *this is what Grandma meant.*

*You'll come off the stand with your heart going and think, that's who he really is.*

She takes his hand, and lets him help her down, and lets him kiss her, afterwards, in the car park behind the Pegasus, under the jacaranda. It's the first time anybody's kissed her properly. He does it very well. She thinks he's probably done it a great deal. She doesn't mind.

She knows exactly what she's doing.

---

Mummy alters the blue dress for her. She does it on the Thursday before, in the front room, with Pearl standing on the kitchen chair and Mummy on her knees with a mouth full of pins. It's the same chair Pearl stood on at six to sing the Easter hymn for Reggie.

Mummy doesn't say anything about Julian. Daddy's said a good deal, at dinner, in a careful voice. Auntie Joy has written twice. Mummy just pins. She takes the waist in and lets the hem down half an inch, because Pearl's grown, and she sits back on her heels to look at it.

"Turn," she says.

Pearl turns.

"Does he listen to you?" says Mummy, round the pins. "Off the stand."

Pearl stands on the kitchen chair and doesn't answer for a while.

"Sometimes," she says.

Mummy nods. She takes a pin out of her mouth and puts it in the hem. She doesn't say anything else about it, then or after.

"There," she says, when she's done. "It'll fit. Whatever you do in it, it'll fit." She holds up her hand to help Pearl down off the chair, the way she's done since Pearl was small enough to be lifted. "Mind the pins."

---

*Feb. 1989. Julian Rowe. 21. Piano. Plays Fri. & Sat. at the Pegasus, Tues. at a club in New Kingston. Lives in a room in Half Way Tree. Studied at the Edna Manley School 2 years, left. Says he'll go to Miami, New York. Says I'm the real thing.*

*He is with me every bar. Every single bar. It is like Grandpa Des. It might be better.*

*Kissed me under the jacaranda. 10.40 p.m. Very good at it.*

And then, underneath, because the blue book is for true things, and because she's been doing columns since she was eight:

*DOWN: He is the best musician I have ever sung with. He listens to every note. He is funny. He is kind to Ike (mostly). He thinks I am the real thing. So do I, when I'm singing with him.*

*UP: He was 40 minutes late on Tuesday. He did not say sorry, he said a story. He asked what I want to read at the University and then talked about Miami. He called Ike "little man" twice and "Isaac" once. It is IKE. He has asked me for 5 dollars twice for the bus.*

She looks at the two columns for a long time, sitting up in bed in Barbican with the torch.

They don't match.

She knows what Grandma would say. She knows what the day book says when the columns don't match. *You find it.*

She puts the torch out. She's not ready to find it yet. She'd like, just for a while, not to have to.

---

The thing nobody tells you, she thinks, is that it's *good*. That's what nobody says. Grandma didn't say it and Mummy doesn't say it and Auntie Joy, in her letters from London, certainly doesn't. They all say *be careful*. And *you're very young*. And, in Auntie Joy's big round hand, *Pearlie, MUSICIANS*, underlined twice. They talk as though it's a trap, as though you'd have to be stupid to walk into it.

It's not a trap. It's the best thing that's ever happened to her. On Friday nights at the Pegasus, for three minutes at a time, she's more herself than she's ever been. Julian's at the piano and he knows exactly where she's going. It's like being understood all the way down, in a language only the two of them speak. And the tourists stop eating, and the Ministry wives put their forks down. Once, in March, a man from a record company in Miami comes up afterwards and gives Julian a card.

It's not stupid. It's not a trap. It's the realest thing she knows.

And on the Saturday after the card he's an hour and ten minutes late to take her to the pictures. He says he was on the telephone to Miami. When they get there the film's started, and he talks all through the first half about the man with the card. He doesn't ask her anything, and she doesn't mind. And then she minds.

She can have both, she thinks, on the bus home. That's what Grandma said. *Have both.*

She doesn't know yet what the two things are.

---

By April the grown-ups are going mad.

Ike knows because he can hear them. The flat's not big, and the wall between his room and the kitchen is only board. Ike's been listening through it all his life. It's how he knew Auntie Joy was getting married to her registrar before anybody told him. It's how he knew Daddy got the office with his name on the door before Daddy told anybody. So on the Friday nights when Pearl's at the Pegasus, he lies in bed with his ear to the wall and listens to the grown-ups go mad.

"He's *twenty-one*," says Daddy. "He's got no job. He's got a room in Half Way Tree and a card from a man in Miami. He's —"

"I know what he is, Lloyd."

"He's my *father*." Daddy's voice goes up. It doesn't usually. "He's my father all over again. I sat at the Pegasus on Friday at the back and watched him, Marcia. I watched him play for her. It's the same. It's *exactly* the same. The way he bends over the keys. The way he looks at her when she comes in. I was nine years old at the back of the Myrtle Bank watching him do it to every girl singer he ever had. I *know* it."

"And what are you going to do? Forbid her?" Mummy's voice doesn't go up. It goes down. That's worse. "She's eighteen, Lloyd. She's got a place at the University, at Mona, in October and a voice like your mother's and a head like your mother's. If you forbid her she'll marry him on Monday out of spite."

"She wouldn't."

"*I* would have. At eighteen. If my father had forbidden me you." Mummy's quiet. "He did, very nearly. Do you remember? He said you were a bank clerk with a father who owed half of Kingston. And I went and stood on your mother's doorstep at the Bay, and said I'd marry you whether he liked it or not."

Ike didn't know that. He lies with his ear to the wall and thinks about Mummy on Grandma's doorstep at eighteen. He can't make the picture work.

"That's different."

"It's not different. It's exactly the same." Mummy sighs. "Your mother wrote. Did you see? And Joy. Three pages, capital letters. And your mother's rung twice from the harbour office, and she doesn't ring, Lloyd. She's frightened. She's more frightened than you are."

"And Reggie?"

There's a pause.

"Reggie hasn't said anything," says Mummy.

"*Nothing*?"

"Your mother says he's writing Pearl every week the same as always, about the tide and the calf's calf and the new cistern. Not a word about the boy. Your mother says she asked him why, and he said it wasn't his to say." Mummy's quiet for a moment. "She says he sits up at night. She says she's found him twice on the verandah at two in the morning with his pad, and he puts it away when she comes."

---

Ike lies in the dark.

He's been thinking for three months. He's been thinking since January, in the church hall, when Julian turned round on the bench and said *where have you been all my life*, and Ike went cold and didn't know why.

He knows why now. He worked it out last week, in the middle of the Clementi, with his hands going. He just stopped and sat there, and Mrs Beckford said *Isaac?*, and he couldn't say.

It was Grandpa Des. Christmas 1984, when Ike was six, on this bench, in this hall. Ike did the going-off bit in the second verse of *O Little Town*, and Grandpa Des put his hand on the back of Ike's neck and turned round on the bench, and said it. *Where have you been all my life, Ike?* Exactly like that. Exactly that voice. And Ike had felt like the most important boy in Jamaica.

And then Grandpa Des went to Half Way Tree at eight o'clock. And he's not been back since.

Ike's not stupid. He's Mummy's son, Grandma says, and Mummy's never been stupid in her life. He knows the thing about Julian that Daddy's frightened of, and Grandma, and Auntie Joy in capital letters. He knows it better than any of them, because he's the only one who can hear it.

Julian's real.

That's the thing. That's what the grown-ups don't understand, because they can't play. When Julian plays for Pearl, he's not pretending. He's *with* her. Ike can hear it in every bar. It's not the voice, the thing Mummy calls *the voice*, the one Grandpa Des does at people and Julian does at the Ministry wives. When Julian's at the piano and Pearl's singing, he's true. He's truer than he is anywhere else. Ike would bet his whole Clementi on it.

And that's what makes it so bad.

Because he's not true anywhere else. Ike's watched him. He's watched him be forty minutes late and tell a story instead of saying sorry. He's watched him ask Pearl what she's going to study at Mona and then talk about Miami. He's watched him call Ike *little man*, and *Isaac*, and once, in March, *Ike's brother*. He meant *Pearl's brother*, and didn't notice. He's watched him borrow five dollars from Pearl for the bus, and then another five, and then ten.

He's watched Pearl write it down.

Ike's seen the blue book. He's not supposed to. But he's eleven, and she leaves it on the kitchen table sometimes when she's doing her maths. And there's a page with two columns on it, DOWN and UP, and they don't match.

He's Grandma's grandson too. He knows what you do when the columns don't match.

---

The thing he minds most, though, is the church hall.

Pearl hasn't sung with him since January. Not once. On Saturdays after Mrs Beckford, when they used to do *Stardust* and *Abide With Me* and the going-off bits, she's at the Pegasus practising with Julian. Or she's in the church hall practising with Julian, while Ike does his scales and Julian says *nice* at the hard bits. And then Julian takes over the bench.

On the Saturday after the grown-ups go mad, Ike says it.

"You don't sing with me any more."

Pearl's at the kitchen table with the blue book and her maths. She looks up.

"What?"

"You don't. Not since January. You only sing with *him*." Ike can feel his face going hot. He's eleven, and he hates it, and he can't stop. "He's better than me. I know he's better than me. You don't have to *tell* me."

"Ike, that's not —"

"We were better *together*." It comes out of him louder than he means. "You wrote it. In the book. In 1984. *We are better together, do not tell him I said so.* I *saw*." He's crying now, which is the worst thing, at eleven, in front of your sister. "And now you've got *him*, and he's got a card from Miami, and he calls me *Ike's brother*, and you don't even *notice* —"

He turns round and goes to his room and shuts the door and lies face down on the bed.

He hears her come after him. He hears her stand outside the door for a long time. Then he hears her sit down on the floor in the passage with her back against his door. He knows she's doing it because the door goes *click* against the frame.

"I noticed," says Pearl, through the door. Very quietly. "About *Ike's brother*. I wrote it down."

Ike doesn't say anything.

"And you're right," says Pearl. "About the church hall. I'm sorry. I'm really sorry, Ike." She's quiet a while. "He's not better than you. He's older. That's all. You'll be better than him by the time you're twelve. He said so himself."

"He *says* things."

"Yes," says Pearl. "He does."

There's a long silence through the door.

"Ike," says Pearl. "Do you think he's real? When he plays. With me. You'd know. You're the only one who'd know."

Ike lies on his bed with his face in the pillow and thinks about lying.

"Yes," he says. "When he plays. He's real."

"*Only* when he plays?"

Ike doesn't answer that. He doesn't have to. He hears her not ask again.

After a while she says, "Saturday. After Mrs Beckford. *Stardust*. You and me. I'll tell him I'm busy."

"You won't."

"I will," says Pearl. "Go on. Open the door. I want to see if your face is as blotchy as mine."

It is.

It's the third draft. He's on the verandah at Harbour House at two in the morning with the pad on his knee and the lamp turned low, and he's on the third draft.

He's seventy. He's allowed one small rum on a Saturday and no ladders and no stairs faster than Marguerite can count. He's been careful of himself for fifteen years, for Win, and then for the children. And he's sitting up at two in the morning, which Marguerite would have something to say about, writing a letter he isn't going to send.

*My dear Pearl,*

*I have been asked by nobody, and I know it isn't mine to say. I am going to say it anyway, once, and then never again.*

He stops. He crosses out *never again*, because he's seventy and has learnt not to promise *never*. He writes *not again*.

*I know that young man. I don't mean I have met him; I haven't. I mean I know him. I have been him. I have sat at a table in front of a Board, and in front of an inspector, and at a great many dinners. I have leaned back and turned it on, the thing your mother calls the voice, and been charming at people so they would not look at what was behind it. And I will tell you the thing about that voice which nobody tells you: it is not a lie. That is what makes it so dangerous. When it comes on, it means it. It means it entirely. It is only that it means it for three minutes at a time, and then it wants something, or it has somewhere to be.*

*Your grandfather is the same. I say that with great respect. He is the best accompanist in Jamaica and on the stand he means every note. Your grandmother married him for the three minutes. It cost her twenty years.*

*I don't want it to cost you anything.*

He stops again.

He sits with the pen over the page. Across the channel the cay's a long dark shape with the long-room lamp at the near end. At the far end, where his hut is, where Baptiste's hut is now, there's a light. It's small and yellow. It's Baptiste up late with the calf's calf, who's off her feed.

He looks at it for a long time.

He thinks about a corridor in 1946, and a man who told him in front of four people that his curve was wrong. He thinks about twenty-five years. He thinks about a lamp.

He thinks about what he wasn't told. Twenty-five years of Alec measuring things with his thumb and never once saying what they cost him. Then a photograph with a figure on the back, after, when it was too late to be any use to either of them. Reggie would have given a great deal to be told. Even if he'd not listened. Even if he'd read it leaning back, in the voice, and gone on exactly as before. He'd have *known*. He'd not have had to find it out from the back of a photograph.

So he'd tell her. If she didn't know, he'd tell her tonight, all of it, and post it at six.

But she does know. That's what stops his pen. He's read her letters every week since January, and he can hear the columns in them, the way you can hear a tide under a jetty. She's done DOWN and she's done UP. She knows every single thing in these three drafts, except one. And that one isn't his.

"Reggie."

It's Win in the verandah door, in her dressing gown, with her hair down. She's looking at the pad.

He doesn't put it away. It's the third time she's found him. There's no point.

"You're writing to Pearl," she says.

"I'm writing a letter to Pearl."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," says Reggie. "It isn't."

She comes and sits down in her chair. She doesn't ask to see it. She never does. She sits with her hands in her lap and looks across the channel at the light in Baptiste's hut.

"What does it say?"

"That I know him. That I've been him. That the voice isn't a lie, it's just short." He looks at the page. "That Des is the same, and you married him for three minutes on a stand and it cost you twenty years. And that I don't want it to cost her anything."

Win's quiet.

"All true," she says.

"All true."

"Then why don't you send it?"

He turns the pen over in his fingers.

"If she didn't know, I'd send it tonight," he says. "I'd have given anything to be told, in 1946. Even if I'd not listened." He's quiet a moment. "But she's got the columns already, Win. I've seen them. She writes to me every week and I can hear her not saying it. She's done DOWN and she's done UP and they don't match, and she's sitting with it. She's *sitting* with it. She hasn't found it yet because she doesn't want to find it yet, and she's allowed not to want to." He puts the pen down. "If I write and tell her the answer, it's mine, not hers. And she'll either not act on it, because it's not hers. Or she'll act on it and never trust it, and spend her life wondering if she'd have found a different one."

"Everything in this," he says, and puts his hand flat on the pad, "she knows. Except one thing. And that's not mine to tell her. It's yours."

Win goes still.

"She knows you stopped singing," says Reggie. "She knows about the books, and the baby. She knows you couldn't sing with anybody else and couldn't sing with him. She wrote me that, in 1984, and underlined the part that didn't come out." He looks at her. "She doesn't know about the three minutes. On the stand. That you'd come off it believing him. That it was the first cheque you didn't sign." He's quiet. "That's the thing she hasn't got, Win. What it cost. From the one who paid it. I'll not tell her what to do. But she ought to know what it cost."

Win doesn't say anything for a long time. She looks across the channel.

"I told her it wasn't any use to her," she says at last. "When she was thirteen."

"It wasn't, when she was thirteen."

Win shuts her eyes.

"August," she says. "When she comes. I'll tell her."

"And if she still doesn't get there?"

He doesn't answer for a long time.

"Then she'll go to Miami," he says at last. "And sing. And it'll cost her. And she'll pay it, and she'll come home, and we'll still be here." He looks at the light across the channel. "At supper. That's the only thing I can do for her that's any use. Be here at supper whatever she does."

Win reaches across and takes his hand. She holds it on the arm of the chair.

"It's costing you," she says. "Not sending it."

"Yes."

"How much?"

"About as much," says Reggie, "as anything's cost me since the lamp."

She doesn't let go of his hand. They sit there together on the verandah at two in the morning, looking at the small light in Baptiste's hut, until it goes out.

Then he tears the three drafts off the pad and folds them in four. In the morning he puts them in the drawer in the office, the one with Win's unsigned cheque in it and the decree absolute and the day book from 1959. And on Monday he writes to Pearl about the new cistern and the calf's calf, who's eating again. It runs to six pages, with arrows, and not one word about the boy.

---

She brings him to the Bay in August. She wants to. She tells herself it's because Julian's never been out of Kingston except to Ocho Rios for a hotel job. She tells herself it's because she wants him to see the cay, and the calf's calf, and the harbour steps where Reggie taught her to count. It's partly true.

It's also because she wants Grandma to hear him. And Reggie to see him. She wants to watch their faces, and go up as well as down.

He comes on the Friday boat, a day late, because of a gig. He comes up the harbour steps in his white shirt with his jacket over his shoulder and a bunch of flowers for Grandma. He bought them in the market at Port Maria, he says, when the boat stopped, and they're a bit sick, like him. Grandma laughs. Pearl hears her laugh, and watches her face while she does it, and sees something go behind it, quick, like a shutter.

He's lovely all weekend. He's lovely to Grandma, and to the Kilburns, and to the two Ministry men in rooms three and five. He plays the bar piano on the Friday night, the one Ike and Reggie took the front off. He plays it for an hour. The whole Bay comes in off the street to listen, the way it did, Grandma told her once, in 1973, when Grandpa Des played it.

Grandma stays in the kitchen.

Pearl goes to find her at ten. She's at the sink with her hands flat either side of it, looking out at the harbour. She doesn't turn round.

"He's very good," says Grandma.

"I know."

"He's *very* good, Pearl." Grandma's voice is odd. "He plays like —" She stops.

"I know," says Pearl. "Grandma, I know."

Grandma turns round then. She looks at Pearl for a long time, in the kitchen, with the piano going through the wall.

"Do you?" she says.

"I've done the columns."

Something happens to Grandma's face. It's not relief, quite. It's the look she has at the office desk when Pearl's done a column right and Grandma's not going to say so yet, not till she's checked it.

"And?"

"They don't match," says Pearl. "I'm still finding it."

Grandma looks at her for a long time. Then she dries her hands on the tea towel, very slowly, finger by finger, and puts it over the rail of the stove, square.

"Sit down," she says. "I'm going to tell you the part I didn't. When you were thirteen. The bit in the middle."

Pearl sits down at the kitchen table.

Grandma doesn't. She stands with her back against the sink, with the piano going through the wall. She tells it quite plainly, the way she'd read out a bill. The stand at the Myrtle Bank, and the three minutes. How Grandpa Des was *with* her, every bar, and she'd come off with her heart going and think *that's who he really is*. How by one o'clock he'd be in the back room with the cards. How the next Saturday he'd play *Stardust* for her and she'd believe him all over again, and forgive him the whole week, because for three minutes he'd been true. How she couldn't stop believing him while she was singing, so she stopped singing. How it was the first cheque she didn't sign. How he'd sat up all night after Daddy was born, rewriting *Stardust* lower for her voice, and she'd put it in a drawer, because she knew if she sang it she'd never leave him.

"Twenty years," says Grandma. "It cost me twenty years to pay off three minutes. I'm not telling you what to do, love. Reggie'd have my head, and he'd be right. I'm telling you what it cost. Because nobody told me. And I'd have liked to know."

Pearl sits at the kitchen table with her hands flat on it.

Through the wall, Julian's playing *Stardust*. It's in Grandpa Des's key. The one that's hers.

"Thank you," she says, when she can.

Grandma nods. She picks the tea towel up off the rail. And on her way past, going out of the kitchen, Pearl feels her hand touch her shoulder, once, very briefly.

---

Reggie doesn't say anything at all.

That's the thing Pearl can't get over, all weekend. Everybody else says something. Uncle Pascual, who's come across from the cay especially and is seventy-three and louder than ever, shakes Julian's hand and says *so you're the one* in a voice you could hear on the cay. Auntie Marguerite takes Julian's pulse, *just for interest*. Mr Wilmot, who's in room three, looks at Julian for a long moment over his newspaper and then looks at Pearl with an expression she can't read. Old Mrs Kilburn says, loud enough for the whole bar, *Lord, Win, it's Des all over again*, and Grandma says *Hush, Mother Kilburn*.

Reggie's polite. He shakes Julian's hand. He asks him where he trained, and listens to the answer, all of it, with his hands folded, the way he listens. He asks him what he thinks of the bar piano. He listens to that too, a long answer about the action and the flat top, and nods. He asks about the chord in the second bar of *Stardust*, and Julian lights up, and talks about it for ten minutes with his hands going. Reggie writes it down in the little notebook he keeps in his pocket. And then Reggie asks a fourth question, the way anybody would who'd been doing sums about it for months. He asks how the two of them mean to manage in October. Pearl starts at Mona on the second, with lectures all week and her reading at night. Will they keep the Fridays at the Pegasus going, or move them to Saturdays?

And Julian does the thing.

Pearl sees it happen. She's across the bar. She sees Julian lean back in the chair and raise his eyebrows and let the warmth come into his voice. He starts telling Reggie a story about a man in Miami with a record label, in a voice like honey. It's the voice he uses on the Ministry wives.

And Reggie doesn't do anything.

Pearl's heard about it. The duel, on this verandah, in 1973, with Grandpa Des. Grandma told her, laughing, one summer, both of them leaning back in their chairs selling each other a car. She'd expected Reggie to do it. She'd half wanted to see it.

He doesn't. He just sits there with his hands folded and listens to the whole of the story, and waits. When it's done, he says, quite pleasantly, "Yes. But in October?"

Julian stops.

For a second, just a second, there's nothing on his face at all.

Then he laughs. "Oh, we'll work it out. Mona's not going anywhere, is it? And if Miami comes off —" He looks across the bar at Pearl, and winks. "She'll tell you. She's cleverer than me."

He hasn't thought about it. Pearl can see that from across the bar, all at once, like a column coming out. It's not that he's decided anything. It's that October, and Mona, and her reading at night haven't come into it at all. Reggie doesn't press him. He just nods, and doesn't write anything down. And Julian looks at him, all the rest of the evening, from across the bar, with an expression Pearl has never seen on him before. It's like somebody who's been played a note he can't place.

---

She asks him on the Sunday night. On the verandah, at ten, with Grandma doing the day book and Julian gone to the Kilburns' to play for the grandchildren.

"You haven't said anything."

"No." Reggie's in his chair with his feet on the stool. He's got his glasses pushed up. "I haven't."

"Everybody else has. Mummy. Daddy. Auntie Joy, in capitals. Mrs Kilburn in front of the whole *bar*. Grandma, without saying anything, which is worse." Pearl pulls her knees up. "You haven't said one word. You write to me every week and it's all *cisterns*."

"It's a very good cistern."

"*Reggie*."

He looks at her. He doesn't smile.

"What do you want me to say?"

"I want you to tell me what to do," says Pearl, and hears her own voice crack. She hadn't known she was going to say it. "Everybody else is *telling* me not to. And you're the only one I'd — you're the only one I'd listen to. And you won't."

He's quiet for a long time. Across the channel there's the lamp on the long-room verandah, and a light at the far end of the row, in Baptiste's hut.

"No," he says at last. "I won't. I'm sorry, Pearl. I'd like to. You've no idea how much." He looks at his hands. "But it's yours. It's your column. If I tell you the answer, it's my answer, not yours. You'd not do it, because it isn't yours. Or you'd do it and never trust it, and wonder all your life if you'd have found a different one."

"What if I don't get there?"

"Then you'll not. And I'll be here." He says it quite simply. "At supper. Whatever you do. That's the only thing I've got that's any use to you. I'll be here."

Pearl sits with her knees pulled up and her face hot.

"That's *not* fair."

"No," says Reggie. "It isn't."

She's quiet for a while.

"Will you tell me what you *see*?" she says. "Not what to do. What you *see*. You're always telling me to look."

He considers that. She watches him consider it, the way he does, where you can see. She watches him decide it's allowed.

"I see a young man who plays for you better than anybody alive," says Reggie. "Better than your grandfather, perhaps. I sat in that bar on Friday and I can't carry a tune, and even I could hear it. He's with you. It's real. I'd not tell you it wasn't, because it is."

Pearl can't say anything.

"And I see a young man," says Reggie, "who was a day late for your grandmother's dinner and didn't notice she'd kept it hot for him. Who's called your brother three different names since January, according to your brother, who writes to me about it at some length. Who's borrowed money from you four times, according to you, though you've not said so in so many words, and wouldn't. And who, when I asked him how you'd manage once you start at Mona, hadn't thought about it. Not for a minute. So he did a thing with his voice that I've done myself for twenty-five years, in front of Boards, when I didn't know the answer. I know exactly what it's for." He looks at her. "That's what I see. Both. They're both true. I'd not tell you only one."

Pearl sits on the verandah in the dark.

"You said you wouldn't tell me what to do."

"I haven't," says Reggie. "I've told you what I see. You asked. What you do with it is yours."

She puts her face down on her knees.

After a while she feels his hand on the top of her head. It's dry and cool. It stays there a long time, and then it goes, and she hears him get up, slowly, with his hand on the arm of the chair. She hears him go in, and hears Grandma say something to him low in the passage, and him say something back.

She stays on the verandah till the light in Baptiste's hut goes out.

---

Ike's recital is the sixteenth of September, a Saturday, in the church hall, at six.

It's his first proper one. Mrs Beckford's put him on last, which means best, and he's doing the Clementi, all three movements, and then something of his own. It's not written down. It's the going-off bits, all put together, that he's been doing since he was four. Mrs Beckford says it's allowed, as long as he calls it *Improvisation* in the programme and doesn't go on longer than four minutes.

He's had it ready since July. He's played it for Reggie over the telephone at the harbour office. Reggie listened to the whole thing down a crackling line from the Bay and said *again*, and then asked about the bit in the middle where it goes up.

Julian said he'd come. He said it in August, at the Bay, on the Sunday, in front of Pearl. *Wouldn't miss it, little man. Front row.* And Ike had decided to believe him, because Pearl was there, and because he wanted to.

The hall's full. Grandad Hinds is in the front row with Great-Aunt Merle. Mummy and Daddy are in the second. Pearl's beside them in the blue dress, with the seat next to her empty, with her handbag on it. Ike can see it from the side of the stage the whole time the little ones are doing their pieces. It stays empty.

At a quarter past six Pearl looks at her watch.

At half past six she looks at it again, and then she takes the handbag off the seat and puts it on her lap.

At a quarter to seven Mrs Beckford says *Isaac Carew*, and Ike goes out.

---

He plays the Clementi. He gets the left hand in the second bit. He gets all of it. He doesn't look round, because Mrs Beckford says you mustn't, and because if he looks round he'll look at the empty seat.

Then he plays the *Improvisation*.

He hasn't played it all the way through for anybody but Reggie on the telephone, and Pearl, once, in the kitchen, on the table, with his fingers. It's all the going-off bits. It's the one from *O Little Town* when he was six, and the one from *Abide With Me*. It's the one that Grandpa Des went with him on, at Christmas. It's the bit in *Stardust* where Pearl goes up and he goes up with her. And it's the bit he made up in August at Harbour House, on the flat-topped bar piano, when he'd heard Julian play it for an hour, and gone out, and come back when everybody was asleep, and tried to play it the way *he* would.

He plays it all. He goes off, and he comes back. He goes off further, and he comes back. And at the end, where it goes up, he goes all the way up to the top of the piano, to the flat notes. He plays them anyway, because they're flat, because that's this piano, because it's been played a long time and got tired at the top and that's not the same as broken.

He stops. He sits with his hands on the keys.

The hall's quiet. Then it isn't.

He looks up, then. You're allowed to, at the end. He looks for Pearl. She's on her feet. She's clapping with her hands up over her head, and her face is wet. Beside her Mummy's crying and Daddy's got his hand over his mouth. Grandad Hinds is on his feet with his big cello voice going *Amen, amen*.

The seat beside Pearl's still empty.

---

Julian comes at a quarter past nine.

They're outside the hall, under the streetlamp, waiting for Grandad Hinds to lock up. Ike's got his programme rolled up in his hand. He's still going hot and cold all over from the clapping, and Daddy's hand keeps finding the back of his neck and staying there. There's a car stops at the kerb, a friend's car, and Julian gets out of the back with a great armful of roses. He comes across the pavement in his white jacket with his face all lit up.

"*Pearl*." He's got his arms open. "Pearl, I'm so sorry, you'll never believe it. The man from Miami rang the Pegasus at five. At *five*, the tour's on, it's *on*. He's got us three weeks in Miami and two in New York and he wants you, Pearl, he wants the *voice*, he said so, and I've been on the telephone all —" He pushes the roses at her. "These are for you. For the real thing."

Pearl doesn't take them.

Ike watches her not take them. He watches Julian stand on the pavement under the streetlamp with his arms full of roses, and Pearl not reach out. And Julian's face does the thing. Just for a second. Nothing on it at all.

Then the lamp comes up.

"And the little man," says Julian, turning. "How did it go, little man? I bet you were brilliant. I *bet*."

Ike looks at him.

He's thinking about the empty seat. About the Clementi, and the left hand in the second bit. About the *Improvisation*, which had Julian's bit in it, the August bit, that Ike had stayed up to learn so Julian would hear it tonight and say *nice*. He's thinking about the flat notes at the top.

"You don't know," says Ike. "You weren't there."

Julian's face doesn't change. But he looks at Ike properly, for the first time since January. Ike thinks it might be the first time.

And Ike sees it. He's Mummy's son, and Grandma's grandson, and he's listened through the kitchen wall his whole life. And he sees what Pearl's been finding in her columns since February. He sees what Daddy saw at the back of the Myrtle Bank at nine, and what Grandma saw at the sink, and what Reggie saw from his chair in the bar, not doing the voice back.

Julian doesn't know what Ike played. He doesn't know it was Ike's night. He came for Pearl, to tell her about Miami. He brought roses for Pearl. And now he's standing on the pavement asking *how did it go*, bright, with his face turned towards Ike and already half turned back to Pearl. He's asking the way Grandpa Des asked Pearl *what do you like at school*. Asking so he can talk next.

He doesn't know Ike's name. Not really. He never has.

"Ike," says Pearl.

It's the way she says it. Quite quietly, beside him, under the streetlamp. Ike turns his head. She's not looking at Julian. She's looking at Ike.

"Go on in with Mummy and Daddy," she says. "I'll be up in a minute."

"Pearl —"

"*Go on*, Ike." She puts her hand on his shoulder, very briefly. "I've done the column. I've found it. I'll be up in a minute."

He goes.

He goes up the road with Daddy's hand on the back of his neck and Mummy holding his other hand. At the corner, he looks back. He can't help it.

Pearl's standing under the streetlamp with her arms folded. Julian's in front of her with the roses. He's talking, and his hands are moving, and his face is doing everything. Pearl's just standing there. She's listening to all of it, the way Reggie listens, with her whole face.

Then she shakes her head.

---

"The twentieth," Julian's saying. "Wednesday. The plane's Wednesday at ten. He's paying, Pearl, he's paying for everything, the hotel, the plane, the whole lot. Three weeks in Miami and two in New York. There's a man in New York who heard the tape I sent and he wants —" He takes a breath. "He wants *you*. The voice. I told him, I said she's the real thing, she's the best voice I've ever played for. And he said bring her."

"Mona starts on the second of October," says Pearl.

"Mona'll *wait*. Mona'll be there next year. Mona'll be there in ten years." He's got the roses under one arm now, like a parcel. He's holding out his other hand. "Pearl. Pearl, *listen* to me. This is the thing. This is what happens once. You don't get two of these. I've been waiting my whole life for somebody who could do what you do, and I found you in a *church*. You can't go and sit in a lecture hall doing *accounts* with a voice like that. It's —" He shakes his head. "It's a crime. It's a waste. It's a *criminal waste*."

Pearl stands under the streetlamp and looks at him.

*A criminal waste.* She's heard it before. In this hall, when she was nine, with her hands in Grandpa Des's hands. *She had the best voice on the north coast and she gave it up. Criminal. A criminal waste.*

She's been doing the columns since February. DOWN and UP. She's been sitting with them. She's been sitting with them because the DOWN column is so good. It's the best thing that's ever happened to her, and it's *real*. She knows it's real. Ike knows, and Ike's the only one who'd know. On Friday nights at the Pegasus, for three minutes at a time, she's more herself than she's ever been.

And the UP column. The forty minutes, and the hour and ten. The day late at the Bay. *Little man*, and *Isaac*, and *Ike's brother*. The five dollars and the five dollars and the ten. The empty seat.

She's been sitting with it. She's known since March that they don't match, and she's known since August, on the verandah, with Reggie's hand on her head, where they don't. She's just not wanted to put the tick.

She puts it now. Under the streetlamp, on Constant Spring Road, with the roses.

"No," says Pearl.

Julian stops.

"I'm not coming to Miami," says Pearl. "I'm going to Mona. On the second of October. To do accounts." She keeps her voice level. It isn't easy. "And I'm going to sing. In church, and with Ike on Saturdays, and anywhere I like. Maybe I'll sing at the Pegasus again, some Friday, if they'll have me. I'm going to have both. My grandmother told me to."

"*Pearl* —"

"But not with you, Julian."

He stands there with the roses under his arm.

"You're with me every bar," says Pearl. "Every single bar. You're the best I've ever sung with. You might be the best I'll ever sing with, in my whole life. I know that. I'm not going to pretend I don't." Her voice goes, and she lets it, and brings it back. "And you weren't here tonight. It was Ike's night. He played his *Improvisation*, and it had your bit in it, from August. He stayed up to learn it, so you'd hear it and say *nice*. And you weren't here. And you didn't know. You came with roses for *me*."

Julian doesn't say anything.

"You're with me on the stand," says Pearl. "You're not with me at supper. And I've got a family who's at supper, every night, all of them. Even the ones who are seventy and can't carry a tune. I'm not going to trade it for three minutes on a stand. Not even the best three minutes there are."

For a long moment Julian doesn't say anything at all.

His face has gone quite still. There's nothing on it. He's twenty-one and standing on a pavement in a white jacket with an armful of roses, and for a second he looks about fifteen.

"Nobody's ever said no to me," he says.

It isn't the voice. It's just his. It's lower than she's ever heard it, and not very steady.

"I know," says Pearl. "I'm sorry."

"You're the only one who ever heard me." He's looking at the roses. "Properly. When I play. You're the only one who ever *heard* what I was doing. Everybody else just claps." He looks up. "I'm only any good at the piano, Pearl. I'm not any good at anything else. I know that. I'm not stupid."

"I know you're not." She feels her eyes go, and doesn't stop them. "You're very, very good at the piano, Julian. You're the best I've ever heard. Go to Miami. Go to New York. Play for everybody. They'll hear you. Somebody will."

"Not like you."

"No," says Pearl. "Probably not."

He looks at her a long time. And then, very slowly, the lamp comes back up. She watches it come. She's almost glad to see it. It's the only way he's got of getting to the end of the street.

"You'll regret it," says Julian, and smiles. "You'll be sitting in some office in King Street in ten years doing somebody's books, and you'll hear *Stardust* on the radio, and you'll think, *that could have been me*."

"Maybe," says Pearl. "I'll check."

He laughs. It's a real one, short and surprised. Then he steps forward and puts the roses into her arms before she can stop him. He kisses her on the forehead, once, and turns round, and walks off down Constant Spring Road in his white jacket, under the streetlamps, towards Half Way Tree.

He's whistling. She knows the tune.

He doesn't look back.

She stands on the pavement with the roses until she can't hear him any more. Then she goes into the church hall, where Grandad Hinds is waiting with the key. She puts the roses in the big vase by the piano, the one for Sunday. Then she sits down on the bench, all by herself, with the hall dark except for the one light over the door.

She puts one finger on a key. A high one. It's flat.

She sits there for a long time.

When she gets home it's gone midnight. The flat's dark except for the front room, where there's a lamp on. There's a sound she hasn't heard at this hour since she was seven: the needle going like rain on a tin roof.

Mummy's at the machine.

She's got the cloth off it and a length of grey cotton under the foot. There's a pattern pinned to the back of the sofa. Pearl knows the pattern. It's the two skirts and the jacket for Mona, the ones they chose together in August at the haberdasher's on Constant Spring Road. Mummy said she'd do them in September, when she had a minute.

She doesn't look round when Pearl comes in. She finishes the seam. She takes her foot off the treadle and cuts the thread.

"Well?" says Mummy.

Pearl sits down on the floor beside the machine, the way she used to when she was little and couldn't sleep, with her back against Mummy's legs.

"I said no."

Mummy's hand comes down on her hair. It stays there.

"Good girl," says Mummy. And then, after a while, much more quietly: "I'm sorry, darling. I'm so sorry."

"Both?" says Pearl, into her knee.

"Both," says Mummy. "It's allowed to be both."

They stay like that for a long time. Then Mummy takes her hand off Pearl's hair and puts her foot back on the treadle, and the needle starts again.

"I'll have the grey done by Tuesday," she says, over it. "The jacket by Friday. And when you want to sing at the Pegasus again, some Friday, you tell me. I'll let the blue out."

Pearl sits on the floor with her back against her mother's legs and listens to the needle, and doesn't go to bed till it stops.

---

On the Saturday after, when Mrs Beckford's gone, Pearl comes into the church hall.

Ike's at the piano doing nothing much. He's been doing nothing much all week. Julian's plane went on Wednesday at ten; Mummy heard it from a woman at the haberdasher's whose sister cleans at the Pegasus. Pearl's been very quiet all week. She's been doing her Mona forms at the kitchen table, and going to bed early, and at night Ike's heard her through the wall, not crying exactly, but not asleep either.

She comes down the hall and sits on the bench beside him. She sits the way she did when he was six and she said *do it again, the way you want*. She's got the blue book under her arm, and a letter, a thin one.

"Reggie wrote," she says.

"What did he say?"

She gives it to him. It's one page. Ike's never known Reggie write one page. He writes Ike four pages about the generator.

*My dear Pearl,*

*Thank you for telling me. I know it cost you. I know what it was worth, too, and I'd not pretend otherwise; he's the real thing at the piano, and you'd every right to want it.*

*We'll be at supper. Your grandmother's doing the fish.*

*Your loving*

*Reggie.*

*P.S. Your brother rang me from the harbour office on Sunday, collect, which he is not supposed to do, to tell me that the bit in the middle of his Improvisation went all the way up to the flat notes at the top and he played them anyway. I told him that was exactly right. It's been played a long time and got tired at the top. That's not the same as broken. Tell him I said so again.*

Ike reads it twice. He goes hot all over.

"You rang him collect?" says Pearl.

"Daddy said I could."

"Daddy did *not*."

"Daddy said I could *ask* Daddy," says Ike. "And then he wasn't there."

Pearl laughs. It's the first time he's heard her laugh all week. It's wet, and it goes on longer than the joke, and at the end of it she puts her head down on his shoulder, right there on the bench. He sits very still, because he's eleven now and doesn't know what you do. And then he does know. He puts his arm round her, awkwardly, the way Reggie does, and leaves it there.

"Are you sad?" he says, after a while.

"Yes."

"About Julian?"

"About the piano," says Pearl. "Not about him. About the three minutes." She's quiet. "I'll not get that again, Ike. Not like that. Not from anybody."

Ike thinks about this. He thinks about it for a long time, with his arm round his sister on the bench. He thinks about Julian bending over the keys with his whole face turned towards her. He thinks about Grandpa Des at Christmas, with his hand on the back of Ike's neck. He thinks about the bit in *Stardust* where she goes up and he goes up with her, the way Julian did. Only not quite the way Julian did. His own way.

"You might," he says.

She lifts her head.

"Not from him," says Ike. "Not from Grandpa Des." He's gone red to his ears and he knows it. "But I'll be twelve in March. Julian said I'd be better than him by the time I'm twelve. He *said*." He puts his hands on the keys. "He says things. But I think that one was true."

Pearl looks at him for a long time.

Then she opens the blue book. She turns to the page he's seen, on the kitchen table, when he wasn't supposed to. It's the one with the two columns, DOWN and UP, from February, that don't match. Under them, in her neat small hand, there's a line ruled right across the page. And under the line it says:

*Reconciled. 16.ix.89. The music is true. The rest isn't. Both columns correct. Declined.*

And a tick, tiny, like a bird's foot.

She turns further back, all the way back, to the page with the big careful heading from when she was nine. *Things That Come Back The Same Twice.* Ike knows the list. He's seen it too. There's the tide, and the column, and the calf, and the back wall, and Reggie, and Grandpa Des only in the music. There's Daddy, and Ike, and Grandma, and *Me*.

She takes her pencil and writes at the bottom, and turns the book round so he can see it.

*Julian. Only in the music. That's all right.*

And then, under that, a line that makes Ike look up at her:

*Mummy. Daddy, properly this time, not for the standing order. Grandad Hinds. Every single time. They were never on the list because they were never not there. I should have put them first.*

Ike reads it. He doesn't say anything. He doesn't have to. He thinks about the sound of Mummy's machine at midnight last Saturday, through the wall, going on and on, and nobody asking anything. He thinks about Daddy's hand on the back of his neck all the way up the road, and Grandad Hinds waiting in the hall with the key, as long as it took. He thinks it's the truest thing in the book.

"*Stardust*," says Pearl, and shuts the book, and puts it on top of the piano. "In your key. Go on. Go off wherever you like. I'll come in."

He plays the start of it. Not Grandpa Des's way. Not Julian's. His own way, with the chord in the second bar that he worked out in August on the flat-topped bar piano, after everybody had gone to bed.

She comes in on the second line.

He's not as good as Julian. Not yet. He knows it, and she knows it, and it doesn't matter. He's with her. He's with her every bar, and when she goes up he's there, and when he goes off she goes with him. Nobody's following. It isn't a parade.

At the end he plays two bars after her, soft, and stops.

The hall's quiet. Ike can hear the traffic on Constant Spring Road, and a dog, and Grandad Hinds in the vestry humming to himself.

"Well," says Pearl, very quietly. "We'll do."

She sits beside him on the bench with her shoulder against his. After a while she reaches up and plays one note at the very top of the piano, the flat one, and leaves her finger on it till it's gone.

"Again," says Ike.

"Again," says Pearl.
