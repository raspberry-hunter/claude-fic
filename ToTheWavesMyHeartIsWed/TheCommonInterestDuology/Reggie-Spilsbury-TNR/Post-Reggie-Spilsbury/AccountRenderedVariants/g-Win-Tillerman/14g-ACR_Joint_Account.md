# Joint Account

At seven in the morning Pascual lets himself into the hut at the end of the row without knocking. Reggie's standing in the middle of it in his shirt and trousers and socks, holding a tie in each hand. He's looking at them as though one of them had given him the wrong figures.

"The grey," says Pascual.

"You said the blue on Thursday."

"On Thursday I was lying. Today I'm your best man." He takes the blue out of Reggie's hand and throws it on the bed. "The grey. Win's in blue. You'll not match her like a pair of salt cellars."

Reggie looks at the grey tie. He puts it round his neck. He gets the knot wrong, and takes it out, and gets it wrong again. On the third go his hands aren't doing what he tells them, and Pascual watches them not do it. He waits until Reggie's hands drop to his sides, and then he comes and does it himself. He stands close in front of him, the way Reggie stood in front of him in the vestry of the Anglican church in 1962 and did his, because Pascual's hands were shaking too badly.

He doesn't mention that. He's saving it.

"You slept?" he says, doing the knot.

"No."

"You ate?"

"Mrs Joseph brought something. I looked at it." Reggie's looking over Pascual's shoulder at the door, at the jetty, at the Bay across the water. "The tide's making. It'll be high at fourteen minutes past one. If we leave the jetty at half past eleven with the launch and Alleyne's boy's —"

"*Hombre*. I have the boat plan. You gave me the boat plan. You gave me four copies of the boat plan. Jean has two. Mrs Joseph has one pinned in the kitchen, next to the menu for the Director's lunch in 1965." Pascual pulls the knot up snug. "Item twenty-one."

Reggie looks at him.

"Four *copies*," says Pascual, very gently, and smooths the tie down against his shirt. "Of the boat plan. I'll be writing that down at the breakfast. In front of the whole Bay."

"You wouldn't."

"I have twenty-one items, Reggie." Pascual pats his own breast pocket, where the cards are. Six index cards, in his smallest hand, both sides. He's been at them since April. "Nineteen from *before*. Twenty-one now. The Tuesday boat. The foxtrot. *Your physician*. The callipers." He smiles up at him. "I've had fourteen years."

Reggie shuts his eyes.

Then he opens them and looks at Pascual, properly, for the first time this morning. His face goes through several things at once. It settles on something Pascual last saw on it in the vestry in 1962, looking at him.

"You'll be all right," says Reggie. "You and Marguerite. When I'm — over there. Most of the week."

Pascual stops with his hands on the tie.

"*Hombre*," he says. "You're getting married. You're not *emigrating*. It's a mile and a half. There's a boat. You've drawn it."

"I know. I only —" Reggie stops. "Twenty-two years, Pascual. I've been at the end of this row twenty-two years, and you've been at the other end of the path."

Pascual looks at him.

Then he takes his hands off the tie and puts them on Reggie's shoulders. He holds them there, the way he's done more times than either of them has counted.

"I'll be at the other end of the path," he says. "Tuesdays and Thursdays and whenever the calf's mother's off her feed. And Sundays on the hill. And on this step whenever you like, with a bottle. I'm not going anywhere, Reggie. Neither are you." He gives him a small shake. "You're only getting somewhere to come in to."

Reggie doesn't say anything. He can't, Pascual can see. He just nods.

"Good," says Pascual. "Now put your shoes on. You're in your socks, and Mrs Joseph's been waiting since six to see you in your suit, and she's going to cry, and I want to watch."

---

The telegram comes at ten, while Joy's doing her hair.

Win's sitting on the bed in her slip, with Joy standing behind her with the pins in her mouth, and Marguerite at the window with the blue dress over her arm. There's a knock, and it's the kitchen boy, in a new shirt. He's holding a yellow envelope out at arm's length as though it might go off.

"From the harbour office, Mrs Carew. Just come. It says *Miami*."

The room goes quiet.

Joy takes the pins out of her mouth. Marguerite turns round from the window. Win looks at the envelope in the boy's hand for a long moment, and then she holds out her hand for it, and he gives it to her and goes.

She opens it. It's one line, in the capitals, on the pasted strip.

*NO HARD FEELINGS STOP GOOD LUCK WINNIE STOP HE WILL NOT SING BUT YOU COULD STOP D*

She reads it three times.

"Mum?" says Joy. "What's it say? Is it Daddy? Is he —"

Win doesn't answer. She sits on her bed on her wedding morning with the telegram in her lap, and she finds she's laughing. Not much. Quietly. With her eyes full.

*He will not sing but you could.* Des, on the harbour wall in August, saying *she'll not sing for you*. And Reggie saying *I'll ask her anyway*. And Des, somewhere in Miami, in a bar, working out a thing to say that would cost him a dozen words at the harbour office and a sum he hasn't got. And he's said this. Of all the things he might have said.

"It's your father," she says to Joy. "He says good luck."

"*That's all?*"

"That's all that matters." She folds it in four. She holds it out to Marguerite. "Put it in my bag, love. With my handkerchief."

Marguerite takes it and does. She doesn't ask what it says. Marguerite never does.

Win sits a moment longer. Then she looks up at her daughter.

"Well," she says. "Are you doing my hair, or are you going to stand there with the pins?"

Joy puts the pins back in her mouth. Her eyes are wet too. She does her mother's hair.

---

Lloyd comes up at a quarter to twelve in the dark suit, with a white rose in the buttonhole that Pearl chose from the garden and insisted on. He stands in the doorway of his mother's room and looks at her in the blue. And Win watches her son, who's been wound so tight since Monday she's been afraid he'd snap, let out his breath. All of it, all the way down.

"You look beautiful, Mum," says Lloyd.

"Don't," says Win. "I'll go. And Joy's done my face."

"You look *beautiful*," says Lloyd again, more firmly, the way his grandmother would have said it. He holds out his arm.

She takes it.

On the stairs he says, not looking at her, "I'm not going to stop. Not yet."

She misses a step. He steadies her.

"Not yet," says Lloyd. "I can't. Not with him like he is. But I've cut it to twenty. From July. Marcia can stop the sewing." He keeps his eyes on the stairs. "And I know I can. Now. That's — I just wanted you to know I know. He told me. On the wall."

Win doesn't say anything. She can't. She holds her son's arm tight on the stairs, and they go down together, and out of the front door into the sun.

---

The chapel holds a hundred and eighteen. There are a hundred and sixty-three people in it, by his count, and thirty more outside the open doors in the sun. He counted them from the front, standing beside Pascual, because it was the only thing he could think of to do with his hands.

The cay came across on the flood exactly as planned, in three boats. Jean was in the bow of the workboat in a hat of such dimensions that Alleyne's boy had to steer round it. Mrs Joseph cried on the jetty before they'd even cast off, and Pascual watched her with enormous satisfaction. Hester Bevan came, upright and eighty-one, and sat in the second pew on the groom's side as though she'd been asked to adjudicate. Baptiste is behind her with the students, in a row, looking as if she'd like to take notes. And the whole of the rest of the groom's side is the cay. Every soul on it. He's got no family to speak of, and he looks at the groom's side of the chapel and finds that he was wrong about that.

The bride's side is the Bay. It's full to the back wall. Mrs Kilburn's in the front row in purple. The Kilburns, all of them. The Alleynes. The schoolmaster and his wife. Mr Wilmot from the Ministry, who wasn't asked because Win didn't like to, and who came anyway on the Friday boat with his colleague. They're sitting together halfway back in grey suits, very correctly, and Mr Wilmot nodded to Reggie on the way in, once, and Reggie nodded back.

Joy's at the front on the bride's side with Pearl on her knee. Pearl's in white with a sash, and she's been told three times that she has to sit still. She waves at him.

He waves back. He can't help it. Joy rolls her eyes at him.

"Twelve fifty-eight," says Pascual, beside him, very low, without moving his lips.

"I know what time it is."

"You've looked at your watch nine times."

"I know."

"*Item twenty-two*," says Pascual.

And then the man at the harmonium, who's the schoolmaster's brother and plays for the chapel on Sundays, stops playing whatever it was. There's a pause. Everybody in the chapel turns round at once, like a field of something in a wind.

He doesn't turn round. He finds he can't. He stands facing the minister, who's looking at him over his spectacles with an expression of the deepest benevolence. Beside him Pascual's face is doing all six things. The harmonium starts again, something slow, and he hears it begin behind him: the rustle, and the feet, and a hundred and sixty-three people breathing in.

"*Hombre*," says Pascual, very softly. "Turn round. You'll want to have seen it."

He turns round.

She's coming up the aisle on her son's arm, in the blue, with her hair up the way Joy did it and a white rose that matches Lloyd's. She's walking slowly, because the aisle's short and she wants it to take a while. That's what her face says. She's looking at him the whole way.

He's seen her in the blue before. He's seen her in her slip on her bed, and in her dressing gown at six in the morning with the day book. He's seen her soaked to the skin in the back passage in the dark, holding his glasses. He's seen her laughing so hard she had to sit down in her own kitchen. He's seen her crying in her office over a cheque. He's seen her every Monday for two years. He's watched her over the fish, and from the verandah step, and across Marguerite's table, and he's never once been able to stop looking.

He doesn't know what his face is doing. He knows it's doing all of it, and there's nothing in front of it, and a hundred and sixty-three people can see.

He finds he doesn't care in the least.

Lloyd brings her up to the front and stops. He looks at Reggie. There's a moment, very short, when Reggie sees him decide something. Then Lloyd takes his mother's hand off his arm and holds it out, and puts it in Reggie's.

"There you are," says Lloyd Carew, quietly, to him. Only to him. "Look after her."

"She looks after me," says Reggie. "I'll carry the buckets."

Lloyd's mouth does something that's nearly his mother's. He steps back.

And Reggie stands in the Methodist chapel at the Bay with Win's hand in his. It's warm and square and steady, with the burn across the back from the copper. And the minister clears his throat and begins.

---

He'd thought he'd remember it. Every word. He'd thought it'd be like a paper, or a set of figures, that you'd have by heart after.

He doesn't. He remembers pieces.

He remembers the minister saying *if any man can show just cause*, and a silence in which he distinctly hears Mrs Kilburn say *hmph*. He remembers Pearl saying *Auntie Joy, that's* my *Reggie* in a carrying whisper, and the whole of the front three rows shaking. He remembers his own voice saying *I will*. It's quite steady. It's only his, and he's surprised by it.

He remembers the ring. Pascual handing it over without fumbling, and looking at him as he does it, and Reggie knowing he's thinking of 1962. He remembers putting it on her hand, beside the other one with the stone, and her fingers closing on his for a second as he does it.

He remembers her voice saying *I will*. Low. Where it sits when she's tired, or when she means it. And he remembers thinking, very clearly, in the middle of it: *contralto*.

And he remembers the minister saying *you may*, and her face coming up to his, and the chapel going up like a wave over the harbour wall, a hundred and sixty-three people and thirty in the sun. And under all of it, right beside him, Pascual, who has given up on his face entirely and is crying like a fountain, and cheering through it.

---

The breakfast is at Harbour House, because where else. Win said she'd not have anybody else cater it. Reggie said she was not cooking her own wedding breakfast. In the end Mrs Joseph and the Harbour House kitchen did it between them, in a state of armed truce, with Marguerite adjudicating. There are tables down the length of the yard under the almond trees, and on the verandah, and in the bar, and out into the road. The generator shed has flowers on it. Somebody's put a garland on the generator.

Pascual sits at the top table between Joy and Marguerite with six index cards in his breast pocket. He's never been so happy in his life.

He's had fourteen years. He's got twenty-two items. He's got the Tuesday boat, and the foxtrot, and *your physician* in front of the Board. He's got the seating plan, *with tides*, and four copies of the boat plan. He's got the callipers. He's got *nine weeks and a day*, which Win told Marguerite, and which Marguerite told him in bed, weeping with laughter. He's got the egg-price notebook with the error bars. He's got the roof. He's got it all, both sides of six cards, in his smallest hand. And he's going to stand up in front of the Bay and the cay and the Board and Win's children and a man from the Ministry, and read out every single word.

He's been rehearsing on the step. Marguerite's heard it twice. She laughed so much the second time that she had to go and lie down.

Jean taps her glass. The yard goes quiet. Everybody looks at him.

Pascual stands up.

He takes the cards out of his pocket. He squares them on the tablecloth, the way Reggie squares things. He looks down the top table, and there's Reggie.

Reggie's sitting beside Win with his jacket off, because it's June, and his tie loosened, and his glasses pushed up. He's got Pearl on his knee, asleep, with her face in his shirt and her sash come undone. He's got one arm round Pearl and the other hand under the table, and Pascual knows without looking that it's holding Win's. He's looking up at Pascual. He's braced for it. He's perfectly braced. His face says, *go on, then, I've earned it*.

And under that there's nothing at all. It's only his face. The one Pascual has watched across a table for twenty-two years.

Pascual looks at it. He looks at it for rather a long time, with the whole yard waiting.

He thinks about the Tuesdays. Two years of Tuesdays, Reggie coming up the jetty off the Bay boat having slept, and eaten, and laughed at something in the night he couldn't explain. He thinks about the first one, in December 1972, when Reggie came up the path whistling. He'd not known the man could whistle.

He looks down at the six cards.

Then he picks up the top one, and reads it out, because he's owed that much.

"*Item twenty*," says Pascual. "*The seventeenth of April 1974. He has drawn a seating plan.*" He pauses. "*With tides.*"

The yard roars. Reggie puts his free hand over his eyes. Pearl wakes up, and looks around, and goes back to sleep.

Pascual waits for it to stop. Then he puts the card down on top of the others, face down, and puts his hand flat on them. He doesn't pick them up again.

"I had," he says, "twenty-one more of those."

He hears Marguerite beside him draw in her breath.

"I've been writing them since 1960. I've got the foxtrot. I've got the Tuesday boat. I've got the callipers, and you all know about the callipers, because I told Mrs Joseph." Laughter. "I was going to read every one. I've been looking forward to it for fourteen years. He deserves every one." He looks down the table. "But I've just looked at him. And I find I can't."

The yard's gone quiet.

"I've known this man twenty-two years," says Pascual. "Inside two days of my getting here he'd told me I was wrong, in front of the students. We've been doing it to each other twice a week ever since. So I think I'm entitled to tell you what I've seen." He looks at Win. "And I want to tell you about the last two years. Because I've never seen anything like them."

The yard's gone quiet.

"In November 1972," says Pascual, "there was a storm. Some of you were in it. The Board was at Harbour House, and the lights went, and the roof went in room four. He came back to the cay the next morning and talked to me about that roof for an hour. He *drew* it for me. The joists. With arrows." Laughter. "I thought he'd had a stroke. I went and told Marguerite. Marguerite said, *Pascual, I don't think it's the roof.*"

Marguerite, beside him, puts her hand over her eyes.

"And then he started going across on Mondays," says Pascual. "To see about the Board's account. Which Jean pays. In December. In one envelope." Jean, down the table, raises her glass to him very slightly. "And he came back on the Tuesdays and he'd *eaten*. He'd slept. I'd been trying to get him to sleep for twenty years. He came back one Tuesday and he'd laughed at something so hard in the night he'd hurt his chest, and he couldn't tell me what it was, because it was Win's, and it was funny, and he said I'd not understand it without having been there. He was right. I didn't. I was so glad I couldn't speak."

He stops. He hadn't meant to stop. It's his voice.

"I'll tell you what I've watched her do," he says, when he can. "I've watched her take a bucket off him on a stair and not say a word about it. I've watched her laugh at him. To his *face*, when he's being impossible, which nobody's ever managed. He doesn't know what to do when she does it. He just stands there and goes red. I've watched her make him wait nine weeks and a day, and I've watched her tell him when he's being a fool about her son. And I've watched him hear it, and go and do better." He looks at Win. "He's a better man than he was two years ago, Win. I didn't think that was possible. I've been telling him how to be a better man since 1952, and he's never once listened —"

"I *listened*," says Reggie, from the other end of the top table, with great indignation. Pearl, asleep on his knee, stirs and settles.

"You listened," Pascual concedes, over the laughter, "*eventually*. After I'd shouted. In two languages. Twice a week for twenty-two years." He looks down the table at him. "And I listened to you. I'll say that in front of everybody, since you're making a fuss. You made me a better man too, *hombre*. I'd not be standing here with Marguerite if you hadn't. We made each other better. It's the truth." He turns back to Win. "But it took us twenty-two years, Win, and a great deal of shouting, and at least one bucket thrown across the long room in 1958. You did it in two. Without raising your voice once. You just let him carry the buckets."

Win's got her hand over her mouth.

"The fishermen come in on Win's window now," says Pascual. "Kilburn told me. You put the light in her top window over the church roof and come straight in, and you clear the bar with a fathom to spare." He picks up his glass. "So does he. Every Monday. I've watched him. He comes in on it like a man who's found his mark."

The Kilburns are grinning at the tablecloth. Old Kilburn raises his glass.

"In 1962," says Pascual, "he stood up at my wedding and said he was handing me over with extremely bad grace. I'm not going to say that about him. I'm not handing him over at all, Win. I'll be at the other end of the path on Tuesdays whatever either of you say about it." Laughter. "But I'll say this. I'm giving you my friend with the greatest possible relief. He's been impossible about you for two years. He's added up your laundry wrong. He's talked about high water to your son. He's measured your *glove*." The yard's laughing again, through it. "He's yours. God help you. To Win and Reggie."

"*Win and Reggie*," says the yard, and the cay, and the Bay, and the Board, and a man from the Ministry halfway down the second table, very quietly, into his glass.

Pascual sits down.

Marguerite takes his hand under the tablecloth. She doesn't say anything. She holds it, hard.

He doesn't look at Reggie. He can't. He looks at his plate, and at the cards face down beside it, and waits for his face to come back.

After a while, something touches his shoulder.

It's Reggie. He's come round behind the top table, with Pearl still asleep on his shoulder. He's standing behind Pascual's chair, and he's put his free hand on Pascual's shoulder, and he's holding on.

He doesn't say anything. Neither of them does. After a moment Reggie bends down, awkwardly, because of Pearl. He picks up the six index cards from the tablecloth, all of them, face down, and puts them in his own breast pocket, on the left.

"I'll keep those," he says, very low, in Pascual's ear. "If you don't mind."

"*Hombre*," says Pascual, to his plate. "They're about *you*."

"I know," says Reggie. "That's why."

---

It's past eleven before the yard's empty.

The cay went back at nine on the last of the light, in three boats. Jean was in the bow with her hat in her lap, and Hester was asleep against Baptiste's shoulder. Mrs Joseph was still crying, very contentedly. The Kilburns went at ten, singing. Mr Wilmot and his colleague went up to rooms three and five at half past, having shaken Reggie's hand and kissed Win's, and said nothing whatever, and left a parcel on the hall table that turns out to be a silver toast rack with *W. & R.* on it. Lloyd and Marcia have taken Pearl up to room four, asleep, with her sash still on and one of Pascual's index cards clutched in her fist. Nobody can get it off her.

Joy has moved herself to room nine. At the back. Of her own accord, with her own case, at eight o'clock, announcing it to the whole top table.

Win stands in the yard among the almond trees and the wreck of her wedding breakfast. There's a garland on the generator. It's the best night of her life, and she's so tired she could lie down in the road.

She goes in, and through the kitchen, and up the back stairs.

He's on the half-landing. He's sitting on the stair by the window with his jacket over his knees and his tie in his pocket, and she's so surprised she stops.

"What are you doing on the *stairs*?"

"Waiting," says Reggie. "I'm told I'm good at it." He looks up at her. "And I've taken it. Before. I'm not taking any chances tonight, Win, Joy's in room nine and she's got ears like a bat."

She sits down on the stair beside him. It's narrow. Her hip's against his, and her shoulder, in the blue.

"You gave it to her," she says. "Joy. The ECGs."

"She asked. She says the T waves in III are nothing. She says the Kingston man's an idiot." He sounds enormously pleased. "She's going to write to Marguerite about my propranolol. They've a *plan*, Win. I'm frightened to death."

Win laughs. She puts her head on his shoulder.

They sit there on the half-landing for a while, in the dark, not saying anything. The window's open. The harbour's black. Across the channel the cay is a long low shape with the long-room lamp at the near end, and nothing at the far one.

"Des sent a telegram," she says.

She feels him go still.

"This morning. While Joy was doing my hair." She takes it out of her bag and puts it in his hand. He can't read it in the dark. She tells him what it says.

He's quiet for a long time.

"*You could*," he says at last.

"I know."

"He was right about that. On the wall." Reggie turns the yellow paper over in his fingers. "He was right about more than I gave him credit for."

"He usually was," says Win. "It's what made it so hard."

He gives her the telegram back. She puts it in her bag, with her handkerchief, and the white rose, which has gone soft.

Then, very quietly, sitting on her own stairs on her wedding night with her head on her husband's shoulder, she sings.

Not much. Not the whole of it. Just the first verse of the thing, very low, the way she used to do it at the Myrtle Bank on a Saturday, before Lloyd. Contralto. She's not sung it, not all the way through, since 1948. She'd not know she remembered it, if you asked.

She feels him not breathe, the whole time.

When she stops, he doesn't say anything. He can't, she thinks. He's got his face turned into her hair.

"You're not to tell anybody," she says.

"No."

"Not Pascual. *Especially* not Pascual."

"No," says Reggie, into her hair. He's laughing. And he's not laughing. "Oh, Win. Oh, my *love*."

---

In her room she lights the lamp, low, because he asks her to, and she lets him take her dress off.

He does the buttons one at a time. There are twelve again; Joy counted, and told him at the breakfast, to see his face. He does them slowly, the way he did the first time. At each one he puts his mouth to the place on her back it uncovers. She stands with her hand on the bedpost, and she's so tired, and she wants him so much, that she laughs at the fourth one and cries a little at the seventh. She doesn't know which it is by the twelfth.

"Mrs Tar," he says against her spine.

"Don't you dare."

"Mrs *Tar*."

"I'm keeping *Carew* for the house. It's on the sign." She turns round in his arms, with the dress falling. "You can have Mrs Tar in here. Nowhere else."

"That's all I want," says Reggie. "In here."

It's slow. They're both too tired for anything else, and neither of them minds. She lies on Iris's counterpane with the lamp low and her husband over her, and they take a very long time. She holds his face in both hands and watches it the whole way, because she can, because it's hers. He says her name. She says his. Joy's in room nine, at the back, and they're quiet. Not because they have to be. Because they want to keep it, just this once, all to themselves.

After, he lies with his head on her chest. She strokes his hair and listens to his heart come down and doesn't count.

"I'll take the temperature in the morning," he says, drowsy. "Off your steps. Twenty-eight, I should think. It's June."

"With my jelly thermometer."

"With your jelly thermometer." He's nearly asleep. "I'll bring it up and tell you. And you'll put it in the book."

"No," says Win.

He lifts his head.

She reaches over to the table by the verandah door, where the day book is, and the pencil. She opens it at the page for today. It's all in her hand, from the morning: *Wedding. Methodist chapel, 1.30. 163 in, abt 30 outside. Breakfast, yard, all. Mr W. & colleague, Rms 3 & 5.* And at the bottom, in the space where she'd put the sea if there were a sea to put, there's nothing.

She holds the pencil out to him.

"You put it in," says Win. "Tomorrow. In your hand. At the top of the page."

He looks at the pencil. He looks at the book, which nobody but her has written in since 1959. He looks at her.

He takes the pencil.

"That's a joint account," says Win, "that is," and puts her head down on the pillow, and is asleep before he's worked out what to say.

He lies there in the lamplight with the pencil in his hand. After a while he puts it down on the day book, square, and turns the lamp out, and curls round her from behind, and goes to sleep. In the morning, at six, he goes down to the harbour steps with the jelly thermometer. He comes back up. And at the top of a new page, in his small neat hand, the first line anybody but Win Carew has ever written in the Harbour House day book, he writes:

*Sea 28°. R.T.*

And under it, in hers, when she wakes:

*W.T. Brought forward.*
