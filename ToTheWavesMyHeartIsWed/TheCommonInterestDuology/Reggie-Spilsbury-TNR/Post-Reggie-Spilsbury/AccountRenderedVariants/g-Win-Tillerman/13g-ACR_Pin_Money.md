# Pin Money

Marcia has Pearl on her lap in the stern of the Bay boat, and a hatbox between her feet, and a bucket, because Pearl was sick off Port Maria and will be sick again. She has her husband beside her in the dark suit he's worn since Kingston, in June, because he said it was the right thing. She's been married to him five years, and she knows exactly what he means by the right thing. He means armour.

She made the suit. She made it in 1972 for his cousin's funeral, out of a length of good worsted her own mother had put by. She took it in at the waist in March, because he's been losing weight since September, and she knows to the quarter-inch how much.

She's thinking about that, and about the hatbox, when the boat comes round the head and Lloyd goes still beside her.

"That's him," says Lloyd. "On the wall. With Mother."

Marcia looks.

There's her mother-in-law, in blue, with her hand up. And beside her there's a small man in a white shirt with the sleeves rolled, no jacket, no hat. The first thing Marcia sees about him, because it's the first thing she sees about anybody, is that the collar of that shirt wants turning. It's gone soft at the points. It's a good shirt, English cotton, and it's been washed a great many times by somebody who knew how. And nobody's turned the collar. Not for a good while. Which means whoever used to do it stopped, and he's never noticed.

The second thing she sees is that he's holding Mrs Carew's basket.

She's known Win Carew five years. She's never once seen her let anybody hold anything.

---

Lloyd shakes his hand at the top of the steps like a man refusing a loan. Marcia watches him do it, and watches the doctor take it. He doesn't do anything with his face at all, just *Mr Carew, welcome home*, and lets it go. She thinks, *he knows*. He knows exactly what Lloyd's doing and he's letting him do it.

Then the doctor looks at her. He doesn't look at her the way men look at her, which is at her figure and then at her face and then at Lloyd. He looks at her face, and then at the hatbox, and then at the bucket. And then at Pearl, who's green and furious and has her face in Marcia's neck.

"Port Maria," he says. "It's the swell off the point. It catches everybody. There's ginger in the kitchen, Win keeps it for the Kingston boat." He hesitates. "May I take the hatbox?"

She lets him take the hatbox.

Pearl lifts her face out of Marcia's neck. She looks at the small man with the hatbox for a long moment, with her father's frown. The doctor looks back at her, quite seriously, as though he'd been introduced to somebody important and was waiting to be told her name.

"I'm Pearl," says Pearl. "I was sick."

"I'm Reggie," says the doctor. "I'm sorry. I was sick off Port Maria in 1948, and I was sick off it again in 1961 in a Force 7, and I was sick off it last year on a millpond. I think it's a very bad place."

Pearl considers this.

"*Very* bad," she agrees, and puts her face back in Marcia's neck. But she keeps one eye on him all the way up the steps.

---

Marcia has despised Desmond Carew since the second time she met him.

The first time she didn't. Nobody does, the first time. He came to their wedding in 1969 three hours late, in a cream suit. He danced with her mother and made her laugh so hard she had to sit down. He played the piano at the reception without being asked, and the whole of her father's church hall got up off its chairs. She thought, *oh, that's where Lloyd gets it from*. And then she thought, *no, Lloyd hasn't got any of it*. She was glad.

The second time was the autumn of 1970, when she was six months gone with Pearl, and he came to the flat in Barbican. He sat at her kitchen table with his hat on his knee and told Lloyd about a man in Miami. And at the end of it Lloyd went into the bedroom and came back with the envelope they'd been saving for the pram.

She didn't say anything. She was twenty-one. She'd been married a year and a half, and she was frightened of him, a little, and of Lloyd's face. She thought it was once.

It wasn't once. By the time she found the counterfoil, it had a name: *standing order*, forty dollars on the first of every month, to an account in Miami, set up in 1968. Before she'd ever met Lloyd. He'd been paying since he was twenty. Since his mother stopped.

She found it in 1972, when Pearl was a year old and needed shoes. She sat on the edge of the bed with it in her hand for a long time. Then she put it back where she'd found it. She went and got the sewing machine out of the cupboard, and put a card in the window of the haberdasher's on Constant Spring Road.

*Alterations. Dressmaking. Reasonable.*

She's been at it three nights a week ever since. It pays for Pearl's shoes, and the gas, and in a good month the half of the rent that Lloyd's forty dollars would have paid. She's never told him she found it. He's never told her. They sit in the same flat, three nights a week, him at the table with the accounts he keeps for her father's church and her at the machine. The standing order goes out on the first of the month between them like a third person at the table.

And every Christmas the letter comes from Miami. *Lloydie, you're a good boy. You're the only one I can count on.* And Lloyd reads it, and goes quiet for a week. And Marcia hems other women's dresses at eleven at night and thinks things about Desmond Carew that she wouldn't say in her father's church.

---

About Win Carew, Marcia doesn't know what she thinks. She's never known.

She admires her. She can't help it. Nobody who's ever done a set of accounts could look at Harbour House and not admire her. And she's kind, in her way. She sends Pearl a dress every birthday, made up from a length of good cotton, beautifully finished, with French seams. Marcia turned the first one inside out and looked at the seams and felt about four years old.

But she stopped. That's the thing. In 1968 she stopped, and she's told Lloyd since that it was the hardest thing she ever did, and Marcia believes her. And the day she put it down, her son picked it up. And she didn't know. She didn't *know*. She sits over here at the Bay with her ledgers and her French seams and her fifteen years of being strong, and she's never once asked.

Marcia knows that isn't fair. She knows Lloyd never told her, and wouldn't have, and would have died first. She knows it isn't Win's fault that Lloyd picked it up.

She thinks it anyway. Three nights a week, at the machine.

---

On the Monday evening, Pearl is behind her skirts.

She's shy of the house, which she was last in at two and doesn't remember. She's shy of Auntie Joy, whom she's never met, and who swept her up off the harbour wall and kissed her all over, which Pearl did not care for. She's shy of Grandma, who's too tall and too busy. She stands behind Marcia's knees in the kitchen and holds on to her skirt and watches the doctor.

He's at the end of the table with a lot of paper. He's drawing on it with a pencil, very fast, and talking to Lloyd about boats. Lloyd is saying *I see*, the way he says it to men at the bank who are about to be refused. Marcia hears him say it and her heart turns over.

Pearl lets go of her skirt.

Marcia watches her go. She goes the length of the kitchen very slowly, the way she goes up to a strange dog, one foot and then the other. She stops beside the doctor's chair. She looks at the paper. Then she puts both hands on the edge of the table and pulls herself up on her toes, to see better, and says, "What's that?"

The doctor stops talking.

He looks down at her. And Marcia sees something go across his face that she can only describe as alarm. Pure alarm, like a man who's been handed something that might go off. He looks at Pearl, and at Marcia across the kitchen, and back at Pearl.

"That's a boat," he says carefully. "It's a plan of three boats."

"*Why*?"

"Because —" He stops. He looks as though nobody had ever asked him that before about anything. "Because I'm getting married on Saturday. And a great many people have to come across the water on the flood. And I want them all to get here."

Pearl considers this. Then she puts her arms up.

She does it the way she does to Marcia, or Lloyd, or the woman in the flat downstairs: without the least doubt that she'll be picked up. It's a thing she's done her whole life, all three years of it.

The doctor doesn't move.

He sits there with his pencil in one hand, looking at Pearl's arms. Marcia realises, with a lurch of something that isn't quite laughter, that he doesn't know how. He's fifty-five years old. He's got a doctorate, Joy says, and a Fellowship of something. And he's never in his life picked up a child, and he's sitting there working out where the weight goes.

"Mrs Carew," he says, without taking his eyes off Pearl. "Marcia. I'm sorry. Would you — I don't —"

"Under her arms," says Marcia. She's across the kitchen before she knows it. "Like that. Both hands. She'll do the rest. She'll get her legs round you."

He puts his pencil down. He puts both hands under Pearl's arms. He does it very carefully, with his fingers spread, as if she were made of something that would break. And he lifts her up off the floor and holds her out in front of him, at arm's length, with her legs dangling. The two of them look at each other, nose to nose, with equal seriousness.

"*No*," says Pearl, scandalised. "*Sit*."

"Bring her in to you," says Marcia, "against your chest. On your knee. That's it."

He brings her in. He sits her on his knee, with one hand flat on her back and the other under her, and Pearl settles in as if she'd been doing it all her life. She reaches for the pencil. He gives it to her. And Marcia stands beside his chair and watches his face change, slowly, from alarm to something else. It's the look of a man who's worked something out.

"It's like the calf," he says, half to himself. "In the sling. You take the weight under the chest and let them find their own balance. You don't *hold* them. You give them something to hold on to."

"What calf?" says Pearl.

"A very big baby," says Reggie. "In the sea. Her mother weighs as much as this whole kitchen." He looks at Pearl. "I'll show you. If your mother says."

Pearl looks at Marcia with enormous eyes.

Marcia looks down the table at Lloyd. He's watching. He's watching his daughter on the doctor's knee with the pencil in her fist, and his face is doing something Marcia's only seen once before. That was on the harbour wall at the Bay, the Christmas of 1970, when his father didn't come.

Then he gets up and goes out.

---

By the Tuesday morning he's Pearl's.

Marcia doesn't know how it happened. She comes down at seven and there they are at the kitchen table. Pearl's on his knee in her nightdress, he's drawing the boats again, and she's holding the pencil for him, and every so often he lets her draw a line. She's drawn a great many lines. Some of them are on the boats.

"She came down on her own," he says, looking up, apologetic. "At six. I was on the verandah. She wanted to know what the sea was doing."

"What's it doing?" says Marcia.

"It's going *up*," says Pearl, with great satisfaction. "It's the *tide*. It goes up and down. Like *breathing*." She puts her hand flat on his chest, over the pocket, and waits, very seriously, feeling him breathe. "Like *that*."

"Like that," the doctor agrees, and doesn't move. "I showed her on the steps. It's slower than breathing. I've told her. She's been very patient."

Marcia sits down across from them.

She thinks, *I should be angry*. A strange man, on a verandah, at six in the morning, with her daughter. She should be angry, and Lloyd will be. But Pearl's got her head against the man's shirt, where the collar wants turning. She's playing with a button, and he's holding her the way Marcia showed him, one hand flat on her back, very still. He isn't doing anything. He's letting her be there.

He looks up and catches Marcia looking.

"I've not held one before," he says, quietly, over Pearl's head. "A child. I'm sorry. I don't know the rules. If I'm doing it wrong, you'll tell me."

"You're not doing it wrong."

"I had a cousin once, with a baby. In 1938. I was asked to hold it at the christening, and I was so afraid of dropping it that I held it out at arm's length through the whole of the second hymn, like a specimen jar. It screamed the roof off." He looks down at Pearl. "I was never asked again. I didn't mind. I was nineteen and it seemed a very damp sort of thing." He's quiet a moment. "I'd not thought — I didn't know they were like this. That they'd just *decide*."

"She decides," says Marcia. "She's decided since she was born. She gets it from Lloyd's mother."

The doctor looks at her with an expression she can't read. Then he smiles, a little, at Pearl's head.

"Yes," he says. "I'd noticed that."


Lloyd comes to the kitchen door at a quarter past seven, dressed, in the suit. He stops in the doorway and sees Pearl on the doctor's knee with her hand on his chest.

Marcia watches his face. She watches it go through the thing it did last night. Then he turns round without a word and goes out through the passage to the front.

She finds him on the verandah with his hands on the rail.

"She's not his," says Lloyd. He doesn't turn round.

"No. She's ours." Marcia stands beside him. "She's decided she likes him. That's all. She's three. She decided she liked the man at the fish counter in Half Way Tree for a whole month."

"She's never sat on my father's knee like that."

Marcia doesn't answer that. There's only one answer, and it's that Desmond Carew never sat still long enough for anybody to sit on his knee. He was always getting up to go to the piano, or the telephone, or Miami. She's not going to say it. Not on this verandah, not this week.

She puts her hand on Lloyd's back instead, between his shoulders, where the suit's taken in. She can feel through the worsted how tight he's holding himself. She leaves her hand there until he lets some of it go.

---

Joy finds her on the harbour wall on the Tuesday afternoon, with Pearl asleep on her lap under a straw hat.

Joy sits down beside her and puts her head on Marcia's shoulder, the way she has since the wedding in 1969. That was when she was eighteen and the only one of Lloyd's family who didn't make Marcia feel she'd married above herself. They've written every week for five years. Joy's letters come on the thin blue airmail, in the big round hand, four pages of the Hammersmith and the nuns and a registrar called Peter. Marcia's go back on the thick Kingston paper, two pages, neat, all of it true. Joy is the only person in the world Marcia told about the counterfoil. She told her in 1972, in a letter she rewrote four times. Joy wrote back by return, one line, underlined: *Oh Marce. Oh the BASTARD.*

She meant her father. Marcia knew that. It's the only time Joy's ever called him anything.

"I told him," says Joy, into her shoulder. "About the *Gleaner*. The man in the back office."

"Told who?"

"Reggie." Joy lifts her head. "He's been talking to Lloyd about high water for two days, Marce. He didn't *know*. He just didn't know. You should have seen his face. He said *I've been a fool*, just like that, straight off. I've never heard a man say it that fast. Not a consultant, not Peter, not *anybody*."

Marcia looks down at Pearl.

"Pearl thinks he's the sun and the moon."

"I know. I saw." Joy's quiet a moment. "He showed me his ECGs, Marce. On the Monday. His own. He's got them in a folder. He wanted to know what I thought of the T waves." She laughs, a little shakily. "He's *mad*. He's completely mad. And he looks at Mum like —" She stops. "You've seen it."

"I've seen it."

"Daddy never looked at her like that. Not once. Not in any photograph."

Marcia doesn't say anything. There's a fishing boat coming in past the head, low in the water, and she watches it.

"You've got to tell Mum," says Joy. "About the standing order."

"I can't."

"You *can*. You've been carrying it two years. On your own. At that machine." Joy takes her hand. She turns it over. She looks at the tips of the first two fingers of Marcia's right hand, where the needle goes, where the skin's gone hard. "Look at that. That's three years. Marce, you've got to tell somebody who can *do* something."

"It's Lloyd's. It's not mine to tell."

"It's your *rent*."

Marcia takes her hand back.

"And what'll she say?" she says. It comes out harder than she means. "Your mother? She'll look at me. She'll look at me like I let it happen. Like I sat there five years and let him. She'll think, *that girl couldn't manage him*. The way she managed your father. And she stopped. She got to stop, Joy. She stopped and she was strong, and she's been strong at everybody for fifteen years over here with her books. And my husband picked it up off the floor the day she put it down, and she never even *asked*."

Joy looks at her for a long time.

"Yes," she says at last. "I know. I've thought that too." She puts her head back on Marcia's shoulder. "Tell her anyway. She'll not look at you like that. She'll look at you like you're the only person who's ever told her anything straight in her whole life. Because you will be." She's quiet. "Except him. He tells her things straight. It's why she's marrying him, I think."

---

She tells her at eight, in the kitchen.

Win's at the stove with the Ministry's soup for tomorrow. The girls have gone. Lloyd's in the bar with Pearl, who won't go to sleep without being told about the calf again, and Reggie's telling her. Marcia can hear his voice through the wall, low, and Pearl's, saying *and then what*, and then not saying anything.

While she's still in the passage, Reggie comes past the bar door with Pearl asleep on his shoulder, her sash undone, one fist full of his shirt. He sees Marcia and stops.

"She went before the calf's mother," he says, very low. "I'll take her up. Lloyd said I might." He looks at Marcia's face, and then at the kitchen door, and she sees him understand that something's about to happen in there. "And then I'll be in the office. Out of everybody's way."

He goes up the stairs with Pearl. Not fast.

Marcia stands in the kitchen doorway with the counterfoil in her hand.

She took it out of Lloyd's wallet at six. He'll know. He'll know by tomorrow, and she doesn't care.

"Mrs Carew," she says.

Win turns round. She sees Marcia's face and puts the spoon down.

Marcia tells her. She does it the way she does a hem, straight along, not stopping. The standing order. 1968. Forty dollars on the first of the month. The pram money in 1970. The shoes. The card in the haberdasher's window on Constant Spring Road. Three nights a week. She holds out the counterfoil. Win takes it and looks at it, and her face goes grey under the brown, all at once, the way a sky goes.

"Six years," says Win.

"Six years."

"And you never —" Win looks up. "Marcia. Why didn't you *tell* me?"

And it comes out of Marcia. She doesn't mean it to. She's had it at the machine for two years, and it comes out of her in Win's kitchen like the swell off Port Maria.

"Because you'd *stopped*," she says. "You'd stopped. You were the one who got to stop. And everybody said how strong you were. Lloyd says it. Joy says it. The whole *Bay* says it. And he picked it up. He was twenty. He picked it up off the floor the day you put it down, and you never asked. You never once came to Kingston and *asked*." Her voice cracks. "I've been hemming other women's dresses at eleven at night for two years. I can't make him stop. I've tried. I've not even been able to say it, because if I say it, it sounds like I want the money. And I *do* want the money. For Pearl. But that's not why." She's crying now, and furious with herself for it. "And you're the only one who ever did. Stop. You're the only one who knows *how*. So I'm telling you. Even if you look at me like I let it happen."

Win Carew stands at her stove with the counterfoil in her hand and looks at her daughter-in-law.

She doesn't look at her like that. Marcia had been sure she would. She'd been braced for it since six o'clock. But Win looks at her the way Marcia's only ever seen her look at the day book, when she's found a figure that's wrong and she's working out, very carefully, where it went wrong and how long ago.

Then she puts the counterfoil down on the table. She comes across the kitchen and takes both of Marcia's hands. She turns the right one over, the way Joy did, and looks at the tips of the first two fingers.

"You've been doing it by hand," she says. "The finishing. Not on the machine."

Marcia can't answer.

"I can tell. I do mine by hand." Win's thumb goes over the hard place on Marcia's finger, very gently. "I did Pearl's birthday dresses with French seams. Every one. So you'd turn them inside out and see I'd taken trouble. So you'd know I thought you were worth taking trouble for." Her voice has gone low. "I didn't know you were sitting up till eleven doing it for money. For my son's *father*."

"Mrs Carew —"

"*Win*," says Win. "For God's sake, Marcia. Five years. It's Win."

And she puts her arms round her, in the kitchen, with the soup on the stove, and holds on. Marcia stands there with her face in her mother-in-law's shoulder, and smells starch and onions, and cries like Pearl off Port Maria.

"You were right," says Win into her hair. "Every word. I never asked. I was so proud of stopping I never looked round to see who'd picked it up." She holds her tighter. "Thank you for telling me. You're the only one who ever has. Straight, like that."

Then she lets go. She wipes her face with the heel of her hand, and picks up the counterfoil. Marcia sees her face go set, the way it goes over a bad bill.

"Where's Reggie?"

"In the office." Marcia wipes her face. "He took Pearl up. He said he'd be in the office after. Out of everybody's way."

"Right," says Win, and goes.

---

On the Wednesday evening Marcia's at the window of room four with Pearl on her hip. From there she can see the harbour wall, and her husband sitting on it alone in his suit. He's been there an hour.

She watches the doctor come down the harbour steps. Not fast. She watches him sit down on the wall about six feet off. Not close. She watches Lloyd not turn round.

"My *Reggie*," says Pearl, and struggles to get down.

"Not now, sweetheart."

"I want to show him the *tide*."

"He's talking to Daddy." Marcia holds her. "Let him talk to Daddy."

Pearl stops struggling. She puts her arms round Marcia's neck and looks out of the window at the two men on the harbour wall with enormous seriousness.

"Is Reggie making Daddy better?" she says.

Marcia doesn't know what to say.

They're a long time. The sun goes down over the head. A fishing boat comes in past the second head, steering on something Marcia can't see. At some point Lloyd puts his face in his hands, and his shoulders start going. Marcia's heart stops. She can't tell from the window whether he's crying or laughing. She thinks it may be both.

The doctor doesn't move. He sits six feet off on the wall and doesn't come any closer, and doesn't touch him. He just stays.

"Yes," says Marcia, very quietly, into Pearl's hair. "I think he might be."

---

Lloyd comes to bed at eleven. He's been in the kitchen with his mother; she heard their voices through the floor, low, for an hour. He undresses in the dark without putting the light on, so as not to wake Pearl in the cot. He gets in beside Marcia and lies on his back.

She doesn't pretend to be asleep.

"You took it out of my wallet," says Lloyd, to the ceiling.

"Yes."

"You told Mum."

"Yes."

He's quiet for a long time.

"Good," he says.

She turns her head on the pillow.

"He said I could stop," says Lloyd. His voice is very odd. "On the wall. He didn't say I *should*. He said it was mine. He said I could, if I wanted, and it wouldn't mean I didn't love Daddy. It'd only mean I'd stopped paying. He said nobody had ever told me that, and he was right. Nobody ever had." He stops. "I didn't know it was a thing that *could* stop, Marce. I thought it was just — mine. Like my hands."

Marcia lies very still.

She's wanted to say it for four years. At the kitchen table. At the machine. In this bed. *You could stop.* Four words. She's never said them, because she's his wife, and she wants the money. Every time she opened her mouth she could hear how it would sound: like a woman who wanted the pram money back. She couldn't make it sound like anything else. So she said nothing, and did the hems.

And a stranger on a harbour wall, a small grey Englishman with a bad heart and a collar that wants turning, has just said it for her. He's said it in a way Lloyd could hear. He's the one man on this coast who's got nothing to gain from Lloyd's forty dollars either way.

She starts to cry. She doesn't make any noise, because of Pearl. Lloyd feels it anyway. He turns over and puts his arms round her.

"I'm not going to stop," he says into her hair. "Not yet. I can't. Not with him like he is. Mum says he's got a tremor. She says his cuffs are turned." He holds her tighter. "But I'll cut it. Twenty. From July. You can stop the sewing, Marce."

"I'll not stop the sewing," says Marcia, wet, into his chest. "I *like* the sewing. I'm good at it." She lets out a long breath. "I'll just stop doing it at eleven o'clock at night."

He laughs. It's the first time she's heard him laugh since September.

---

On the Friday, the day before, she goes and finds the doctor.

He's on the verandah with the boat plan, which he's not supposed to be looking at any more because Pascual's confiscated it twice. He hides it under the *Gleaner* when she comes out, and then sees it's her, and looks sheepish, and takes it out again.

"Dr Tar."

"Reggie," he says. "Please. Pearl won't call me anything else. It confuses her."

Marcia sits down in the other chair. She looks at him. He's in another of the white shirts, and it's the same: good English cotton, washed a thousand times by somebody who knew how. Win, lately, Marcia supposes, and before that God knows. And the collar gone soft at the points, and nobody's turned it.

"Give me your shirts," she says.

He blinks at her.

"All of them. The white ones. The collars want turning." She holds out her hand. "You unpick the collar and turn it over and sew it back, so the worn side's underneath. Then it's as good as new for another five years. Nobody's done yours in years. I could see it off the boat."

He looks at her. He looks down at his own collar, as though he'd never seen it before. Then he looks back at her, and something happens to his face.

"Iris did them," he says slowly. "Win's mother. She did the Institute's linen till 1963. I never knew that was what she was doing. I thought they just — lasted." He looks at the collar. "The girl who does it now doesn't. I'd not noticed."

"And Win?"

"Win's not said a word." He looks out at the harbour. "She wouldn't. She's very particular about what's whose. She'd not take over my things without being asked, any more than I'd do her books. And I'd not have known to ask."

Marcia looks at him. She thinks about French seams, and about a woman who'd sooner watch a good collar fray than put her hands on something that wasn't hers to mend. She thinks she understands her mother-in-law better than she did an hour ago.

"Well, I'm asking," she says, and keeps her hand out. "I'm family. It's different. I'm not doing it for you. I'm doing it because I can't stand to see a good shirt go to waste. And because Pearl will be at that wedding tomorrow with her face in your collar, and I'll not have her at her grandmother's wedding with her face in a frayed collar." She pauses. "And because you told my husband on the wall that he could stop. Which I've wanted to tell him for four years and couldn't."

He doesn't say anything for a moment. He looks out at the harbour.

"I'm sorry it had to be me," he says at last. "It should have been somebody who loves him."

"It couldn't have been," says Marcia. "That's the whole point. Anybody who loves him, it'd have sounded like we wanted something." She stands up. "Shirts. On my bed by four. I'll have them done by the morning."

"Marcia." He's looking up at her. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me," says Marcia. "You'll owe me. I'll want the calf."

"The calf?"

"Pearl says you're going to show her the calf. On the cay. After." She folds her arms. "You'll take her across in Dr Pobil's boat, and you'll show her, and I'm coming too, and so's Lloyd. And you'll hold her up to see and you'll not drop her."

He looks at her for a long moment. Then he smiles. It's not the voice. She's heard about the voice now; Joy did it for her on Tuesday night, the way Win had described it, leaning back with the eyebrows, and Marcia laughed until she had to sit down. This isn't that. It's small and crooked and it goes up further on one side than the other.

"Under the arms," says Reggie. "Both hands. And she'll do the rest."

"That's it," says Marcia. "You learn quick."

She goes in. At four o'clock there are seven white shirts on her bed in room four, folded, very square, with a note on the top one in a small neat hand.

*With thanks. I'd no idea. R.T.*

She sits up with them till midnight. Not because she has to. Because she wants to. Joy sits on the end of the bed with her feet tucked under her, and talks about Peter, and the Hammersmith, and the nuns. Pearl sleeps in the cot with one arm flung up over her head. Lloyd sits on the floor by the cot with his tie off, where he's been since he got her down, with one hand through the bars on Pearl's back. Every so often he looks over at Marcia's hands, and doesn't say anything, and doesn't have to.

At a quarter to midnight she bites off the last thread. She holds up the last collar to the lamp to see if it's straight.

It's straight.
