# Closing Balance

It comes in the Tuesday post, in a long envelope with a Kingston postmark and the name of Desmond's lawyer printed on the flap. She's been waiting for it for three weeks, since the man wrote to say the six weeks were nearly up. She's been waiting for it, if she's honest, since August.

She doesn't open it at the door. She takes it through to the kitchen, where Reggie's at the table with his tea and the *Gleaner* and his glasses pushed up. It's a Tuesday, so he's still here, because he stays the Monday now and nobody on either side of the channel pretends otherwise. She puts it down on the table in front of him, face up.

He looks at it. He puts the *Gleaner* down.

"Open it," he says.

"You open it."

"It's yours, Win."

She picks up the bread knife and slits the flap. There's a covering letter, two lines, which she doesn't read. And under it there's the thing itself, on thick paper with a crest at the top and a seal at the bottom. *Decree Absolute.* It says a great deal more than that, in the way of lawyers. It says *Carew v. Carew and Tar*, which makes her laugh out loud, at her own kitchen table, before she can stop herself. It says that the marriage solemnised on the ninth of August 1947 at the parish church of St Andrew, between Desmond Aloysius Carew and Winifred May Greaves, is hereby dissolved.

She reads it twice. Then she puts it down very carefully on the table, square.

"Well," she says. "That's that."

She doesn't know what she feels. She'd thought she'd know. She'd thought it'd be like the envelope in 1968, a month of it in her apron, and then done. It's not like that at all. It's a piece of paper with a seal. Twenty-seven years, and a piece of paper, and she's standing in her own kitchen at eight in the morning with the kettle on and the girls coming in at nine.

Reggie hasn't said anything. He's looking at her, not at the paper.

"I'm going to put it in the office," she says. "In the drawer. With the other."

"Yes."

She picks it up. She goes out of the kitchen into the passage, and then, because the office is locked and the key's upstairs on her chain, she goes up the back stairs.

She hears him get up and come after her. Not fast.

She's on the half-landing when he says her name. The one by the window, where he emptied bucket two in November 1972, and took his tablet in May 1973, before, not after. She stops, and turns round, and he's three stairs below her with one hand on the rail. His face is doing something she's seen it do perhaps twice.

"Win," he says. "I told you I'd ask you on the stairs."

She stands very still.

She'd forgotten. No, she hadn't. She'd put it away somewhere, the way you put away a thing that can't be yet, in a drawer, folded in four. He'd said it in her bed in May, with his hand on her back, very quietly. *I'd have married you. If you'd been free. I'd have asked you on the stairs. I'd like that on the record.* And she'd said, *It's on the record. It's in the book.*

"You did," she says. Her voice has gone somewhere.

He comes up two more stairs, slowly, so he's one below her and their faces are level. He puts his hand inside his jacket, on the left, where there's been nothing in the pocket since November 1971.

There's something in it now.

He takes it out. It's a small box, dark blue, gone soft at the corners from being carried. It's been carried a long time, she can see that. Months.

"Since September," he says, seeing her see it. "I went to Kingston with Jean on the Board business and I went into a shop in King Street, and I've had it in that pocket since. I kept putting my hand on it." He looks at the box and then at her. "Marguerite thought it was my chest. She took my pulse about it twice."

Win laughs. It comes out of her wet and shaking, and she puts her hand over her mouth.

"Winifred May Greaves," says Reggie. He's not doing any voice at all. It's only his, a bit hoarse, the one he uses on the verandah at night when he's telling her something true. "I'm fifty-five. I've a heart Marguerite won't insure and a hut at the end of a row, and I'm told I don't see people, and I added your laundry up wrong. I've got no family to speak of and one more friend than I deserve, and he'll be unbearable about this for the rest of my life." He opens the box. "Will you marry me?"

It's a plain ring. Gold, with one small stone, very clear. It's exactly the kind of ring she'd have chosen, if anybody had ever asked her to choose anything.

She takes her hand away from her mouth.

"You'll be on your knees next," she says. "On my stairs. And then I'll have to get Marguerite to get you up."

"I'd considered it," says Reggie. "I decided against. On medical advice."

"*Reggie Tar* —"

"You haven't answered."

"*Yes*," says Win. "Yes. You — of *course* yes. Yes."

He takes her left hand. Her other ring's been gone since August, in the drawer with the cheque, and there's still a pale mark where it was. He puts his on, over the mark, and it fits. Of course it fits. She looks at it, and then at him.

"How did you know the size?"

"I measured your glove," says Reggie. "In October. The one you leave on the hall table. With my callipers."

She kisses him. On the half-landing, in the morning, with the girls coming in at nine and the kettle boiling itself dry in the kitchen, with his hands on her face and hers holding his jacket and the decree absolute crushed flat between them. She hears it crackle and doesn't care.

When the kitchen boy comes up the back stairs at five past nine to ask about the eggs, they're still there. He goes back down without asking. By half past nine, the whole Bay knows.

---

He tells Pascual on the Wednesday, on the step of the hut, at six in the evening, with the beer. He's rehearsed it on the boat. He'd thought he might build up to it.

He doesn't get the chance. Pascual comes round the end of the row with two bottles and sits down on the lower step and looks up at him.

"Alleyne's boy," says Pascual, "says you've been engaged since half past nine yesterday morning. And that the kitchen boy found you on the stairs. And that you had a ring in a box. In your *pocket*. Since *September*."

Reggie shuts his eyes.

"*Since September*, hombre." Pascual's voice is climbing. "Marguerite took your pulse about that pocket. *Twice*. She came home and said, Pascual, I don't like it, he keeps putting his hand there, I think it's the angina coming back. I lay awake. I lay *awake*, Reggie, I thought you were —"

"I'm sorry."

"You're *not* sorry. Look at your face. You're not sorry at all." Pascual puts his bottle down on the step with great deliberation. Then he reaches into the pocket of his shirt and takes out a notebook.

It's small, and black, and very old. The corners have gone round. There's a rubber band round it.

Reggie opens his eyes and looks at it.

"What's that?"

"This," says Pascual, taking off the rubber band, "is a list."

"A list of what?"

"Of everything you said to me," says Pascual, "between the first of January 1960 and the fourteenth of July 1962. About Marguerite. About me and Marguerite. About my *hair*." He opens it. "I started it on the day you asked Hester in front of the whole long room whether she thought the new lady doctor at the Bay might be persuaded to take on a patient with an incurable condition. And then you looked at *me*."

"I don't remember that."

"*I* remember it. It's the first entry." Pascual runs his finger down the page. His handwriting's gone small to fit. "Item two. The eighth of February 1960. You told Hester I'd been across about the drugs order three times in one week and it must be a very large order. Item three. You asked me whether a man of my age ought to take dancing lessons before the Colonel's Christmas party, and when I said no, you showed me the foxtrot in the long room. With *Jean*. In front of the students."

"That was a public service. You were going to tread on her."

"Item seven," says Pascual, ignoring him. "*The Tuesday boat.* You'd sit on this step and time me combing my hair before the Tuesday boat. Out loud. *Four minutes, Pascual. That's a record. Is it for the drugs order?*" He looks up. "Item eleven. You called her *your physician* for a year and a half. *How's your physician. Did your physician say anything about your blood pressure.* In front of the *Board*."

Reggie has started, helplessly, to laugh.

"Item nineteen," says Pascual, raising his voice over it. "The twentieth of May 1962. I showed you the seating plan for the schoolroom. I'd drawn it very carefully, because of the Colonel and Mr Purdie not being able to sit together since the sugar wharf. And you looked at it, and you said —" He finds the line with his finger and reads it out, slowly, with enormous relish. "*A man who draws a seating plan for his own wedding, Pascual, has entirely lost his reason. I'd have you certified if I thought Marguerite would sign.*"

Reggie stops laughing.

He's got his hand, he discovers, in the inside pocket of his jacket on the left, where the box was. There's something else in there now. It's folded in four. He'd drawn it on the boat.

Pascual watches him not take it out.

"Show me," says Pascual softly.

"No."

"Reggie. *Show me*."

He takes it out. He unfolds it on his knee. It's a plan of the Methodist chapel at the Bay, which he went and measured on Tuesday afternoon with Win's tape. It has the pews numbered. It has the capacity worked out, a hundred and eighteen seated and perhaps thirty standing at the back if the doors are open. There's a column down the side for the cay, with the boats: Pascual's launch, Alleyne's boy, and the Institute's workboat if Jean will let it go. It has the tide for the twenty-second of June in the corner, high water at 1.14, because you'd want to bring the cay across on the flood.

Pascual looks at it for a long time.

Then he takes out a pencil, and writes something at the bottom of the list in the little black book, very carefully, and holds it up so Reggie can see.

*Item twenty. 17th April 1974. HE HAS DRAWN A SEATING PLAN. WITH TIDES.*

"I've waited fourteen years," says Pascual, "for this day."

Reggie puts his face in his hands.

---

"You'll stand up with me," he says, when he can.

It's gone dark. They've finished the beer. Pascual's still got the notebook out on his knee, and every so often he looks at it and laughs to himself, and Reggie lets him.

"*Stand up with you*." Pascual turns his head. "You mean best man."

"I mean best man."

"Obviously," says Pascual. "Obviously I'll stand up with you. Did you think you were going to ask anybody else? Who? Baptiste? *Jean*?" He shuts the notebook. "I've been your best man since 1952, *hombre*. It's time somebody made it official."

Reggie can't say anything to that. He sits on the step and looks at the dark.

"And I'll make a speech," says Pascual, very comfortably. "At the breakfast. In front of the whole Bay. And your wife's children. And the Board, if Jean asks them." He taps the little black book against his knee. "I've had fourteen years to write it, Reggie. And now I've got item twenty."

"Pascual."

"*Four minutes before the Tuesday boat*," says Pascual dreamily, to the night. "The *foxtrot*. I'll have them on the floor."

---

He doesn't stop, over the next eight weeks. Reggie hadn't expected him to.

He says *how's your intended* at the long table on Mondays, in front of the students. He asks Reggie, at the Sunday dinner, in front of the minister, whether he's been across about the drugs order. When Reggie goes over to the Bay on a Thursday, which he's not done in twenty years, to see about the flowers, Pascual meets him on the jetty coming back and times him up the path. *Nine minutes, Dr Tar. Was it the drugs order?* He finds out about the callipers from Win, God knows how, and tells Mrs Joseph, and Mrs Joseph tells the kitchen. For a week Reggie can't eat his lunch in the long room without somebody asking whether he's measured it.

And Reggie is so happy he can hardly think.

It's the planning. He'd not expected to love it. He'd thought it'd be a thing to be got through, like a Board. But it's a *problem*. It's a hundred and eighteen seats and a hundred and sixty people who want to come. It's three boats and a tide, and a cake Mrs Joseph says will take four days. There's the Methodist minister, who needs a letter from the Kingston lawyer confirming the decree. And there's Win, who wants it small, and the Bay, which won't have it small at any price. He sits up at night in the hut with graph paper.

He's aware, somewhere at the back, that he's doing the thing. The thing where it comes on and everything else in the room goes quiet. He's done it about curves, and animals, and the roof of room four. He's aware that Win, at her kitchen table, watches him draw the third version of the boat plan with an expression he can't read. He's aware that she's written to her children in London and Kingston, and that one letter's come back quickly and one hasn't, and that she's not said much about it.

He notices. He puts it to one side. There's the tide to think about.

---

Joy's letter comes back in eleven days, by airmail, on the thin blue paper, in Joy's big round hand that the nuns never managed to make smaller.

*MUM!!!* it says, across the top, underlined three times. And then two pages, most of which is capital letters. Joy's coming. Joy's got the leave, she's owed it, Sister can lump it. Joy wants to know what he's like and how old he is and whether he's *kind* to her, and what does *a scientist* mean, what sort? And then, in the postscript, in smaller writing, as though she'd thought of it after: *Mum, is he the one with the heart? Mrs Kilburn's Winston wrote to Cousin Dell and Cousin Dell told me. What's he ON? Don't let him do the stairs.*

Win reads it at the kitchen table and laughs until she has to take her glasses off.

Lloyd's comes in five weeks. It comes in an ordinary envelope, by the Kingston post, and it's one side of a sheet of the bank's paper, with the heading cut off with scissors so it won't look like the bank's paper.

*Dear Mother,*

*Thank you for your letter. Marcia and I will come for the 22nd, and bring Pearl. We will come on the Monday boat.*

*I am glad you are happy.*

*Your loving son,*

*Lloyd.*

She reads it three times. It doesn't get any longer.

She puts it in the drawer of the office desk, with the decree and the unsigned cheque, and sits there with the drawer open.

Lloyd was the one Desmond wrote to. Every Christmas, from 1958 on. *Tell your mother, Lloydie. Ask your mother for me.* He was ten when she brought the children home to the Bay, and he cried on the train all the way from Kingston. Joy was seven and slept. He went back to Kingston at eighteen, to the bank, in King Street. And Desmond was in Kingston then, some years, in and out, and she's always known, without asking, that Lloyd saw him. That Lloyd went and sat in bars with his father, and came home to the Bay at Christmas very quiet.

And in September a lawyer in Duke Street filed a petition that said *Carew v. Carew and Tar*, and it went in the list in the *Gleaner*, the way they do. Lloyd works in a bank in King Street, two streets from Duke Street. Everybody in that bank reads the *Gleaner*.

She'd not thought about it. She'd thought about the house, and the Ministry, and the Board. She'd not once thought about her son at his counter in King Street with the *Gleaner* open on the next man's desk.

She shuts the drawer.

She doesn't tell Reggie. He's at the kitchen table with the graph paper and the third boat plan, and he's so happy he's humming, and he doesn't hum. She stands in the kitchen door and watches him, and doesn't tell him.

---

Joy's decided to dislike him.

She decided it on the plane, somewhere over the Atlantic, with her knees against the seat in front. She'd decided it with great care, the way you'd decide what to say to a consultant who'd written up the wrong drug. She's twenty-three. She's been a staff nurse on the coronary care unit at the Hammersmith for eighteen months. She's seen a great many men of fifty-five with bad hearts and nice manners who thought they knew best. And she's not seen her mother for three years, since Gran's funeral, and her mother's written *I'm to be married* in a letter as though it were the price of eggs. And her mother is the only thing in the whole of Joy's life that was never, ever, not once, silly.

So Joy's going to dislike him, on principle, for as long as it takes to find out whether she has to.

It lasts until about the harbour wall.

The Saturday boat's late, because it always is. When it comes round the head she can see her mother on the harbour wall in the blue with her hand up to shade her eyes. And beside her there's a small grey man in a white shirt with his sleeves rolled, and no hat, holding her mother's basket.

He's holding the basket. Joy watches it all the way in. He holds it the whole time, while her mother waves, and while Alleyne's boy brings the boat alongside. When her mother goes down the steps to meet her, he doesn't give it back. He just carries it down behind her, three steps behind, the way you'd carry a thing for somebody without making a thing of it.

Her mother never let anybody carry anything. Not in twenty-three years.

Joy stands in the boat with her case and looks at him. He looks back. He's got very blue eyes behind his glasses. He's looking at her the way the consultants look at a new ECG: all at once, and then quite still.

"You'll be Joy," he says. "Your mother's told me nothing about you whatsoever, except that you're cleverer than she is and you'll have my notes off me by teatime." He holds out the hand that isn't holding the basket. "Reggie Tar. How was the flight?"

---

She has his notes off him by a quarter past four.

It's not difficult. She sees his hand go to his waistcoat pocket on the stairs up from the harbour, and stop, and come away. She sees it again in the kitchen when he stands up too fast. By the third time she knows exactly what's in the pocket, because she's taken a hundred of those little tins off a hundred men.

She waits till her mother's gone out to the yard. Then she sits down across the kitchen table from him and puts her elbows on it.

"What are you on?"

He looks at her over his tea. He doesn't smile, and he doesn't do anything with his face, which she'd been bracing for. He doesn't say *oh, just a little something*, the way the consultants' private patients do. He puts his cup down.

"Glyceryl trinitrate," he says. "Half a milligram, sublingual, as required. Propranolol, forty milligrams, twice daily. Marguerite cut it from three times in January."

"Who's Marguerite?"

"Dr Sealy. On the hill. She's been my physician since 1970." Something happens at the corner of his mouth that isn't quite a smile. "I'm told that's a joke on the cay. I'll explain it sometime."

"How often do you take the GTN?"

"Twice a week, perhaps. Three times if there's weather and I've been at the pens. Stairs. I take it before, not after. Marguerite's very firm about that."

"Resting pain?"

"No. Not since 1971."

"ECG?"

"Every six months. Marguerite sends them to Kingston to be read. The last was March. There's an old inferior change that doesn't move." He considers. "I've got copies. In the hut. On the cay. I could bring them over on Monday, if you'd like to see them. I'd be interested in what you make of the T waves in III. I've never been satisfied with the Kingston man's report."

Joy stares at him.

She's been a nurse five years. She's never had a patient offer her his own ECGs. She's never had one who knew his own dose to the milligram, and which lead, and wanted her *opinion*.

"Why are you telling me all this?" she says.

He looks at her across her mother's kitchen table. Then he tells her straight.

"Because you asked," he says. "And because she's your mother. You've every right to know what you're getting. I'd want to know, if I were you." He turns his cup round. "And because I'd rather you had my notes than worried. I'm told I'm a worry. I'd not like to be one to you as well."

Joy doesn't say anything for a while.

Then she says, "What's your blood pressure?"

He tells her.

"*Hm*," says Joy, and goes to find Dr Sealy.

---

By the Sunday evening, Joy and Dr Sealy are on Dr Sealy's back verandah with the gin and a pad of paper between them. Joy can hear him, through the open door, saying to Dr Pobil in a low voice, *Pascual, I'm frightened*, and Dr Pobil saying, *Good, hombre. So you should be*.

She's not decided to like him. She's not decided anything. But she's watched him with her mother for a day and a half, and she knows what she's watching.

He looks at her mother the way Joy's only ever seen one other thing looked at. That was a woman on the CCU at the Hammersmith, the night her husband came back after four minutes. She stood at the end of the bed, and she looked at the monitor. Just that. As though it were the only line in the world.

He doesn't make a fuss about it. He just does it. Across the kitchen, over the fish, from the verandah step when her mother's doing the book. All the time. As if he'd forgotten there was anything else to look at.

And her mother knows. Joy can see her know. She can see her mother, who never let anybody carry a basket in twenty-three years, feel him looking and not tell him to stop.

---

"*Mum*," says Joy, at breakfast on the Monday.

Her mother's at the stove. Reggie's at the table with his tea. The girls haven't come in yet.

"Mm?"

"Room six," says Joy, "shares a wall with yours."

Her mother turns round from the stove with the spoon in her hand. Reggie puts his tea down very carefully.

"I'm a nurse," says Joy. "I'm not *shocked*. I've done nights on a men's ward. But I'd be very grateful if somebody moved the chest of drawers." She butters her toast. "It *walks*, Mum. It walks across the floor. At about half past ten."

Her mother goes a colour Joy's never seen on her in her life. Reggie has put his face in his hands. His shoulders are going.

"And he took his tablet on the stairs," says Joy, to the toast. "I heard the tin. So that's something."

---

Lloyd Carew comes off the Monday boat in a dark suit, in June, with a small girl on his hip and his wife beside him holding a hatbox. He shakes Reggie's hand at the top of the harbour steps. He does it correctly and briefly, the way you'd shake hands with a man at the bank who'd come about a loan you weren't going to give him.

"Dr Tar."

"Mr Carew. Welcome home."

"Thank you." Lloyd's already looking past him, at his mother. "Mother."

He's tall. He's got Desmond's height and Desmond's shoulders and none of Desmond's ease at all. He holds himself as though somebody had wound him up very tight at nine that morning and he was waiting to go off. He kisses his mother's cheek and says the house looks well. He says the boat was late. He says Pearl was sick off Port Maria but is better now. He says it all to his mother, and not a word to anybody else. Reggie watches him do it and thinks, *well, he's got the right*.

Then he thinks about the tide.

It's the tide that does it, afterwards. He knows that. He's got the tide on the twenty-second in his head all the time now, high water at 1.14, and the three boats, and whether Jean will let the workboat go if the calf's mother is still in the north pen. He's got the minister's letter from Kingston, which hasn't come. He's got a hundred and sixty people and a hundred and eighteen seats. And Mrs Joseph says the cake has to go across on the Thursday, which means the workboat on the Thursday *and* the Saturday, which means —

So on Monday evening at dinner he talks to Lloyd about the tide.

He doesn't mean to. He means to be civil. He asks Lloyd about the bank, and Lloyd says *very well, thank you*. Then there's a silence, and Reggie fills it, because the tide's the thing in his head. He explains about the flood, and the boats, and bringing the cay across at noon. Lloyd listens with perfect courtesy and says *I see* at the ends of sentences. Reggie thinks it's going quite well.

He talks to him about the tide again on Tuesday at breakfast. And about the cake, and the workboat. He shows him the third boat plan, because Lloyd's in a bank and will appreciate a clear diagram. Lloyd looks at it and says *very thorough*, and gets up to see to Pearl.

Pearl is three. She's small and round and serious, with her hair in two bunches and her father's frown. She has decided, for reasons of her own, that Reggie is hers.

He doesn't know how it happened. On Monday evening she was behind her mother's skirts for all of ten minutes. By Tuesday morning she's on his knee at the kitchen table as a matter of course, while he does the boat plan. She holds his pencil for him and says *what's that* at everything.

"That's the tide," says Reggie.

"What's a tide?"

"It's the sea going up and down. Twice a day. Like breathing."

Pearl considers this. She puts her hand flat on his chest, where the tin is, and waits, and feels him breathe.

"Like *that*?"

"Like that," says Reggie, and doesn't move, and Pearl leaves her hand there for a long time, very seriously, feeling the tide.

He looks up and sees Lloyd in the kitchen doorway, watching. Lloyd's face does something Reggie can't read. Then Lloyd turns round and goes out.

---

"My brother," says Joy, "has not said ten words to you since he got off the boat."

It's Tuesday afternoon. She's found him on the top verandah with the graph paper. She's standing over him with her arms folded exactly like her mother, and it's so exactly like her mother that he nearly laughs. He doesn't.

"He's been perfectly civil."

"He's been *civil*. He's been civil to you the way he's civil to a man who's overdrawn." Joy sits down in Win's chair. "And you've talked to him about high water for two days."

Reggie opens his mouth, and shuts it.

"You showed him a *diagram*," says Joy.

"He's in a bank. I thought he'd —"

"He *is* in a bank." She leans forward. "He's in a bank in King Street, two streets from Duke Street. And in September a man walked in there with the *Gleaner* and opened it at the divorce list, and read out *Carew v. Carew and Tar* to the whole of the back office. And laughed. Did you know that?"

He didn't know that.

"Mum didn't either. I only know because Marcia wrote to me." Joy looks at him steadily. "And then Daddy wrote to him. From Miami. A long letter. About how he'd done what a man had to do, and he was sorry for Lloydie's sake, but he couldn't hold his head up otherwise. Because of *you*. Lloyd showed it me on Monday night. He's got it in his wallet." She pauses. "He's always been Daddy's. Ever since the train. I was seven and I slept all the way, and he was ten and he cried from Kingston to Port Maria. He's never forgiven Mum for the train, not really. And now there's you."

Reggie sits on Win's verandah with the graph paper on his knee.

He's done it again. He knows it the way he knew it on the step with Pascual in August, with the pad full of arrows. He's had the whole of this family arrive in the house, and a man in pain across his dinner table for two days. And he's looked straight through him at the high water, because the tide was the interesting thing, and it was the thing he could do something about. He's so pleased to be marrying her that he's forgotten she comes with people.

"I've been a fool," he says.

"Yes," says Joy, not unkindly. "Mum said you'd say that. She said you're very quick once somebody tells you." She stands up. "So I'm telling you."

---

Win finds him at nine that night in the little office by the fuse board. He's gone there to be out of the way, because Lloyd and Marcia are in the bar with Pearl asleep upstairs. He's sitting at her desk not doing anything, which isn't a thing he does.

She shuts the door behind her and leans on it. She's got something in her hand. It's a letter, or part of one, on bank paper.

"Marcia told me," she says.

He looks up. Her face is grey under the brown.

"Lloyd's been sending money to Des," she says. "Every month. Since 1968. Since *I* stopped." She holds the paper out. "That's the standing order. Marcia found the counterfoil. Forty dollars on the first of every month, to an account in Miami. Out of a bank clerk's wage, Reggie. With Pearl. With the rent in Kingston what it is. Marcia's been taking in sewing." Her voice cracks. "She told me in the kitchen because she didn't know who else to tell. She said, *Mrs Carew, I don't want to make trouble, but I can't make him stop, and you're the only one who ever did.*"

She puts the paper down on the desk. He looks at it. *Standing order. Monthly. First of the month.* In Lloyd's hand, very neat, like his mother's.

"He picked it up," says Win. "The day I put it down. He was twenty. He'll have got the letter I didn't answer. Des'll have written to him instead. And he just — *picked it up*." She puts her hands over her face. "Six years. Six *years*, Reggie. And I never knew. I stopped, and I thought it was done, and I thought I was so *strong*, and all the time my boy was —"

He gets up. He goes round the desk and holds her. She stands against him in the office with her hands over her face and shakes.

"I'll stop it," she says into his shirt. "I'll go in there now and I'll tell him. I'll tell him to stop it. It's not *his*. It was never his to pay —"

"Win."

"— and I'll go to the bank in Kingston myself if I have to, I'll —"

"Win." He holds her by the shoulders, gently, and makes her look at him. "In August. When I sat on your bed and said I'd write Desmond a cheque. What did you tell me?"

She stares at him.

"You said it was yours." He keeps his voice very low. "You said it was your account, and you'd closed it, and nobody was going to open it again behind your back and call it kind. You were right. It was the rightest thing anybody's said to me in years."

"That's not the —"

"It's the same thing. It's his account, Win. He opened it. He's twenty-six and he's got a wife and a child and a job at a bank. If you go in there and close it for him, he's not stopped. He's only been stopped. It's not the same. You know it's not."

She's crying now, properly, without any sound.

"Then what do I *do*?"

"I don't know yet." He takes a breath. "But I've been a fool about him for two days. Joy told me. So I'd like to go and talk to him. If you'll let me." He hesitates. "I'll listen first. I'm told I should."

Win looks at him for a long time.

"What'll you say to him?"

"I don't know," says Reggie. "Something true. I'll think of it when I hear what he says."

---

Lloyd's on the harbour wall on the Wednesday evening because it's the one place in his mother's house where nobody talks about the wedding.

He's sat here since he was ten. He sat here the first week, in 1958, and looked across the channel at the cay and the lights on it and hated all of it, the Bay and the house and the smell of the fishmarket. He hated his mother for bringing them. He hated himself for hating her. There was a light at the far end of the cay, then, by itself, brighter than the rest, and he used to watch it. He used to pretend it was a ship, going to Kingston, going to his father.

It isn't there now. It hasn't been for years. He'd not noticed it go.

He hears somebody come down the harbour steps behind him, not fast, and sit down on the wall about six feet off. He doesn't turn round. He knows the step. He's been hearing it on his mother's stairs for three days.

The man doesn't say anything. Lloyd waits for the tide. The tide doesn't come.

After about five minutes, Lloyd says, without looking round, "Is there something you want, Dr Tar?"

"Yes," says Tar. "I want to say I'm sorry. I've talked to you about high water for two days. It was rude and stupid and I've been told so. You've had a great deal to be angry about and I've looked straight through all of it. I'm told I do that. It's not an excuse." He's quiet a moment. "That's all. I don't want anything else. I'll go, if you'd rather."

Lloyd turns his head and looks at him.

He's small, the doctor. Smaller than Lloyd had thought. He's sitting on the harbour wall in his shirtsleeves with his hands between his knees. He isn't smiling. Lloyd's been waiting three days for him to smile, the way his father smiles. The way he knows men like this smile at the bank, when they want a loan. He's been waiting to catch it.

He hasn't once. The man's face is just a face.

"Joy told you," Lloyd says.

"Joy told me. About the *Gleaner*. And the man in your back office." Tar looks at the water. "I'd not known. I'm sorry for that too. I should have thought. I didn't."

Lloyd doesn't answer.

"And your father's letter," says Tar. "I'd guessed at that. I didn't know it was in your wallet."

Lloyd feels his hand go to his jacket, where the wallet is, before he can stop it. Tar sees it. He doesn't say anything. He puts his own hand flat on his chest, on the left, briefly, where the tin is, and takes it away again. It's so like the thing Lloyd just did that Lloyd stares at him.

"He wrote to you as the wronged husband," says Tar. "The man who had to do what a man has to do. Hold his head up. Yes?"

"Yes."

"May I tell you something about that? You'll not like it. And I'll understand if you'd rather I didn't."

Lloyd looks at him for a long time. Out on the water there's a fisherman coming in late past the head, steering on something. Lloyd can't see what.

"Tell me," he says.

---

Tar tells him.

He tells it plainly, in order, as though he were reading a result. He tells him about August. His father on the verandah wanting eight hundred dollars, and a lawyer in Kingston, and a man in Miami. His father going across to the cay to see the Director, and the Director giving him coconut cake. His mother saying *no*. And then the Sunday night, in the office, with his mother at the desk and a cheque written out to *D. Carew* for eight hundred dollars, and no signature.

"She didn't sign it?"

"She didn't sign it. It's in the office drawer. She keeps it there." Tar turns his head. "She'd been sitting there an hour. She told me she wanted to see what it looked like, written down."

Lloyd feels something go in his chest.

And then Tar tells him about the kitchen, at ten at night. His mother saying *if he petitions, I'm free*. And the rest of it. How it was done, and who did it. The voice, and the hand to the pocket, and the damages that were never going to be anything. His father at the kitchen table writing *T-A-R* with his mother's pen while she made him an omelette. His mother saying *please don't*, because it was the one line he'd never stop for.

Lloyd sits on the harbour wall and listens to all of it, and doesn't say anything. When it's done he sits for a long time and looks at the water.

"You conned him," he says at last.

"Your mother did. I helped."

"You let him *win*."

"She let him win," says Tar. "It was her idea. She said he'd not had a winning hand in fifteen years, and he'd not stop without one. She gave him that. So he could go back to Miami with his head up." He's quiet. "I'd have paid him off. I'd have written him a cheque and put him on the boat, and he'd have been back in a year. She wouldn't have it. She said it was her account."

Lloyd puts his face in his hands.

He doesn't know what he's feeling. He's so angry he can't see. He's angry with all of them: with his mother, with this little grey man, with his father most of all. Because he can see it. He can see the whole thing. His father at the kitchen table, delighted, glowing, writing the letter. Thinking he'd won. Writing to Lloyd from Miami, *I did what a man has to do*. And not knowing, even now, that he'd been handed it by the only woman who ever paid for him. Handed it like the lunch on the boat.

And under the anger, there's something else. It comes up in him slowly. He doesn't recognise it until it's in his throat.

He's laughing. He's sitting on the harbour wall with his face in his hands, and he's laughing, and it hurts like being sick.

"*T-A-R*," he says. "He spelt it. In the letter to me. He spelt your *name*."

"He spelt it to us too," says Tar. "At the kitchen table. With great satisfaction."

Lloyd laughs until he's crying. He doesn't know which it is by the end. The doctor sits six feet off on the wall and doesn't come any closer, and doesn't say anything, and lets him.

---

It's dark when Lloyd stops. The fisherman's in. The harbour's black and flat, with the lights of the Bay on it, and the cay across the channel, dark at the far end.

"Mother knows," says Lloyd. "About the money. Doesn't she. Marcia told her."

"Yes."

"Is that why you came down here? To tell me to stop?"

"No," says Tar.

Lloyd turns his head.

"It's not mine to tell you," says Tar. "It's not your mother's either, though she'd like to. She'd like to go into that bar and close your account for you with her own hands. I asked her not to." He's looking at the water. "Because in August I wanted to close hers, and she told me it was hers, and she was right. It's yours, Mr Carew. You opened it. You've paid into it for six years. I'd not presume."

"Then what did you come for?"

Tar's quiet for a long moment.

"I kept a letter in my pocket," he says. "From 1970. From somebody I'd loved for twenty-five years and given up. The letter asked to see me, and I couldn't answer it, and I couldn't not. I carried it in this pocket, here, for the best part of a year. Till the fold went soft." He touches his chest. "And then there was a death, and it was too late to do either."

Lloyd doesn't say anything. He's not sure he understood. He's not sure he was meant to understand all of it. But he understood the pocket.

"Your mother had one too," says Tar. "In her apron. In 1968. Your father's letter asking for money. She carried it a month, and didn't answer it, and she's told me it's the hardest thing she's ever done. She still loves him. She'll tell you so. She stopped paying, that's all. Those aren't the same."

"I know they're not the same."

"I know you know. I only wanted to say it." Tar looks at him. "I came down here to tell you that you *can*. That's all. Not that you should. You can stop, if you want to. And if you did, it wouldn't mean you'd stopped loving him. And it wouldn't mean you'd turned into anything you'd be ashamed of. It'd only mean you'd stopped paying." He spreads his hands. "And if you don't want to, then don't. It's yours. But I think nobody's ever told you that you could. I think you picked it up the day your mother put it down, at twenty, because somebody had to. And nobody's ever said to you that it's allowed to be put down."

Lloyd sits on the harbour wall.

He thinks about the counterfoil. Forty dollars, the first of every month, in his own hand. Marcia at the sewing machine at eleven at night, not saying anything. His father's letters, every Christmas since 1958. *Tell your mother, Lloydie.* And then, from 1968, *Lloydie, you're a good boy. You're the only one I can count on.*

Nobody's ever said. That's true. He's never once thought of it as a thing that *could* be put down. It was just his, the way his hands were.

"If I stop," he says slowly, "who'll —"

"Nobody," says Tar gently. "That's what stopping is."

Lloyd doesn't answer that. He can't. He sits with it.

After a while the doctor gets up, carefully, with a hand on the wall. He stands for a moment looking down at the water.

"She'd like you to walk her up the aisle," he says. "On Saturday. She's not asked you, because she thinks you'll say no. And she'll not be told otherwise by me." He puts his hands in his pockets. "I thought you ought to know. That's all. It's yours to decide as well."

He goes up the harbour steps. Not fast.

---

Lloyd sits on the wall till the church clock strikes ten.

Then he goes up to the house. His mother is in the kitchen with the day book, at the table, under the lamp, the way she's been every night of his life. She looks up when he comes in. Her face is tired, and her eyes are red, and she's trying to smile at him and can't quite.

He stands in the kitchen doorway in his dark suit.

"Mother," he says. And then, because it's nearly twenty years since he called her anything else, and he's twenty-six, and he's been crying on a harbour wall, he says, "*Mum*."

She puts the pencil down.

"Would you like me," says Lloyd, "to walk you up? On Saturday. If you haven't — if there isn't anybody else."

His mother looks at him across the kitchen for a very long time.

"There isn't anybody else," says Win Carew. "There was never going to be anybody else, Lloyd. I just didn't like to ask."

He comes and sits down at the table across from her. She reaches over and takes his hand. They sit there together under the lamp, not saying anything, with the day book open between them.

He doesn't tell her about the counterfoil. He doesn't know yet what he'll do about it. He doesn't know if he'll do anything.

But he knows now that it's his. That he could. And he finds, sitting at his mother's kitchen table with her hand in his, that the knowing weighs less than the paying ever did.
