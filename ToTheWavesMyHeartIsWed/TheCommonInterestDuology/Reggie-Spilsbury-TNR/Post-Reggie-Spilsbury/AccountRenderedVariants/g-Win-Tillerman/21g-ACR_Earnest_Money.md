# Earnest Money

Marcia is sick off Port Maria.

Nobody told her about the swell off the point. She's never been on a boat in her life except the ferry to Port Royal, once, at a Sunday school treat, when she was nine. So she sits in the stern of the Bay boat in her good grey costume with her handbag on her knee, holding herself very straight, because she's eighteen and going to see her future mother-in-law. And at the point the boat goes up and sideways and down, and she leans over the side and is sick into the Caribbean Sea in front of a woman with chickens.

The woman with chickens gives her a piece of ginger out of her apron without a word. Marcia chews it. She sits back up, and wipes her mouth with the handkerchief her mother ironed this morning, and goes on holding herself straight. She thinks she'll be sick again, and she is, twice more, before the harbour.

She's not told anybody she's coming. Not her father, who said no. Not her mother, who'd have said *Marcia Elizabeth* in the voice and then packed her a lunch. Not Lloyd, who'd have said *don't*, very quietly, and looked at the floor. She left a note on the kitchen table in the manse that said *Gone to the Bay. Back tomorrow. Don't fret. M.* and walked to the bus at six with eleven shillings in her purse.

It's the first thing she's ever done in her life without asking.

---

Harbour House is at the top of the steps, white, with a verandah all round the top floor and a sign by the door that says *Rooms*. She stands outside it for a long time, being sure she won't be sick on the step. Then she goes in.

There's a hall with a table and a jug of flowers on it, and a smell of starch and fish. There's a little office off it by the fuse board, with the door open. And at the desk in the office there's a woman doing a column of figures with a pencil.

She doesn't look up. She goes down the column, not fast, not slow, touching each figure. At the bottom she writes a number. Then she goes back up it from the bottom, and puts a tick beside it, very small.

Then she looks up.

She's tall, and brown, and straight-backed. Forty-two or -three, Marcia thinks; Lloyd's never said, Lloyd never says anything about his mother except that she keeps a house at the Bay. She has a plain dark dress on with an apron over it and her hair up very neat. And she has Lloyd's face. Not his frown; Lloyd's frown is his own. But the shape of it. The way it waits before it does anything.

"Yes?" she says. "Can I help you?"

"Mrs Carew," says Marcia. Her voice comes out very high. She brings it down. "I'm Marcia Hinds. My father's the minister at Constant Spring Road. Your son keeps our church accounts." She swallows. "I'm going to marry him."

Mrs Carew puts the pencil down.

She looks at Marcia for a long time. She looks at her the way she looked at the column: down, and then up.

"Are you," she says. "Does he know?"

"He's asked me. In January." Marcia holds her handbag in front of her with both hands. "My father's said no."

"Has he. Why?"

Marcia had thought she'd lie about that. She'd practised a lie on the boat, between being sick, about Lloyd being young and the bank paying very little. She finds she can't do it with this woman looking at her.

"He says Lloyd's a bank clerk," she says, "with a father who owes half of Kingston."

Mrs Carew doesn't move. Her face doesn't change. But her hand goes to the pocket of her apron, on the right, and rests there flat, over something, as though it were a thing that might get up and go.

"Not half," she says, after a moment. "A third, perhaps. On a good year." She stands up. "You've been sick off Port Maria."

"How did you —"

"You're the colour of the wall, child. Everybody is, the first time." She comes round the desk. "Come into the kitchen. You'll have some soup, and some more ginger, and then you'll tell me about my son. He doesn't write."

---

There's an old woman in the kitchen, at an ironing board, doing sheets. She's small and very dark, with her hair tied up in a cloth, and she looks at Marcia once over the iron and goes on ironing.

"My mother," says Mrs Carew. "Mrs Greaves. Mother, this is Miss Hinds, from Kingston. She's going to marry Lloyd."

"Is she," says Mrs Greaves to the sheet. "Good."

That's all she says, for the whole of the next hour. But she says it in such a way that Marcia sits down at the kitchen table feeling as though she'd been handed something.

Mrs Carew gives her soup. It's fish, and very good, and she's so hungry she has to stop herself bolting it. Then Mrs Carew sits down across from her with her own cup of tea and asks her about Lloyd.

She asks the way she did the column. Not fast, not slow. Every figure.

How long has Marcia known him? Two years. Since he came to the church in 1966 and the old treasurer died and her father asked if anybody in the congregation could keep a ledger, and Lloyd stood up. Is he well? He's thin. He doesn't eat lunch; he says he forgets. Does he have friends at the bank? He plays cricket on Saturdays, for the bank's second eleven. He bowls. He's very good. He doesn't tell anybody he's good.

"No," says Mrs Carew. "He wouldn't."

Does he go to see his father?

Marcia stops.

"Sometimes," she says. "When Mr Carew's in Kingston. They go to a bar on Harbour Street. Lloyd comes back very quiet." She looks at her soup. "He doesn't talk about it."

Mrs Carew's hand is on the apron pocket again. She doesn't seem to know it is.

"And does he laugh?" she says. "Lloyd. With you."

Marcia thinks about it. She thinks about Lloyd at her father's kitchen table on Thursday nights, with the church ledger, writing in very small careful figures, never once crossing anything out. She thinks about him on the bench outside the church after evensong, holding her hand as though somebody might come and take it off him. She thinks about how many times she's heard him laugh in two years.

"Not much," she says.

Mrs Carew nods slowly.

"He didn't," she says. "Not after the train. He was ten. He cried from Kingston to Port Maria and he's not laughed much since." She turns her cup round. "I've always thought somebody would come along and make him. I'd hoped it'd be somebody with sense."

At the ironing board Mrs Greaves makes a small noise, and turns the sheet.

---

She sleeps in a room at the back called seven, which is small and clean and doesn't leak. In the morning Mrs Carew gives her breakfast and walks her down the harbour steps to the boat, and on the bottom step she gives her an envelope.

It's addressed to *The Revd. Hinds, the Manse, Constant Spring Road*, in a small neat hand.

"For your father," says Mrs Carew. "You'll not open it."

"What does it say?"

"It says what I think of my son." Mrs Carew stands on the bottom step with her arms folded. "And what I think of you, which is that you came on the Port Maria boat on your own and were sick three times and didn't go home. And that if he says no after that, he's a bigger fool than his collar." She looks at Marcia. "I've not met your father. I'm told he's a good man. Good men can be told things."

Marcia holds the envelope against her chest.

"Mrs Carew. Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Mind Lloyd." She says it quite abruptly, looking past Marcia at the boat. "He'll pay for everybody, my Lloyd. He'd sooner go without lunch than owe a man a penny, and he'll pay for things that were never his to pay for. He always has. Since he was ten." Her hand's on the apron pocket. "Mind he doesn't."

Marcia doesn't understand her. She thinks it's a thing mothers say.

She gets into the boat. The woman with chickens is there again, going back. Marcia sits in the stern and holds the letter and is sick off Port Maria, twice, and doesn't care.

She'll remember that apron pocket for four years before she knows what was in it. And then for another six before she can forgive anybody for it, including herself.

---

Her father reads the letter at his desk in the study, with the door open, so she can see him from the hall.

He's a very tall man and a very black one, and he has a voice like a cello, and at fifty-nine he's been the minister at Constant Spring Road for twenty-two years. People who are frightened of him say he's hard. People who aren't say he's fair. Marcia's never been able to decide which she is.

He reads it twice. He doesn't say anything. He folds it in three, the way it came, and puts it in the drawer of his desk where he keeps things he means to pray about. Then he takes off his reading glasses and looks at her across the hall.

"You went on the Port Maria boat," he says. "On your own."

"Yes, Daddy."

"Were you sick?"

"Three times. And twice coming back."

Her father looks at her for a long moment. Then something happens to his face that she's not seen on it often. It's very nearly a smile.

"Your mother was sick on the Montego Bay train," he says, "in 1937, when she came to tell my father she'd marry me. All the way from Spanish Town." He puts his glasses back on. "Tell the boy he may come to Sunday dinner. And bring the ledger. I want to see the quarter's figures before I see anything else."

She never finds out what was in the letter. She asks her mother, once. Her mother says, *that's your father's and Mrs Carew's*, and goes on peeling.

It's not the last time somebody tells her a thing is somebody else's. It's only the first.

---

They're married in April 1969, in her father's church, by her father, with her mother in the front pew in a hat with cherries on it and Mrs Carew on the other side in navy. Joy's the bridesmaid. She's eighteen and has never been a bridesmaid in her life and cries from the first hymn to the last, and holds Marcia's train as though it were made of glass, and Marcia loves her from that day on.

Mr Carew is three hours late.

He comes into the church hall in the middle of the wedding breakfast, in a cream suit, with his hat in his hand and a box of cigars for her father, which her father doesn't smoke. He kisses Mrs Carew on the cheek, and Mrs Carew stands like a post. He shakes Lloyd's hand in both of his and says *my boy, my boy*. He kisses Marcia, and holds her at arm's length, and says she's the prettiest bride in Kingston, and asks where Lloydie found her. And before she can tell him, he's turned round to her mother and asked her to dance.

Her mother says she doesn't. Mr Carew says *nonsense*, and in four minutes Lucille Hinds, who is fifty-six and a minister's wife, is laughing so hard on the floor of the church hall that she has to sit down.

Then he plays the piano. The old upright in the corner, under its cloth. He takes the cloth off as though he were unveiling a statue, and says *still flat at the top, bless it*, though he can't have played it before. He plays for an hour without being asked. The whole hall gets up off its chairs. Even her father taps his foot.

Marcia, on Lloyd's arm, in her mother's veil, thinks: *oh, that's where Lloyd gets it from*. And then she thinks: *no. Lloyd hasn't got any of it*. And she's glad, and doesn't know why.

She looks round for Mrs Carew to see what she thinks of it, and Mrs Carew isn't there.

She finds her on the church steps outside, alone, in the navy, with her back to the door. She's standing very straight with her arms folded, looking at the traffic on Constant Spring Road. The piano's coming through the door behind her, faint. Something slow.

"Mrs Carew? Are you all right?"

"Quite all right." She doesn't turn round. "Go back in. It's your wedding."

"Don't you like the music?"

Mrs Carew's quiet for a moment.

"I like it very much," she says. "That's the trouble." And then, in a different voice, the brisk one: "Go back in, Marcia. Lloyd'll be looking for you. He doesn't like rooms where he can't see the door."

Marcia goes back in. She doesn't understand that either. She puts it with the apron pocket.

---

Her mother finds her in the vestry at seven, when she's changing out of the dress. She sits down on the bench by the robes cupboard with her hat in her lap, the cherries all crooked from the dancing, and watches Marcia unpick the hooks.

"Well," says her mother. "That's a charming man."

"Lloyd?"

"Lloyd's a good man. That's different." Her mother turns the hat round. "His father's charming."

"You danced with him."

"I did. He's the best dancer I've had since your father, and your father hasn't danced since 1952." She smooths one of the cherries. "I enjoyed it very much. And I'll tell you a thing about him, Marcia, and then I'll not say any more, because it's your wedding day."

Marcia stops with her hands at the back of her neck.

"I've been a minister's wife thirty-one years," says her mother. "You learn to count. Not heads. Anybody can count heads. You count who's still there at supper." She looks at the hat. "The ones who come for the service, and the ones who come for the dancing, and the ones who stay after for the washing-up. You learn very quickly which is which. You learn you can love all three. But you only lean on the third."

"Mr Carew was three hours late."

"He was three hours late, and he played for an hour, and he was gone before the cake was cut." Her mother gets up and comes and does the last hooks for her. Her hands are warm and quick. "He was never going to be there at supper, that one. Not at anybody's. I'd have known that across the hall without being told." She kisses the back of Marcia's neck, where the hooks were. "Lloyd stayed and stacked the chairs. He's out there stacking them now, in his wedding suit. I watched him."

Marcia turns round.

"That's the one," says her mother, "you lean on."

---

They take a flat in Barbican, the first floor, over a widow called Mrs Chin who has a telephone and lets you use it for ten cents in the tin. It's three rooms and a balcony and a kitchen like a cupboard. Marcia makes curtains for every window out of a bolt of blue cotton from the haberdasher's on Constant Spring Road, French seams, the way her Aunt Merle taught her when she was eleven. She stands in the front room when they're up and looks at them, and Lloyd comes home from the bank and stands beside her and looks at them too.

"You made those," he says.

"I made those."

He doesn't say anything else. But she sees his face, and it's the nearest thing she's seen in two years to the face he had when her father said *bring the ledger*.

---

Mr Carew comes in the autumn of 1970. She's six months gone, and her ankles are like bread.

She's told about it, after, in 1974, in a kitchen at the Bay, and she says *the pram money*, and it sounds like nothing. It's not nothing. It's a Thursday evening, and he sits at her kitchen table with his hat on his knee, telling Lloyd about a man in Miami. He tells it beautifully. He's very funny about the man in Miami. She laughs, twice, before she can stop herself.

And at the end of it, Lloyd gets up and goes into the bedroom. He comes back with the envelope from the tin in the wardrobe, the one with *PRAM* on it in her writing, and gives it to his father.

He doesn't look at her while he does it. That's what she remembers. He gives his father the envelope with his eyes on the table, and his father puts it in his inside pocket without counting it. Then his father kisses her on the cheek and tells her she's blooming, and goes.

She doesn't say anything. She's twenty-one. She's been married a year and a half. She's frightened of Mr Carew, a little, and more frightened of Lloyd's face. She tells herself it's once.

Her mother finds her a pram from a family in the congregation whose last is walking. It's navy, with a dent in the hood. Marcia never tells her why they need one.

---

Pearl comes in the January, in the University Hospital, at four in the morning, after nineteen hours. She comes out furious, with her fists up by her ears and Lloyd's frown already on her, before she's even been washed.

Her mother holds her on the second day. She sits in the chair by the bed in the ward in her church hat, with Pearl in her arms, and looks at her for a long time without saying anything. Then she says, "She's going to *decide* things, this one."

"How can you tell?"

"Look at her," says her mother. "She's deciding now."

Pearl's asleep. But Marcia looks, and it's true. Even asleep she looks like somebody who has views.

Her mother dies in the May.

It's on a Tuesday, at the church, in the middle of the afternoon, in the vestry, doing the flowers for Whitsun. Aunt Merle finds her. She's sitting on the bench by the robes cupboard, where she sat on Marcia's wedding day, with a lily in her lap. The doctor says it was her heart and she'd not have known. Marcia doesn't believe that. She thinks her mother would have known. She thinks her mother would have counted.

She's twenty-one, with a baby of four months, and she can't stop crying for three weeks. She cries feeding Pearl. She cries at the machine. She cries in the haberdasher's on Constant Spring Road when the woman asks after her mother, not having heard.

Lloyd does everything.

She doesn't know, afterwards, how. He's twenty-three. He goes to the bank at eight and comes home at six, and in between, somehow, he sees to all of it. The undertaker and the hymns and the notice in the *Gleaner*. The flowers, and the people who have to be told, and the people who have to be fed after. He does her father's sums for it, at her father's kitchen table, at night, in the little careful figures. He brings her tea in bed at six in the morning and takes Pearl so she can sleep. When she wakes at ten Pearl is fed and changed and asleep in the drawer, and Lloyd is gone to the bank, and there's a note on the pillow in his small hand that says only *Ate lunch. L.*

She cries at that, too. But differently.

After the funeral, Aunt Merle brings her mother's sewing box to the flat, and a length of good grey worsted wrapped in tissue.

"She'd put it by," says Aunt Merle. "For a costume for you. For Pearl's christening. She'd not got round to it." She puts it on the table. "You'll make something for Lloyd out of it instead. It's men's weight. She bought it wrong, God rest her, she always bought cloth wrong. I've told her so for forty years."

Marcia makes Lloyd a suit out of it the next year, when his cousin dies and he's nothing dark to wear. It's the first suit she's ever made. She puts her whole self into it. She does the buttonholes by hand, fourteen of them, and it takes her a week. He wears it to the funeral, and then to the bank on days that matter, and then for years.

She makes Pearl's christening dress out of the tissue paper's worth of white lawn that was underneath. She does French seams on it so small you'd need a glass.

---

She finds the counterfoil in 1972, when Pearl needs shoes.

She's told about this as well, later, at length. She knows how it sounds. A woman sits on the edge of a bed with a slip of paper. *Standing order. Monthly. First of the month. Forty dollars. First Federal, Miami.* Set up in October 1968.

October 1968. Six months after the doorstep at the Bay.

She sits with it, and she thinks about Mrs Carew on the bottom step of the harbour with her hand flat on her apron pocket. *He'll pay for everybody, my Lloyd. Mind he doesn't.*

She knew. Marcia sits on the bed in Barbican with the counterfoil and thinks it in so many words. *She knew he was like that. She told me so herself, on the steps. And then she went back up the steps to her ledger and never looked round again.*

She doesn't know yet what was in the apron pocket. She'll not know for two more years, until a kitchen at the Bay, until Win says *I carried his letter a month in my apron, in 1968, and didn't answer it, and it's the hardest thing I ever did*. And Marcia will stand at the stove and count back on her fingers to March, and the woman at the desk with her hand going to her pocket every time somebody said *Desmond*. And she'll understand that the day she first walked into Harbour House, her mother-in-law was in the middle of the hardest thing she ever did. She was carrying it in her apron, and still gave a frightened girl soup and a letter and a room that didn't leak.

But that's 1974. This is 1972. In 1972 she only has the counterfoil.

She puts it back where she found it. She gets the machine out of the cupboard. She writes a card for the haberdasher's window on Constant Spring Road, in her best hand.

*Alterations. Dressmaking. Reasonable. Apply within.*

---

The haberdasher's is two hundred yards from her father's church. She doesn't think about that till afterwards.

The work comes slowly at first. Hems, mostly. A woman from Half Way Tree with six pairs of her husband's trousers to take up. A girl from the University who wants a skirt let out, red to the ears, saying it must have shrunk. Marcia does them at night, three nights a week, after Pearl's down, and Lloyd sits at the table with her father's ledger, and the standing order sits between them like a third person.

Then, in the spring, the brides start coming.

The first is a girl called Dorothy Pinnock, who sings alto in her father's choir. She comes up Mrs Chin's stairs on a Saturday morning with a bolt of white satin in her arms and says Mrs Beckford said Marcia does beautiful work, and could she possibly make a wedding dress by June.

Then a girl called Grace from the youth fellowship. Then Mrs Beckford's own niece. Then a woman of forty marrying for the second time, who wants something *not white, dear, not at my age, but not mourning either*. Every one of them from Constant Spring Road. Every one of them saying somebody said.

By the autumn Marcia's doing two wedding dresses a month and turning work away. She's put the price up twice, a shilling at a time, very carefully, watching whether they'll stand it. They stand it. Some months it pays the half of the rent Lloyd's forty dollars would have paid, and the shoes, and the gas, and something over.

It's Aunt Merle who lets it out. She doesn't mean to. She's in the flat on a Thursday, holding Pearl on her knee while Marcia pins a hem on Dorothy Pinnock's sister, and she says, very comfortably, to nobody, "Well, he'd announced it from the pulpit, if he could. Every bride on the road. *I've a daughter who sews, and I'll not have any girl in this congregation married in a shop dress.*"

Marcia stops with a pin halfway in.

"Who?"

Aunt Merle looks up. She sees Marcia's face and goes a colour.

"Nobody," she says. "I'm an old woman. I talk."

Marcia doesn't ask her father. She thinks about it for a week, at the machine, and then she understands she's not going to. He walks past the haberdasher's window every morning on his way to the church. He'll have seen the card in her handwriting in February. He'll have stood in front of it in his collar and read *Reasonable*. And he'll have gone up the road to the vestry and said nothing whatever to her, and sent her every bride in his church.

That's her father. He doesn't ask, and he doesn't say. He sees, and he does something about it from round the side, where you can't thank him.

She doesn't thank him. She can't; he's never told her. But on Sunday after evensong she brings him the new cassock she's made him, unasked, because the old one's gone at the cuffs. He holds it up to the light in the vestry, and turns it inside out, and looks at the seams for a long time.

"French seams," he says.

"Aunt Merle taught me."

"I know who taught you." He folds it over his arm. "Your mother never could do them. She tried for forty years. She'd have been very proud." He looks at her over his glasses. "Is the boy all right? For money?"

And Marcia, who has never lied to her father in her life, looks at him in the vestry and says, "Yes, Daddy. We're quite all right."

He looks at her a long moment. Then he says, "Good," and puts the cassock on the hook, and that's all.

She thinks about it all the way home on the bus. She thinks: *we're a family that doesn't say*. Her father doesn't say. She doesn't say. Lloyd doesn't say. The standing order goes out between them on the first of every month and nobody says. And somewhere at the Bay there's a woman with a ledger who doesn't say either, and who sends Pearl a dress every birthday with French seams so fine they make Marcia feel four years old.

She's twenty-three. She wonders if anybody in the world just *says*.

---

Two years later, a small grey Englishman with a bad heart sits on a harbour wall and says it.

She's told that part, too. She tells it herself, sometimes, to Joy, on the thin blue paper. *He said Lloyd could stop. He didn't say he should. He said it was his.* She's never been able to tell it so it sounds like what it was.

What it was, she thinks afterwards, was a man who didn't know the rules. Who'd never been in her family or Lloyd's, or anybody's, and didn't know you weren't supposed to say. So he just went down the harbour steps and sat six feet off and said it, as though it were a figure.

And she found out, sitting up with his shirts till midnight on the Friday before the wedding, that she'd been waiting her whole life for somebody to.

---

After the wedding, they go home, and Lloyd cuts it to twenty.

She watches him do it. Not the form; he does that at the bank. She watches him come home the evening he's done it, in July, and put his briefcase down, and stand in the front room looking at the blue curtains. Then he says, "Twenty. From the first of August," and goes into the kitchen and puts the kettle on.

That's all. She doesn't say anything either. She still gets the machine out after Pearl's down; there are the brides, and twenty dollars is still twenty dollars. But she puts the cloth back over it at half past nine, not eleven. And on the Thursday she does Dorothy Pinnock's cousin's hem at three in the afternoon, with the windows open and Pearl asleep in the heat. It's the strangest thing. It's the same hem. It's the same needle and the same thread and the same French seam. And it's quite different, doing it in the daylight.

She'd not known that till she did it. She'd thought it was the work she minded. It wasn't. It was the hour.

Lloyd starts sleeping. She notices that in September. He's always slept like a man on a raft, on his back, with his hands at his sides, waking at three. In September she wakes at three and he's still asleep, on his side, with one arm over her. She lies very still so as not to wake him, and doesn't sleep herself for an hour, out of sheer surprise.

---

The first letter from Reggie comes in the August after the wedding. It's addressed to *Miss Pearl Carew*, who is three and a half and can't read, and it's two pages with a drawing at the bottom of the calf in her sling, with an arrow.

Marcia reads it to her at the kitchen table. Pearl makes her read it four times, and then takes the drawing to bed.

They come every few weeks after that, and then every week, as soon as Pearl can write back. Marcia reads every one aloud until Pearl's seven and can do it herself. She learns the hand. It's small and very neat and leans slightly forward, like a man walking into a wind. She learns that he writes the way he talks, in a straight line, and that when he's not sure of something he says so. She learns that the drawings are better than they need to be. She learns that there's nearly always a postscript.

She learns, quite quickly, that some of the postscripts aren't for Pearl.

*P.S. Your mother says you were sick off Port Maria again. Tell her I've written to the Kingston boat about it, and they've not answered. I'll try the Minister of Transport.*

*P.S. Ask your father whether the second eleven are playing at Sabina this year. I'd like to know how he bowls. Your grandmother says he was very good at twelve and has never told anybody since.*

*P.S. Your mother's hem on your blue dress is the best piece of work I've seen since I left England. I can't sew a button. I've tried. Tell her that.*

Marcia reads that one out before she's seen what it says, and stops. Pearl, who is five and can't read the small hand yet, looks up from her drawing and says, "*Is* it?"

"Is what, darling?"

"Your hem. Is it the best piece of work."

Marcia stands at the stove and doesn't know what to say.

"It's a good hem," she says.

"He says it's the *best*." Pearl looks back at the letter with great severity. "He doesn't say *best* about anything. He says the calf's *a good calf*. He says *it's a very good cistern*." She considers. "So it must be."

Marcia turns round to the stove so Pearl won't see her face.

That night she looks at Pearl's blue dress, which is on the back of Pearl's door, for church. She takes it down and turns it inside out under the lamp and looks at the hem. It's a good hem. It's an ordinary good hem. And she thinks about a man on a verandah at the Bay, with his glasses pushed up, looking at it in August. She thinks about him turning it inside out the way she would, because he'd want to know how it was done. And then sitting down to write to a child of five, and deciding Marcia ought to be told.

Nobody's ever told her that about a hem. Not her mother, who couldn't do them. Not Aunt Merle, who could and thought praise was bad for the hands. Not Lloyd, who says *you made those* and means everything by it and has never once in his life said a single word more.

She folds the dress and hangs it back on the door.

---

He doesn't stay in the flat. Not at Christmas, not ever. In September 1976 he writes to Lloyd, at the bank, and asks him how big the rooms are.

Lloyd reads the letter at the kitchen table, and frowns at it, and reads it again. Then he gets up, without a word. He takes his shoes off. And he measures the front room, heel to toe, in his socks, down the long wall and across the short one, counting under his breath.

Marcia stands in the kitchen door and watches her husband walk heel to toe across the front room in his socks. She has to hold on to the door frame.

"What are you *doing*?"

"He's asked." Lloyd doesn't look up. "Eleven and a half. He wants it in feet. He says mine are eleven inches, he'd guess, from the look of me, and I'm to check." He reaches the wall. "Thirteen and a bit. Marce, have we got a tape?"

"In the machine drawer."

He gets the tape. He measures his own foot, sitting on the floor of the front room, very seriously. It's eleven inches and a quarter. He writes it on the back of the letter. He looks up at her, and his face is doing something she's not seen it do in seven years of marriage. It's trying not to laugh, and failing.

"He was a quarter of an inch out," says Lloyd. "From *Port Maria*."

Then he does laugh. He sits on the floor of the front room with the tape in his hand, and laughs till Pearl comes out of her room in her nightdress to see.

Reggie writes back that there isn't room for two more grown people without somebody on the floor. He says he'll not have Lloyd on the floor in his own house at Christmas, and he's taken a hotel in New Kingston with a lift, and he hopes they'll forgive him. Pearl is furious. She measures the flat herself, with her own feet, six and a half inches, and is furious for a week.

Marcia's not furious.

She's in the kitchen, peeling, when she understands it. She's thinking about her own father, and the brides, and the card in the haberdasher's window. She's thinking: *he did that from round the side. He made Lloyd measure it so it'd be Lloyd's figures, Lloyd's flat, Lloyd's say. He could have just booked the hotel. He made it so it was ours.*

She puts the knife down and laughs, the way she does when something hurts in a way she likes. Pearl asks why, and she tells her about Daddy's feet, and doesn't tell her the rest. Pearl's five. It's not hers yet.

---

It's a bad Christmas in Kingston, 1976. Everybody knows it, and nobody says it in front of the children.

There's been a state of emergency since June. There've been shootings since before that, in Trench Town and Tivoli and, in the autumn, once, at the bottom of Constant Spring Road. There are soldiers at the corner by the post office some nights. Her father's been preaching on Isaiah since September, *they shall beat their swords into ploughshares*, to a church that gets thinner every month as people stop coming out after dark. He's buried two boys from the youth fellowship since Easter. He doesn't talk about them at home. He just sits in his study with the door open and doesn't read.

Lloyd tells Win on the telephone from Mrs Chin's that perhaps they shouldn't come this year. Marcia hears him. He does it very carefully, without saying *guns*, because Win doesn't like the telephone and Mrs Chin is listening.

And the next week there's a letter from the Bay, in the small neat hand, addressed to Lloyd. One page.

*Dear Lloyd. Thank you. We'll come. We'll come on the boat and the bus by daylight, and we'll be in the hotel by dark, and we'll not go anywhere after dark that you don't take us. Your mother says she has not missed a Christmas with her grandchild yet and does not mean to start for the Prime Minister. I'm inclined to agree with her. I generally am. R.T.*

Lloyd reads it, and passes it to Marcia without a word, and goes out on to the balcony and stands there for a while.

They come. They come on the boat and the bus by daylight. And on Christmas Eve, Reggie sits in the aisle seat in her father's church, two-thirds empty, with his leg stretched out, and Pearl sings the solo.

---

Her father meets him in the vestry, before.

Marcia's there because she's doing Pearl's sash, which has come undone, and Pearl's standing on a chair being very still. Her father's in his robes at the table, looking over the order of service. And Reggie comes to the vestry door with his hat in his hand, and stops there, waiting to be asked in, the way he waits at every door.

They've not met. Her father wrote to him once, in 1974, about the wedding, minister to bridegroom, and had four pages back that he's never shown anybody. But they've not met.

Her father looks up. He takes his glasses off. He stands up, which he does for bishops, and holds out his hand across the table.

"Dr Tar."

"Reverend Hinds."

They look at each other. Her father's six inches taller and fifty pounds heavier, and very black, in his robes. Reggie's small and white and thin, in a grey suit that wants pressing. Marcia sees her father look him over, down and then up, the way he looks over a new man in the congregation.

"You'll read the second lesson," says her father. "Isaiah nine. *The people that walked in darkness*. My reader's not come out tonight. Most people haven't."

Reggie doesn't answer at once.

"I'd rather not," he says. "If you'll forgive me. I'd read it badly."

"You've an English voice. They'll like it."

"It's not the voice." Reggie turns his hat round in his hands. "I'm not a believer, Reverend. I've not been since I was fourteen. I'd be standing up in your church in front of your people reading it as though I meant it, and I wouldn't. I'd not want to do that to them." He looks up. "Not tonight. Not with half of them stopping at home for fear of being shot. They've come out for something. They ought to have it read by somebody who believes it."

Marcia's hands stop on the sash.

Her father doesn't say anything for a long moment. He's looking at Reggie with the face he has in the pulpit just before he says something he's been working on all week. Pearl, on the chair, looks from one to the other with enormous eyes.

"An honest unbeliever," says her father at last. "In my vestry. I've not had one of those since 1952." He sits back down and puts his glasses on. "You'll take the aisle, then. For your leg. And you'll stand for the hymns if you can, and sit for the rest. I'll read the lesson myself."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me." Her father's already looking at the order of service again. "I'll be praying for you, Dr Tar. You'll not be able to stop me. That's mine."

And Reggie, in the vestry door, does a thing she'll remember for years. He smiles, small and crooked, up further on one side.

"I'd not dream of trying," he says. "I've a friend who does it in Spanish. He's never asked my permission either."

Her father makes a sound into the order of service that might be a laugh. Marcia's never been sure.

---

Then Pearl sings the verse, and the church goes quiet, and Marcia stops watching her daughter and watches her mother-in-law.

She doesn't mean to. She's watching Pearl, the way she always does, ready to be proud and to cry, which she does in church. And Pearl takes the note from Mrs Beckford and holds it, and lets it go up out of the middle of her, to the back wall. And out of the corner of her eye Marcia sees Win go white.

Not pale. White. As though somebody had drawn the blood out of her face with a needle. Her hand's gone to the base of her throat. And she's looking at Pearl the way you'd look at somebody you'd buried, coming up the aisle.

Marcia knows about the singing. Mr Carew told the whole church hall at the wedding, in 1969, between numbers. *My wife sang, you know. Best voice on the north coast. Gave it up.* Joy's told her more, since, in the letters. But she's never put it next to anything.

She puts it next to the church steps in 1969, now. Win standing outside with her back to the door, while her husband played inside. *I like it very much. That's the trouble.*

And she thinks, sitting in her father's church in the dark with her daughter's voice going up into the roof: *it's not that she gave it up. It's that she had to.* She doesn't know why. She doesn't know what it was about Mr Carew at the piano that a woman like Win would have to get away from by going outside and standing in the traffic. But she knows that look. She's seen it in the vestry on brides who are marrying the wrong man and know it, at the moment they hear the organ.

She looks past Win, at Reggie.

He's not looking at Pearl. He's looking at Win. He's got his hand on her back, low, very still. He doesn't say anything. He's just there.

In the vestry after, when Pearl says *did I sing it wrong*, Win kneels on the floor in her good coat and says *no, you sang it exactly right*. And Reggie says *your grandmother used to sing*. And Pearl asks why she stopped, and Win says *because your father was born, and somebody had to keep the books*.

Marcia stands at the robes cupboard with Pearl's coat over her arm and listens to her mother-in-law say a thing that's true and isn't the truth. She knows it the way you know a hem's been done by somebody else and then gone over: it's straight, and it's tidy, and it's not the first stitching.

She doesn't say anything. It's not hers.

---

On Christmas Day, in the flat, nine of them in a room for four, Win comes into the kitchen at nine in the morning with an apron over her arm and stands beside Marcia at the stove.

Marcia's been dreading it for a month. She's been dreading it since 1969. Win Carew, who runs a hotel and has done the Bay's Christmas dinners for twenty years, in Marcia's kitchen like a cupboard. She's going to watch. She's going to put the fish on Marcia's way and say nothing and do it better.

Win doesn't watch. She rolls up her sleeves and asks what Marcia wants doing.

Marcia tells her the yam. Win does the yam. They work side by side for an hour, not talking much. At ten Marcia does the sorrel her mother's way, which isn't the Bay's way, with the ginger in at the start instead of the end. Win stops, with the knife in her hand, and watches her do it.

"Your mother's?" she says.

"My mother's."

Win's quiet for a moment.

"Do it your way," she says. "No. Go on. Do it your way, love, yours is better." And she goes back to the yam.

Marcia goes pink to the ears and does it her way. Nobody's ever called her *love*, except her mother.

It's at the sink, later, washing up with the others in the front room, that Win says the other thing. She says it to the window, with her hands in the water.

"I want a dress."

Marcia stops drying.

"For the Board's dinner. In November, when they come out. Reggie's to speak, God help us, and I'm to sit at the top table and look as though I meant to." Win's scrubbing a pan that doesn't need scrubbing. "I make my own. I always have. I've no money for a dressmaker and I've not trusted one since 1955. But I'd like one made. Navy. Not too much. With proper sleeves." She doesn't turn round. "If you'd do it. I'll pay your rate. Your full rate, mind, not family."

Marcia stands with the tea towel.

She thinks about the French seams on Pearl's birthday dresses. Every year since 1972, so fine they made her feel four years old. She thinks about a woman who's made her own clothes for thirty years, with her own hands, and never once let anybody carry anything. Standing at Marcia's sink, asking.

"I'll do it," she says. "On one condition."

"What?"

"You'll not turn it inside out," says Marcia. "When it comes. You'll not look at the seams."

Win turns round from the sink. She looks at Marcia, with her wet hands held up, and her face does the thing Lloyd's does, the waiting before anything happens.

Then she laughs. It's a big laugh, much bigger than Marcia's ever heard out of her, and it fills the kitchen.

"I'll not promise that," says Win. "I'd be lying. But I'll not tell you what I find."

She sends the measurements in January, in a letter, every one in inches and eighths. Marcia makes the dress. She does the seams so well she'd not be ashamed if Win turned it inside out under a glass. In November a postcard comes from the Bay with a picture of the harbour on it, in Win's hand, the first postcard Win's ever sent her.

*Board dinner. Sir Hugh asked who made it. I said my daughter-in-law. I turned it inside out when I got home. I'll not tell you what I found. W.*

Marcia keeps it in the machine drawer with the tape.

---

Pearl gets the measles that Easter, and Reggie stays.

Marcia's said all there is to say about that, she thinks, to Joy, at the time. *He sat up four nights. He kept a chart. He rang Win from Mrs Chin's and said he'd not leave till it came down.* She's said it to her father, who nodded and said *good*. She's said it to Lloyd, who didn't say anything.

There's one thing she's not said to anybody.

It's the Saturday, the bad night, at about two in the morning. She's in the kitchen making tea, because somebody has to, and Lloyd's asleep on the sofa in his clothes. And she hears him, through the board wall, from Pearl's room.

Not talking. He's not talking to anybody. Pearl's asleep. He's on his own in the chair with her wrist in his hand and the torch on the chart. And she hears him say, very low, so low she nearly doesn't catch it, to nobody:

*Come on. Come on, then. Turn.*

The way you'd talk to the sea.

And then, after a long time, lower still: *I didn't know. I didn't know it would be like this. Nobody told me.*

Marcia stands in her kitchen with the kettle in her hand and doesn't move.

She thinks about the kitchen at the Bay in 1974. A man of fifty-five with a pencil in his hand, looking at Pearl's arms held up to him, working out where the weight went, because he'd never once in his life held a child. She'd thought it was a sweet thing, then. A funny thing. She'd shown him under the arms.

It's not a funny thing. She understands that standing in the kitchen at two in the morning. He's fifty-eight years old and he's sitting in the dark in her daughter's room finding out what it is to be frightened for a child. He's finding out all at once, the whole of it, that she and Lloyd have had six years to get used to. And nobody told him. And he's not going anywhere.

She makes the tea. Three sugars, because Win says. She goes and wakes Lloyd, on the sofa, with her hand on his shoulder.

"Take him this," she says.

Lloyd sits up. He looks at the tea, and at her, and at the board wall.

"He's all right," says Marcia. "He's just — take it in. Sit with him a bit. Don't say anything if you don't want to."

Lloyd takes the tea. She hears him go through. She hears Reggie say *thank you*, and Lloyd say something low, and the end of the bed creak as Lloyd sits on it. And then nothing, for a long time, except the two of them breathing, and once, a little later, Lloyd's voice saying something about the ferry steps.

She doesn't listen to the rest. She sits down at the kitchen table on her own in the middle of the night, and puts her face in her hands, and finds that she's crying, and doesn't know which of the three of them it's for.

---

Lloyd says it in the June, in bed, with the window open and the frogs going.

"We could have another."

She's nearly asleep. She opens her eyes.

"Another what?"

"Another one." He's on his back, looking at the ceiling, the way he says important things. "A baby. Mr Lim's put me up for the desk. Assistant to the accountant. It's a third more. From September." He's quiet. "And the — it's down to ten now. From the first. I did it in May."

Marcia lies very still.

She's never asked him to cut it. Not once, not since 1974. She's never asked him how much it was. It's his. She's known since the harbour wall that it's his, and she's not going to be the woman who wants the pram money back. She's watched it go from forty to twenty, and now he's telling her, to the ceiling, ten. She didn't know.

"Since May," she says.

"After the measles." Lloyd's voice is odd. "I sat on the end of her bed and watched him hold her wrist all night. He wasn't anything to her. He wasn't even — he's my mother's *husband*, Marce. He came for a Board. And he sat there four nights." He stops. "And I thought about my father. Who'd have sent a card. With a palm tree on it. And I thought, I've been paying for the card."

She doesn't say anything. She puts her hand out in the dark and finds his, on the sheet, and holds it.

"I can't stop it," says Lloyd. "Not yet. I've tried. I sit with the form. I can't." He turns his hand over under hers. "But I did ten. And with the desk, there's room. I did the sums. Down and up. There's room for another one, and you'd not have to sew at night again, ever, not if we had twins." He's quiet. "If you want."

Marcia lies in the dark with the frogs going and her husband's hand in hers.

She does the sums herself, in the morning, at the kitchen table, on the back of a dress pattern, while he's at the bank. She goes down the column, and then up, the way her father taught her for the church collections. She's got the rent, and the gas, and Pearl's school, and her own work, and the desk, and the ten. It comes out. Both ways, the same.

She puts a tick beside it.

That night she puts the cloth over the machine at seven, and doesn't take it off again after dark. By the autumn she's carrying, and it's in the cupboard except for daylight and the baby's things. Pearl notices; Pearl notices everything. Marcia sees her look at the cupboard door in the evenings with her head on one side, as though somebody had moved the furniture.

And Lloyd laughs at the radio.

It's a Tuesday, the first time, a comedy programme on RJR at eight, a man doing a politician's voice. Lloyd's on the sofa beside her with her feet in his lap because of her ankles, and he laughs. Out loud. All of a sudden, with his head back, the way she's never heard him laugh at anything that wasn't Pearl. Marcia looks at him. He doesn't notice her looking. He's listening to the radio, and on the Saturday he does it again.

Pearl writes to Reggie about it. Marcia knows, because Pearl reads her letters out before they go, for the spelling. *The machine is in the cupboard. Mummy does not sew at night now. Daddy laughs at the radio. I do not know why.*

The reply's all about the calf, four pages. At the very bottom, after *Your loving*, there's a line on its own.

*P.S. I'm very glad about the radio.*

Pearl reads it six times and asks what it means. Marcia says she expects he likes the radio. Then she goes into the kitchen and stands at the sink for a minute with the tap running, because she knows exactly what it means, and so does he. They've been waiting three years, the pair of them, a hundred miles apart, to hear that man laugh. And now they've told each other so, through a child of six, in six words.

---

He's born in March 1978, at the University Hospital, much quicker than Pearl. He's small and round and not furious at all, with her own face, and he looks at everything as though he's trying to work out how it goes.

Lloyd has the first name. He's had it for months, and he won't say why. It's *Isaac*.

"There's no Isaac in your family," says Marcia. "Or mine."

"No," says Lloyd. "That's why." He's holding the baby very carefully, the way Marcia once showed Reggie, one hand under the head. "It's not anybody's. Nobody gave it him. He'll not owe it to anybody."

Marcia looks at her husband for a long time. Then she says, "Reginald."

Lloyd's head comes up.

"For the second," says Marcia. "Isaac Reginald."

"*Marce*."

"He sat up four nights with your daughter."

"He'll be mortified. He'll go out of the room."

"Then he'll go out of the room," says Marcia. "He can come back in."

Lloyd looks at her. Then he looks down at the baby, who is looking up at him with her face, working out how he goes.

"Isaac Reginald," says Lloyd, quietly, trying it.

Pearl, aged seven, sitting on the end of the hospital bed with her feet swinging, says, "*Ike*."

That's that.

At Christmas, when Reggie comes and holds him on his knee and is told the second name, he goes out of the room. He goes out on to the balcony and stands there with his back to them and his hand over his eyes for quite a long time. Win goes out after a while and stands beside him and doesn't say anything.

Pearl never knows it was Marcia's doing. Marcia doesn't tell her. It's not that it's a secret. It's that it's done, and it's his, and saying so would only be asking to be thanked.

---

Pearl and her father are thick as thieves by the time she's eight.

Marcia doesn't quite know when it happened. Her father's never been a man for children. He was a good father to her, she supposes, if you count the ledger and the collar and the sermon on Sunday. He was never one for sitting on the floor. He's seventy now, and a widower these eight years, and he's been getting more silent every year since her mother, the way an old house gets quieter when there's one less person walking about in it.

And Pearl walks about in it.

She goes to the manse on Saturday mornings, on her own, from the age of seven. She goes up Constant Spring Road with her blue book under her arm and sits in her grandfather's study while he writes his sermon. Marcia doesn't know what they do in there for three hours. She asks Pearl once and Pearl says, "Grandad writes and I count things."

"What things?"

"In the Bible. He's got a big book that tells you every place a word is. It's called a *concordance*." Pearl says it very carefully. "I count how many times things are. *Sea* is in it three hundred and something. *Tide* isn't in it at all. Not once. I told Reggie and he didn't believe me and I had to send him the page." She looks pleased with herself. "Grandad says that's because the Lord's in Jerusalem and Jerusalem doesn't have one."

Marcia goes to the manse that afternoon on a pretext and stands in the hall where she can see the study door.

Her father's at his desk, writing, with his glasses down his nose. Pearl's on the floor at his feet with the concordance open across her knees, a book nearly as big as she is, going down a column of tiny print with her finger. Every so often she makes a mark in her blue book. Every so often her father, without looking up, says *what have you got*, and she tells him a number. He says *hm*, and writes something down, and goes on.

Marcia stands in the hall and thinks: *that's Reggie*. That's the thing Reggie does. The asking, and then the waiting. And then she thinks: *no, it isn't*.

Because when Pearl says, a minute later, "Grandad, I've done *mercy*, it's two hundred and sixty-one, is that a lot," her father doesn't ask her what she thinks. He doesn't wait to find out. He puts his pen down and turns round in his chair and says, "It's not a lot, child. It's *enough*. Write that down. *Mercy: enough.*" And Pearl writes it down.

Reggie would never have told her what to write. Reggie would have asked her whether she thought it was a lot. Then he'd have waited ten minutes while she worked it out, and asked about the bit she wasn't sure of.

Her father tells her. He tells her every Saturday. *Stand up straight when you sing. Sing to the back pew; the Lord sits with the latecomers. Don't say* ain't *in this house. Mercy is enough.* He tells her what's right the way he's told a congregation for thirty years, from a height, in the voice like a cello. And Pearl, who argues with everybody, who argues with Marcia and Lloyd and Joy and, by letter, with Reggie, doesn't argue with him. She stands up straight. She sings to the back pew. She writes it down.

It's not that she obeys him, Marcia thinks. It's that she believes him. They're different things, and Pearl's the only child Marcia knows who could have told you so at eight.

She's two different children, Marcia sees. With one old man she's the child who's told, and believes it. With the other she's the child who's asked, and finds out. And she needs both, and she's got both, and she doesn't know it's unusual.

Marcia never had the second one. Not till she was twenty-four and a man on a verandah wrote that her hem was the best piece of work he'd seen since England.

---

In the autumn of 1979 she hears them through the study door. She's come up with her father's shirts, which she does on Mondays now, and the door's ajar, and she stops in the hall without meaning to.

"Grandad."

"Mm."

"Will Reggie go to heaven?"

The pen stops.

Marcia stands in the hall with the shirts over her arm and doesn't breathe.

"Why do you ask that, child?"

"Because he doesn't go to church. Only when I sing. And he doesn't pray. I asked him, and he said he doesn't, he never has since he was fourteen. And Mrs Beckford says if you don't believe you don't go." Pearl's voice is quite steady. It's the voice she uses for a column that won't come out. "And he's *old*. He's got the heart. And I want to know if he'll go."

Her father doesn't answer for a long time.

Marcia knows what he thinks. She's heard him preach it. She's heard him preach it at funerals, at the gravesides of men who didn't come, and she's seen their widows' faces. He's a fair man and a hard one. He doesn't say things he doesn't believe to make a child feel better. He never has. He never did it to her.

"That's not mine to say," says her father at last.

Marcia shuts her eyes.

"That's what *Reggie* says," says Pearl. "*It's not mine to say.* He says it about everything."

"Does he." Her father's voice has gone odd. "Then he and I agree on one thing, at least." The chair creaks. "It's not mine to say, Pearl, because it's the Lord's. Not because it's yours. You understand the difference?"

"No."

"Reggie says it's not his because he thinks it's yours to work out. I say it's not mine because I know whose it is." Her father's quiet. "And I'll tell you what *is* mine. Praying for him. That's mine. I've done it every night since 1976, and I'll do it every night till one of us goes. So you can stop worrying, and leave it to the two of us. Me and the Lord. We've got it in hand."

There's a silence.

"Does Reggie *know*? That you pray for him?"

"He knows," says her father. "I told him in the vestry. He said he'd not dream of stopping me. He said he's got a friend who does it in Spanish." Something that's very nearly a laugh. "So that's two of us. He's very well covered for an unbeliever."

Marcia stands in the hall and finds her eyes have gone, and she's got her father's clean shirts pressed against her chest like a girl with a letter.

She goes back down the stairs very quietly and comes up again noisily, saying *Daddy, your shirts*. By the time she gets to the study door, her father's writing his sermon and Pearl's counting *sea*.

---

She knows, by 1980, that she's going to have to say something about Mr Carew.

She doesn't know when. She doesn't know what. But she's watched the Christmas cards come every year, a palm tree and a pink hotel and *To my little Pearlie, love Grandpa*, in the big loops. She's watched Lloyd put them on the mantelpiece and then in a drawer after Twelfth Night. She's watched Pearl read them, and turn them over, and look at the stamp. Pearl's never met him. He's not been up Mrs Chin's stairs since the pram.

She knows Pearl's going to meet him. You can't keep a man like that from a child; he comes when he comes. And she knows what Pearl will see. She'll see the most beautiful old man she's ever seen, and the funniest. And she'll love him, the way the church hall loved him in 1969, in about four minutes.

Marcia doesn't mind that. Let her love him. Marcia's mother loved him, on the floor of the church hall, for one dance. You can love all three, her mother said. You only lean on the third.

What she minds is that Pearl doesn't know there's a third.

Pearl's grown up in a family that's there at supper. All of it. Every one of them. She's never had a reason to count. She thinks it's what people do. She thinks everybody comes the long way round at Christmas on the boat and the bus by daylight, and sits up four nights with a chart, and is still there on Boxing Day. She's never met anybody who goes before supper.

She's going to meet one. And Marcia thinks, quite clearly, at the machine one afternoon in March: *she'd better meet him here, at nine, with all of us round her, than at nineteen on her own.*

---

He comes on Easter Saturday, at four, without writing. Of course he does.

Marcia's in the kitchen. She hears the knock and Lloyd's step and then Lloyd go quite still in the doorway, and she knows before she hears the voice.

"*Lloydie*. Look at you. *Look* at you."

She doesn't go out at once. She stands at the stove with her hands flat on either side of it, the way Win does at the sink, and gets her face right. She's despised Desmond Carew for ten years. She despised him across her kitchen table in 1970 with the pram envelope in his pocket. She despised him every Christmas he wrote *you're the only one I can count on*, and every first of the month for six years. She's thought things about him at the machine at eleven at night that she wouldn't say in her father's church.

She gets her face right. She goes out.

He's in Lloyd's chair with Ike on his knee. Ike, who's two and doesn't go to strangers, is pulling his moustache, and he's letting him. He looks up at her and smiles, the smile, the one that got the church hall off its chairs in 1969. It comes at her across the front room as though it had never been away. And it's the most terrible thing, but she feels it. She feels it land, the way it landed on her mother.

She's thirty. She's a grown woman. She's been married eleven years to his son. And for about a second and a half, in her own front room, with her apron on, she's eighteen again and wants him to like her.

"Marcia," he says. "Prettier than ever. Lloydie, you don't deserve her."

"No," says Lloyd. "I don't."

---

She watches him for an hour. She watches him the way she'd watch a hem somebody else had done, to see where it would go.

He's funny. God help her, he's funny. He tells a story about a man with a boat who went to Havana in a storm, and she laughs out loud before she can stop it, and hates herself. He's kind to Ike. He's beautiful with Ike. He lets him have the moustache and the watch chain and the hat, and when Ike drops the hat he doesn't flinch. Lloyd sits on the sofa with his hands between his knees and watches his father hold his son, and his face is doing the thing it did on the harbour wall at the Bay at Christmas 1970, when his father didn't come.

And he asks Pearl things.

That's what Marcia's watching for. She watches him turn to Pearl in the middle of a story and say *and what about you, Pearlie, what do you like at school*. She watches Pearl open her mouth to tell him about the columns, about going up as well as down. She sees him already laughing, already off. *A girl after her grandmother's heart.* Another story, and a lovely one, about Win and a hotel bill.

Three times. She counts.

She watches Pearl notice. She sees it go across her daughter's face, the way things go across Reggie's, where you can see them. Not hurt. Pearl isn't hurt. She's interested. She's nine and she's been keeping a blue book since she was five, and she's seen something come back twice, and now three times. She's filing it.

*Good girl*, Marcia thinks. *Good girl. Count.*

---

And then he says *the best voice in Kingston except one*, and she hears herself say it.

"Pearl sings."

She doesn't decide it, exactly. Or she decided it in March, at the machine, and it's only coming out now. She's in the kitchen door with a tea towel. The room goes quiet. Lloyd's head comes round and he looks at her as though she'd dropped a plate.

And Desmond Carew turns round in Lloyd's chair and looks at Pearl. Marcia sees it happen. She sees the smile go off his face like a lamp turned down, and something else come up under it, slow. It goes all the way into Pearl. It's the only time all afternoon he's looked at anybody as though he wanted to know what they'd say.

She knows what she's doing. She'd like that on the record, later, when Lloyd asks her in bed what she was thinking. She knows exactly what she's doing.

She's giving Pearl the one true thing in him.

Because there is one. She's known it since 1969. She saw it at her own wedding, when he sat down at the flat-topped piano and the whole hall stood up. And she saw Win go out and stand on the steps because she couldn't bear it. He's a liar, and a debtor, and a man who'd take the pram money out of a pregnant girl's wardrobe. And he's not lying at the piano. Whatever else he is, he's not lying there.

Pearl's got her grandmother's voice. Win said so at Christmas. Nobody in this family can give her what that voice wants. Not Lloyd, who can't hold a tune. Not Marcia. Not her father, who has a voice like a cello and has never once in his life accompanied anybody, because he leads. Not Reggie, God love him, who can't carry a tune in a bucket.

Only him. Only this man, for an hour, in her father's church hall. And then he'll go.

She wants Pearl to have the hour. And she wants her to see him go.

---

She doesn't go in to the hall. She stands at the back by the door with her arms folded, beside Lloyd with Ike on his hip, beside her father with the key. And she watches her daughter sing *Stardust*, which she's never heard in her life, with a man who'd never heard her before that afternoon.

He's with her. Every bar.

Marcia's heard Pearl sing since she was three. She's heard her in the bath and at the window and in the front row of her father's church every Christmas Eve. She's never heard her like this. Pearl's being carried. She can see it on her face: the face of somebody who's been understood all the way down, in a language only two people in the room speak. And Desmond Carew's at the piano with his head bent and his face gone quite still, giving her room. He's listening to every single thing her voice does, as though there were nothing else in the world.

Marcia stands at the back of the hall and hates him, and is grateful to him, and can't tell which is bigger.

At the end he says *Winnie*. Very quietly. To nobody.

Beside her, her father makes a small sound. She looks up. He's looking at the man at the piano with an expression she's never seen on him. It's not disapproval. It's the face he has at a graveside, for a man who didn't come.

Then the lamp comes back up, and Desmond spins round on the stool and holds his hands out to Pearl, and tells her she's got it, the Thing, and not to let anybody make her a clerk.

And Pearl says, "I like the books."

And he laughs.

Marcia watches her daughter's face go still and cold, like the tide going down the steps. And she thinks: *there. There it is. She's seen it.*

It's the worst thing she's ever done on purpose, and she's not sorry.

---

In the kitchen, after, she's giving Ike his bath and she can hear them through the wall. Lloyd and his father. Desmond's voice going up and down, warm, the way it does in the stories. She knows what it's going up and down about. She's heard it at her own kitchen table in 1970.

Then Lloyd's voice. It doesn't go up and down at all.

"Ten," says Lloyd.

Desmond's voice again, warm, longer.

"Ten, Daddy," says Lloyd. "Same as it's been. On the first."

Desmond laughs, and the kitchen door opens. *Well, I'll not stop, Lloydie. There's a man in Spanish Town.* Marcia kneels by the bath with her sleeves rolled up and her hands in the water, holding Ike up under the arms. She shuts her eyes.

*Ten, Daddy.* Not *no*. Not yet. But not a word more. Nothing about the children, nothing about the flat. *Same as it's been.* Like a man reading a figure off a ledger.

She knows what it costs him. She's watched him sit with the cancellation form in his hand, at this kitchen table, four times since the measles. Then put it back in his briefcase.

She also knows what it costs him to say *ten* in that voice to his father's face, in his own kitchen, without once looking at the floor. Two years ago he couldn't have. Six years ago he'd have gone into the bedroom and come back with whatever was in the tin.

She lifts Ike out of the bath and wraps him in the towel and holds him against her, wet, and doesn't care.

---

She comes to Pearl's door at nine with the bath towel over her shoulder, and stops.

Lloyd's on the end of the bed in the dark with his tie off. Pearl's lying very straight under the sheet, with her eyes open. She's talking, carefully, the way she does when she's trying to get a column to come out.

"I like Grandpa Des," Pearl's saying. "But I don't like him the way I like Reggie. And I don't know why."

Lloyd doesn't answer. Marcia sees his hand go to his jacket, where the wallet is, and stop. The letter's not in the wallet any more. The one from Miami in 1973, *I did what a man has to do*. He took it out the week Ike was born and put it in the drawer with the Christmas cards. She's never said she saw. The hand still goes.

"I don't know either, sweetheart," says Lloyd. "I've never known."

And Marcia stands in the door and thinks: *yes, you have. You've always known. You've just never been let say it.*

She thinks about her mother in the vestry, with the cherry hat in her lap. *You count who's still there at supper.*

She comes in. She sits down on the other side of the bed. She puts her hand on Pearl's hair.

"I'll tell you," she says. "If you want. Both of you."

---

She tells them.

She tells them about listening like a man waiting for his turn to play, and listening like a man who wants to know. She doesn't know where it comes from. It comes out of her straight, like a hem. And then she tells them the other thing, the true thing, because Pearl says *but he listened when I sang* and it's true and Marcia's not going to pretend it isn't. *That's the one thing he really listens to. That's his. Nobody else can give you that.*

And then Pearl says *then why*, and Marcia says it.

"Because he left before supper."

She says it in her mother's voice. She can hear it. She says the rest of it, about the boat and the bus the long way round, and the aisle seat, and the floor on Christmas Day, and the measles, and the telephone in Mrs Chin's hall. And then she says the last of it, which is her mother's, word for word, from a vestry in 1969.

"That's all it is, darling. It's not the music, or the stories. It's who's still there at supper."

Lloyd's put his face in his hands.

She reaches across Pearl in the dark and puts her hand on the back of his neck. He's warm, and he's shaking a little, and he leans back into her hand the way he didn't used to lean into anything.

She doesn't tell either of them that it was her mother's. That's hers.

---

She finds the blue book on the kitchen table on the Wednesday after, open, when Pearl's at school.

She doesn't mean to read it. She never reads it. It's Pearl's; it's been Pearl's since Christmas 1976, when Reggie wrote *you decide what goes in it* on the first page, and Marcia's always thought that was the best present anybody ever gave her daughter. She's never once looked.

It's open. She's moving it to wipe the table. And the heading's in big careful letters, on a fresh page, and she reads it before she can stop herself.

*Things That Come Back The Same Twice.*

*The tide.*

*A column, if you go down and then up.*

*The calf, after 31.*

*The back wall at church.*

*Reggie.*

And smaller, underneath, as though Pearl weren't sure yet:

*Grandpa Des. Only in the music.*

Marcia stands at the kitchen table with the cloth in her hand and reads it twice.

She's not on it. Lloyd's not on it. Her father's not on it.

She stands there for a long time. She feels something go in her, small and sharp, like a needle into the pad of a finger that's gone hard. It's not much. It's only a little.

And then she understands it. She stands at the kitchen table and understands it, all at once, the way you understand a pattern when you've cut it out and laid it flat. *Things that come back.* Pearl's nine. She's written down the things she's watched go and come back. The tide goes out and comes in. The calf goes down and comes up. Reggie goes home on the boat and comes back at Christmas. Grandpa Des goes before supper and comes back, perhaps, once, at the piano.

Marcia never goes anywhere.

Nor does Lloyd. He goes to the bank at eight and comes home at six, and has done every working day of Pearl's life, and on Saturdays he takes her to the ferry steps to look at the weed. Nor does her father, who's been at his desk on Constant Spring Road every Saturday morning since she was seven, with the concordance on the floor for her. None of the three of them has once not been there. Not for a night, not for an hour, not in nine years. Pearl's never had to watch them come back, because she's never had to watch them go. They're not on the list for the same reason the floor's not on it, or the roof. You don't write down the thing you're standing on.

She closes the book. She puts it back exactly where it was, open at the same page, square to the edge of the table.

Then she goes to the front room and takes the cloth off the machine. It's three in the afternoon, with the windows open and the traffic going by on the road. She has Pearl's white Easter dress to let down an inch, because Pearl's grown again, and she takes it out of the basket and turns it inside out and looks at the hem.

It's a good hem. It's an ordinary good hem.

She unpicks it, and lets it down, and does it again by hand, every stitch, so that nobody will ever be able to see it was done twice.
