# Letter of Credit

The letter from the Royal College of Music comes in July, two days after the concert. It's on thick cream paper with a crest, and it says *Isaac Carew* at the top as though Isaac Carew were somebody, which Marcia supposes he is now.

It says they're delighted. It says *a place for the academic year commencing September 1997*. It says *partial scholarship*. And then, on the second sheet, it says a figure, in sterling, which Lloyd reads at the kitchen table three times and then turns face down.

Ike's in the church hall. He doesn't know about the second sheet yet. He's read the first one forty times and taken it up to the manse to read to her father, who's put his hand round his good ear and made him read it twice more.

"It's the fees," says Lloyd. "And the board. And the fare. It's — Marce, it's more than I make in a year. In a year and a half." He's got his hand flat on the face-down sheet. "Joy's said he can stay in Hammersmith. That's the board. And the fare's the fare. But the fees."

Marcia sits down across from him.

She knows what's in PIN MONEY. She checked it in April at the counter on King Street. Twelve years of the first of the month. It's a great deal. It would do the fare, and some of the first year. It wouldn't do London.

"We'll find it," she says.

Lloyd doesn't answer. He's looking at the sheet. She watches his face do the thing it did in 1974, on Win's verandah, before the wall. Wound up, one turn at a time, from nine in the morning.

She's not seen it do that in twenty years.

---

The letter from the Bay comes on the Thursday. It's addressed to Lloyd, at the bank, in the small neat hand, a little shakier than it was. Lloyd brings it home in his briefcase and doesn't open it till after supper. He reads it at the kitchen table with his glasses on, and then he puts it down in front of Marcia without a word and goes out on to the balcony.

*Dear Lloyd,*

*Ike will have heard from the College by now, and you'll have the second sheet. I know what's on it. I asked Joy to find out in May, and she rang them.*

*I'd like to pay it.*

*Not lend. Pay. The fees, for the three years, direct to the College, by banker's order from the London account, the first of each term. Ike needn't know where it comes from unless you want him to. I'd rather he didn't; he'd feel he had to thank me, and he'd do it badly, and so would I.*

*I've no children. You know that. I've got two grandchildren I'd no right to, because your mother married me and Pearl decided I was hers. I've got more money than I've any use for, and a wife who won't let me spend it on her. She says she's got a roof that doesn't leak and that'll do. I'd like to spend some of it on the boy who took the front off the bar piano with me when he was six.*

*I'll understand if you'd rather not. It's yours to say. I'll not ask again either way.*

*R.T.*

Marcia reads it twice. Then she goes out on to the balcony.

Lloyd's at the rail, looking at the lights of Half Way Tree. He doesn't turn round.

"No," he says.

She stands beside him.

"I'll not take it, Marce." His voice is quite level. "I'll not have my son's fees paid by a man who's not his family."

"He's Ike's grandfather."

"He's my mother's husband." Lloyd's hands are tight on the rail. "I've paid for everything in this family since I was twenty. Everything. Every first of the month. I'll not start being paid *for* now. Not by him. Not for my own son."

Marcia doesn't say anything.

She thinks about the counterfoil. About Win on the bottom step of the harbour in 1968. *He'll pay for everybody, my Lloyd. Mind he doesn't.* She thinks she's been minding him for twenty-nine years. She thinks she's never once in all that time seen anybody try to pay for *him*.

She's not going to say it. It's his.

"Sleep on it," she says, and goes in.

He doesn't sleep on it. She hears him get up at three and go and sit at the kitchen table, and she hears the scrape of the chair at five when he comes back. He lies down on his back and doesn't close his eyes.

---

On Sunday they take the dinner up to the manse in the basket, the way they have since her father's knees went. Her father's eighty-eight. He's in his chair in the kitchen with Aunt Merle beside him, who's eighty-five and deafer than he is, the two of them shouting at each other comfortably about the new minister's sermon.

Ike plays the manse piano after dinner. He plays the Handel from the concert, and then the *Improvisation*, and then *Stardust*, because her father asks for it. Then he goes down the road to the church hall to practise, because the manse piano's worse than the hall's.

And her father puts his hand round his good ear and says, "Lloyd. What's the matter with you?"

Lloyd looks up from the washing-up.

"Nothing, sir."

"You've a face like a man whose ledger won't balance. You've had it since you came in." Her father points at the chair across the table with his stick. "Sit down."

Lloyd dries his hands. He sits down.

He's forty-nine years old. He's the assistant manager of the King Street branch, with his name on the door in gold. Marcia watches him sit down across the kitchen table from her father exactly the way he did in 1968, the first Thursday, with the church ledger under his arm. Back straight. Hands flat.

"Well?"

Lloyd tells him. Not well. Lloyd's never told anything well. He tells it in figures, the way he'd tell a loan: the College, the second sheet, the fees, the fare, Hammersmith. Then the letter from the Bay. Then *no*.

Her father listens to all of it with his hand round his ear. When it's done he sits back in his chair. It creaks.

"Take it," he says.

Lloyd stares at him.

"You heard me. Take it. Write to the man tonight and say thank you, and take it."

"Sir, I can't —"

"You can't *what*? Be given a thing?" Her father leans forward on his stick. "I've watched you thirty years, Lloyd Carew. You've kept my church's books since you were eighteen without a penny wrong. You stacked the chairs at your own wedding. You buried my wife for me, because I couldn't. You've paid for every soul in this family and half of your father's creditors in Miami, and I've never once seen you let anybody pay for you." He taps the stick on the floor. "That's not pride. Don't tell yourself it is. That's a man who doesn't believe he's owed anything. And a man who'll not be given anything can't be loved, son. Not properly. He'll not stand still for it."

Lloyd doesn't say anything.

"I learnt that from her," says her father. He nods at the wall, where there's a photograph of Lucille in the cherry hat. "Took me fifteen years. I'd not let her give me a thing either. She had to go round the side." He sits back. "You'll take it. You'll write to the man and say *thank you*, which is two words and you can manage them, and you'll let him. And then you'll let the boy go to London and not think about it again."

There's a long silence in the manse kitchen. Aunt Merle, who hasn't heard a word, says *more tea, Lloyd?* very loudly.

Lloyd looks across the table at Marcia.

She doesn't say anything. It's still his. But she looks back at him, steadily, and lets him see her face.

"Yes, sir," says Lloyd.

---

He writes it that night at the kitchen table. It takes him an hour and he won't let her see it. She sees the envelope go into his briefcase in the morning. She doesn't see what's in it till 2003, in a drawer at Harbour House, and then she'll not be able to read it for a while.

It says:

*Dear Reggie,*

*Thank you.*

*Lloyd.*

The reply comes in ten days. It's addressed to Lloyd, and he reads it at the bank and doesn't bring it home. He tells her about it in bed, to the ceiling.

"He said *good*," says Lloyd. "That was the first line. *Good.*" He's quiet. "And then he said he'd sat on a harbour wall once and told me nobody would pay, if I stopped. That that was what stopping was. And he'd been wrong. That it was only half of it. The other half was letting somebody pay for you. And he'd not known that either, till my mother."

Marcia lies very still.

"He said it took him fifty years," says Lloyd. "He said I'd done it in ten days. He said that was very good going for a Carew."

She turns her head on the pillow. He's smiling at the ceiling. It's not the radio smile. It's a smaller one, tired and surprised.

"He called you a Carew," she says.

"He did. He's not one." Lloyd turns his head and looks at her. "He signed it *Reggie*, Marce. Not *R.T.* He's never signed anything to me but *R.T.*"

---

Ike goes on the fourteenth of September, on the evening flight to London, with Joy.

Marcia makes him a tailcoat.

He doesn't ask for it. He says he'll hire one, the way the other students do, from a shop in London that Joy's found. Marcia says *you'll not hire a coat to play the Brahms in*, and gets the black wool out of the cupboard where it's been since April, and makes it. She measures him on the kitchen chair the way she measured Pearl. He stands on it at nineteen with his arms out, six foot one, his head nearly touching the light, while she goes round him with the tape and her mouth full of pins.

She leaves two inches in every seam.

"Mummy, that's too much. It'll bag."

"It won't bag. It's turned in. You'll not see it."

"Then why —"

"So it can be let out," says Marcia, round a pin.

"I've *stopped growing*. I'm nineteen."

"Men don't stop. They spread." She puts the pin in. "And when you spread, you'll bring it home to me, and I'll let it out. You'll not take it to some man in London who'll cut the turnings off and charge you for the privilege. You'll bring it *home*."

Ike looks down at her from the kitchen chair, from a great height, with her own face. She watches him understand what she's saying. She watches him go red to the ears.

"Yes, Mummy," he says.

"Turn."

He turns.

---

At the airport her father comes, though he's eighty-eight and can't do the stairs at Norman Manley and has to be pushed in a chair by a porter. He insists. He sits in the chair in the departure hall with his stick across his knees and his hat on, in his good suit, which she made him in 1990, and he looks at Ike for a long time.

"You'll go to church," he says. "In London."

"Yes, Grandad."

"Every Sunday. Whether you feel it or not."

"Yes, Grandad."

"You'll write to your mother. Every week. Not four pages about boilers. A proper letter. How you are."

"Yes, Grandad."

"And you'll not marry the first girl who turns your pages."

Ike goes scarlet. Joy, behind the chair, makes a noise like a kettle. Lloyd looks at the departures board with great concentration.

Her father holds out his hand. Ike bends down and takes it. Her father pulls him down further, with surprising strength, and puts his other hand on the back of his head, and holds it there. He says something in Ike's ear that Marcia can't hear. She'll never know what it was. Ike never tells her.

Then he lets go, and sits back, and says, "Go on, then. You'll miss it."

Ike hugs Lloyd, hard, briefly, the way men hug. He hugs Marcia, and she holds on longer than he means her to, and smells the soap she bought him and the wool of the tailcoat in the bag over his shoulder. She doesn't cry. She's decided not to. She lets go.

He goes through with Joy. At the barrier he turns round. He's six foot one and he's nineteen, and he lifts his hand, the way his grandfather lifted his hat at the corner of Constant Spring Road in 1984.

Then he's gone.

---

She cries in the car park.

She doesn't mean to. She's holding the car door for her father while Lloyd and the porter lift him in, and she gets him in, and shuts the door, and turns round. And it comes up out of her all at once, standing between two cars in the dark at Norman Manley with the planes going over. She can't stop it.

Lloyd comes round the car. He doesn't say anything. He puts his arms round her and holds her against him in the car park. Through the window her father looks out at them both, very steady, from under his hat.

"He's got your face," says Lloyd, into her hair.

"I know."

"He's got your face and he's gone to London."

"*I know*, Lloyd."

"I'm only saying," says Lloyd, "that I'll miss it too."

---

The flat's very quiet.

She'd not thought about that. She'd thought about the airport and the tailcoat and Joy's spare room in Hammersmith and whether he'd eat. She'd not thought about the flat. Ike's never practised in it; there's no piano. But there's been the sound of him for nineteen years. The scales under his breath at the kitchen table, his fingers going on the oilcloth. Him on the telephone at Mrs Chin's. Him through the board wall at night, writing very slowly, the way he wrote to Andrea Chung.

Pearl's moved out two years since, into a flat of her own in New Kingston near the Pegasus. So it's the two of them. Marcia and Lloyd. In the flat in Barbican with the blue curtains, for the first time since 1971.

She doesn't know what to do with it. She sits at the machine in the evenings because she always has, though there's nothing on it that wants doing at night, and listens to the quiet.

On the Thursday Lloyd comes home with the *Gleaner* and his briefcase and puts them both down by the door. He stands in the front room looking at her at the machine.

"Marce."

"Mm."

"Leave that."

She looks up. He's gone to the radio. He's turning the dial, very carefully, the way he does a ledger, till he finds RJR. There's a dance band on, something slow, from the old days.

He holds out his hand.

She stares at him.

"Lloyd, you can't dance. You've never danced in your life. You trod on me at our *wedding*."

"I know," says Lloyd. "There's nobody to see."

She gets up from the machine. She takes his hand. He puts the other on her waist, very correctly, the way her father must have been taught in 1937, and they dance in the front room of the flat in Barbican between the sofa and the machine, in a space about the size of a tablecloth.

He's terrible. He's exactly as terrible as he was in 1969. He treads on her twice. He counts under his breath, *one two three, one two three*, with his frown on. She laughs into his shoulder until she's crying again, and he doesn't stop, and he doesn't let go.

Mrs Chin bangs on the ceiling from below with her broom at eleven. She's ninety and she does it every time anybody walks across the floor after ten. Lloyd says *sorry, Mrs Chin* to the floor, quite loudly, and goes on dancing.

---

Pearl stands on the kitchen chair in April 1998 in her slip, with her arms out, and Marcia measures her for the dress.

It's white silk. Real silk, Italian, from a warehouse in Miami that Mrs Beckford's niece put her on to, a whole bolt of it. It came in a long box in February with a customs form that Lloyd had to go down to the wharf and sign. She paid for it out of PIN MONEY. It took a great deal of PIN MONEY. There's some left, not much. She knows exactly what she means to do with the rest.

She's been thinking about this silk since April 1996, at the north window above the haberdasher's. She's thought about it every day since Pearl came home from Easter at the farm on the Chesapeake and sat at the kitchen table and talked for an hour, not about Sammy, about his grandmother. About his sister who's slow at everything but the piano. About his brother who's the clever one, and his other sister who keeps her receipts in a coffee tin.

She's thought about it every Tuesday, when Sammy comes to supper. He came the first Tuesday because Marcia asked him, once, on the church steps. He's taken it as a standing invitation, and he's never once been late.

She thought about it at the manse, too, the Sunday in 1996 when her father had him to dinner to look at him.

Her father put his hand round his good ear and asked him, before the soup, whether he was a churchgoing man. Sammy said, *not much, sir. We sing at home. Gram says that's the same thing, near enough.* And her father looked at him for a long time and said, *your grandmother's wrong. But not by much.* And Sammy laughed. It was a big surprised laugh, and her father laughed back, the cello one, and Aunt Merle said *what? what did he say?*

And after dinner Sammy got up and dried.

Marcia was at the manse sink, washing. Nobody asked him. He got up from the table and took the tea towel off the rail and stood beside her and dried every plate as she put it on the rack. Then he stacked them in the cupboard in the right order, which he'd watched her take them out in. He didn't say anything about it. He didn't seem to know he'd done anything.

She stood at her father's sink with her hands in the water and thought about her mother in the vestry in 1969, with the cherry hat in her lap. *You count who stays for the washing-up.*

She counted. She's been counting every Tuesday since. He's not missed one.

"Turn," she says.

Pearl turns on the kitchen chair.

"Mummy." She's looking down at Marcia, with her arms still out. "You've been quiet all morning."

"I'm measuring."

"You're quiet when you're measuring. You're not *this* quiet."

Marcia writes a figure in the back of her pattern book. Bust. Waist. She doesn't look up.

"You're going to Baltimore," she says.

The kitchen's very still.

"Not yet," says Pearl. "Not for a year. We've worked it out. Sammy goes in June, after the wedding, because the contract's up. I stay at the Pegasus till next summer, because I can't leave them without a controller. We do it long distance for a year, the way James said. Then I go."

"I know."

"Mummy, we've *told* you all this."

"I know you have, darling." Marcia puts the tape round Pearl's hips. "I'm not asking. I'm only saying it out loud. So I've heard it." She writes the figure down. "You're going to Baltimore."

Pearl's quiet on the chair.

"You could come," she says. "Visit. There are planes."

"I've never been on a plane in my life."

"Auntie Joy's been flying for thirty years."

"Auntie Joy's a nurse. Nurses aren't afraid of anything." Marcia stands up. Her knees crack. She holds out her hand, the way she has since Pearl was small enough to lift, to help her down off the chair. "Mind the pins."

Pearl takes her hand. She doesn't get down. She stands on the chair holding her mother's hand and looking at her.

"Will you be all right?" she says. "Without me. And Ike. Both of us."

Marcia thinks about the dance band on RJR, and Lloyd counting under his breath, and Mrs Chin's broom.

"I'll be all right," she says. "Your father dances now."

Pearl's mouth falls open.

"*Daddy*?"

"Badly," says Marcia. "Get down off that chair. I want to cut."

---

She doesn't remember much of the wedding, afterwards, and she's told people so. She remembers the dress going up the aisle of the chapel at the Bay on Lloyd's arm. She remembers that the hem hung exactly right, and that she'd been up till two the night before at Harbour House doing it again by hand. She remembers Ike at the bar piano, which they'd carried up the road for the occasion, playing Pearl in.

She remembers her father at the front, eighty-nine, standing beside the young minister from the Bay, the grandson of the one who married Win and Reggie. He insisted. He'd not take the service, he said, his voice wasn't up to it. But he'd do the blessing at the end. Nobody else was going to put his hands on that child's head but him.

He did it. He put his big hands on Pearl's head and on Sammy's, together. He said the words in a voice that wasn't what it was, but was still a cello, and the whole chapel heard him.

And then he did the thing he did at the confirmation. He looked up over their heads, down the chapel, to the aisle seat. Reggie was standing. He'd stood up at *who gives this woman*, without being asked, and nobody'd made him sit down. Her father looked at Reggie and Reggie looked back, the two old men, across the whole of the chapel at the Bay. The one who believed and the one who didn't.

Then her father looked down, and took his hands off, and said *amen*.

That's what Marcia remembers. That, and the singing after, in the bar, which she'll not try to describe either.

---

Pearl moves back into her old room in July, to save for Baltimore.

Marcia hadn't expected that either. She'd expected the flat in New Kingston till next summer, and then the airport. Instead, in July, a fortnight after the wedding, Pearl comes up Mrs Chin's stairs with two suitcases and the box of letters and stands in the front room.

"I've given notice on the flat," she says. "It's a hundred and ten a month for a room I sleep in. I've done the columns. If I'm here, I've got Baltimore by next July with some over." She looks at Marcia. "If that's all right. I'll pay board."

"You'll not pay board."

"I'll pay *board*, Mummy, I'm a married woman."

"Then you'll pay it into PIN MONEY," says Marcia, "and I'll do what I like with it." She takes one of the suitcases. "Your room's as you left it. I've not had the heart to do anything with it."

So for a year she has her daughter home.

Not the way it was. Pearl's twenty-seven and married and works till eight at the Pegasus most nights, and on Fridays she sings in the Starlight. She telephones Baltimore from Mrs Chin's on Sunday nights at nine, when it's cheap, and sits on the bottom stair for an hour with the receiver against her ear and her knees pulled up, laughing, very low. And twice a week a letter comes with an American stamp, in a large plain hand, and Pearl reads it at the kitchen table and goes quiet and smiles at nothing.

But she's home. She's in the room across the passage. Marcia hears her through the board wall at night, writing in the blue book, the way she did at nine.

She knows it ends in July. She doesn't let herself forget it. She counts it the way her mother counted: not heads, but who's still there at supper. Every night for a year, Pearl's there at supper.

She tries to make it enough. Mostly it is.

---

Her father goes in March 1999.

It's not quick, and it's not slow. It's his heart, in the end, the doctor says, like her mother's. But it's not sudden, the way her mother's was. He has a bad night in January and then a better week, and then a worse one, and by February he's in bed most of the day at the manse. Aunt Merle sits with him in the mornings and Marcia in the afternoons, and Pearl comes every evening straight from the Pegasus in her work clothes.

He's ninety. He's very thin. His voice has gone right down, to a cello played very softly, in another room. But he tells everybody what to do. He tells the doctor. He tells the new minister, who comes every Tuesday and sits by the bed looking frightened. He tells Aunt Merle, who can't hear him, and Marcia, who can.

He tells Pearl most.

"You'll go in July," he says. It's a Thursday evening in February, and Pearl's in the chair by the bed in her Pegasus suit, with the concordance on her knee because he asked for it. Marcia's at the window, darning one of his socks because there's nothing else to do. "As you planned. You'll not stay for me."

"Grandad —"

"You'll not stay for me, Pearl." He turns his head on the pillow. "I'm not staying for you. I've an appointment."

Pearl laughs. It comes out wet.

"You heard me. You'll go to Baltimore in July, to that young man who dries plates, and you'll go to church there on Sundays whether you feel it or not, and you'll do his family's books, God help you. And you'll sing. And you'll not come back for my funeral if it falls badly. Your mother'll see to it."

"I'll come back."

"You'll do as you're told," says her father. "For once." He closes his eyes. "Do me *morning*."

Pearl looks down at the concordance.

"*Morning*?"

"*Morning*. Psalm thirty. Your mother knows it."

Marcia, at the window, stops darning.

She knows it. Her mother had it on the wall of the kitchen at the manse, worked in cross-stitch, when Marcia was small. It came down when her mother died, and her father put it in a drawer, and she's not thought of it in years.

"*Weeping may endure for a night*," she says. Her voice comes out very low. "*But joy cometh in the morning.*"

"That's the one," says her father, with his eyes shut.

Pearl's found it in the concordance. Her finger's on the line. Marcia sees her read it, and look up at her grandfather, and go very still.

"Grandad," she says. "Is that — are you telling me? That I'll see you again?"

Her father doesn't answer for a long time.

Marcia holds her breath at the window. She knows what he'll say. *That's not mine to say. That's the Lord's.* He's said it to Pearl since she was eight. He's said it at the gravesides of men who didn't come. He's a fair man and a hard one, and he doesn't say things he doesn't believe to make a child feel better.

He opens his eyes and looks at his granddaughter.

"Yes," he says. "I'm telling you."

Pearl's face goes.

"You always said it wasn't yours to say."

"It isn't. It's His." Her father's voice is very low and very steady. "But you've had fifteen years of me saying so. And fifteen of that Englishman saying the other. You've had both of us. You've had as much not-telling as any child alive." He moves his hand on the sheet, a little, towards her. "And I'm ninety, and I'm going, and you're going to Baltimore. And I'll not go without telling you what I know. Not this once. You can go up and down on it after. You'll want something to go up and down *on*."

Pearl takes his hand.

"I'm the back wall, child," says her father. "I told your mother so. Sing against it."

---

He dies on the eleventh of March, at five in the morning, at the manse, with the window open. Marcia's in the chair. She's been in it all night. Aunt Merle's asleep in the next room. Lloyd's on the kitchen floor on the cushions from the settee, because he wouldn't go home.

He doesn't say anything at the end. He's not said anything since the evening before, when he opened his eyes and looked at Marcia and said, quite clearly, "Lucille. The drawer." And then something she couldn't hear.

She sits with his hand in hers while the light comes up grey over Constant Spring Road. She feels it go. Not the hand. The thing in it. She doesn't know the moment. She thinks afterwards she ought to have known, and counted, the way her mother would have. She didn't. She was holding his hand and looking at the window.

At half past five she gets up and goes to wake Lloyd on the kitchen floor.

She doesn't have to say anything. He looks up at her face from the cushions and gets up and puts his arms round her, there in her father's kitchen, under the space on the wall where the cross-stitch used to be. And she thinks, with her face in his shoulder: *weeping may endure for a night*.

It's morning. She doesn't feel the joy. She feels Lloyd's hand on the back of her head, holding it, the way her father held Ike's at the airport.

She thinks that will have to do for now.

---

The drawer is in his desk in the study. She knows which one. The top left, where he kept the things he meant to pray about. She's known since 1968.

She doesn't open it till after the funeral.

The funeral's on the Saturday, in his church, which is full. It's fuller than she's ever seen it, fuller than Christmas, people standing at the back and out on the steps on Constant Spring Road. There are forty years of brides and grooms and the children he christened and the men whose fathers he buried. The young minister from Montego Bay takes it, very well, and is frightened the whole way through.

Ike flies home from London. He plays the church organ, because the hall piano's too flat even for him. Pearl sings. She sings *Abide With Me*, the second verse on her own, to the back wall. Marcia hears it hit the back wall of her father's church and come back. She thinks it comes back a little late, softer, like an echo.

She thinks perhaps it's him.

She doesn't say so to anybody. It's only hoping.

Win comes. Win comes on the boat and the bus and Lloyd's car, on her own, in navy. Reggie's not allowed. He's seventy-nine, and Marguerite's not as strong as she was, and the new man from Port Maria said *no* very firmly, and for once Reggie listened to a doctor who wasn't Marguerite. So Win comes alone. She sits in the second pew with Lloyd's hand in hers, and she doesn't sing the hymns, and at the graveside she stands beside Marcia without saying anything, with her arms folded.

There's a letter from Reggie. It comes the day before the funeral, addressed to Marcia, at the flat. She doesn't open it till the Sunday.

---

On the Sunday evening she goes up to the manse on her own and sits down at her father's desk and opens the top left-hand drawer.

It's very full. It's full of letters and cards and folded papers, forty years of things he meant to pray about. There's a telegram from 1952. There's a child's drawing, crayon, of a church with a tall black man in front of it, signed *Pearl, aged 6*. There's the order of service from her mother's funeral, with a lily pressed in it, gone brown. There's her own note, from 1968, in pencil, on the back of a church notice: *Gone to the Bay. Back tomorrow. Don't fret. M.*

He kept it. She never knew.

And under it, on top of everything, very worn, folded in three and unfolded and folded in three a great many times, there's an envelope addressed to *The Revd. Hinds, the Manse, Constant Spring Road*, in a small neat hand.

She takes the letter out. Her hands aren't steady. She smooths it flat on the blotter.

---

*Harbour House, the Bay. 14th March 1968.*

*Dear Reverend Hinds,*

*Your daughter came on the Port Maria boat this morning on her own. She was sick three times and did not go home. She has told me she means to marry my son, and that you have said no, and why.*

*You are right about his father. You are wrong about my son.*

*I will not tell you Lloyd is charming. He isn't. He doesn't laugh much. He will never in his life owe a man a penny, and he will pay for things that were never his to pay for, and somebody will have to mind him for it. I have not been able to. I think your daughter could. She sat at my kitchen table for an hour and told me more about my son than he has written me in two years, and every word of it was something she had watched.*

*When he was ten I brought him home on the Kingston train, and he cried from Kingston to Port Maria. And when we got here he carried every case up the harbour steps himself, and then he washed the supper things without being asked. He has washed them every night he has been in this house since. His father has never once in his life washed a cup.*

*I have been married twenty-one years to a man who was charming. I would sooner your daughter married one who stays for the washing-up.*

*If you say no, she will marry him anyway. She is the sort. And you will have said no for nothing, and you will have it at your table every Sunday after. I have done that sum. It does not come out.*

*Yours sincerely,*

*W. Carew.*

---

Marcia sits at her father's desk for a long time.

*One who stays for the washing-up.*

She thinks about her mother in the vestry in 1969 with the cherry hat in her lap. *You count who's still there at supper. The ones who stay for the washing-up.* *Lloyd stayed and stacked the chairs. I watched him.*

She'd watched him. Her mother had *watched* him. At the wedding, all through the breakfast, while Mr Carew played and the hall stood up, her mother was watching Lloyd. Because she'd read this letter. Because her father had shown it her, *that's your father's and Mrs Carew's*. And her mother had read what a woman she'd never met said about her son, and gone to the wedding, and checked it.

Down, and then up.

And then she'd come into the vestry and told Marcia, as though it were her own.

Marcia puts her hands over her face at her father's desk.

She's said it to Pearl. *It's who's still there at supper.* She said it in 1980, in her mother's voice, and thought it was her mother's. She's thought for thirty years that she was the only one who knew where it came from. That it was hers, and her mother's, and nobody else's.

It was Win's. Win wrote it first, at a desk at the Bay, with a letter in her apron she couldn't answer. Then her mother read it, and checked it against Lloyd, and found it came out, and gave it to Marcia. And Marcia gave it to Pearl. And none of them, not one, ever said so to any of the others.

*We're a family that doesn't say.*

She laughs. It comes out of her at her father's desk in the empty manse, wet, and it doesn't stop for some time.

---

Reggie's letter is one page.

*Dear Marcia,*

*I'm very sorry. I'd have come. I've been told I'm not to, and I find I've got to the age where I listen.*

*Your father told me in his vestry in 1976 that he'd pray for me every night, and that I couldn't stop him. I didn't try. Pearl tells me he kept it up for twenty-three years. I've no way of returning it that he'd accept, and I'll not insult either of you by pretending to one. But I've sat on this verandah every night since Pearl wrote that he was failing, at the hour she said he'd be saying it, and I've not done anything at all. I've only sat, and been there. I thought he'd understand that. I think it's the nearest thing I've got.*

*He told her things I couldn't. I told her things he wouldn't. I think she's needed both, and I think she knows it. I'm very glad she had him.*

*I'm very glad you did.*

*Reggie.*

*P.S. Win will have told you she's coming alone. She'll not say so, but she minds very much that it's alone. Make her sit down.*

Marcia reads it at the kitchen table in Barbican, with Lloyd across from her. Then she gives it to him and goes out on to the balcony and stands there for a while, looking at Half Way Tree.

---

She tells Win about the letter on the Monday, on the way to the bus for the Bay. Win's going back. Lloyd's carrying her case.

Marcia doesn't plan to. They're walking down Constant Spring Road, past the haberdasher's, and Win stops to look at the sign over the door, *M. Carew. Dressmaking & Bridal*. And Marcia says, before she can think, "Daddy kept your letter. From 1968. In his drawer. I read it last night."

Win stands very still on the pavement.

"Did you," she says.

"*One who stays for the washing-up*."

Win doesn't say anything. She's looking at the sign.

"My mother read it," says Marcia. "Before the wedding. She went to the wedding and watched Lloyd all through the breakfast, to see if it was true. And then she told me. As if it were hers." Her voice goes. "I've been saying it to Pearl since she was nine. I thought it was my mother's."

Win turns her head and looks at her.

"It was your mother's," she says. "If she checked it. That's what makes a thing yours." She looks back at the sign. "It's only an observation till somebody goes back up the column."

Lloyd's stopped a few yards on, with the case. He's looking back at them both, not hearing, with his frown on.

"Did it come out?" says Win. Quite low. "The sum. In the letter. I told your father it wouldn't, if he said no."

Marcia looks at her husband on the pavement with his mother's case in his hand, waiting for them, not hurrying them, the way he's waited at every door for thirty years.

"Thirty years," she says. "It came out."

Win nods. She takes Marcia's arm. They walk on down the road together towards Lloyd, not fast, the way you'd walk with somebody who has a heart.

---

Pearl goes on the third of August.

Sammy comes to fetch her. He flies down from Baltimore on the Friday, for one night, to fly back with her on the Saturday. Pearl says it's a waste of money, she's twenty-eight and perfectly able to get on a plane by herself. Sammy says, in his flat American voice, at the kitchen table, *I know you're able. I wanted to come and get you.* And Pearl goes pink and stops arguing.

Marcia counts that, too.

She's made Pearl a coat. It's the first coat she's ever made in her life. Jamaica doesn't need coats, and she's never had a reason. She's spent the spring finding out how. She wrote to Joy, who sent a pattern from a shop in Kensington and a long letter about Hammersmith winters. She wrote to Sammy's sister Dicey, who sent a postcard of a boat that said *it gets cold, wool, lined, below the knee, Pearl will say it's too long, ignore her*.

It's charcoal wool with a dark red lining, silk, from the last of the bolt from Miami. Below the knee. Pearl says it's too long. Marcia ignores her.

The last of PIN MONEY bought the wool. There's eleven dollars left in the account, and the reference, and the first of the month.

---

At Norman Manley, Pearl hugs Lloyd for a very long time. Lloyd doesn't say anything. He holds her with his eyes shut and his hand on the back of her head. Marcia sees him not say it, whatever it is, the way he's not said things for thirty years. Then he lets go and shakes Sammy's hand, very correctly, the way he'd shake hands with a man at the bank. And then, after a moment, as if he'd thought better of it, he pulls Sammy in and claps him on the back, twice, hard.

Sammy looks astonished. Then he grins.

Pearl turns to Marcia.

She's in the coat, though it's August and ninety degrees in the departure hall, because she says she'll not trust it to a suitcase. She looks like her grandmother in it, tall and straight. She looks like the woman at the desk at Harbour House in 1968, with her hand on her apron pocket.

"Mummy."

"You'll write."

"Every week."

"Not four pages about clients. How you are."

Pearl laughs. "You sound like Grandad."

"I know I do," says Marcia. "Somebody's got to."

She puts her hand on her daughter's face. Then she takes it away and does up the top button of the coat, because it's undone, and she can't bear an undone button on a coat she's made.

"There," she says. "It'll fit. Whatever you do in it, it'll fit."

Pearl puts her arms round her.

She holds on. Marcia holds on back. She smells the wool and the silk lining and Pearl's hair, and she doesn't cry, because she's decided not to, and because Pearl's crying enough for both of them into the shoulder of her good grey dress.

"I'll come back," says Pearl, into her shoulder. "I'll come back the same. Every Christmas. I'll put myself on the list."

"You're on the list," says Marcia. "You've been on it since you were fifteen. *Me.*"

Pearl lifts her head.

"How do you *know* that?"

"Your grandfather told me. You showed him." Marcia smooths the lapel. "He told me most things, in the end. He just took forty years about it." She takes her daughter by the shoulders and turns her round, gently, towards the barrier and Sammy, who's waiting with both cases, not hurrying anybody. "Go on. You'll miss it."

Pearl goes. At the barrier she turns round, in the coat, in August, with her husband beside her. She lifts her hand.

Then she's gone too.

---

Marcia doesn't cry in the car park this time. She waits till they're home.

The flat's empty. Not quiet. Empty. Both rooms across the passage, with the beds made and the windows shut. She stands in the passage between them for a long time.

Lloyd comes and stands beside her.

"Twenty-eight years," he says.

"Twenty-eight."

"We've not been on our own since 1971." He looks at the two doors. "I don't know what we do now."

Marcia thinks about it.

"I expect," she says, "you put the radio on."

He looks at her. Then he goes and puts the radio on.

---

The telephone rings in Mrs Chin's hall at four in the morning on the fourteenth of March 2000. It's never rung at four in the morning in thirty years. Marcia's down the stairs before she's properly awake, in her nightdress. She's got the receiver against her ear while Mrs Chin, who's ninety-two, is still shouting through her door to know who's died.

"Mrs Carew? Marcia? It's Sammy." His voice is very odd. "It's a girl. She's here. Pearl's fine. She's asleep. She went to sleep in the middle of a word." A pause, and a sound she thinks is a laugh. "She's loud. The baby. She's *loud*, Mrs Carew. She came out holding a note."

Marcia sits down on Mrs Chin's bottom stair in the dark with the receiver in both hands.

"What's her name?" she says.

"Iris," says Sammy. "Iris Winifred. After —"

"I know after who," says Marcia. "I know."

---

She flies on the twentieth.

It's the first time she's ever been on an aeroplane. She's fifty years old. She sits in the window seat with her handbag on her knee and her back very straight, in the grey travelling costume she made for it, and when the plane goes up off the runway at Norman Manley she shuts her eyes and holds the armrests and thinks about the Port Maria boat.

She's not sick. She'd have been very surprised if she were. She's been sick off Port Maria four hundred times in thirty years, and her stomach, she's decided, has learnt better.

She paid for the ticket out of PIN MONEY. There wasn't enough. There was eleven dollars and some months of the first of the month since August. Lloyd put the rest in, on the Thursday, without telling her. She found out when she looked at the passbook at the counter in King Street. There was a new line, in the bank's type, *Credit, L. Carew, PIN MONEY*. She stood at the counter and laughed out loud, and the girl looked at her.

He's not coming. Somebody has to mind the bank, and somebody has to mind Mrs Chin, who's ninety-two and has taken to ringing their bell when she can't find her glasses. He'll come at Christmas, he says, when they all come to the Bay. He drove her to the airport and carried her case and shook her hand at the barrier, very correctly. Then he pulled her in and held her for a long time, in front of everybody, and didn't say anything at all.

---

Baltimore's cold. Nobody told her it'd still be cold in March. She stands outside the airport at a place called BWI in her grey travelling costume and the wind comes round the corner and goes straight through her. Sammy, who's come to fetch her, takes off his own coat without a word and puts it round her shoulders.

The row house is very narrow and very tall, three storeys, like a piano stood on end. Pearl's in the front bedroom at the top, in bed, with the baby beside her in a basket. When Marcia comes in, Pearl sits up and says *Mummy* in a voice Marcia's not heard since Port Maria. Then she bursts into tears.

"I know, darling," says Marcia. "I know. It's the third day. It's always the third day." She takes her coat off, Sammy's coat, and sits down on the bed. "Give her here."

Pearl gives her the baby.

She's small. She's so small. Marcia's forgotten. She's got a great deal of black hair and her mouth open and her fists up by her ears, and Lloyd's frown. It's been coming down the family since 1948, and it's turned up on this child as if it had been posted.

Marcia puts her hands under the baby's arms. Both hands. And Iris opens her eyes and looks at her.

She looks at her all at once, and then quite still.

"She's going to decide things," says Marcia. "This one."

"How can you tell?"

"Look at her," says Marcia. "She's deciding now."

She hears her own mother's voice come out of her, in a bedroom in Baltimore, thirty years and a thousand miles off. *She's going to decide things, this one.* She doesn't stop it. She doesn't tell Pearl whose it is.

It's hers. She checked.

---

There are a great many Tillermans.

They come in shifts, the first week. Sammy's grandmother comes up from the farm in a truck driven by Dicey. She's very small and very old, eighty-something, she won't say what, and she looks at Marcia across the narrow kitchen of the row house for a long moment, the way Marcia's father used to look at a new man in the congregation. Down, and then up.

"You'll be the mother," she says.

"I'll be the mother."

"You made the coat. The long one."

"I made the coat."

"She says it's too long." Mrs Tillerman sits down at the kitchen table. "It's not. It's the only sensible thing she's brought with her. You'll have a cup of coffee."

Marcia has a cup of coffee. It's very strong and very bad. She drinks it.

Maybeth comes on the Sunday and plays the upright in the front room, slowly. Pearl comes down the stairs in her dressing gown and sings with her, sitting on the bottom step, holding the baby, very low. The whole house stops in the doorways to listen. Marcia stops too, in the kitchen door with a tea towel over her shoulder.

James comes from the hospital, where he works, in a white coat he's forgotten to take off. He shakes Marcia's hand and asks her how long the flight was, to the minute, and writes it down. Then he goes up and looks at the baby for a long time without saying anything, and comes down looking as if he'd seen something he needed to think about.

"He's the clever one," says Sammy, in the kitchen, very proud, as if James weren't standing right there.

"I'm the one who worries," says James.

Marcia looks from one to the other. She thinks about Lloyd and Joy, and Pearl and Ike, and what it is to have a brother. She thinks this family talks about each other the way Pearl talks about hers. She's heard Pearl do it on the stairs at Mrs Chin's for three years, every Sunday, very low.

She thinks Pearl has married into a family that *says*. All of it, at the table, out loud, in parts.

She doesn't know what she thinks about that. She thinks it'll take her some time.

---

There's one thing she sees in Baltimore that she doesn't tell anybody, not Pearl, not Lloyd on the telephone, not Joy.

It's the second week. She takes the baby out in the pram, because Pearl's asleep and somebody has to, along the street with trees, to the shop on the corner for milk. It's a grey morning and very cold. The woman at the counter is white, and young, and not unkind. She looks at the pram, and at the baby in it, pink and cross with a great deal of black hair. Then she looks at Marcia.

And Marcia sees her work it out, and get it wrong. She sees her decide that Marcia's the woman who minds the baby.

"Isn't she sweet," says the woman. "Whose is she?"

"She's my granddaughter," says Marcia.

The woman's face does a thing. It's very quick. Then it's gone, and she's smiling, and she says *oh, how lovely*, and gives Marcia her change.

Marcia walks back along the street with trees with the pram and the milk. She's not angry. She was angry, she thinks, for about the length of the counter. She's not now.

She's thinking about Pearl.

Pearl, who'll walk to this shop every week for years with this child. And with the next, and the next, if there are more. Pearl will see that face do that thing at a great many counters. Pearl married a man who'll never see it done to him, and who'll stand beside her every time it is, and not know what's happened unless she tells him.

She thinks: *she knows*. Pearl's not a fool. She's checked. She'll have done the column on that before she ever got on the plane, down and up. It'll have come out, or she'd not be here.

And she thinks: *I'll not say anything*. It's not hers.

But at the bottom of the steps of the row house, she stops, and looks up at it, three storeys, narrow, like a piano. She thinks about Win in 1968 at the desk at Harbour House. A frightened girl in a grey costume at the door, and Win with her hand on her apron, saying *come into the kitchen*. Asking every question. Listening to every answer. Writing to a man she'd never met to tell him what she'd seen.

She thinks she'll write to Sammy's grandmother. When she's home. Not about this. About the coat, perhaps. About the length. About how to keep a baby warm in a Baltimore winter, which she doesn't know and that old woman will.

She'll ask every question. She'll listen to every answer.

That's the nearest thing she's got.

---

On the last night Pearl comes down to the kitchen at two in the morning with the baby, because the baby's awake and won't settle. She finds Marcia at the table, awake too, because the house is too quiet and she's not used to the radiators.

Pearl sits down across from her with Iris against her shoulder. The baby hums. It's very small and very low, against Pearl's neck, and quite tuneless, and it goes on.

"She does that," says Pearl. "Maybeth says she'll hum in tune by the time she's two. James says that's not how it works."

"Your father's sister-in-law says," says Marcia, "and your grandfather says, and your husband's brother says." She smiles. "You've married into a great many people who say."

"I know." Pearl rocks. "Is that all right?"

Marcia looks at her daughter across the kitchen table in Baltimore at two in the morning, in Sammy's old dressing gown, with Lloyd's frown and Win's straight back and Marcia's own way of holding a baby, under the arms.

"Grandad said I was to go," says Pearl, before she can answer. "He told me. He said *you'll go*, and *you'll not stay*. He *told* me." She's looking at the baby. "And then he said I'd see him again. He said *I'm telling you*. He'd never told me that, Mummy. Not once. Not in twenty-seven years."

"I know. I was at the window."

"I've been going up and down on it ever since. Like he said." Pearl's quiet. "I sang *Abide With Me* at the funeral. To the back wall. And it came back late. Softer. And I thought —" She stops.

"I know," says Marcia. "I heard it too."

Pearl looks up.

"You thought it was him."

"I hoped it was," says Marcia. "That's all."

Pearl's quiet a long time. The baby hums.

"I've not put the Lord on the list," she says at last. "Grandad told me to, when I was fourteen. I said I'd put Him on when I'd seen it twice." She rocks the baby. "I've seen it once. At the funeral. I think. Maybe."

"Then you'll wait," says Marcia. "For the second."

"And if it doesn't come?"

"Then you'll have hoped," says Marcia. "That's allowed. Your grandfather said so." She gets up. She takes the baby, gently, under the arms, and holds her against her own shoulder, and the humming goes on against her neck. "Go to bed, darling. I've got her. It's my last night. Let me."

Pearl stands up. She doesn't go. She stands in the kitchen of the row house in her husband's dressing gown, and looks at her mother holding her daughter.

"Mummy," she says. "You never went anywhere. My whole life."

"No."

"And now you've come all this way. On a *plane*."

Marcia rocks the baby.

"Well," she says. "Somebody had to bring the coat for the next one."

Pearl laughs, wet, and goes up the narrow stairs. Marcia hears her go, all three storeys, like somebody going up the inside of a piano.

She sits down at the kitchen table in Baltimore with Iris Winifred Tillerman asleep on her shoulder, humming. She doesn't sleep. She doesn't want to. She sits there till the window goes grey over the street with trees, and counts the baby's breaths, under her own, the sea going up and the sea going down.

Somebody taught that to Pearl. Pearl taught it to her, on the telephone, years ago, without knowing she was doing it.

She doesn't know who taught it to Reggie. She's never asked. It's his.

At six the radiators knock, and the baby wakes, and looks at her, all at once, and then quite still.

"Good morning," says Marcia.
