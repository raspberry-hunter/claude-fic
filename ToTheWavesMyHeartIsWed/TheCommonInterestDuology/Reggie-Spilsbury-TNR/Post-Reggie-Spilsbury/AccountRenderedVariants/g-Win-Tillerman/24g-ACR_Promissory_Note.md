# Promissory Note

Ike wakes up because the house is breathing differently.

He doesn't know that's why. He's five. He only knows he's awake, in the little room at the back of Harbour House that Grandma calls seven, and it's grey at the window. Pearl's asleep in the other bed with her mouth open. And downstairs somebody's opened a door very quietly, and the house has let a breath out.

He gets up. He goes down the back stairs in his pyjamas with his hand on the rail, one step at a time, the way he's been told, and out through the kitchen, where the stove's cold. Then through the yard, past the generator shed, which is shut, and across the road to the top of the harbour steps.

Reggie's halfway down them.

He's going very slowly, with one hand on the rail and the other holding a string, and on the end of the string there's a thermometer. He gets to the bottom step, where the water is, and lets the thermometer down into the sea on the string. Then he stands there, quite still, with his head bent, looking at his watch.

Ike sits down on the top step.

He doesn't say anything. He's not sure he's allowed to be here. But Reggie looks up, and sees him, and doesn't say *go back to bed*, or *what are you doing up*, or any of the things Mummy would say. He just looks at him for a moment, and then looks back at his watch.

After a long time he pulls the string up and holds the thermometer close to his glasses, and says, to nobody in particular, "Twenty-eight and a half." He writes it in a little book from his pocket. Then he comes back up the steps, slowly, and sits down on the top one, next to Ike, a bit out of breath.

They sit there. The sea's grey and flat, and the boats are going out round the head, and the sky over the cay's going pink.

"Every day?" says Ike.

"Every day. Since 1952."

Ike thinks about that. He doesn't know how long since 1952 is. He knows it's before Daddy.

"Why?"

"To see if it's changed."

"Has it?"

"A bit," says Reggie. "Not much. Half a degree, perhaps, in thirty years. You'd not notice it, in the water. You'd only notice it in the book."

Ike looks at the sea. It's going up the steps. He can see it, very slowly, a little way over the weed, and then a little way back, and then over again.

"It goes up and down," he says. "Like the float. In the generator."

"It does."

"What makes it?"

"The moon, mostly," says Reggie. "The moon pulls on it. It goes round the earth and it pulls the sea after it, and the sea comes up the steps and goes down again. Twice a day."

Ike thinks about the moon pulling the sea up the harbour steps. He can see it. He can see the sea being pulled, like the sheet when Grandma pulls it off the line, all of it coming at once.

"What makes the moon go round?"

"The earth pulls on it," says Reggie. "The same way. Everything pulls on everything. That's what keeps it all going round."

"What makes it pull?"

Reggie's quiet for a moment.

"Nobody knows," he says. "Not properly. It's called gravity. We know how much it pulls, very exactly, and which way. We don't know why it pulls at all. It just does."

Ike looks at the sea. He thinks about it for a long time.

"No," he says. "Somebody."

He doesn't say it to argue. He doesn't know what arguing is, not properly; that's Pearl's. He says it the way he'd say the float sticks, or the top of the church piano's flat. Because it does, and it is.

Reggie turns his head and looks at him.

He doesn't say *no, nobody*. He doesn't say *your grandfather Hinds would agree with you*, though Ike will think later that he must have wanted to. He just looks at Ike for a long moment, the way he looks at the float when it's sticking and he's not sure why.

"All right," he says. "Somebody. Would you like to see the book?"

Ike would. He looks at the book, which is full of numbers in very small writing, all the way down, every page. Reggie shows him where today is, *28.5*, at the bottom. And then he turns back, a long way back, to the front, where the writing's different, younger, and the numbers are the same.

"Every day," says Ike again.

"Every day."

"Even when you're sick?"

"Your grandmother does it, when I'm sick. She doesn't hold with it. She does it anyway."

Ike looks at the book, and then at the sea, going up the steps and coming down. He thinks that's what *every day* means. It means somebody goes down the steps, even when they're sick, even when they don't hold with it, and puts the string in.

He doesn't know yet that he'll think about it for the rest of his life.

---

Pearl's crying in the night. It's March 1985, the flat in Barbican, and Ike's seven. He wakes up because her bed's creaking across the room, the way it creaks when she's trying not to make a noise.

He lies there for a while. He's not good at crying, other people's. He never knows what to do. Mummy does, and Daddy does in a different way, by bringing a cup of something and sitting on the end of the bed. Ike doesn't know either way. So he lies there and listens to Pearl not making a noise for a long time.

Then he says, "Is it the confirming?"

The creaking stops.

"Go to sleep, Ike."

"Is it?"

There's a long silence across the dark room.

"You have to stand up," says Pearl, "and say you believe it. All of it. In front of Grandad. And I don't *know* it, Ike. I've been going up and down on it for two years and it won't come out." Her voice goes. "How can I say *I believe* if I don't know?"

Ike lies on his back and looks at the ceiling. There's a streetlamp outside, and it makes a square on the ceiling with the window bars across it.

He doesn't understand. He's been trying to understand Pearl his whole life, and mostly he can't. She goes up and down on everything. She writes it in the book. She checks. She cried for a week about the calf's mother. Ike was sorry too, because Reggie was sorry, but he didn't cry, because he knew where the calf's mother had gone. He didn't need to go up and down.

"I know it," he says.

The creaking starts again. Pearl's sat up.

"You *what*?"

"I know it. The resurrection and that." He's not sure what the resurrection is. He knows it's in the creed and it's good. "I know it's true."

"*How*?" says Pearl. It comes out loud. Then she brings it right down, because of Mummy. "How can you *know*? You're *seven*. Grandad's ninety, nearly, and he says he doesn't know it every Sunday. Reggie says it isn't true at all. Mummy hopes. How can *you* know?"

Ike thinks about it. He thinks very hard, because Pearl's asked him properly and she's crying.

"I don't know how," he says. "I just do."

"That's not an *answer*."

"It's like —" He tries. "It's like when the top of the piano's flat. Mrs Beckford's piano. You don't *work it out*. You just hear it. You put your finger down and you hear it's wrong." He's quiet. "It's like that. Only right."

Pearl doesn't say anything for a long time.

"I can't hear it," she says at last. Very small.

"I know." Ike turns over on his side, towards her. "You can't hear the flat either. Not properly. You only know it's flat because I told you."

There's a silence. Then Pearl laughs. It's wet, and it surprises both of them.

"That's not *fair*," she says.

"No," says Ike. "But it's true."

She lies down again. After a while the creaking stops, and her breathing goes long, and she's asleep. Ike lies awake a bit longer, looking at the square on the ceiling. He wonders why it's so hard for Pearl and so easy for him. He decides it's like the piano. Some people hear it and some people don't, and the ones who don't have to do it with columns.

He thinks that's probably harder. He thinks Pearl's probably braver.

He doesn't tell her that. He goes to sleep.

There isn't a piano in the flat. There's never been room. So from the time he's eight, Daddy walks him up to the church hall every evening at six.

Every evening. Monday to Friday, and Saturday mornings for Mrs Beckford. Daddy comes in from the bank at a quarter to six and puts his briefcase down by the door and doesn't take his jacket off. He says *ready?* and Ike says *ready*, and they go down Mrs Chin's stairs and up Constant Spring Road, a mile and a bit, with Daddy's hand on the back of Ike's neck at the crossings.

Daddy has a key. Grandad Hinds had it cut for him. He opens the hall and puts the lights on, the two at the front, not the ones at the back, because the church pays for those. Then he goes and sits in the back row.

He sits there for an hour. He has the *Gleaner* with him. He reads it, or Ike thinks he reads it. He never says anything about the playing. Not once. Not *that was nice*, or *that bit's better*, or *what's that one called*. He sits at the back with the paper while Ike does his scales and his Czerny and his Clementi. When the hour's up he folds the paper, and stands, and says *ready?*, and they walk home.

Ike doesn't think about it. It's just what happens. It's like the tide.

He's fifteen before he thinks about it.

It's a Wednesday in October 1993. He's doing the Chopin for the exam, the nocturne, the one with the long line in the right hand that has to sing. He's been at it for three weeks and it won't. It's all right. It's not singing. And he gets to the end of it for the fifth time that evening, and stops with his hands in his lap, and swears. Out loud, in the church hall. A word he got off a boy at school.

And from the back row, without the paper moving, Daddy says, "The left hand."

Ike turns round on the bench.

Daddy's sitting in the back row with the *Gleaner* up. He doesn't lower it.

"What?"

"The left hand's too loud," says Daddy, from behind the paper. "In the middle. It's been too loud since Monday. It's sitting on top of the other one. The other one can't get out from under it."

Ike stares at the *Gleaner*.

He turns back to the piano. He plays the middle again with the left hand down, very soft, hardly there. And the right hand comes up out of it, all on its own, as if somebody had taken a weight off it. It sings.

He sits there with his hands on the keys.

"Daddy."

"Mm."

"You can't hold a tune."

"No," says Daddy.

"You can't tell *Stardust* from *Abide With Me*. Mummy says."

"I can tell them," says Daddy. "I can't sing them." The paper doesn't move. "I've been sitting here seven years, Ike. Five nights a week. I'd have to be a fool not to have learnt *something*."

Ike gets up off the bench. He walks down the hall to the back row. Daddy lowers the paper, finally, and looks up at him, with his frown on and his reading glasses down his nose.

"Why do you sit at the back?" says Ike.

Daddy thinks about it.

"In case," he says.

"In case what?"

"In case you look round."

Ike doesn't understand. "Mrs Beckford says you're not supposed to look round. She says if you look round you've lost it."

"I know," says Daddy. "You never have. Seven years." He folds the paper. "I'm here anyway. In case." He stands up. "Ready?"

They walk home. Daddy's hand's on the back of Ike's neck at the crossing at Half Way Tree, though Ike's fifteen and nearly as tall as he is and could perfectly well cross a road. Ike doesn't say anything about it. He doesn't want to.

He gets a distinction in the exam. The examiner writes, beside the nocturne, *a singing line, very well balanced*. Ike shows it to Daddy at the kitchen table. Daddy reads it, and nods, and gives it back. Then he goes into the bedroom. When he comes out he's got a folder, a cardboard one from the bank, and he puts the examiner's sheet in it, at the back, behind a great many other sheets.

Ike looks at the folder.

It's every programme. Every recital since he was six. Every exam sheet, every certificate, every cutting from the *Gleaner* with his name in it, the church newsletter from 1984 that says *Master Isaac Carew (6) played*. In order. With the dates written on in Daddy's small careful hand.

Daddy puts the folder back in the bedroom. He doesn't say anything about it, then or ever.

Ike doesn't either. But the next night, at the church hall, when he's done the nocturne and it sings, he looks round. Just once, on purpose, at the back row.

Daddy's got the *Gleaner* up. But Ike can see his hand on the edge of it, holding on.

The summer he's fifteen, he asks Reggie.

Not on purpose. He's on the cay, in August, at the north pen, with Reggie on the bench by the rail and the calf's calf going round and round. He's been sent across with the Institute's post because he's the one who isn't busy. Reggie asked him to stay and count, so he's counting. He's not as good at it as Pearl. He loses track when she goes under, because he starts listening to the water instead.

"Pearl says you know," says Reggie. "About God."

Ike stops counting.

"She wrote me," says Reggie. "In 1985. When she was confirmed. She said she couldn't hear it and you could, and you'd said it was like the flat at the top of the piano." He's looking at the pen, not at Ike. "I've been meaning to ask you for eight years. I've not found a proper time. I don't think there is one, for this. So I'm asking now."

Ike sits very still on the bench.

He's never talked about it to anybody. Not since that night in Barbican. Pearl doesn't ask any more. She goes to church and sings to the back wall and goes up and down. Grandad Hinds never asks, because he doesn't need to. Ike's never thought Reggie would.

"I don't know how," says Ike. "I told Pearl that."

"I know. I don't want how." Reggie turns his head and looks at him. "I'd only like to know what it's like. If you'd tell me. I've never been able to hear it. I'd like to know what it is I can't hear."

Ike looks at the pen.

He thinks about it the way he'd think about a bit of music he can play but can't explain. He thinks about the steps at the Bay at six in the morning, and the sea going up, and *somebody*. And the hall in the evening, when the line sings.

He reaches into his pocket.

He's got the fork there. The A. He's always got it there. He's had it since 1984, when Reggie took it out of his own pocket in the bar with the front off the piano and said *you can borrow it*. Ike has never given it back. He takes it out now and taps it on his knee, the way Reggie showed him. He holds it up between them, and it hums. Very small, very high, and quite steady, going on and on in the air over the pen.

"Like that," says Ike.

Reggie looks at the fork.

"You hit it," says Ike, "and it's the A. You don't work out that it's the A. You don't go up and down. It just is. And then you tune everything else to it. And if something's not right, you can hear it's not right, because the A's there underneath." He watches the fork. The hum's going. "It's like that. There's an A. Under everything. I can hear it. I don't know how. I just always could."

The fork stops humming.

Reggie doesn't say anything for a long time. He's looking at the fork in Ike's hand, and his face is doing the thing it does when he's thinking very hard about something he can't see.

"I can't hear it," he says at last. Quite simply.

"I know," says Ike. "It's all right."

"Is it?"

"You can't carry a tune either," says Ike. "Grandma won't let you sing in the house. And you've been to every single thing I've ever played."

Reggie looks at him. Then he laughs. It's the real one, the one that comes up out of him all at once, that Ike's only heard a few times. Grandma says it was the first thing she ever heard out of him that she liked. He laughs on the bench at the north pen until he has to take his glasses off.

"That's not an argument, Ike," he says, when he can. "Your sister would tear it to pieces."

"I'm not arguing," says Ike. "I'm just telling you."

Reggie puts his glasses back on. He looks at the fork, and then at Ike, for a long moment.

"Keep it," he says.

"I've *got* it. You lent it me."

"I know I did. Keep it anyway." He turns back to the pen. "Thirty-two. You lost count. She's been under thirty-two seconds."

Ike didn't lose count. He just wasn't counting. He puts the fork back in his pocket and starts again.

He doesn't play at Aunt Marguerite's funeral.

He's home that August, 2001, the first summer since he finished at the College. He's working at a school in Fulham, three days a week, and accompanying a choral society in Hammersmith on Thursday nights, round the corner from Auntie Joy's. He's come home for a month, the way he's promised Mummy, with the tailcoat in his case. The tailcoat needs letting out at the waist, two inches, which Mummy does at the kitchen table on the first night without saying a word, though her mouth goes in at the corner.

He's at the Bay the week Aunt Marguerite dies. He offers to play. Uncle Pascual says no.

He doesn't say it unkindly. He puts his hand on Ike's shoulder, in the kitchen at Harbour House, the morning after, and says, *no, hijo, gracias. She delivered the Kilburn boy. He'll play the harmonium. Badly. She'd want him.* And Ike understands, and doesn't mind. He sits in the third pew with Mummy and Daddy and listens to old Mrs Kilburn's great-grandson play the harmonium very badly, and thinks it's exactly right.

He goes up the hill on the Wednesday.

Nobody sends him. He goes because Reggie went up on the Sunday, on his own, and it took him three-quarters of an hour, and Grandma's face when he came back down was a face Ike doesn't want to see again. So Ike thinks somebody else should go. Somebody who can do the hill.

Uncle Pascual's on the back verandah, in the chair that faces the cay. There's a bottle on the table and two glasses. One's full and hasn't been touched.

"Isaac." He doesn't get up. "Sit down. Have Marguerite's."

Ike sits. He doesn't drink it. He doesn't much like rum. He holds the glass.

They sit there. It's very hot, and the cay's very clear across the water, the long row of huts and the north pen and the jetty. Uncle Pascual doesn't say anything for a long time, and neither does Ike. He's good at that. He's had practice. He's sat in church halls, and on harbour steps, and at the north pen counting.

"Your grandfather," says Uncle Pascual eventually. "He came up the hill. Did they tell you?"

"Grandma told me."

"Forty-five minutes." Uncle Pascual shakes his head. "Eighty-two. With his stick. And he held out his wrist to me." He drinks. "*Somebody's got to count it, hombre.*"

Ike doesn't say anything.

"He thinks she's gone," says Uncle Pascual. "Reggie. He thinks she's stopped. He's always thought that, about everybody. He'll have sat there counting my pulse and thinking, *she's stopped*." He looks at the cay. "And being sorry for me. That I think otherwise."

Ike holds the glass.

"Do you?" he says. "Think otherwise?"

Uncle Pascual turns his head and looks at him. He's very old now. He's eighty-six, and his hair's gone white, and his eyes have gone pale. But they look at Ike exactly the way they did when he was six and took the front off the piano. As if Ike had said something worth hearing.

"Yes," says Uncle Pascual. "I think she's somewhere. I think she's somewhere telling somebody their pulse is too high." He nearly smiles. "And you?"

"Yes," says Ike.

"How do you know?"

"I don't know how," says Ike. "I never have."

Uncle Pascual looks at him a long moment. Then he laughs, short, and puts his glass down.

"*Ni yo*," he says. "Nor me. Seventy years I've been going to Mass. I've never known how." He looks back at the cay. "Marguerite used to say I was a peasant. She'd say, *Pascual, you believe things like a man believes in rain*. She said it like it was an insult. It wasn't. She knew it wasn't." He's quiet. "She wanted to believe like that. She never could. She was a doctor. She went up and down, like your sister."

"Pearl hopes," says Ike.

"So did Marguerite. Hoping's harder." Uncle Pascual picks his glass up again. "I'd sooner do it our way. It's easier on the knees."

They sit there. The sun goes over. The cay goes gold, and then pink.

"I'll tell you a thing," says Uncle Pascual. He's not looking at Ike. He's looking at the far end of the cay, past the north pen, where the last hut in the row is. "Since you're here. And since you're the one who hears it."

Ike waits.

"There was a lamp," says Uncle Pascual. "At the end of the row. In that hut. For fourteen years. From 1957. Every night." He turns his glass round. "I filled it. Every week. Paraffin, from the store. Nobody asked me to. I never once said why. And the man it was for never once asked me." He's quiet. "It was for a friend. Somebody who couldn't come. I don't say who. It's not mine."

Ike sits very still.

"I'm telling you because I prayed for it," says Uncle Pascual. "Every night. In Spanish. While I was filling it. For the man who couldn't come, and the man who lit it. Fourteen years. And it went out in 1971 and I stopped filling it, and I never stopped praying." He drinks. "And I've never told anybody that. Not Marguerite. Not him. He'd have —" He stops. "He'd have minded. Not the praying. Being known."

"I'll not tell him," says Ike.

"I know you won't. That's why I'm telling you." Uncle Pascual puts his glass down on the table. "You're the one in that family who knows things and doesn't say. Your sister says everything. Your mother says nothing. You *know*, and you keep it. Like your grandfather. The other one, I mean. The minister." He looks at Ike. "Drink Marguerite's. She'd be furious it's going to waste."

Ike drinks it. It burns all the way down. He coughs, and Uncle Pascual laughs at him, properly, for the first time all week.

He walks down the hill in the dark, slowly. At the bottom he looks back across the harbour at the cay. There are lights in some of the huts. The last one in the row is dark.

He doesn't ask anybody about it. Not Reggie, not Grandma. It's not his.

But he keeps it.

Daddy meets him at the airport in Kingston on the second of August 2003, the way he always does, and puts his case in the boot of the car. And then, instead of driving home to Barbican, he drives out on to the Junction road, north, towards the Bay.

"Daddy, Mummy said —"

"Your mother's coming on the bus tomorrow. She said you'd want to go straight there." Daddy's watching the road. "You'll want to go straight there."

Ike doesn't say anything. He knows about the bad night. Pearl rang him in July, crying, from the farm. He's been on the telephone to Grandma every Sunday since, from the box on the Fulham Palace Road, three minutes at a time, because Grandma rings off at three minutes whatever's happening. She says Reggie's well. She says he's careful. She says Dr Lindo said *tired*.

He knows what *tired* means. He's twenty-five. He's not a fool.

They drive for an hour without saying anything. Daddy doesn't put the radio on. Ike watches the hills go by, green, very close, and the river at the bottom of the gorge.

At Castleton, where the road goes over the bridge, Daddy pulls over. He stops the car on the verge, under a tree, with the engine running. He reaches into his inside pocket.

He takes out an envelope. Old, gone soft at the corners, with the bank's frank on it, from 1997. He holds it out across the gearstick without looking at Ike.

"You should know," says Daddy. "Before."

Ike takes it.

It's a letter. One page. In the small neat hand, a bit less shaky than it is now, dated July 1997. It says *Dear Lloyd*. It says *I'd like to pay it*. It says *Not lend. Pay.*

It says *the boy who took the front off the bar piano with me when he was six*.

Ike reads it twice. Then he sits in the car under the tree at Castleton with the engine running and the letter in his lap, and doesn't say anything at all.

Six years. Three years of fees at the College, and then the postgraduate year, which he'd thought was a bursary. He'd thought it was all a bursary. He'd thought it was the College, and the scholarship, and Daddy, somehow, at the bank. He'd never asked. Daddy had said *it's seen to* in September 1997, at the kitchen table, and Ike hadn't asked, because you didn't, in his family. You let things be seen to.

"He said you weren't to know," says Daddy. He's still looking at the road. "He said you'd feel you had to thank him, and you'd do it badly. And so would he."

"I would," says Ike. His voice has gone odd.

"I know you would. You're mine." Daddy puts his hands on the wheel. "I'd have kept it. I've kept it six years. But he's —" He stops. "You should know. Before. I'd want to. If it were me."

Ike looks at his father. Daddy's fifty-five. He's gone grey all over. He's sitting very straight behind the wheel of the car under a tree at Castleton, with his frown on, looking at a road with nothing on it.

"Did you want him to?" says Ike. "Pay?"

Daddy's quiet a long time.

"No," he says. "I said no. Your grandfather Hinds made me." Something happens at the corner of his mouth. "He said a man who can't be given anything can't be loved. He said it in the manse kitchen, in front of your mother and Aunt Merle, and Aunt Merle asked if I wanted more tea." He shakes his head. "I wrote and said thank you. It was two words. It was the hardest letter I ever wrote."

Ike folds the letter back into its envelope. He holds it out.

"Keep it," says Daddy. "It's about you. It's yours." He puts the car in gear. "Don't tell him I showed you. Or do. You'll know which."

He pulls out on to the road.

They drive the rest of the way without saying anything. At the top of the last hill, where you come round the bend and see the Bay all at once, the harbour and the steps and Harbour House pale above them and the cay across the water, Daddy takes one hand off the wheel. He puts it on the back of Ike's neck, the way he did at the crossings at Half Way Tree, and leaves it there all the way down the hill.

Ike doesn't say anything about it. He doesn't want to.

---

Reggie's on the verandah.

He's in his chair, with his feet on the stool and a rug over his knees, though it's August and ninety in the shade. He's very thin. He's much thinner than at Christmas. His face has gone in round the eyes and the bones of his hands show through, white, on the arms of the chair. But he looks up when Ike comes up the stairs, and his face does the thing.

All at once, and then quite still.

"Ike," he says.

"Reggie."

"You've grown."

"I haven't. I'm twenty-five. I stopped growing years ago."

"Your mother says men spread," says Reggie. "She told me so in 1997. I've been watching for it ever since." He looks Ike up and down. "You've spread. About an inch. At the waist. She'll have let the coat out."

"Two inches," says Ike, "in 2001," and Reggie laughs.

It's the real one. It's thinner than it was. It comes up out of him all at once and stops sooner. But it's the real one.

Ike sits down in the other chair. Grandma's chair. Grandma's in the kitchen doing the fish. He can hear her through the floor, telling the girl about the pepper.

They sit there. The harbour's very blue. A boat's coming in round the head with the sail down and the motor going. Reggie watches it. Ike watches Reggie watch it.

He's got the letter in his pocket. He can feel it there, against the A.

He doesn't take it out. Not yet.

He stays three weeks.

He doesn't do very much. Nobody does very much. Reggie's not allowed to. He's to sit, Dr Lindo says, and take one before he gets out of the chair, and not go across to the cay on his own. So he sits. He sits on the verandah in the mornings and watches the boats, and in the afternoons he sleeps, and in the evenings he sits again and watches the lamps come on at the Institute across the water.

Ike sits with him.

He brings his music, the scores for the autumn. He sits in Grandma's chair with them on his knee, marking fingerings in pencil, and Reggie sits in his chair with his eyes half shut. Every so often Reggie says *what's that one*, and Ike tells him, and Reggie says *play me a bit*, and Ike goes down to the bar and plays a bit on the old upright, the one with the front that comes off. Reggie listens through the floor. When Ike comes back up Reggie says *the bit in the middle* or *the bit where it went up*, always the bit Ike was worried about, and Ike stops being surprised by that.

In the mornings, at six, he goes down the harbour steps with the string.

Reggie's not allowed down the steps. He's not been allowed since July. So Ike goes, and Reggie sits on the bench at the top with the book on his knee and his watch in his hand, and calls the time. Ike stands on the bottom step with the sea round his ankles and the thermometer on the string, and waits, with his head bent, the way he watched Reggie do it when he was five. Then he pulls it up and reads it, close, and calls it up the steps.

"Twenty-eight."

"Twenty-eight," says Reggie, and writes it down.

Grandma did it in July. She said Ike's to do it while he's here. She says she never held with it and her knees don't hold with the steps, and Ike's are younger. Ike thinks that's not the reason. He thinks Grandma wants Reggie to have the three weeks of watching somebody else do it, from the top, and calling the time. The way he did for fifty years, and nobody watched.

---

On the second Tuesday the generator stops.

It's the float. It's always the float. Ike hears it go from the bar, the long dying cough and then nothing, and every light in the house goes out at once. Grandma in the kitchen says something she'd not say in church. Reggie on the verandah says *Ike*, in a voice that's very nearly glad.

They go down together. Reggie on Ike's arm, very slowly, one stair at a time, with a stop on the landing. Grandma comes out of the kitchen with a tea towel and says *Reginald Tar, you'll not*, and Reggie says *I'll sit on a chair in the door, Win, I'll not touch it*, and Grandma looks at him a long time and then goes and fetches a chair.

So Reggie sits in the door of the generator shed on a kitchen chair, with the rug over his knees, and Ike takes the cover off.

He's done it a hundred times. He did it the first time at three, with Reggie's hand over his. He's done it every summer since, and written about it in ninety-odd letters to the Bay. He knows exactly what's wrong before he's got the cover off. He gets the float chamber open and looks at it, and there it is, sticking, the way it's been sticking since 1959.

He doesn't hit it. He could. Grandma hits it, with the heel of her hand, and it goes. But he takes it out instead, and cleans it, very carefully, and looks at the needle, and the seat, and the pin. Reggie watches him from the door.

"There's a burr," says Ike. "On the pin. Look. That's why it catches."

He holds it out. Reggie takes it, with his white thin hand, and holds it up to his glasses, close.

"So there is," he says.

"It's been there forty years, probably."

"Forty-four," says Reggie. He's looking at the pin. "Your grandmother bought it second-hand in 1959. I've had it apart a dozen times. I never saw that." He turns it over. "I never looked. I always hit it."

"Everybody always hit it."

"Yes," says Reggie. "That's what you do, with a thing that sticks. You hit it, and it goes, and you don't look why." He gives the pin back. "You looked."

Ike takes it. He gets a fine file from the box and takes the burr off, very gently, three strokes. He puts it back. He puts the float back. He closes the chamber and pulls the cord.

The generator goes. It goes first time, smooth, without the cough, and every light in Harbour House comes on at once. Upstairs they hear Grandma say *well*.

Reggie sits in the door of the shed in the noise of it with the light coming through the window on him. He doesn't say anything. He's looking at Ike, the way he looked at him on the harbour steps when he was five and said *somebody*.

"It'll not stick now," says Ike, over the noise.

"No," says Reggie. "I don't suppose it will." And then, so quietly Ike nearly doesn't hear it: "I'd have liked to see that. When I was your age. Somebody looking."

---

On the last Sunday he goes to church with Grandma.

Reggie doesn't. He's not been since July, not even to the chapel, which is two hundred yards. Grandma goes on her own, every Sunday, the way she has since 1958. And this Sunday Ike goes with her. He walks her down the road on his arm, because she's seventy-eight and won't take a stick. He sits beside her in the second pew in the chapel at the Bay, and the young minister preaches on Lazarus, and Grandma sits very straight beside him and doesn't sing.

Ike sings. He's got a voice like Daddy's, which is to say no voice at all. He sings anyway, under his breath, the way you do.

And in the last hymn, *Abide With Me*, the third verse, he stops singing. He just stands there with the book open.

Because it's there. The A. Under everything, the way it always has been, in the chapel at the Bay, with the harmonium flat and the Kilburn great-grandson pumping too hard. He can hear it under all of it, steady, the way he's heard it since he was five on the harbour steps at six in the morning. And he thinks of Reggie on the verandah, two hundred yards off, in his chair. Who can't hear it. Who's never once been able to hear it. Who's going to find out, one way or the other, very soon.

He stands in the chapel with his book open and finds he's praying. Not words. He's never been much good at the words; that's Grandad Hinds's, and Uncle Pascual's in Spanish. He's just holding it out. The way you'd hold the fork up between you and somebody, and let it hum, and hope they'd hear it.

Grandma puts her hand on his arm.

He looks down at her. She's looking at the front of the chapel, very straight, not at him. But her hand's on his arm, and it stays there to the end of the hymn.

---

That evening, on the verandah, he gives Reggie the letter.

He doesn't plan it. He's been carrying it three weeks. He's got the boat tomorrow at eight, and the flight on Tuesday, and he's still not taken it out. And then, at nine, with the day book done and Grandma gone down to lock the back, Reggie says, "Your father showed you something. In the car. At Castleton."

Ike goes very still.

"Your grandmother says you came in with a face," says Reggie. He's looking at the cay. "And you've had your hand on your pocket for three weeks. I know what it is. I wrote it." He doesn't turn his head. "I asked him not to."

"He said you'd say that."

"Did he." Reggie's quiet. "He's a good man, your father. He's the best man I know at doing a thing without saying it. I asked him for one thing in 1997 and he's kept it six years. And then he broke it at Castleton, because he thought you'd want to know before." He turns his head now and looks at Ike. "He was right. I'd have wanted to know. I always do. I don't know why I thought you'd be different."

Ike takes the letter out of his pocket. He holds it in both hands.

"I'm going to do it badly," he says.

"I know you are."

"You said I would. In the letter. You said I'd do it badly and so would you."

"I did," says Reggie. "I was right. Go on, then."

Ike looks at the letter. He tries.

"Thank you," he says. "For the College. And the year after. I thought it was a bursary. I never — I'd never have —" He stops. It's not right. It's coming out like a scale, every note in the right place and nothing singing. "I'd not be anything. Without. I'd be at the bank. I'd be —" He stops again.

Reggie waits.

He just waits. He doesn't help. He doesn't say *it's all right* or *I know* or *say no more*. He sits in his chair with his hands on the arms and his face turned to Ike, and waits, the way he's waited since Ike was five, for the whole of the answer.

And Ike puts the letter down on his knee and says the other thing instead. The thing that isn't thank you. The thing that's been under the thank you for three weeks, under everything, like the A.

"I love you," he says.

It comes out all at once. It comes out terribly, much too loud, on the top verandah, where the Kilburns in the bar below can very likely hear it. He goes red to the ears, the way Daddy does, and looks at the floor.

Reggie doesn't say anything.

Ike looks up.

Reggie's taken his glasses off. He's holding them in his lap in both hands, and he's looking at Ike without them, which means he can't see him properly at all. His face has gone completely still, all of it, the way it goes when he's counting. His mouth's not quite shut.

"I've put it in a letter," he says. His voice isn't right. "For after. Your grandmother has it. I couldn't — I've never been able to say it out loud first. To anybody. Not even your grandmother. She had to say it first." He stops. "Pearl got it in ink, every week, for twenty-six years. You'd have died, I thought. You'd have died of embarrassment. So I didn't. And I put it in a letter for after."

Ike sits very still.

"I'd rather you had it now," says Reggie.

He doesn't put his glasses back on. He sits there with them in his lap, not able to see, and turns his face towards where Ike is.

"I love you, Ike," he says. "I have since you were three and stood in front of the shed door with your hands behind your back. I've never been able to hear what you hear. But I've never once doubted you could hear it. And I'd not have missed a single minute of watching you listen."

The harbour's very quiet. Down in the bar, somebody's stopped talking.

Ike gets up out of Grandma's chair. He doesn't know what he's going to do until he does it. He goes across to Reggie's chair and kneels down on the boards of the verandah beside it, the way Grandma says Pearl knelt on the harbour wall with Iris. He puts his arms round him.

Reggie's so thin. He's like holding a bird. Ike can feel his heart, through his shirt and the rug, going much too fast and not quite even, like a metronome somebody's knocked.

Reggie's hand comes up and goes on the back of Ike's head. It just rests there. It's very light.

"That's done it," says Reggie, into his shoulder, quite unsteadily. "Your grandmother will have heard that in the kitchen. She'll never let me forget it."

"Good," says Ike.

Reggie laughs. It shakes all through him, the whole thin length of him, against Ike's arms.

"*Good*," he says. "You sound exactly like her."

---

In the morning, at six, Ike goes down the harbour steps with the string for the last time, and Reggie sits on the bench at the top with the book.

"Twenty-eight and a half," Ike calls up.

"Twenty-eight and a half," says Reggie, and writes it down. "Same as the day you came down in your pyjamas. 1983. I looked it up."

Ike comes up the steps. He sits down on the top one, next to the bench, the way he did at five.

They sit there. The boat's at the harbour wall, with Alleyne's grandson in the stern and the engine going. Ike's case is already in it. Daddy's at the bottom of the steps, waiting, not hurrying anybody.

"I've got the A," says Ike. "In my pocket. I'll bring it back. Next time."

Reggie doesn't answer.

He's never answered. Ike's offered in every letter for three years, at the bottom, in a P.S. *I've still got your fork. I'll bring it back if you want it.* And Reggie's answered every other thing in every letter, four pages, the generator and the boiler and the Brahms and Ruth. And never once that.

He doesn't answer now. He looks at the sea.

"Go on," he says. "You'll miss it."

Ike stands up. He goes down the steps to the boat. At the bottom, where Daddy is, he turns round and looks up.

Reggie's on the bench at the top, very small, very thin, with the book on his knee and the rug over his legs. And behind him, at the top of the steps, with her arms folded and a tea towel over her shoulder, there's Grandma, who was in the kitchen and has come out.

Reggie lifts his hand.

Ike lifts his.

He gets into the boat. Alleyne's grandson casts off. They go out round the head, and Ike sits in the stern and doesn't look back, because you don't. Mrs Beckford said so. If you look round you've lost it.

He looks round anyway. At the head, just before the cliff takes it, once.

They're both still there.

---

He's at the organ in Hammersmith when it comes.

It's Sunday the fourteenth of September. He's been up since a little after seven for no reason; he woke and couldn't sleep again, and lay there in the flat in Fulham beside Ruth listening to the traffic. He got up at eight and went to the church early and practised the voluntary for an hour, though he knew it. He's played the service. It's the last hymn, *O God Our Help in Ages Past*, the fifth verse, and he's got the full organ on for it, the way the vicar likes.

And in the mirror over the console, the one he uses to see the vicar, he sees the back door of the church open, and Ruth come in.

She doesn't come down the aisle. She stands at the back, by the font, in her coat, with her hands in her pockets. She waits.

She doesn't come to church. Not ever. She's a piano technician and a Quaker's granddaughter and she says she gets enough of the sound of other people thinking at work. She's never once come in the eighteen months he's had this job.

Ike plays the fifth verse. He plays it to the end, the way you do, with the congregation singing and the vicar at the front in his surplice. He plays it with the full organ and doesn't drop a note, and he looks at Ruth in the mirror the whole time.

She's waiting. She's not going to come down the aisle and stop him. She's going to wait at the back until he's finished, because she knows he'd want to finish. She knows it the way she knows why the top of a piano goes flat.

He thinks of Daddy in the back row of the church hall in Kingston with the *Gleaner*. *In case you look round.*

He finishes the verse. He holds the last chord as long as the vicar wants, and then a bit longer, and then lets it go. It goes up into the roof of the church in Hammersmith and comes back off the back wall, late, softer, the way Pearl says it does.

Then he turns round on the bench and looks at Ruth, properly, down the whole length of the church.

She takes her hands out of her pockets.

He knows. He knew in the mirror. He knew, he thinks, at seven this morning, when he woke up for no reason and the flat was breathing differently. He doesn't say that to anybody. He'll never say it. He doesn't know it. He only heard it.

The vicar's saying the blessing. Ike sits on the organ bench with his hands in his lap and waits for it to finish, because you do. Then he gets down and walks the length of the church to where Ruth is.

"Your father rang," she says. "At twenty past twelve."

"I know."

She looks at him. She doesn't ask how. She puts her arms round him, there at the back by the font, in her coat. She's got very strong hands. She holds on.

---

They play *Stardust* at the funeral, on the bar piano, which the Kilburn boys bring up the road on a handcart. Grandma says it's to be that one and no other.

Ike sits down at it in the front of the chapel at the Bay. He doesn't tune it. Nobody's tuned it since the man from Kingston in 1984, and he's not going to start now. He puts his hands on the keys and they're just as they were. The A in the middle's a little under. The top's flat. It's the piano he and Reggie took the front off when he was six. He knows it like his own hands.

He plays the introduction. Pearl's key, and his. He gets to where Pearl comes in, on the second line, and there's nobody to come in. Pearl's in Maryland, eight months gone and forbidden to fly. Ike leaves the space anyway, because it's hers. He plays under the space, the way he'd play under her.

And a voice comes into it.

It's from the front pew. It's not a third below. It's the tune. Pearl's line, where Pearl comes in, exactly.

He turns his head. You're not supposed to. Mrs Beckford would have his hands off. He turns his head on the bench and looks.

Grandma's standing up in the front pew. She's let go of Uncle Pascual's hand. She's standing very straight in her navy, with her hands at her sides, and she's singing Pearl's line. The voice is old. It's seventy-eight, and it's thin at the top, and it's the most astonishing sound Ike's ever heard in his life. It's going up into the roof of the chapel the way Pearl's goes, to the back wall, and coming back.

He looks back at the keys. He makes room.

He doesn't follow her. Grandpa Des said nobody follows, and Grandpa Des was right about that one thing. He goes *with* her. He holds when she holds. He goes up where she goes up, a beat before, with the chord ready, like a hand held out. And she leans on it. He can feel her lean on it, all the way down the chapel, the whole of her weight, the way Pearl does.

At the end he goes all the way up to the top. To the flat notes. The ones nobody ever tuned, in sixty years of sea air. He plays them anyway. He holds them, the way he held the chord in Hammersmith, until they're gone.

And under them, under everything, in the chapel at the Bay, he can hear it. Steady. The A.

He doesn't know how. He never has.

---

At dusk Uncle Pascual goes across to the cay.

Ike watches him go. Daddy takes him in the launch, because Uncle Pascual's eighty-eight and can barely walk the length of the jetty. Ike stands on the harbour wall with Sammy, who's flown down alone. They watch the launch go across the flat grey water in the last of the light. They watch Uncle Pascual get out at the Institute jetty with Daddy's arm under his, and go very slowly up the path, past the north pen, to the end of the row.

To the last hut.

They wait. The Kilburns are on the wall too, the old men, the fishermen. Nobody says anything.

And then, about a quarter of an hour after, there's a light in the window of the last hut. Just one. A lamp, very bright, by itself at the end of the row.

Old Winston Kilburn's grandson takes his hat off.

"That's the Doctor's Light," he says. He says it very quietly, to nobody. "My great-grandfather used to come in on it. It went out in the seventies." He looks at it. "Never came back on."

Along the wall, all the old men take their hats off.

Sammy turns to Ike.

"What is it?" he says. Very low. "Ike. What is that?"

Ike looks at the lamp at the end of the row.

He knows what it is. He knows it's the lamp. Uncle Pascual told him on the back verandah, two years ago, with Aunt Marguerite's rum. Fourteen years. Every week, the paraffin from the store. For a friend who couldn't come. And the prayers in Spanish for the one who couldn't come and the one who lit it.

He doesn't know who the friend was. He's never asked. It's not his.

And he knows Uncle Pascual's over there now, in the last hut in the row, with the lamp lit for the first time since 1971. Praying, in Spanish, for both of them.

"I don't understand it," says Ike.

It's true, as far as it goes. He doesn't understand it. He only knows it.

Sammy looks at him a long moment. Then he nods, and turns back to the lamp, and doesn't ask again. He's a man who knows when not to ask. Ike likes him very much for it.

Behind them, at the top of the harbour steps, Grandma's standing with her arms folded, looking at the light. She says, very quietly, *oh, Pascual*. Then she goes in and does the day book.

---

Grandma gives him the letter in the morning.

She gives it him in the office, at the desk, where she's been sitting since six because she can't sleep. She doesn't say anything. She takes it out of her apron pocket and holds it out to him across the desk, and he takes it. Then she gets up and goes out and shuts the door, and leaves him in there with it.

It's got *Ike* on the front. Just that. In the shaky hand.

He sits down at Grandma's desk and opens it.

---

*To be given after. Harbour House, the Bay, 23 July 2003*

*Dear Ike,*

*Your grandmother has given you this, and gone out and shut the door. She'll be on the other side of it, in the passage, not listening. She's very good at not listening. She's had a great deal of practice, with me.*

*I've written to your sister, and to Pascual, and to your grandmother. Theirs are long. Yours won't be. You'd not want it to be, and I'd not know how to make it so.*

*The A is yours. It always was. I lent it you in 1984 in the bar with the front off the piano, and I knew perfectly well when I did it that I'd never want it back. I've had a great deal of pleasure, these three years, watching you offer. I never answered because I couldn't think how to say so without making a speech. So I'm saying so here. Stop offering. It's yours.*

*Your father may have told you about the College. If he has, I asked him not to, and he'll have had a good reason. Don't thank me. You'll do it badly, and I'd do it worse. If he hasn't, stop reading this paragraph now and forget it. You're very good at not saying things. I'm relying on it.*

*You told me on the cay, when you were fifteen, at the north pen, that there's an A under everything, and you can hear it, and you don't know how. I've thought about that a good deal more than you'd guess. More than I've thought about most things anybody's told me.*

*I can't hear it. I've tried, once or twice in my life, when it mattered very much. I'll not tell you when. There was nothing there. There's nothing there now, and I don't expect there will be.*

*But I've never once in my life doubted that you can hear something. You've the best ear I've ever known. You heard the flat at the top of the church piano at four. You heard the wrong chord in the second verse of everything. You heard the burr on the float pin that I'd not seen in forty-four years. I've never once known you hear a thing that wasn't there.*

*So I'll say this, and then I'll stop. If I'm right, neither of us will ever know it, and you'll have lost nothing by listening. If you're right, I'll find out before you do. That seems to me a very fair arrangement, for a man who's spent his whole life wanting to know things first. And I'll have to tell your grandfather Hinds he won. He'll be insufferable. I'll not mind.*

*There's one more thing. I've not said it to you. I've never once said it out loud, to you or anybody, without being said it to first, and I'll not manage it now. You'd die of embarrassment, and so should I. So I'm putting it here, where neither of us has to watch.*

*I love you, Ike. I have since you were three and stood in front of the shed door with your hands behind your back and wanted to know what a generator was. I'd not have missed a minute of it.*

*Reggie.*

*P.S. The float will stick again. It's a float. When it does, don't hit it. Look.*

---

Ike sits at Grandma's desk with the letter for a long time.

Then he laughs.

He can't help it. It comes up out of him all at once, in the office at Harbour House, and he puts his hand over his mouth. *I'll not manage it now.* Written on the twenty-third of July, at this desk, by a man who didn't think he'd ever manage it out loud. And then, five weeks later, on the top verandah, with his glasses in his lap, he did. Because Ike said it first, badly, much too loud, and the Kilburns heard.

He was wrong about that one thing. And he found out he was wrong, on the verandah, with his glasses in his lap, three weeks before the end.

Ike thinks that of all the things Reggie ever found out, that might have been the one he liked best.

He folds the letter. He puts it in his inside pocket, with the other one, Daddy's, from Castleton. Then he takes the A out of his other pocket and taps it on the edge of Grandma's desk, the way Reggie showed him. He holds it up in the office at Harbour House, in the grey morning, and lets it hum.

It hums. Very small, very high, quite steady. It goes on and on in the quiet room, the way it's gone on since 1984.

Under it, under everything, he can hear the other one.

He doesn't know how. He never has. He sits there and listens until it stops.

Then he goes out into the passage, where Grandma's standing with her back to the door, not listening, with her arms folded. He doesn't say anything. He puts his arms round her from behind, and she puts her hand up over his, on her arm, and holds it there.

In the drawer of the desk behind them, Mummy will find a card later that morning, with two words on it and Daddy's name. Ike will see her face when she does. He'll not say anything about that either.

It's not his.
