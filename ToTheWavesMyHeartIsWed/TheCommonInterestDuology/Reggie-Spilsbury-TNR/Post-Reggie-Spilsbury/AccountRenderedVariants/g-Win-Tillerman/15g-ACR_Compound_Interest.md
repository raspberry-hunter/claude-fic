# Compound Interest

The calf has grown. That's the first thing Pearl finds out, the summer she's four.

She saw it last summer, after the wedding, from the rail of the north pen on Reggie's hip. She remembers it the way you remember things from when you were three: a grey back, a noise, Reggie's shirt in her fists. And she's been told about it for a year since. She was told about it in the kitchen at Harbour House, and on the telephone at the harbour office when Grandma rang on Sundays, and in letters that Mummy read out. The letters had drawings at the bottom, done very small with a sharp pencil: the calf in her sling, the calf's mother blowing, the tide going up the steps. The drawings had arrows. Pearl liked the arrows best. You could follow them with your finger and they told you where things went.

So she's ready for the calf. She's ready for the very big baby in the sea she remembers, whose mother weighs as much as a kitchen. And when Dr Pobil's boat comes alongside the jetty on the cay, and Reggie takes her up the steps, not fast, she's ready.

And then she's at the rail of the north pen, on Reggie's hip, and the calf comes up.

It's enormous. It's grey and huge and it comes up out of the water right beside the rail with a noise like somebody letting go of a breath they've held a long time. It's not the calf she remembers at all. It's bigger than Daddy. It's bigger than the boat.

Pearl grabs Reggie's shirt in both fists and doesn't scream, but only because she's too surprised.

"She grew," says Reggie. "They do. It's been a year. You've grown too." He shifts her on his hip. "She's still a calf. She's only big. She's not grown *up*."

"She's *enormous*."

"Yes. She eats a great deal." He's watching the calf, not Pearl, and he's smiling. "About as much as your Uncle Pascual."

"I heard that," says Dr Pobil, behind them.

The calf goes down. The water shuts over her. Pearl holds her breath without meaning to, and waits, and counts in her head the way Reggie taught her on the harbour steps. One, the sea going up. Two, the sea going down. She gets to thirty-one before the calf comes up again, blowing, and she lets her own breath out with it, and Reggie laughs.

"You held it," he says. "You held your breath with her."

"I didn't *mean* to."

"No. I do it too. I've been doing it for twenty years." He looks at her. "Thirty-one?"

Pearl stares at him. "How did you know?"

"Because I was counting too," says Reggie. "Under my breath. Thirty-one. She's a good calf."

---

He doesn't carry her back down the steps.

She notices that. On the way up he carried her, on his hip, and stopped twice to breathe. At the top he put his hand in his waistcoat pocket, where the little tin is, and took one of the white things out and put it under his tongue. Pearl knows about the white things. Auntie Joy told her about them at the wedding, when she was three. They're for Reggie's heart. You mustn't touch the tin, ever, and you mustn't ask him for one because they're not sweets.

On the way down, at the top of the steps, he puts her down. He does it very carefully, under the arms, so she's standing, and then he holds out his hand.

"You'll walk," he says. "With me. Hold on."

"You carried me *up*."

"I did. And now I'm going to walk down. Slowly. Holding your hand." He looks down at her. "Your grandmother made me promise."

"Why?"

He thinks about it. Pearl can see him think. He's the only grown-up she knows who does it where you can see, instead of just saying something.

"Because my heart's not very good," he says. "You know that. Joy told you. And it's better if I don't carry heavy things down steps. You're a very heavy thing, now. You've grown." He takes her hand. "And because I'd like to be around a long time, and your grandmother would like me to be. So I'm careful now. I didn't used to be. I'm careful for her. And now for you as well."

Pearl walks down the steps holding his hand. It's dry and cool, and it holds hers the way you'd hold a thing that mattered.

"*How* long?" she says, at the bottom.

"How long what?"

"How long do you want to be around?"

Reggie considers this. They're on the jetty. Dr Pobil's already in the boat. The sea's going up; Pearl can see it on the steps.

"A long time," says Reggie. "Until you're very old and tired of me. Say, eighty."

"*Eighty*?"

"You'll be very old. You'll have grey hair. You'll say, *Reggie, please, go away*, and I'll say, *not yet, I'm still counting*." He hands her down into the boat, into Dr Pobil's arms. "That's the plan. I've drawn it. With arrows."

Pearl laughs so hard Dr Pobil nearly drops her.

---

Christmas is in Kingston, the year Pearl's five.

Grandma and Reggie come on the boat and then the bus, the long way round, because Reggie says the Port Maria road is no better than the Port Maria swell. They don't stay in the flat. They stay at a hotel in New Kingston, which has a swimming pool and a lift and a man in a red coat. Pearl is outraged.

"There's *room*," she says to Mummy, in the kitchen. "Grandma could have my bed. *I* could sleep with you."

"There's not room," says Mummy.

"There *is*. I measured. With Reggie's tape. On the telephone, he told me how."

Mummy stops peeling. She looks at Pearl for a long time.

"He told you how to measure the flat?"

"He said to count in my feet. Heel to toe. He said everybody's feet are a ruler if you know how long they are." Pearl holds up one foot. "Mine's six and a half inches. He *told* me."

Mummy puts the knife down. And then she does a funny thing. She laughs, but it's not the laugh she does at Daddy's jokes. It's the other one, the one she does when something's hurt her in a way she likes.

"He did the same thing," she says. "Well. He got Daddy to. He wrote to Daddy at the bank in September and asked how big the rooms were, and Daddy measured them with his feet, heel to toe, and wrote back. And Reggie said there wasn't room for two more grown-ups without somebody sleeping on the floor, and he'd not have your father sleeping on the floor in his own house at Christmas. So he took the hotel." She picks the knife up again. "That's why, darling. It's not because he doesn't want to be here. It's so we can have our own beds and still have him."

Pearl thinks about this.

"He's very *rude*," she says finally. "Measuring our flat."

"Yes," says Mummy. "Isn't he." And she laughs again, the hurting-nice way, and doesn't stop for a while.

---

On Christmas Eve Pearl sings in Grandad Hinds's church.

Grandad Hinds is Mummy's father. He's the minister, and he's very tall and very black, and he has a voice like a cello, Reggie says, which is a big violin. On Christmas Eve the children sing at the front, all in white, with candles they're not allowed to light. Pearl's at the end of the front row because she's the smallest. And when it comes to the verse where one child sings on her own, it's her.

She's not frightened. She doesn't know why people keep asking if she's frightened. She stands up at the front of Grandad Hinds's church in her white dress and the lady at the organ gives her the note. Pearl takes it, and holds it, and sings the verse the way she sings everything. She doesn't know how else. It comes out of the middle of her, where her breath is, and goes up and out over everybody's heads to the back wall. She can feel it hit the back wall and come back to her.

It's the best feeling in the world. It's better than the calf.

When she's done, and sits down, the church is very quiet for a second before everybody starts again. She looks along the pews for her family.

There's Daddy, smiling so hard his face looks strange. There's Mummy, crying, which she does in church. There's Grandad Hinds in the pulpit with his big hands folded. And there's Reggie, in the aisle seat, where he can stretch his leg. He isn't smiling. He's sitting very still, looking at her, the way he looks at the calf when she comes up. As though there were nothing else in the whole church.

And beside Reggie there's Grandma.

Grandma has gone quite still. Her face has gone the colour of the candles. She's got her hand at the base of her throat, and she's looking at Pearl as if she'd seen a ghost.

Pearl doesn't understand it. She thinks perhaps she sang it wrong.

After, in the vestry, with everybody hugging her, she goes and finds Grandma. Grandma's standing by the door with her coat on, and Reggie's beside her with his hand on her back.

"Did I sing it wrong?"

"*No*." Grandma kneels down, right there in the vestry in her good coat, and takes Pearl's face in both hands. Her hands are shaking. "No, my darling. You sang it right. You sang it *exactly* right."

"You looked sad."

Grandma doesn't say anything for a moment. She looks up at Reggie. Reggie looks back at her, very steadily, and then he puts his hand on the top of Pearl's head.

"Your grandmother used to sing," he says. "A long time ago. She had a voice like yours. It surprised her, hearing it again."

Pearl looks at Grandma. Grandma's never sung. Not once. Not in the kitchen, not in the day book, not even the hymns at the Bay chapel. She stands with her mouth shut and her hymnbook open, and lets everybody else do it.

"*Did* you?" says Pearl.

"Once," says Grandma. "Before your father was born."

"Why did you stop?"

Grandma gets up. She does it slowly, holding Reggie's arm. She smooths her coat.

"Because your father was born," she says. "And somebody had to keep the books." And she smiles at Pearl, the ordinary smile. "Come on. Grandad Hinds says there's cake, and Reggie's not allowed any, so you'll have to eat his."

Pearl eats Reggie's cake. It's very good. She doesn't think about it again for a long time. But she puts it away somewhere, the way you put away a shell you don't know what to do with. *Somebody had to keep the books.*

---

Christmas Day is in the flat, all of them, which is nine people in a front room built for four. Pearl counts them, because she can. There's Mummy and Daddy and Pearl, and Grandma and Reggie, and Grandad Hinds and Great-Aunt Merle, who's his sister and taught Mummy French seams. There's Mrs Chin from downstairs, because Mrs Chin's on her own at Christmas. And there's Auntie Joy, on Mrs Chin's telephone from London at eleven o'clock. That's only half a person, Pearl decides, but she counts her anyway.

It's the best Christmas there's ever been.

Grandma and Mummy are in the kitchen from nine in the morning, and they don't quarrel once. Pearl knows they sometimes nearly do, because she's heard Mummy's voice go careful on the telephone. But today they stand side by side at the stove with their sleeves rolled up, and at one point Pearl hears Grandma say, *no, love, do it your way, yours is better*. And Mummy goes pink and says nothing, and does it her way.

Reggie sits on the floor.

There's nowhere else for him, once Grandad Hinds has the good chair and Great-Aunt Merle has the other one and Mrs Chin has the end of the sofa. So he sits on the floor with his back against the sofa and his legs out, and Pearl sits beside him. She shows him everything she's got. She's got a doll from Grandad Hinds, and a dress from Grandma, blue, with French seams. Mummy turns it inside out at once to look, and then goes out to the kitchen for a minute. And she's got a parcel from Reggie that's heavy and flat.

It's a book. It's got a blue cloth cover and nothing written on it at all. Inside, every page is squared paper, like the chart, only it's not the chart yet because the measles is next year. On the first page, in the small neat hand, it says: *Pearl Carew. Observations. Begun Christmas 1976.* And underneath, smaller: *For the tide, and anything else that wants writing down.*

Pearl holds it in both hands for a long time.

"It's for *writing things down*," she says.

"It is."

"Things I *see*."

"Things you see. Things you count. Anything that comes back to you the same twice." He looks at her. "You can put songs in it too, if you like. What you sang, and whether it came back off the wall. It's yours. You decide what goes in it."

She puts her arms round his neck. She does it so hard that he says *oof* and his glasses go crooked. And Grandad Hinds, in the good chair, laughs his big cello laugh, and Mrs Chin claps.

Daddy's in the kitchen doorway. Pearl sees him over Reggie's shoulder. He's watching them, and he's not smiling exactly, but his face isn't wound up tight either, the way it is sometimes. It's just watching. After a moment Mummy comes up behind him and puts her chin on his shoulder, and they both watch.

---

On Boxing Day Reggie takes her swimming.

The hotel with the lift has a swimming pool on the roof, blue as the chart ink, with chairs round it and a man in a white coat who brings you drinks with umbrellas in them. Pearl's never been in a swimming pool. She's been in the sea at the Bay, holding Daddy's hand at the edge where it's warm. But a swimming pool is different. It's the same all over, and it's blue right to the bottom, and you can see your feet.

Grandma and Mummy sit in the chairs in their hats and don't go in. Daddy's at the bank; even on Boxing Day, somebody has to check the vault, and it's his turn. So it's Reggie who goes in. He comes out of the changing room in a pair of old grey trunks. He's very thin and very white, whiter than anybody Pearl's ever seen, and she stares. And he goes down the steps at the shallow end very slowly, holding the rail.

"Dr Sealy says nothing deeper than my chest," he says, "and nothing colder than a bath. This is a bath. And I'll not go past the second step on the ladder." He holds out his hands. "Come on. I'll not let go."

Pearl comes down the steps. The water's warm. It comes up to her middle, and then her chest, and she grabs his hands.

"Now," says Reggie. "Lie back."

"*Back*?"

"Like the calf. When she's asleep, at the top of the pen, with her nose up. She lies on the water and lets it hold her. You remember." He's got one hand flat under her back and the other under her head. "The water's very strong. Much stronger than you. You don't have to do anything. You just have to let it."

She lies back. She's stiff as a board and she's sure she's going to sink.

"Let it," says Reggie, very quietly. "Breathe in. Like the sea going up."

She breathes in. And she feels it happen. The water comes up under her and holds her, all along her back, the way his hand's holding her, only everywhere. She's lying on the water with the sky over her and her ears full of the blue hum of the pool, and she's floating.

"Is your hand there?"

"My hand's there."

She floats. She looks at the sky. After a while she feels his hand go away, very slowly, so slowly she hardly knows. And she doesn't sink. She stays up. She's lying on the water by herself, and he's standing beside her with his hands in the air where she can see them, not touching.

"*Reggie*. I'm doing it."

"You're doing it."

"*By myself*."

"By yourself. I've not been touching you for about a minute." He sounds enormously pleased. "You're a very good calf."

She laughs, and gets a mouthful of water, and sits up spluttering. He catches her under the arms. She stands on the bottom of the pool and holds on to him, and looks up at him with water running down her face.

And she remembers.

"Mummy says you measured our flat."

He blinks at her. "I — well. Your father did. I asked him to."

"You asked Daddy to measure our *flat*. So you'd know if there was room for you." She's very stern. "That's *rude*, Reggie."

He opens his mouth. He shuts it. She watches it go across his face, the way things do with him, where you can see: surprise, and then thinking, and then something else.

"Oh," he says. "*Oh*. Right." He considers it. "Yes. I suppose it is. I'd not thought of it that way. I was thinking about the beds." He looks down at her, very seriously, standing in the swimming pool in his old grey trunks. "I'm sorry, Pearl. It was rude. I should have asked you."

"*Me*?"

"It's your flat as well. You're the one who measured it with your feet." He considers. "Next time I'll ask you. And you can tell me if there's room."

Pearl thinks about this.

"There *is* room," she says. "I measured."

"I know you did. Six and a half inches, heel to toe." He's smiling now. "Your mother told me. I've written it down."

Over by the chairs, Grandma's got her hand over her mouth, and Mummy's laughing so hard her hat's fallen off.

That night, in the hotel's writing paper because she's forgotten her pencil, and then copied out properly at home the next day, the first page of the blue book says:

*26 Dec. 1976. Floated. By myself. About a minute (R. says). Shallow end is 3 ft 6. R. is very white and says it is because of England. R. measured our flat. That was rude. He said sorry. He is going to ask ME next time.*


---

She writes her first letter the year she's six, in pencil, on the lined paper from school, with Mummy spelling the long words.

*Dear Reggie,*

*I am well. I hope you are well. I am learning to write. This is my first proper letter. Mummy is doing the long words. The tide is not here in Kingston it is only the harbour. Daddy says the harbour has a tide but it is a small one. I do not believe him. How big is the calf now. Please send a drawing with the arrows.*

*Love from Pearl.*

*P.S. I sang in church again.*

She gives it to Mummy for the post. And then she waits, which is the worst thing in the world, for eleven days, until the envelope comes back with the Bay postmark. It's fat. It's got four extra stamps on it, stuck on crooked, because Reggie knows perfectly well how long his letters are.

It's four pages. It's written in the small neat hand that Pearl can't read yet, so Mummy reads it to her at the kitchen table. There are two drawings. One is the calf, much bigger, with a little drawing of Uncle Pascual beside her so you can see how big, and an arrow that says *5 ft 11 in (P. Pobil, approx.)*. The other is the harbour at Kingston, which Reggie says he's drawn from memory and from a chart. It has the tide on it, going up and down, with arrows, and underneath it says: *Your father is right. It is a small one. About a foot, at springs. But it is there. You can see it on the steps at the ferry pier if you look at the weed. Look at the weed, and tell me.*

And then, on the last page:

*You sang in church again. What did you sing? Was it the same verse, or a different one? Did you hear it come back off the wall? Your grandmother says the church on Constant Spring Road has a very good back wall. I should like to know.*

*Your loving*

*Reggie.*

Pearl makes Mummy read the last page three times.

"He asked me," she says.

"He did."

"He asked me *what I sang*. And if it came back off the wall." She's got her hands flat on the letter. "How did he *know* about the wall?"

Mummy looks at her across the kitchen table. She's got the look she gets sometimes when Pearl's said something she wasn't expecting.

"Because he listens," says Mummy. "Some people listen so they can talk next. He listens so he can find out."

Pearl doesn't understand that. But she goes with Daddy to the ferry pier on Saturday and looks at the weed on the steps, and it's true, there's a line where it's wet and a line where it's dry. She writes it in the blue book first, standing on the step, with Daddy holding the page flat against the wind: *Weed wet to 4th step. Dry above. Daddy checked.* And then she writes it all down in a letter, very slowly, in pencil, with Mummy spelling the long words. *The weed is wet up to the fourth step. It is dry above. I sang O Little Town of Bethlehem, the second verse, and it did come back off the wall. It comes back a little bit late, like an echo but softer.*

The envelope that comes back is five pages.

---

She gets the measles the Easter she's six.

Reggie's come to Kingston on his own, for three days, because the Board is meeting in New Kingston in Easter week and Jean says he has to be there to be consulted. He's staying at the hotel with the lift. He comes to the flat on the Wednesday evening with a jar of Grandma's guava jelly and a new drawing of the calf. Pearl shows him the weed on the ferry steps, in the notebook, and he checks her figures and says they're very good figures. She's perfectly well. She sings him the Easter hymn she's learning, standing on the kitchen chair, and he listens to the whole of it with his hands folded.

On the Thursday he's at the Board all day. He comes back to the flat for dinner, and Pearl's on the sofa under a blanket, hot and cross and covered in spots.

She doesn't remember much of the next few days. She remembers them the way you remember a dream, in pieces, some very bright and some gone.

She remembers the doctor, who's Mummy's cousin, saying *measles* and *keep her in the dark* and *watch the temperature*. She remembers her eyes hurting in the light, so they draw the curtains in the middle of the day. And she remembers being so hot that the sheets feel like the inside of an oven. Mummy puts cold cloths on her head and they're warm again in a minute.

And she remembers Reggie in the chair by her bed.

He's there on the Thursday night when she wakes up in the dark, crying, not knowing where she is. He's there on the Friday morning. Every time she opens her eyes he's in the chair from the kitchen, with his reading glasses on and a pad of squared paper on his knee, and the little torch from his pocket pointed at it. He's writing something down.

"What are you doing?" she says, the first time. Her voice comes out wrong, thick and croaky.

"Keeping a chart." He turns the pad round and shows her, with the torch. It's got lines on it, going across and up, and dots joined together in a wobbly line going up and up. "That's you. Your temperature. Every hour. Your mother takes it and I write it down. That's so the doctor can see what it's doing when he comes back, without anybody having to remember." He points with his pencil. "There's six o'clock. There's seven. That's where we are now."

The line's going up. Even Pearl, who's six and hot and can't see properly, can tell it's going up.

"Is that bad?"

"It's what measles does," says Reggie. "It goes up for a while. And then it turns, and comes down. Like the tide. We're waiting for it to turn." He looks at her over his glasses. "You're doing very well. You're doing exactly what you're supposed to."

"I don't *feel* like I'm doing well."

"No. I don't suppose you do. It's a very dull job, being ill. Most of it's waiting." He puts the pad down. "Would you like me to count with you? It helps the waiting."

So they count. He takes her wrist in his cool fingers, the way she's seen Dr Sealy do to him on the verandah at Harbour House. She asks what he's counting, and he says *you*, and counts it under his breath and writes it down. And then they count the calf's breaths, from memory, one, the sea going up, two, the sea going down, until she's asleep.

---

On the Friday after lunch, she hears Mummy in the passage.

"You'll go," Mummy's saying. "You'll go to your Board, Reggie, or I'll carry you down Mrs Chin's stairs myself."

"Marcia, she's at a hundred and two —"

"She's at a hundred and two and she'll be at a hundred and two when you get back. I've got the thermometer and I've got your chart and I can put a dot on a line as well as anybody." There's a pause. "Jean's paid for your room, and your fare. Go and be consulted. Be back for five. I'll have the tea on."

Pearl hears the door. She hears him go down the stairs, one at a time. And at five o'clock she hears him come back up, one at a time, stopping on the landing to breathe. He comes in with a folder of the Board's papers under his arm, still tied up with its string. He sits down in the chair and looks at the chart, and at Mummy's dots, and says, "Those are very good dots, Marcia," and doesn't open the Board's papers at all.

It's the same on the Saturday. Mummy pushes him out after lunch with his hat. He's back by five with the folder, still tied up.

---

The Saturday night is the worst. She doesn't remember most of it. She remembers the chart. She remembers waking up and the line being so high it's nearly off the top of the paper, and Reggie's taped another piece of paper on above, to make room.

And she remembers Daddy.

She wakes up very late. It's so late that it's gone quiet outside, the dogs and the traffic and Mrs Chin's radio. Reggie's in the chair, but he's not writing. His glasses are pushed up, and his head's back against the wall, and his eyes are shut. His hand's still round her wrist, very loosely. She doesn't move, because she doesn't want to wake him.

Then the door opens and it's Daddy, in his vest and trousers, with two cups.

He stops in the doorway. He looks at Reggie asleep in the chair with his hand round Pearl's wrist. He looks at the chart on Reggie's knee, with the extra paper taped on the top. He stands there a long time.

Then he comes in and puts one cup down on the floor by the chair, very quietly. And Reggie opens his eyes.

"Tea," says Daddy. Very low. "Three sugars. Mum says."

"Thank you." Reggie takes it. He doesn't let go of Pearl's wrist.

Daddy sits down on the end of the bed. Pearl shuts her eyes most of the way, so they'll think she's asleep, and watches them through her lashes.

"You should go back to the hotel," says Daddy. "Get some sleep. You're meant to be going home on Monday. Marcia and I can —"

"I'd rather not. If you don't mind."

Daddy's quiet.

"I'll ring Win in the morning," says Reggie. He drinks some of the tea. "I've done my Boards. I've not done this." He looks down at Pearl, and she shuts her eyes all the way, quick. "I didn't have any, Lloyd. I didn't know. I'd not have known what it was like to sit up with one. I'd no idea you'd — that it'd be —" He stops. "I'd rather be here. That's all."

Nobody says anything for a long time. Pearl can hear them both breathing. She can hear the tea.

"She wrote to you," says Daddy at last. "About the weed. On the ferry steps."

"She did."

"She made me take her. On a Saturday. She stood on the bottom step for twenty minutes looking at the weed, and wrote it down in a notebook, and made me check it." Daddy's voice has gone odd. "Nobody ever asked me to look at anything. Daddy was always —" He stops. "He was always going somewhere."

Reggie doesn't say anything. Pearl opens her eyes a crack. He's looking at Daddy over the top of the tea, the way he looks at the calf.

"You took her," says Reggie. "You took her to the ferry steps on a Saturday and stood there twenty minutes. And you checked it."

"Of course I did."

"Not of course," says Reggie quietly. "Not everybody would. You did."

Daddy doesn't answer that. He sits on the end of the bed with his cup in both hands. After a long time he reaches over and puts his hand on the blanket, on Pearl's foot, and leaves it there.

Pearl goes to sleep like that. Reggie's got her wrist and Daddy's got her foot. She thinks, just before she goes, that she's the most held person in the whole of Kingston.

---

It turns at five in the morning. She doesn't know it's turned. She only knows that she wakes up and she's not as hot, and the sheets are damp instead of burning, and there's grey light round the edges of the curtains. Reggie's in the chair, awake, with the chart.

He turns it round and shows her with the torch, though it's nearly light enough without.

The line's gone up and up, and over the top onto the extra paper. And at the very end, at five o'clock, it's come down. Not much. But down. A little dot, lower than the one before.

"There," says Reggie. His voice is very hoarse. "That's the turn. That's the tide going out."

Pearl looks at it for a long time. Then she goes back to sleep.

When she wakes again it's full light, and it's Easter Sunday, and she can hear the church bells from Constant Spring Road. And she can hear Reggie on the telephone.

There isn't a telephone in the flat. There's one downstairs, at Mrs Chin's, and Mrs Chin lets people use it for ten cents in the tin. Pearl's door is open and the stairs are just outside, and she heard him go down, slowly, one step at a time, holding the rail. She hears him ask for the harbour office at the Bay. And then she hears him say, *Win*.

She can't hear all of it. She hears *measles*, and *a hundred and three last night*, and *it turned at five*.

And then she hears him say, very clearly, "No. I'm not coming back tomorrow. I'll not leave till it's all the way down."

There's a pause.

"I rang Jean last night. From the hotel, when Marcia sent me to change my shirt. I'm on leave. She said she'd put it in the book." Another pause. "I know. I'll take them. Marcia's watching me like a hawk. She made me sit down on the stairs on Friday and I'd not even got to the landing." Then something that isn't quite a laugh. "Yes. Yes, I know you would. I know."

Pearl lies in her bed with the curtains drawn and the bells going, and listens to him come back up the stairs, one at a time, stopping on the landing to breathe. And she thinks, very fuzzily, *he's staying*. She doesn't know why that makes her cry. It does anyway, a bit, into the pillow, so nobody can hear.

He stays till the Wednesday. He's there the whole time, every day, from breakfast till she's asleep, with no Board to go to. The line comes down a little more every day, and he puts a dot for every hour. When it's all the way down, he takes his pencil, and at the top of the first sheet he writes, in the small neat hand: *P. Carew. Measles. Easter 1977. Temp. hourly (M.C.), plotted (R.T.)*. And he gives it to her.

"It's yours," he says. "You did all the work. I only wrote it down."

At the door on the Wednesday he says goodbye to her very seriously, the way he did the first time on the harbour steps, as though she were somebody important. And he goes down Mrs Chin's stairs one at a time, holding the rail, with Mummy behind him watching like a hawk.

The chart goes in the shoebox under her bed. It's the first thing in it that isn't a letter.

In the blue book, when she's allowed to sit up with the curtains open, she writes:

*Easter 1977. Measles. Hottest was 103 and a bit, Sat. night. Turned 5 a.m. Sunday, like the tide. R. did the chart. Mummy did the dots. Daddy brought tea at night, 3 sugars. R. was meant to go home Monday. He did not.*

---

Her brother comes the year she's seven.

She's not been told much about it, only that Mummy's going to have a baby. And that Daddy's got a new job at the bank, the same bank, but higher up, behind a desk instead of behind the cage. And that everybody's very pleased.

What Pearl notices is the sewing machine.

It's been in the corner of the front room her whole life, under a cloth. Three nights a week Mummy takes the cloth off and sits at it. She sews other people's dresses and trousers and curtains after Pearl's in bed. Pearl goes to sleep to the sound of it, the needle going like rain on a tin roof, and in the morning there are parcels in brown paper on the table with names on them. She's always thought that's what mothers do at night. Mrs Chin downstairs doesn't, but Mrs Chin doesn't have a machine.

The spring before the baby comes, the sewing machine goes back in the cupboard.

Not all the way. Mummy still uses it. She makes Pearl's school dresses on it, and she makes the baby's things, a whole pile of tiny white things with French seams. She shows Pearl the seams and says, *your grandmother taught me that*, which isn't true, because Pearl's heard Mummy say she learnt it from Grandad Hinds's sister. But she doesn't sew at night any more. There are no parcels in the morning. At night Mummy sits on the sofa with her feet up, because of the baby, and Daddy sits beside her. And sometimes Daddy laughs at something on the radio, out loud, in a way Pearl doesn't remember him laughing before.

She doesn't know what's changed. She only knows that it has. She writes it in the blue book, because it's a thing she's seen twice and it came out the same:

*Machine in cupboard. No parcels. Daddy laughed at the radio (Tues. and Sat.). Do not know why.*

She writes and tells Reggie. *The machine is in the cupboard. Mummy does not sew at night now. Daddy laughs at the radio. I do not know why.*

The letter that comes back doesn't say anything about the machine at all. It's all about the calf, who's going to be let out into the sea next year, and a new animal on the reef with a white scar. But at the very end, after *Your loving Reggie*, there's a postscript, in a smaller hand, as though he'd thought of it after.

*P.S. I'm very glad about the radio.*

Pearl reads it over and over. She doesn't know what he means. She thinks perhaps she'll know later.

The baby's a boy. They call him Isaac Reginald, and Pearl calls him Ike. When Reggie comes at Christmas, and sees him for the first time, and hears the second name, he has to go out of the room.

---

The summer she's eight, Pearl goes to the Bay for the whole of August, on her own. Well, not on her own. Daddy takes her on the bus and the boat and hands her over on the harbour steps and goes back the same afternoon, because of the bank. But after that she's on her own, at Harbour House, for four weeks, which has never happened before.

She thinks she'll spend it all on the cay with the calf. The calf's been let out now, the way Reggie said, and comes back to the pen sometimes to be fed and looked at. She thinks she'll spend it with Reggie at the rail, counting.

She does some of that. But mostly, it turns out, she spends it in the little office by the fuse board, with Grandma, and the day book.

It starts because it rains. It rains for three days, the way it does in August, straight down, and there's no boat to the cay. Pearl's bored, and when Pearl's bored she follows people. She follows Grandma. Grandma goes into the office after lunch with the week's bills, and sits down at the desk, and Pearl sits on the other chair and watches.

Grandma has a pencil and a column. She goes down the column with the pencil, not fast, not slow, touching each figure. And at the bottom she writes a number. And then she goes down it again, from the bottom up, and gets the same number. And she puts a tiny tick beside it, very small, like a bird's foot.

"Why do you go up as well?"

"To check." Grandma doesn't look up. "Going down, you see what you expect to see. Going up, you've got to look."

Pearl thinks about that. She thinks it's the cleverest thing she's ever heard.

"Can I do one?"

Grandma looks at her over her glasses. Then she tears a sheet off the pad, and copies a column onto it, eleven figures. It's the laundry for the week, Pearl will find out later, sheets and pillowslips and towels at so much each. She gives Pearl the pencil.

"Down," she says. "Then up. Then tell me."

Pearl goes down. It's hard. The figures are in Grandma's writing, which is small, and some of them have cents. She has to carry, which she's only just learnt at school. She gets to the bottom and writes a number. Then she goes up, from the bottom, touching each figure with the pencil the way Grandma does, and she gets a different number.

She stares at it. She feels her face go hot.

"Good," says Grandma.

"It's *wrong*. They don't match."

"That's what's good." Grandma leans over. "If they'd matched the first time, you'd not know if you'd been lucky. Now you know one of them's wrong. So you find it."

Pearl finds it. It takes her ten minutes. She'd carried a one that wasn't there, on the way down. When she fixes it the two numbers match, and she puts a tick beside it, tiny, like a bird's foot. And a feeling goes through her that's almost the feeling of the back wall of the church. It isn't quite, but it's almost. The feeling of a thing coming back to you exactly right.

She looks up. Grandma's watching her with a very strange expression.

"Another," says Pearl.

Grandma gives her another.

By the end of the three wet days, Pearl's done the whole of the week's laundry and most of the kitchen, and found a mistake in the Ministry's bill that Grandma says she'd have missed. She hadn't. Pearl's fairly sure she hadn't. But Grandma says so anyway, and writes *checked P.C.* at the bottom in the day book, in her own hand. It's the first time Pearl's initials have been in the day book, and Pearl looks at them for a long time.

In her own book that night she writes:

*Aug. 1979. Harbour House. DOWN then UP. If they match you are right. If they don't you find it. I found a carried 1 that wasn't there. Grandma's tick is like a bird's foot. Mine is getting like it.*

That night at dinner, Reggie's come over on the Monday boat. He listens to Grandma tell him about it over the fish, with his fork halfway to his mouth.

"She goes *up* as well as down," says Grandma. "Without being told twice. And she found the one I'd put in wrong on the Ministry's bill."

"You'd put it in wrong on purpose," says Reggie.

"I had *not*."

"Win. You've not put a figure in wrong since 1959."

"I put one in wrong on *purpose*," says Grandma, "to see if she'd find it. And she found it." She looks at Pearl across the table. And there's that strange look again, the one from the office, as though Pearl were somebody else as well as herself.

"She's got your head," says Reggie, quietly.

"She's got my *head*," says Grandma. "And my voice. And Des's ear." She puts her fork down. "God help her."

Pearl doesn't know what that means. But she sees Reggie reach across the table and put his hand over Grandma's, on the cloth, and hold it. And she sees Grandma let him, which she almost never does at the table.

"God help her nothing," says Reggie. "She's got all three and she's got Marcia and Lloyd. She'll not have to choose. That's the difference, Win."

Grandma looks at him. Then she looks back at Pearl, and the strange look goes, and she's just Grandma again.

"Eat your fish," she says. "You've been doing sums all day. You'll want your strength."

Reggie makes a noise into his napkin that Pearl doesn't understand at all.

---

Grandpa Des comes the Easter she's nine, on the Saturday, without writing first.

Pearl's never met him. She knows about him. He sends a card at Christmas with a picture of Miami on it, all palm trees and blue water and a pink hotel, and *To my little Pearlie, love Grandpa* in big looping writing. Daddy puts them on the mantelpiece and then, after Twelfth Night, in a drawer. She knows he's Daddy's father and Grandma's husband from before. She knows he plays the piano. She knows Mummy's face does something when his card comes that it doesn't do for anybody else's.

So when there's a knock on the door of the flat at four o'clock on Easter Saturday, and Daddy opens it, and goes completely still, Pearl knows who it is before she sees him.

He's tall. Taller than Daddy, even. He's old, with white hair and a white moustache, and he's wearing a cream suit and a panama hat and holding a paper bag of mangoes. He's the most beautiful old man she has ever seen. He looks like somebody in a film.

"Lloydie," he says. "Look at you. *Look* at you." He's got his arms open. "Aren't you going to let your old father in?"

And Daddy lets him in.

---

He stays four hours. Pearl will remember every minute of them for the rest of her life.

He sits in Daddy's chair, with his hat on his knee and Ike on the other knee. Ike's two and doesn't like strangers, but he sits on Grandpa Des's knee as good as gold, pulling his moustache, and Grandpa Des lets him. He tells stories. He tells about Miami, and a man with a boat who went to Havana in a storm. He tells about a hotel where the band played on the roof and a lady fell in the pool in a ball dress. He tells about the Myrtle Bank in 1947, when the band was the best on the north coast, and people came from Montego Bay to dance.

He's funny. He's so funny. Pearl laughs until she gets hiccups and has to drink water upside down. Even Mummy laughs, once, before she can stop herself, and then looks cross about it.

And he asks Pearl things. He turns to her in the middle of a story and says, "And what about you, Pearlie? What do you like at school?" And she opens her mouth to tell him about sums, and about going up as well as down. But before she's got to the second word he's laughing. He's saying, "Sums! A girl after her grandmother's heart. Your grandmother could add up a hotel bill in her head faster than the manager with his machine, did you know that? I remember one night at the Myrtle Bank —" And he's off into another story. It's a good story. Pearl laughs at it. But she notices that he never gets to the end of her question.

He does it three times. *And what do you want to be when you're big?* *And do you have a best friend?* *And what's your favourite thing?* Each time she opens her mouth, and each time he's already answering it for her, or telling a story it reminds him of, and it's always a lovely story.

She doesn't mind. Not really. It's like being in a warm bath. But she notices.

Reggie doesn't do that. Reggie asks what you sang, and then he waits. Sometimes he waits so long it's embarrassing. He'll sit there at the kitchen table at Harbour House with his hands folded, looking at you, until you've said the whole of it. Then he'll ask about the one bit you weren't sure of.

She doesn't know why she's thinking about Reggie. She puts it away.

---

It's Mummy who says it, in the end. Pearl sees her decide to. Grandpa Des is telling a story about a singer he had in 1952, the best voice in Kingston except one. Mummy's in the kitchen door with a tea towel, and she says, quite quietly, "Pearl sings."

Grandpa Des stops.

He turns round in Daddy's chair and looks at Pearl. It isn't the way he's looked at her all afternoon, bright and quick and already on to the next thing. It's a different look. It's slow. It goes all the way into her.

"Does she, now," he says.

"She sang the solo in the Christmas Eve service when she was five," says Mummy. "She's sung it every year since. My father says she's the best voice in the church." She folds the tea towel. "She's got her grandmother's voice. Win says so."

Grandpa Des is still looking at Pearl.

"Sing me something, Pearlie."

Pearl looks at Daddy. Daddy's face is doing something she can't read. Then he nods, very slightly.

"There's no piano," says Pearl. "I sing with the organ at church."

"No piano." Grandpa Des puts Ike down, very gently, on the floor. He stands up. "No piano. Well. That'll never do." He looks at Mummy. "Your father's church. The hall. There was a piano in that hall, in 1969. An old upright, very sweet in the middle, flat at the top. Is it still there?"

Mummy looks at him for a long moment.

"It's still there," she says. "My father has the key."

---

The church hall's cool and dim and smells of floor polish. The piano's in the corner under a cloth, and Grandpa Des pulls the cloth off like a man unveiling a statue. He sits down, and runs his hands over the keys once, all the way up and all the way down, and laughs.

"*Still* flat at the top," he says. "Bless it." He looks round at Pearl, who's standing by the piano with her hands behind her back. Daddy's by the door with Ike. Mummy's beside him with her arms folded, and Grandad Hinds, who came with the key, is behind them, very tall and not smiling. "What do you know, Pearlie?"

"Hymns."

"Hymns. Hmm." He plays something. Pearl doesn't know it. It's slow and soft and it goes round in a way that makes something happen in her chest. "Do you know this one?"

"No."

"Listen, then." He plays it through. Then he sings it, very softly, under the piano, in a voice gone rough and thin with age that still somehow goes exactly where the tune goes. Pearl can't catch all the words. "That's *Stardust*. Your grandmother sang it with my band. Every Saturday night. I'll play it again, and you come in where you like."

He plays it again. And Pearl, who's never heard it before in her life, comes in on the second line.

She doesn't decide to. It just comes out of her, the way the hymns do, out of the middle, where her breath is. She's got the tune from once through. She hasn't got all the words, so she sings the ones she's got and hums the rest. And it goes up, and out, into the dim hall, and comes back to her off the far wall. It's a different wall. It sounds different. Warmer.

And Grandpa Des is with her.

That's the thing she'll remember, afterwards, for years, when she's trying to explain it to somebody. He's with her. Not behind, not in front. Right with her, every bar. When she slows down, because she's not sure of the next bit, he slows too, before she's even done it, as though he'd known. When she goes up at the end of a line, because it feels right, he goes up with her. He makes room. He listens to every single thing her voice does and gives it somewhere to go. It's like the organ at church, only the organ doesn't listen. It's like being carried.

She gets to the end. He plays two more bars after her, very soft, and stops.

Nobody in the hall says anything.

Grandpa Des sits at the piano with his hands still on the keys. He's looking at her. And his face is quite different from the face he's had all afternoon. There's nothing on it. It's only an old man's face, and his eyes are wet.

"*Winnie*," he says, very quietly. Not to her. To nobody.

Then the face comes back on, the smile, like somebody turning up a lamp.

"Well!" He spins round on the stool. "Well, well. Hinds, did you hear that? Lloydie? Did you *hear* that?" He holds out both hands to her. "Pearlie. Come here. Come here to me."

She goes. He takes both her hands and holds them.

"You've got it," he says. "You know that? You've got the thing. Your grandmother had it, and I had a bit of it once, and you've got it. Don't you ever let anybody tell you different." He squeezes her hands. "She gave it up, your grandmother. Did you know that? She had the best voice on the north coast and she gave it up. Criminal. A criminal waste."

"Somebody had to keep the books," says Pearl.

Grandpa Des goes still.

"Who told you that?"

"Grandma. At Christmas. When I was five."

He doesn't say anything for a moment. He's looking at her, but not at her. At something behind her, a long way off. Pearl feels his hands go tight on hers and then loose.

"Yes," he says. "Yes. I dare say she did." He lets go of her hands. And when he speaks again it's lighter, bright, with the smile. "Well, don't you let anybody make *you* keep the books, Pearlie. Not with a voice like that. You'll be on the stage at the Myrtle Bank. You'll be in *Miami*."

"I like the books," says Pearl.

He laughs.

"I *do*," says Pearl. She feels her face go hot. "I found a mistake in the Ministry's bill. At Grandma's. I go up as well as down."

Grandpa Des is laughing. Not unkindly. He's laughing the way you'd laugh at a puppy that thought it was a lion. "*The Ministry's bill*," he says. "Oh, Pearlie. Oh, you're Winnie all over. With a voice like an angel and she'd sooner sit in a back room with a pencil." He shakes his head. "Figures. Well. You'll grow out of it."

Pearl doesn't say anything. But something in her goes quite still and cold, like the sea going down the steps.

She won't grow out of it. She knows that. She knows it the way she knows the calf will come up. And she knows, too, standing in Grandad Hinds's church hall with her hands still warm from Grandpa Des's hands, that he'll never understand that. He heard every single note she sang. He knew where she was going before she did. And he doesn't care a straw about the tick like a bird's foot at the bottom of a column. He thinks it's a thing you grow out of.

She thinks he's silly about it. She thinks it quite clearly, for the first time in her life about a grown-up. *He's silly.*

And at the same time she wants to sing *Stardust* with him again more than she's ever wanted anything.

---

He doesn't stay for supper.

They go back to the flat. He's in the kitchen with Daddy for ten minutes with the door shut while Mummy gives Ike his bath. Pearl's in her room, and she can hear their voices through the wall, low. She can't hear the words. She can hear Grandpa Des's voice going up and down, warm, the way it does in the stories. And then Daddy's voice, which doesn't go up and down at all. It says something short. And then it says it again, the same, even shorter.

Then Grandpa Des laughs, and the kitchen door opens.

"Well, I'll not stop, Lloydie," he says, in the passage. "There's a man in Spanish Town I promised I'd see. You know how it is."

"I know how it is," says Daddy.

Grandpa Des comes to Pearl's door. He's got his hat on. He looks at her sitting on her bed.

"Keep singing, Pearlie," he says. "You hear me? Whatever they tell you. Keep singing."

"I will."

"Good girl." He comes in, and bends down, and kisses the top of her head. He smells of bay rum and cigarettes and something sweet. "You're the best of the lot of us. Don't you let them make you a clerk."

Then he's gone. She hears him go down the stairs, not fast, whistling. It's *Stardust*. She goes to the window and watches him cross the road in his cream suit in the dusk, and go round the corner, still whistling. He doesn't look back.

She stands at the window for a long time after.

Then she gets out the blue book. She sits on her bed with it for a while before she writes anything, because it's hard to know what's an observation and what isn't. In the end she writes:

*Easter Sat. 1980. Grandpa Des. Came 4 p.m., went 8 p.m. (Spanish Town). Asked me 3 questions. Did not wait for the answers. Played Stardust in Grandad's hall. Piano flat at the top. He was WITH me, every bit. It came back off the far wall warmer than church. He said Grandma gave it up and it was a criminal waste. He said I will grow out of the columns.*

And then, underneath, pressing hard:

*I will not.*

---

Daddy puts her to bed, which he doesn't usually on a Saturday. He sits on the end of her bed in the dark with his tie off, and doesn't say anything for a while. Ike's asleep in his cot across the room, breathing the way the calf breathes, only faster.

"Did you like him?" says Daddy at last.

"Yes." Pearl thinks. "He's very funny. And he played it exactly right. He was *with* me, Daddy. The whole time."

"He always could." Daddy's voice is odd. "He could play for anybody. He played for your grandmother like that. Every Saturday."

Pearl lies in the dark and thinks. She's been thinking since the window. It's like a column that won't come out.

"Daddy."

"Mm."

"I like Grandpa Des." She says it carefully, because she's trying to get it right. "But I don't like him the way I like Reggie. And I don't know why."

Daddy doesn't answer.

"Reggie's not funny like that," says Pearl. "He doesn't tell stories like that. He can't sing. He can't even *hum*, Grandma says. He can't play anything. And Grandpa Des played it so beautifully. But I'd rather — I'd rather have Reggie at the rail than Grandpa Des at the piano. And that's *silly*. Isn't it? Because the piano was the best thing I ever —" She stops. "I don't know why."

Daddy still doesn't say anything. She can see him, a little, in the light from the passage. He's sitting on the end of her bed with his head down. His hand's gone to his jacket pocket, where his wallet is, and stopped.

"I don't know either, sweetheart," he says at last. His voice has gone very quiet. "I've never known."

And then Mummy's in the doorway.

Pearl doesn't know how long she's been there. She's got Ike's bath towel over her shoulder, and she's leaning on the door frame, and she's looking at Daddy. Then she comes in and sits on the other side of the bed, and puts her hand on Pearl's hair.

"I'll tell you," says Mummy. "If you want. Both of you."

Daddy lifts his head.

"Grandpa Des listens," says Mummy, "like a man waiting for his turn to play. He's very good at it. He's the best there is. He hears everything, so he'll know what to do next. And what he does next is always lovely." She strokes Pearl's hair. "Reggie listens like a man who wants to know what you'll say. He's not waiting for his turn. He hasn't got a turn. He just wants to *know*."

Pearl thinks about that. She thinks about *and what do you like at school*, and the story about the hotel bill that came instead of the answer. She thinks about *was it the same verse, or a different one*, in the small neat writing, and the envelope that came back five pages.

"But he listened when I sang," she says. "Grandpa Des. He *really* listened. I could tell."

"Yes," says Mummy. "He did. That's the one thing he really listens to. Music. It's the only place he's ever been all the way there." She's quiet for a moment. "And it's a very big thing, darling. Don't let anybody tell you it isn't. He heard you tonight the way nobody else in this family can. Not Reggie. Not me. He heard you the way one singer hears another. That's his. Nobody else can give you that."

"Then why —"

"Because he left before supper," says Mummy.

Daddy makes a sound in the dark. Not a word.

"He always leaves before supper," says Mummy. Her voice is very gentle, but it doesn't have any give in it at all. "He came at four and he left at eight. There's always a man in Spanish Town. And Reggie comes on the boat and the bus, the long way round, at Christmas. He sits in the aisle seat at Grandad's church, and he sits on the floor with Ike, and he's still here on Boxing Day. He was still here when you had the measles, that Easter he'd only come for three days, because he rang the Bay and told Win he'd not leave till your temperature came down." She strokes Pearl's hair. "That's all it is, darling. It's not the music, or the stories. It's who's still there at supper."

Pearl lies very still.

On the other side of the bed, Daddy's put his face in his hands.

Mummy reaches across Pearl, in the dark, and puts her hand on the back of his neck, and leaves it there.

---

*Dear Reggie,*

*Grandpa Des came at Easter. You probably know. He played the piano in Grandad Hinds's church hall and I sang Stardust which Grandma used to sing with his band. I did not know it before but I learnt it off him once through. He played it EXACTLY right, he was with me the whole way, it was like being carried. Then he went to Spanish Town. He said I have got The Thing and not to let anybody make me a clerk. He laughed when I said about the Ministry's bill. He said I will grow out of it.*

*I will not grow out of it.*

*Mummy says he listens like a man waiting for his turn and you listen like a man who wants to know. I think that is right but I do not know what it means all the way yet.*

*Why did Grandma stop singing? She said somebody had to keep the books. Grandpa Des said it was a criminal waste. I do not think Grandma would do a criminal waste. I think she would have a reason. What was the reason?*

*Love from Pearl.*

*P.S. The weed on the ferry pier steps is wet up to the sixth step now, it is springs.*

---

The envelope that comes back is only two pages, which has never happened before. Pearl reads it herself now, at the kitchen table, and she can read all of it, even the small neat hand.

*My dear Pearl,*

*I'm very glad he came. And I'm very glad you sang with him. I'd have given a great deal to hear it. You'll know by now that I can't carry a tune in a bucket, and your grandmother has forbidden me to try in the house. So I've never been able to give you what he gave you in that hall. I don't suppose I ever shall. He's the finest accompanist in Jamaica. He heard things in your voice that night that I haven't the ears for. I'm glad somebody did. Keep it.*

*You won't grow out of the columns either. I'm quite sure of that. I've watched you do them. People don't grow out of the things that come back to them exactly right. They only get better at them.*

*As for why your grandmother stopped singing: that's hers to tell, not mine, and I think she should be the one to tell you. Ask her in August, when you come. Ask her properly, when there's time, not at the table. I think she'll tell you, if you ask her the way you ask me things.*

*You were right, though. She'd have had a reason. She usually does.*

*Your loving*

*Reggie.*

*P.S. Springs. Well done. I'd have said the fifth step, from the chart. You've corrected me. I've written it down.*

Pearl reads it three times. Then she folds it in four and puts it in the shoebox under her bed with the others. There are thirty-one of them now. She counted.

Then she goes and finds her pencil and a sheet of the lined paper, and starts a column. It's her school dinner money for the term. She goes down it, and gets a number. And then she goes up it, from the bottom, touching each figure, and gets the same number.

She puts a tick beside it, tiny, like a bird's foot.

Then she goes to the window, where she can see the harbour between the buildings, very small and far off, and sings *Stardust*, quietly, all the way through. She's learnt all the words now. Nobody's listening. It comes back to her off the glass.

Afterwards she opens the blue book to a new page. At the top she writes, *Things That Come Back The Same Twice*. It's the most important heading she's ever written, and she takes a long time over the letters.

*The tide.*

*A column, if you go down and then up.*

*The calf, after 31.*

*The back wall at church.*

*Reggie.*

She looks at the list for a long time. Then, after a while, she adds one more line, smaller, because she's not sure yet.

*Grandpa Des. Only in the music.*
