# In Credit

Auntie Marguerite has him on the verandah with her fingers on his wrist when Pearl comes up the harbour steps.

It's the Monday before the concert. Pearl's come on the early boat, the long way round from Kingston, to fetch them. She's taken three days off from the Pegasus, which the general manager said he'd not survive. Pearl said he'd survive very well, since she'd done the month-end on Friday night till two, and left it on his desk reconciled, with a tick.

She stops at the top of the steps and watches. Auntie Marguerite is eighty-one and has given up the surgery. She hasn't given this up. She sits in Grandma's chair with her little black book on her knee, looking at her watch, while Reggie sits in his and looks at the harbour and lets her.

"Well?" says Grandma, from the door.

Auntie Marguerite doesn't answer for a whole minute. Then she lets go of his wrist and writes in the book.

"Seventy-two," she says. "At rest. At seventy-eight years of age." She shuts the book. "He'll go to Kingston. On the boat and in Lloyd's car, not the bus. He'll take one before the chapel steps, and one before he stands for the anthem, and he'll not stand for the anthem if it's longer than four verses. And he'll be home by Wednesday, and I'll have it again." She looks at Reggie over her spectacles. "I've been taking this pulse since 1970, Reggie Tar. You're the only patient I've ever had who did every single thing I told him. It's very nearly insulting."

"I had reasons," says Reggie.

"You had reasons," agrees Auntie Marguerite. "And you'll see eighty on them. Eighty-five, I shouldn't wonder, if Win keeps feeding you." She gets up, slowly, with a hand on the arm of the chair. She's small and white-haired and quite straight. She looks at Pearl at the top of the steps, and something happens at the corner of her mouth. "You'll be wanting him. Go on. I've done."

She goes in past Grandma. Reggie turns his head and sees Pearl.

He's old. She knows that, and she always knows it, but it comes at her new every time she comes up these steps. He's small in the chair, and thin, thinner than ever, and his hair's gone all the way white and very fine. His hands on the arms of the chair are all bones and spots. But his eyes are the same. They're very blue, behind the same kind of glasses, and when they see her they do the thing they've always done. They go all at once, and then quite still. As though there were nothing else on the verandah.

"Pearl," he says. "You came on the early boat."

"I came to fetch you."

"I know. I've been told." He holds out his hand. "Your aunt says I may go. She's written it down."

---

She asks him on the Monday night.

She's had it in her for a month, since June, since Samuel asked. She's had it in the blue book, which is the twenty-first blue book now, on a page by itself. She's had it on the boat all morning, and on the harbour steps, and all through dinner, which was fish, because it's always fish. She's waited till Grandma's done the day book and gone in, and the Ministry men are in the bar, and it's just the two of them. Her in Grandma's chair with her feet up on the rail, him in his with his feet on the stool, with the cay across the channel, long and dark, and the lamp at the near end.

"Reggie."

"Mm."

"There's somebody I want to marry."

He doesn't move. He doesn't take his feet off the stool. But she sees his hands go still on the arms of the chair.

"Samuel," he says. "The pilot. You've written."

"I've written about him since last year."

"Since last April," says Reggie. "The fourteenth. You said there was a man at the back of the Starlight Lounge who'd been coming on the last Friday of every month for a year and never talked through a song. And that you'd found out he was a harbour pilot, and sang bass in your grandfather's choir, a quarter-tone flat." He turns his head. "You said you'd asked him why he never talked, and he said *because you were singing*."

Pearl stares at him.

"You *remember* that."

"I've got it in a drawer," says Reggie. "With the others. I've got four hundred and twelve of them now. I've got them in order." He's quiet a moment. "He asked you. In June."

"On the harbour wall at Port Royal. At slack water." She hears herself laugh, a little. "He timed it. He said it was the only quarter of an hour in the day he could be sure of."

"And you said yes."

"I said yes." She pulls her knees up. "Daddy's said yes. Mummy said yes before I'd finished the sentence. She says she knew in February. Grandma likes him. Auntie Joy's written from London in capitals, all good ones." She stops. "And I want yours."

"My what?"

"Your blessing." It comes out smaller than she means. "I want your blessing, Reggie."

He's quiet for a long time. Across the channel there's the lamp on the long-room verandah, and at the far end of the row, in the hut that was his and then Baptiste's and is now Baptiste's student's, a light. It's small and yellow.

"I don't know that it's mine to give," he says at last. "A blessing. It's not a thing I've got in my pocket, Pearl. Your father's got more claim to it than I have. Your mother most of all."

"I've got theirs."

"I know you have."

"I want *yours*." She doesn't look at him. She looks at the light across the channel. "You're the one who never told me what to do. Not once. Not about Julian, not about anything. You'd tell me what you saw, if I asked, and then you'd let me find it." She swallows. "So I want to know what you see. About Samuel. And then, if it's all right, I'd like you to say it's all right. Because you've never said that about anybody. And if you say it, I'll know."

Reggie doesn't say anything for a while. She hears him breathing, slow and careful, the way Auntie Marguerite taught him.

"Tell me about him," he says. "Not what he is. What he does."

Pearl thinks about it. She thinks about it the way he taught her to, where you can see.

"He comes back," she says. "That's the first thing. He came to the Pegasus every last Friday for a year, before he ever spoke to me, and he sat at the back, and he didn't talk. And when he'd spoken to me he came every last Friday the same, and every Sunday to church, and every Tuesday to the flat for supper, because Mummy asked him once and he took it as a standing invitation." She laughs. "He's never been late. He can't be. The tide won't wait for him, he says, so he's got out of the habit."

"Go on."

"He listens. Like you. When I tell him something he waits till I've finished, and then he asks about the bit I wasn't sure of. He doesn't know anything about music. He sings hymns with his whole chest, a quarter-tone flat, and he knows it, and he doesn't care, and neither do I. He can't tell a C from a G. He sat through Ike's scales on Saturday for an hour and said, at the end, *I don't understand a note of it and I've never been so happy*." Her voice goes. "He doesn't want me to choose, Reggie. He's never once asked me to. He thinks the singing's mine and the books are mine and the Pegasus is mine, and he's just very glad to be let near any of it."

She stops. She's crying, a little. She lets it.

"He comes back the same twice," she says. "Every time. I've checked. I've been checking for a year. Up as well as down. I put him on the list in March."

Reggie doesn't say anything.

She turns her head at last and looks at him. He's got his glasses pushed up, and his hand over his eyes.

"Reggie?"

"Give me a minute," says Reggie, behind his hand.

She gives him a minute. She gives him two. Out on the water a boat comes in past the second head, low, steering on the light in the window behind them.

He takes his hand down. His face is wet and he doesn't do anything about it.

"You asked me what I see," he says. "I'll tell you. I see a girl who's done her columns. Who's checked, up as well as down, for a year. Who's been told by everybody on two coasts what to do since she was eighteen and has done exactly as she liked, and been right." He looks at her. "And I see your face, Pearl. I've been waiting a very long time to see your face look like that, about somebody who'll be there at supper. I wasn't sure I'd be here to see it. Your aunt was. I wasn't."

Pearl can't say anything.

"I don't know that a blessing's mine to give," he says. "I've never given one. I'm not sure I know how." He holds out his hand across the gap between the chairs. "But I can tell you I'm glad. I'm gladder than I've got any way of saying. And that it's all right. It's more than all right." His voice goes, and he lets it. "If that's a blessing, you've got it. All of it. Whatever it's worth."

She takes his hand. It's dry and cool, all bones, and it holds hers the way it did on the jetty steps when she was four, as though it were a thing that mattered.

"It's worth everything," says Pearl.

"Then that's settled," says Reggie. "And I'll want to meet him. Properly. Not at a concert. I'll want to ask him about the tide at Port Royal."

"He'll tell you. For an hour. He's got a chart."

"*Has* he," says Reggie, and his whole face changes.

---

He takes one on the chapel steps. Before, not after. There are eleven steps up to the University Chapel at Mona, grey stone, and he counts them on the way up, with Win on one side and Lloyd on the other, and stops on the sixth and the tenth. Nobody says anything about it. Win's hand is under his elbow, not holding, only there. At the top he stands in the doorway with the evening coming in behind him and gets his breath. He looks at the chapel.

It's old. Older than anything on the cay, older than anything at the Bay. It was a sugar warehouse, Lloyd told him in the car, brought over stone by stone from Trelawny and put up again here. It's got a high timber roof like the inside of a boat, and it's full. There are three hundred people in it, perhaps more. He can't count them from the door. He's seventy-eight and his eyes aren't what they were.

He can count the front pew.

There's Marcia at the far end, with her handbag on her knee and her back very straight, in a grey dress she's made herself, beautifully. There's Lloyd beside her, going grey himself now, at forty-nine, with his hand already on the back of the pew in front, gripping. There's Joy, flown in from London on Thursday to take Ike back with her in September. She's forty-six now, and a nursing officer at the Hammersmith, and she's been watching Reggie's colour since the car. Next to Joy there's the Reverend Hinds, who's eighty-eight and deaf and has come anyway, and Great-Aunt Merle.

And at the near end, on the aisle, where he can stretch his leg, there are two places left. And a young man standing up beside them, waiting.

He's tall. Taller than Lloyd. He's broad in the shoulders and dark and quite still, in a suit that fits him, which Reggie suspects is Marcia's doing. He's got a hymnbook in his hand and he's holding it the way you'd hold a chart. He sees Reggie in the doorway, and doesn't come forward, and doesn't smile the smile. He waits. When Reggie and Win get to the end of the pew he moves back to let them in, and holds out his hand.

"Dr Tar," he says. "Mrs Tar. Samuel Grant. Pearl said you'd take the aisle."

His voice is low, and a bit rough, and there's nothing in it but what it says.

Reggie shakes his hand. It's a big hand, hard in the palm the way a man's hand is who works ropes.

"Mr Grant."

"Samuel," says the young man. "Please. Pearl won't call me anything else."

Reggie sits down, carefully, on the aisle. Win sits beside him. Samuel sits beside Win, and puts the hymnbook on his knee, and looks at the front of the chapel, where there's a grand piano with the lid up and a music stand, and nothing else.

After a moment he says, quite quietly, without turning his head, "High water at Port Royal was six fifty-one. It'll be making still. You'll have come in on the flood."

Reggie turns his head and looks at him.

"Pearl said you'd want to know," says Samuel. "She said you'd ask, and I'd better have it ready." He's still looking at the front. "I've the chart in the car, if you'd like it after. The whole harbour. I did it myself."

Win makes a sound beside him that Reggie knows very well. It's the one she makes when she's trying not to laugh in church. Reggie finds, sitting on the aisle of the University Chapel at seventy-eight, with one under his tongue and his heart going carefully, that he likes the young man so much he can't speak.

"After," he manages. "Yes. I'd like that very much."

---

Then Ike comes out and sits down at the piano, and Pearl comes out and stands beside it, and he stops being able to think about anything else.

He's heard them before. He's heard them since Ike was four and Pearl was eleven, in the bar at Harbour House, quarrelling about tempo. He's heard them every summer since, and every Christmas, and down the telephone at the harbour office, crackling. He heard them in the bar the night Win came in from the kitchen. He knows every piece they do. He knows where Pearl will go up and where Ike will go off. He knows, because he's been told at length in letters, which of them won which argument about the second verse of *Abide With Me*.

He's never heard them like this.

It's the chapel, partly. The stone, and the high wooden roof. Pearl told him once, when she was six, that the church on Constant Spring Road had a good back wall. This has a better one. When she sings the first note it goes up into the roof and comes back down on everybody like something falling out of the air.

But it's them. It's the two of them. They've grown up. Ike's nineteen, and tall now, taller than Lloyd, with Marcia's round serious face and Desmond's hands. He sits at the grand piano as though it had been built for him. Pearl's twenty-six, in the blue, the same blue, let out again by Marcia. She stands with one hand on the curve of the piano and sings as though there were nobody in the chapel but her brother.

Reggie can't carry a tune in a bucket. He's never been able to. But he's sat at the rail of the north pen for fifty years listening for things coming up. He knows what it sounds like when two things breathe together.

They go together. Nobody follows. It isn't a parade. When Ike goes off, in the places he's always gone off, Pearl goes with him, a hair ahead, as if she'd known before he did. When Pearl goes up, Ike's there already, the chord waiting. They don't look at each other. They don't need to. They've been doing it since the bar at Harbour House, since the flat-topped upright, since *We are better TOGETHER, do not tell him I said so*. Pearl wrote that to him, underlined, when she was thirteen. It's in the drawer.

He sits on the aisle and listens, and doesn't breathe.

Win's hand finds his on the pew between them. She holds it. He doesn't look at her. He knows what her face is doing without looking; he's known for twenty-four years. Her thumb moves once across his knuckles and stays.

---

They do the hymns, and then something by Handel, and then a thing of Ike's own, the *Improvisation*. It's grown. It's twice as long as it was at eleven and goes off in a great many more places. And at the end it still goes all the way up to the top of the piano, where the notes on this one aren't flat at all, and Ike plays them anyway, as though they were.

Then there's a pause. Pearl steps forward from the piano.

"This is the last one," she says. She says it to the whole chapel, but she's looking at the front pew. "It's for our grandmother. She sang it first, before any of us. In 1947. At the Myrtle Bank." She stops. "And it's for our grandfather, who taught it to us. He couldn't be here. He's in Miami. He sent a card." Something happens at the corner of her mouth. "It says *Both*."

Win's hand goes tight on his.

"And it's for Reggie," says Pearl, "who can't carry a tune in a bucket. Who taught me to count between breaths when I was four, and never once told me what to do." Her voice goes, and she brings it back. "*Stardust*. Ike's key."

Ike plays the start of it. Slow.

Pearl comes in on the second line.

Reggie hears Win beside him draw in her breath. He feels it through her hand. He knows what's coming before it comes. He's known her for twenty-four years and he knows every sound she makes.

And in the front pew of the University Chapel at Mona, in the second verse, a third below the tune, low, so low that only the front pew can hear it, Win Tar sings.

Pearl hears it. He sees her hear it. Her eyes go to the front pew, and she doesn't stop, and her whole face opens. At the piano Ike doesn't look round. You don't. But his shoulders change. He makes room. He makes room for all three of them, the voice at the front and the voice in the pew and his own hands, and nobody's following.

Reggie sits on the aisle with one under his tongue and his wife singing beside him and his grandchildren at the front of the chapel. He's not breathing. He hasn't been breathing for some time, and his physician would have something to say about it, but she's on the verandah at the Bay with her black book, a hundred miles off.

He thinks, quite clearly, the way he'd think a figure: *this is the last time I'll come this far for anything.*

It isn't sad. It's only true. It's a thing you'd write down.

And then he thinks, with his wife's hand in his and her voice under Pearl's: *it's enough. It's more than enough. If it's the last, I'd have picked this one.*

Beside Win, Samuel Grant has his hymnbook shut on his knee. He isn't singing, for once in his life. He's listening, with his whole face, the way Reggie's watched him listen for the last hour. He's not waiting for his turn. He hasn't got one. He just wants to hear.

Reggie watches him listen, and breathes out, at last, all the way.

---

The girl who turns his pages is called Andrea Chung, and Ike has been trying for three weeks not to think about her.

She's a violinist. She's at the Edna Manley School, second year, and she's eighteen. Mrs Beckford, who's retired and still comes to every concert Ike plays, found her for him. She said a pianist who'd been playing twenty years ought to be able to turn his own pages, and a pianist with any sense would let somebody else do it on the night. Andrea came to the church hall three times to practise. She sat at his left on a little stool and turned exactly when he nodded, never early, never late. On the third time she said, *you go off in the second verse*, and he said *yes*, and she said *good*.

That's all. That's the whole of it. He's going to London in September.

She's on the stool at his left now, in a black dress, with her hands folded, waiting for the nod.

He's got the card from Miami in his music case, under the Handel, because Pearl gave it him in the vestry. It came to the flat on Thursday, addressed to *Master Isaac and Miss Pearl Carew, c/o the Revd. Hinds*, in the big looping writing gone very shaky now. It's got a picture of the pink hotel on it, the same one as always. On the back it says only *Both. Keep going off. D.* Pearl read it and went into the vestry lavatory for ten minutes. Ike read it and put it in the case, under the Handel, where he can feel it.

He doesn't look at the front pew. You don't. But he knows exactly who's in it, because he looked through the door from the vestry before they came out. Mummy at the far end, very straight. Daddy with his hand gripping the pew in front. Auntie Joy, watching Reggie. Grandad Hinds with his hand cupped round his good ear. Grandma, in the navy, with her handbag. Reggie on the aisle, very small, very white, with his hands folded, the way he folds them at the rail of the north pen.

And Samuel, on the end, with his hymnbook.

Ike likes Samuel. He decided to in January, the first Tuesday Samuel came to supper. Samuel asked Ike what the thing was on the kitchen shelf. It was the float out of the old Harbour House generator, which Grandma had let Ike keep when the new one came, in a jar. And when Ike told him, Samuel didn't say *how interesting*. He said, *they've one the same on the pilot launch, in the auxiliary; mine sticks too.* And the Saturday after, he took Ike out on the launch at slack water and let him hit it.

He comes back, Samuel. Every Tuesday. Pearl said it to Ike in March, in the church hall, and then she wrote it in the blue book and showed him: *Samuel. Every Friday for a year, and every one since.* She'd put it right under *Mummy*.

Ike nods. Andrea turns the page.

---

In *Stardust*, in the second verse, he hears Grandma.

He doesn't look round. He doesn't need to. He's been hearing Grandma through walls since he was four, at the sink, in the bar, humming when she thought nobody was there. He knows her voice the way he knows Pearl's, only lower, and darker, and further off. It's coming from the front pew, a third below, so quiet only the front pew and the piano can hear it.

He makes room.

He doesn't have to think about it. It's what Grandpa Des taught him on the bench at Christmas when he was six: *nobody follows*. It's what Julian did, whatever else Julian was. It's what he's been doing for Pearl since he was four with one finger. He makes room for Grandma's voice under Pearl's, and his own hands under both. For a verse and a half the three of them go together in the University Chapel, over three hundred people's heads. And the roof sends it back down on them like rain.

He plays two bars after, soft. He stops.

Beside him, on the little stool, Andrea Chung has forgotten to turn the page. There isn't one to turn. She's sitting with her hand in the air, and she's crying, and she doesn't seem to know it.

He looks at her. He can't help it. You're allowed to, at the end.

She looks back at him. And then she takes her hand down and wipes her face with the back of her wrist, very fast, and says, under the noise of three hundred people getting up off the pews, "*Write* to me. From London."

Ike's gone hot to his ears. He knows it.

"I'll write," he says. "I've been taught by the best. Four hundred and twelve letters. Reggie's got them all in a drawer."

"I don't want four hundred and twelve," says Andrea. "I want one. And then another one." She gets up off the stool. "About the going-off bits."

She goes off the side of the stage into the vestry without looking back. He sits at the piano with three hundred people standing up in front of him and can't think of anything at all.

Then he looks at the front pew.

Reggie's looking at him. Not at Pearl, not at Grandma. At him. And Reggie's face is doing the thing, the thing Ike's seen it do at the rail when the calf's calf comes up after a long time down. Reggie raises one hand off the pew, just a little, and points, very slightly, at the vestry door, where Andrea went.

And then Reggie smiles. It's small and crooked, and it goes up further on one side than the other.

---

Supper's at the flat in Barbican, because where else.

There are fourteen of them. Pearl counts, because she always counts. It's a front room built for four, and there were nine in it the Christmas she was five, and there are fourteen tonight. There's Mummy and Daddy. There's Grandma and Reggie, Auntie Joy, and Grandad Hinds and Great-Aunt Merle. There's Mrs Chin from downstairs, who's ninety and still comes. There's Mrs Beckford. There's Ike, and Pearl, and Samuel. There's Andrea Chung, whom Mummy asked on the chapel steps in front of everybody before Ike could stop her.

That's thirteen. She counts Grandpa Des as well, because of the card. Fourteen.

Mummy and Grandma are in the kitchen. Pearl can hear them through the wall. They're side by side at the stove with their sleeves rolled, and at one point she hears Grandma say, *no, love, your way*. And Mummy says, *Win, we've been doing it my way since 1976*, and they both laugh.

Ike's in the corner with Andrea. They're not saying anything. They're looking at the Handel together, as if it had something in it they'd missed. Every so often Andrea says something, very low, and Ike goes red to the ears, and Auntie Joy, across the room, catches Pearl's eye and raises both eyebrows so high they disappear.

And Reggie and Samuel are on the balcony.

They've been there an hour. Pearl can see them through the door. Reggie's in the good chair, which Daddy carried out for him, with his feet on the upturned laundry basket and a cushion behind his back. Samuel's sitting on the balcony floor beside him with his long legs crossed. The chart's spread out between them on the concrete, weighted at the corners with a jam jar, a saucer, the Reverend Hinds's hymnbook, and the float from the old generator, which Ike brought out without being asked.

She can't hear what they're saying. She doesn't need to. She can see Samuel's big hand moving on the chart, slow, following a line. She can see Reggie leaning forward with his glasses pushed up, and his finger on the same line. She can see his face doing the thing where you can see him think. Every so often Reggie asks something. And Samuel waits till he's finished, and then answers, and then Reggie asks about the bit Samuel wasn't sure of.

They're both listening. Neither of them is waiting for a turn.

Pearl stands in the balcony door with a cup of tea going cold in her hand and watches her whole life sit on the balcony floor in Barbican, looking at the tide.

---

At ten Reggie looks up and sees her.

"Pearl."

"Mm?"

"Come here." He holds out his hand. She comes and kneels down on the other side of the chart, on the concrete, in the blue dress. "Look at this. Samuel's done the whole harbour. Every sounding. By hand." He puts his finger on a place near Port Royal. "He's been out at slack water, every quarter for six years, and taken the lead line himself. It's better than the Admiralty's. I've told him so. He doesn't believe me."

"I believe you," says Samuel. "I just don't think it's true."

"It's true," says Reggie. "I've been looking at charts since 1938." He looks at Pearl. "Have you got your book?"

She's always got her book. She takes it out of her handbag, the twenty-first blue book, and opens it on her knee.

"High water at Port Royal tomorrow," says Reggie, "is seven thirty-eight in the evening. By Samuel's reckoning. Fifty minutes later than today, near enough." He looks at Samuel. "Is that right?"

"Seven thirty-eight," says Samuel. "Give or take. It'll depend on the wind."

"Write it down," says Reggie.

Pearl writes it down. *Sun. 27 July 1997. H.W. Port Royal 7.38 p.m. (S.G., by hand). R.T. says better than the Admiralty.*

"And tomorrow," says Reggie, "at seven thirty-eight, go out on the harbour wall at Port Royal, the two of you, and look at the weed on the steps. And see if he's right."

"He'll be right," says Pearl.

"I know he will," says Reggie. "Check anyway. Up as well as down." He sits back in the chair, carefully, and puts his hand on his chest, where the tin is, and leaves it there, not taking one, only resting. "I'd like to know. Write and tell me."

---

That night, very late, when everybody's gone and Samuel's gone down the stairs saying *Tuesday*, as he always does, Pearl sits up in her old bed in her old room with the torch. Ike's asleep across the passage, or pretending to be. She can hear him writing something, very slowly, on the other side of the wall. The flat's quiet. Mummy's machine is under its cloth in the corner of the front room.

She turns back to the front of the first blue book, which she's brought from the shoebox, because she always brings it to Kingston. There's the page with the big careful heading from when she was nine.

*Things That Come Back The Same Twice.*

She reads down it. It's longer now. It's gone over onto the next page. There's the tide, and the column, and the calf, and the back wall. There's Reggie, and Grandpa Des only in the music, and Daddy, and Ike, and Grandma, and *Me*. There's Julian only in the music, that's all right. There's Mummy, every single time, underlined twice, where she should have been first. And there's Samuel, every Friday for a year, and every one since, written in March.

She takes her pencil. She writes, underneath, in her twenty-six-year-old hand, which is small and neat now, like Grandma's and like Reggie's and like nobody else's:

*Grandma. In the front pew, a third below. The second time.*

*Ike and me. Together. Every time. I told him so tonight, in the vestry. He said he knew. He said he'd read it in 1984.*
She looks at it for a long time. Then she turns to a fresh page, not the list, because this isn't a thing that comes back, it's a thing that happened once. It's true, though, and the blue book's for true things.

*21 July 1997. Blessing: given. On the verandah. He said he didn't know how. He did.*

*Reggie at 78, on the aisle, not breathing. Auntie Marguerite says he'll see 80. He said so first, on the jetty steps, when I was four. Nobody can know a thing like that. I'm going to hold him to it anyway.*

She puts the torch out.

Across the passage, Ike's pencil stops. After a moment she hears him get up, and cross the passage, and push her door open a crack.

"Pearl."

"Mm."

"How do you start a letter?" His voice is very quiet. "To somebody. When it's the first one."

Pearl lies in the dark and smiles so hard it hurts.

"You say what you saw," she says. "And then you ask them a question. And then you wait for the answer."

There's a silence at the door.

"That's how Reggie does it," says Ike.

"That's how Reggie does it," says Pearl. "Go to bed. Write it in the morning. And go up as well as down."
