# Reconciliation

Ike doesn't care about the tide.

Pearl finds this out the summer she's ten and he's three, when he comes to the Bay for the first time old enough to walk down the harbour steps on his own. She's been looking forward to it for a month. She's going to show him everything. She's going to show him the weed line on the steps and the calf, who's grown now and comes to the pen only sometimes. She's going to show him how you count between breaths, and the day book, and going up as well as down. She's written a list, in the blue book, which is the third blue book now: *Things To Show Ike*.

Ike looks at the weed line on the steps for about four seconds. Then he turns round and walks back up the steps and across the road and into the yard of Harbour House. He stands in front of the generator shed with his hands behind his back, looking at the door.

"*Ike*." Pearl goes after him. "Ike, the tide's *this* way."

"What's in there?"

"The generator. It's just the generator. It makes the lights go when the lights go off. Ike, the *tide* —"

"What's a generator?"

And Reggie, who's come up the harbour steps behind them, not fast, with his hand on the rail, stops. He looks at Ike standing in front of the shed door. Pearl sees his face do the thing it does, where you can see him think.

"Would you like to see it?" says Reggie.

Ike turns round. He's got Mummy's face, round and serious. He looks at Reggie for a long time the way Mummy looks at a hem.

"Yes," says Ike.

---

They're in the shed an hour. Pearl stands in the door for the first ten minutes with her arms folded, and then she goes and sits on the harbour wall on her own and looks at the tide, which is going up exactly as she said it would. Nobody comes to look at it with her.

When they come out Ike's got oil on both hands and a streak of it down his nose. He's walking in a way Pearl hasn't seen before, quite straight, like a man with a job. Reggie's behind him, wiping his own hands on a rag.

"It's got a *float*," Ike tells her, at the wall. He's never told her anything before; he's three. "In a little box. And it goes up and down with the petrol. Like the tide." He holds his hand out flat and moves it up and down. "And sometimes it sticks. And Grandma *hits* it." He looks back at the shed with enormous respect. "*Here*."

"Your grandmother's been hitting it there since 1959," says Reggie. "It's the only way it'll start. I tried to take it apart once and she wouldn't let me."

"*I* want to take it apart."

"When you're older," says Reggie. "Your grandmother will have to say. It's her generator."

Ike looks at him. Then he looks at Pearl.

"It goes up and down like the tide," he says again. He says it as though he's giving her something, because he's heard her talk about the tide all the way from Kingston.

And Pearl, who has been cross for an hour on the harbour wall, finds she isn't cross any more.

"Show me," she says.

He takes her by the hand, with his oily one, and pulls her across the road to the shed.

---

That night she writes in the blue book:

*Aug. 1981. Ike does not care about the tide. He cares about the float. The float goes up and down like the tide. So he cares about the tide really, he just came in by a different door. R. says most people do.*

And underneath, because it's true, and the blue book's for true things:

*I was cross. Then I wasn't.*

---

The year after, it's the piano.

There's an old upright in the bar at Harbour House, against the wall by the window, under a cloth. Nobody plays it. Grandma says it's for guests. But the guests are mostly men from the Ministry and scientists from the cay, and none of them play either. So the cloth stays on, and the jug of flowers stays on the cloth, and the piano's just part of the wall.

Ike finds it on the second day. He's four. He takes the flowers off, and puts them very carefully on the floor, and takes the cloth off, and lifts the lid. And then he stands in front of it, because he's not tall enough to sit, and puts one finger down.

It's a note. Just one. Pearl, in the office doing the laundry column, hears it go through the house like somebody calling.

She hears him put his finger down again. And again. Different ones. Then it stops for a while, and then it starts again. And it's not different ones any more. It's the same five, over and over, one after another, going up. It's *O Little Town of Bethlehem*. It's the first line. It's the second verse, the one she sang the Christmas she was five, and every Christmas since. He's got it wrong on the fourth note, and he goes back and does it again, and gets it right.

Pearl puts her pencil down. She goes and stands in the bar door.

Ike's got his tongue between his teeth. He's got one finger on the keys, and he's doing it again, and again. Each time it's a bit better, a bit quicker, and each time he gets to the end he looks at the keys as though they'd done something surprising.

"How do you *know* that?" she says.

He doesn't look round. "You sing it. All the time. In the bath."

"I do *not* sing in the —"

"You *do*. Mummy says you sound like a ship." He finds the next line. He gets it wrong. He goes back. "Sing it."

"*What*?"

"Sing it. So I can hear where it goes."

Pearl stands in the door. She hasn't sung in front of Ike on purpose, ever. He's four. He's her baby brother. He wets the bed and cries at the dark and eats things off the floor.

She sings it.

Ike listens with his whole face screwed up. And then he puts his finger down under her, one note to every note she sings, and it's slow and it's wrong in two places. But it's under her. It's holding her up.

When she gets to the end he looks round at her. His face is shining.

"*Again*," says Ike.

---

They quarrel about it all that summer, and all the next, and after.

They quarrel about how fast. Ike wants to go faster, because it's more exciting, and Pearl wants it slower, because that's how it goes. They quarrel about who starts. They quarrel because he plays too loud, and because she stops in the middle to tell him he's wrong, and he says she's always stopping in the middle to tell people they're wrong. When he's six, he's learnt proper chords from Mrs Beckford at Grandad Hinds's church, both hands. He starts putting things in that aren't in the hymnbook, extra notes, going off on his own. Pearl stops dead in the middle of *Abide With Me* and says, "*That's not how it goes*."

"It's how it *could* go," says Ike.

"It's *not*. You're supposed to follow *me*. You're the *piano*."

Ike takes his hands off the keys. He's six, and he's small for six, and he's got Mummy's round face and Daddy's frown, and he looks at her with both of them.

"Grandpa Des said," says Ike, "the piano and the singer go *together*. He said nobody follows. He said if somebody's following it's not music, it's a *parade*."

Pearl stares at him. She hadn't known Ike remembered anything from that Easter. He was two. He sat on the floor of the church hall with Daddy and ate a mango.

"He never said that to *you*."

"He said it to *you*. I was there. I heard." Ike puts one finger on a key, a low one. "You were singing *Stardust*. I remember. It went like this." And he plays the first line. With one hand, very slowly, from memory, from when he was two.

Pearl can't say anything at all. She sits down on the piano bench beside him, hard, because her knees have gone.

"Do it again," she says, when she can. "The way you want. The going-off way. I'll come in."

He does it again. She comes in. It's different, and it's strange, and in two places it's awful. But in one place, just one, near the end, she goes up and he goes up with her, not behind, *with* her, and she feels the hair on her arms stand up.

They look at each other.

"*That* bit," says Ike.

"That bit," says Pearl.

Grandma's in the bar door. Pearl doesn't know how long she's been there. She's got a tea towel in her hands and she's standing very still, and her face has gone the way it went in Grandad Hinds's church when Pearl was five. Then she turns round without a word and goes back to the kitchen.

---

Reggie can't play the piano. He can't sing, and Grandma won't let him try in the house. But he wants to know how it works, because Ike wants to know. So the summer Ike's six, Reggie and Ike take the front off it.

Grandma says they can. She says it in the voice she uses for the generator, which means *if you break it, you'll buy me another*. And Reggie says *understood*, and fetches a screwdriver and a sheet to put the screws on. He writes down, on a card, which screw came out of which hole. Ike watches him do it with his tongue between his teeth.

Inside, it's all hammers. Rows and rows of them, little felt hammers on wooden arms, and wires behind them going up and up in a slant, thin at one end and fat at the other. Ike puts his head right inside and breathes in the smell of it, dust and felt and old wood.

"Press a key," says Reggie.

Ike presses one. A hammer jumps forward and hits a wire, and the wire hums, and the note comes out into the bar.

"*Oh*," says Ike.

"Now press the one next to it."

Ike does. A different hammer. A shorter wire.

"The shorter the wire, the higher the note," says Reggie. "And the tighter it is. Look." He's got a little steel fork out of his pocket, a tuning fork. Pearl's seen it before, in his hut on the cay, in a drawer. He taps it on his knee and holds it against the wood, and it sings. "That's an A. Four hundred and forty times a second. That's how many times that fork goes backwards and forwards. The wire does the same. Faster, the higher it goes."

"Four *hundred*?"

"And forty. Every second."

Ike looks at the fork as though Reggie had given him the moon.

"Why's it flat at the top?" says Pearl, from the bench. She's been listening. She knows it's flat at the top; she can hear it. "Grandad Hinds's is flat at the top too. Grandpa Des said so."

Reggie looks round at her.

"The thin wires stretch," he says. "Over time. With the heat, and the damp, and being played. They go a little looser, and a little lower. And the sea air gets in here, which it does in every house on this coast." He puts his hand flat on the side of the piano, the way he puts it on the rail of the north pen. "It's not broken. It's just been here a long time, being played, getting a bit tired at the top. You can tune it back up. A man comes from Kingston."

Ike's still got his head inside, looking at the long slant of wires.

"Can *I* tune it back up?"

"When you're older," says Reggie. "And your grandmother says."

"Everything's *when you're older*," says Ike bitterly, from inside the piano. And Pearl laughs so hard she falls off the bench.

That night, in the blue book, Pearl writes:

*Aug. 1984. A = 440 per second. Short wire = high. Thin wires stretch with the sea air and get tired at the top. Not broken. R. says you can tune it back up.*

She looks at it for a while. Then she writes, underneath, smaller:

*Ike is better at the piano than me at singing. No he isn't. He is different. We are better TOGETHER. Do not tell him I said so.*

---

She asks Grandma that summer. She's thirteen. It's four years since Reggie wrote *ask her properly, when there's time, not at the table*, and she's been waiting all that while for the proper time. She's not sure what it looks like. She thinks she'll know.

It turns out to look like the top verandah, at ten at night, with the day book done and the coffee gone cold, and Reggie gone to bed early because it's been a pen day and his chest was tight. Ike's asleep. The Ministry men are in the bar. Grandma's in her chair with her feet up on the rail, which she only does when nobody's looking, and Pearl's in Reggie's chair, which is still warm.

Across the channel the cay is a long low shape against the stars. There's the lamp on the long-room verandah at the near end. At the far end, where Reggie's old hut is, there's nothing.

"Grandma."

"Mm."

"Why did you stop singing?"

Grandma doesn't move. She doesn't take her feet off the rail. But Pearl sees her hands go still in her lap, where they'd been turning her wedding ring round.

"Reggie told you to ask me," says Grandma, after a while.

"He said to ask you properly. When there was time." Pearl pulls her knees up. "He said it was yours to tell. He wouldn't tell me. I asked him four times."

"No. He wouldn't." Something at the corner of Grandma's mouth. "He's very particular about what's whose."

She's quiet for so long that Pearl thinks she isn't going to answer at all. A boat comes in past the second head, low, steering on the light in the window behind them.

"I told you once," says Grandma. "When you were five. In your grandfather's church."

"You said somebody had to keep the books."

"Yes. Well. That was true." Grandma takes her feet off the rail. She sits up. She looks at Pearl across the dark, and Pearl can see her decide something, the way she decides a bill. "It's true and it's not the whole of it. You're thirteen. You can have more of it."

Pearl waits.

"I sang with your grandfather's band from 1946 till 1948," says Grandma. "Saturday nights at the Myrtle Bank, and sometimes Fridays, and once, for a week, at a hotel in Montego Bay where they put us in rooms with a bath each. I was twenty-one. I'd never had a bath of my own in my life." She smiles a little. "I sang *Stardust*, and *Stormy Weather*, and all of it. And your grandfather played for me. You've heard him."

"He was *with* me," says Pearl. "Every bit."

"Yes. He was with me too." Grandma's voice doesn't change, but something in it does. "That's how it was. On the stand. And then your father came, in 1948, and there was a baby, and there was a band that didn't pay. And a man who played cards after the last set till four in the morning. And there was nobody else to keep the books. So I kept them." She turns her ring. "You can't sing at the Myrtle Bank till two and be up at six with a baby and a ledger, Pearl. Not for long. Something goes. It was the singing went."

Pearl thinks about it. It makes sense. It's like a column. You go down it and it comes out.

But she's gone down a lot of columns now, in the office, with Grandma watching. She knows what it feels like when one comes out too easily.

"You could have gone back," she says. "After. When Daddy was big. When you came here. You could have sung in the chapel. At the Bay. You don't even sing the *hymns*, Grandma."

Grandma doesn't answer that.

"Joy said you could have sung on the radio," says Pearl. "Grandpa Des said you'd the best voice on the north coast. And you just — stopped. And didn't start. Even when you could." She hears her own voice go up. She doesn't mean it to. "Why didn't you *start*?"

Grandma's quiet for a very long time.

"Because I couldn't sing with anybody else," she says at last. "And I couldn't sing with him any more."

Pearl sits in the dark with that.

"That's all?" she says.

"That's what I can tell you," says Grandma. She says it gently. It's the voice she uses when a guest asks for a room that's taken. It's quite kind, and it's quite final. "The rest's mine. And some of it's his. You can have it when you're older, perhaps. Or perhaps not. It's not a thing that's any use to you, love. It's only a thing that happened to me."

She leans across, and puts her hand on Pearl's knee, and holds it there.

"But I'll tell you this, because it *is* some use to you. You've got a voice. You've got my voice, and your grandfather's ear, and God knows what Ike's got but it's in his hands. And you're going to be told, one day, by somebody, that you've to choose. The voice or the books. The music or the living. The singing or the man." Her hand's tight on Pearl's knee. "Don't you choose. Do you hear me? Don't you let anybody make you. Not a baby, not a husband, not a house, not a bank. You have them both. You've got your mother and father behind you and Reggie and me and a roof that doesn't leak. You've got more than I had. Use it. Have both."

Pearl can't say anything. She puts her hand over Grandma's on her knee.

"Now go to bed," says Grandma, "and don't tell Reggie I cried. I didn't. It's the sea air."

---

In the blue book Pearl writes, by torchlight, under the sheet, the way she used to do when she was little and Mummy said *lights out*:

*Aug. 1984. Asked Grandma properly. She said: baby + band that didn't pay + Grandpa Des at cards = somebody had to keep the books. The singing went.*

*Then she said: she couldn't sing with anybody else and she couldn't sing with him any more.*

*The first bit came out too easy. The second bit didn't come out at all. There is something in the middle she didn't tell me.*

*She said have both. She said I've got more than she had.*

She lies in the dark and thinks about the column that won't come out. Then she writes one more line, very small.

*I will have both.*

---

Win goes up at half past eleven. She'd thought he'd be asleep. He isn't. He's lying on his back in the dark with the verandah door open and his hands folded on his chest, the way Marguerite's told him to lie on a tight night, and he turns his head when she comes in.

"She asked you," he says.

"You knew she would."

"I knew she'd ask some time. I didn't know it'd be tonight." He watches her undress in the light from the harbour. "What did you tell her?"

"The books. The baby. The band." She sits down on the edge of the bed with her back to him, and takes the pins out of her hair, slowly, one at a time. "And that I couldn't sing with anybody else, and I couldn't sing with him any more."

"Is that true?"

"It's true." She puts the pins in the dish. "It's not the whole of it."

He doesn't say anything. He doesn't ask. He never does; it's the thing about him she'd not known she needed, the first year, and now can't think how she lived without. He just lies there with his hands folded, and waits, and lets her have the whole of the dark to say it in or not.

She lies down beside him. She lies on her side, facing the verandah door, where the cay is.

"He was true on the stand," she says. "Des. Every note. You heard it, the way he played for Pearl. He was *with* me. He never missed a beat, he never went off anywhere, he never looked at the door. For three minutes at a time I had all of him. And I'd come off that stand with my heart going and think, *that's the man I married*. That's him. That's who he really is, underneath." She stops. "And by one o'clock he'd be in the back room with the cards. And I'd not see him till four. And he'd have lost the band's money for the week."

Reggie's quiet.

"And the next Saturday I'd get up on the stand," says Win, "and he'd play *Stardust* for me, and he'd be *with* me again. And I'd believe him again. Every Saturday. I'd come off the stand and forgive him the whole week, because for three minutes he'd been *true*." She hears her own voice going, and lets it. "I couldn't stop believing him, Reggie. Not while I was singing. It was the one place I could hear him mean it. So as long as I sang, I'd go on forgiving him. And there was a baby. And there was rent."

She puts her hand over her eyes in the dark.

"I didn't stop singing because I'd no time. I'd have found the time. I stopped singing because it was the only way I knew how to stop *believing* him. It was the first cheque I didn't sign." She takes a breath. "It took me another ten years to stop the rest. But that was the first one. 1948. The week after Lloyd was born. He came to the hospital with the band's arrangement of *Stardust* in a new key, for my voice, lower, because he said I'd have dropped a bit after the baby. He'd sat up all night doing it. And he was right. He'd have been right. I looked at it, and I knew if I sang it I'd never leave him. So I put it in a drawer."

"And didn't sing it."

"And didn't sing anything. Not a note. Not the hymns. Twenty-six years." She takes her hand off her eyes. "Till the stairs."

Reggie turns his head on the pillow.

"Why the stairs?" he says quietly. "I've never asked."

"No. You wouldn't." She turns over and looks at him. His face is quite close, and she can see it in the harbour light, and there's nothing on it but him. "Because you weren't asking me for anything. You'd never asked me for anything. You'd have sat on that stair till morning and never asked me to sing." She stops. "And because he'd sent the telegram. That morning. *He will not sing but you could.*"

Reggie doesn't say anything.

"He gave it back," says Win. "Des. Of all people. He knew. He always knew; that's what I'll never forgive him and never stop loving him for. He knew I'd put it down to stop believing him. And on my wedding morning he sent me twelve words from Miami that said, *you can have it back now, Winnie. It's not mine any more. It's yours.*" Her voice cracks. "So I sang. On the stairs. To a man who can't carry a tune in a bucket. It was the first time in my life I sang for somebody who wasn't going to play."

He puts his hand on her face. He holds it there, the way she held his on the verandah in 1973, with his palm along her jaw.

"You didn't tell Pearl that," he says.

"No. It's not any use to her."

"Isn't it?"

Win's quiet.

"She's thirteen," she says. "She's got Ike, who plays like Des. She's got Des, who'll come back, you know he will. She sings like me. And one day somebody's going to play for her, Reggie. Some boy. And he'll be *with* her, every bar, and she'll come off the stand with her heart going and think, *that's who he really is*." She shuts her eyes. "I told her to have both. I didn't tell her how."

"She'll work it out," says Reggie.

"How do you know?"

"Because you told her to go up as well as down," he says. "When she was eight. And she's never done a column since that she didn't check."

Win opens her eyes.

"That's not the same thing."

"Isn't it?" He's smiling now, a little, in the dark. "Going down, you see what you expect to see. Going up, you've got to look. You told her that. I heard you." He strokes his thumb along her cheekbone. "She'll go up as well as down, Win. With the boy as well. You'll see. She'll check."

Win lies there with his hand on her face.

"You're very sure."

"I've been reading her letters for eight years," says Reggie. "I've got a hundred and forty of them in a drawer on the cay. I'm *quite* sure."

---

Grandpa Des comes back the Christmas she's thirteen.

He doesn't write first. He never writes first, Pearl knows that now. He knocks on the door of the flat on the twenty-third of December at five in the afternoon. He's in the cream suit, which is thinner at the elbows, and he's got a cane now, black, with a silver top. He's got a bag of mangoes, the same as last time, as if no time had gone by at all. His hair's gone all the way white.

"Lloydie," he says. "Marcia. Pearlie. Look at you all." And then his eyes go past them, down, to Ike, who's six, and standing behind Mummy with his hands behind his back, looking at the cane.

"And who's this?"

"That's Ike," says Pearl. "Isaac. He was two. Last time. He ate a mango."

"*Did* he." Grandpa Des leans on his cane and looks down at Ike. "Did you, now. And what do *you* like, Ike? What's your favourite —"

"The piano," says Ike.

Grandpa Des stops.

It's the stop Pearl remembers from the church hall, four years ago. It's the second when there's nothing on his face at all. Then it goes, and the smile's back. But he doesn't go on with the question. He looks at Ike for a long time.

"The piano," he says softly. "Is that right."

"Mrs Beckford teaches me. At Grandad Hinds's. On the one that's flat at the top."

"Still flat at the top," says Grandpa Des. "Bless it." He straightens up, slowly, on the cane. "Hinds. Lloydie. Where's that key?"

---

This time Pearl watches him.

She's thirteen, and she's been doing Grandma's columns for five summers, and she knows now how to go up as well as down. So all the way to the church hall, in the dusk, with Grandpa Des walking slow on his cane between her and Ike, she watches.

He's still funny. He tells a story about a man in Miami with a parrot that could whistle *In the Mood*, and Ike laughs so much he has to stop walking. He's still the most beautiful old man she's ever seen. He's still warm, and quick, and everybody on Constant Spring Road looks at him as he goes past, and he tips his hat to all of them.

And he asks her things. *And how's school, Pearlie? And what do you want to be?* And she watches the question come out of him, bright, and his face already turning to the next thing before she's opened her mouth.

So she doesn't let him.

"I want to run a hotel," she says. "Or a bank. Something with books. And sing." And when he laughs, and starts on the story about Grandma and the hotel bill at the Myrtle Bank, she says, quite loudly, "I'm not *finished*."

Grandpa Des stops walking.

Everybody stops. Mummy and Daddy, a few steps behind. Grandad Hinds with the key. Ike, holding Grandpa Des's other hand.

"I'm not finished," says Pearl again. Her face is hot but she keeps going. "You asked what I want to be. I want to run something. Grandma's taught me the books. I'm good at it. And I want to sing too. Both. Grandma says I'm to have both. So that's what I want to be. I've finished now."

Grandpa Des looks at her.

He looks at her for a long time, standing on Constant Spring Road in the dusk, leaning on his cane. He's not smiling. His face isn't doing anything at all.

"Both," he says.

"Both."

"Winnie said that."

"Yes."

He's quiet. Then something happens to his face that Pearl's never seen on it. It's not the lamp coming up. It's something going down instead, slowly, the way the sun goes down over the head at the Bay.

"Well," says Grandpa Des, quite quietly. "She'd know."

And he takes Ike's hand again, and walks on.

---

In the church hall he pulls the cloth off the piano, and runs his hands up and down it, all the way, and laughs. Then he sits down on the bench, and leans his cane against the side, and pats the bench beside him.

"Come here, Ike."

Ike goes. He sits on the bench beside Grandpa Des. His feet don't reach the floor.

"Play me something."

Ike plays him *O Little Town of Bethlehem*. Both hands, the way Mrs Beckford taught him, with the chords. And then, because it's Christmas, and because he's six and showing off, he does the going-off bit Pearl hates, in the second verse. He puts in the extra notes that aren't in the hymnbook.

Grandpa Des doesn't say anything. He sits beside Ike and listens with his head on one side. When Ike's done, he's quiet for a moment.

"Who taught you that bit?" he says. "The bit in the middle. The going-off bit."

"Nobody," says Ike. "It's just how it could go."

Grandpa Des puts his hand on the back of Ike's neck. Just for a second. Then he takes it away, and puts both his hands on the keys, down at the bottom end.

"Play it again," he says. "Just the right hand. The tune. And the going-off. I'll do the rest."

Ike plays it again with his right hand. And Grandpa Des plays under him. He's in the bass, with the chords, slow and full, and every time Ike goes off, Grandpa Des goes with him. He's not behind, not in front. He's *with* him. Ike's face changes. Pearl sees it change. It's the face she had, in this hall, four years ago, when she was being carried.

"*Pearlie*," says Grandpa Des, without looking up. "Come in."

She comes in.

She doesn't know afterwards how long they're at it. The three of them. *O Little Town*, and then *Stardust*, which Ike knows the tune of now and Grandpa Des does the chords to. Then something Pearl's never heard, that Grandpa Des sings her the words of line by line, and she picks it up and sings it back. And something else, a hymn, with Grandad Hinds joining in from the door with his big cello voice, and Mummy laughing. Grandpa Des is with them, every bar, both of them at once. He makes room for Ike's going-off and Pearl's going-up at the same time, so that neither of them has to follow and nobody's in a parade. It's like being carried and like carrying all at once.

It's the best music Pearl's ever made. She knows that while it's happening. She knows it the way she knows a column's right before she's gone back up it.

And all the time, while she's singing, she's watching.

She watches Grandpa Des's hands on the keys, old and spotted and perfectly sure. She watches his face when Ike does something clever: the second of nothing, and then the delight. She watches him look at Ike the way Reggie looks at the calf. And she thinks, very clearly, in the middle of *Stardust*: *he's all here. Right now. All of him.*

And then she thinks: *he'll go before supper.*

She knows it. She knows it the way she knows the tide. It's not sad, exactly. It's just true. It's in the blue book, under *Things That Come Back The Same Twice*, in her own hand, from when she was nine.

She sings anyway. She sings the whole of it, all the way to the end, with everything she's got. Because he's here now, and he's *with* her, and that's his. And she's not going to not have it just because he'll go.

---

He goes at eight.

There's a man in Half Way Tree. He says so at the door with his hat on and his cane under his arm, and he kisses Pearl, and he kisses Ike on the top of the head, and he shakes Daddy's hand. He holds it a moment longer than a handshake.

He was in the kitchen with Daddy for five minutes with the door shut, before. Pearl didn't listen through the wall this time. She didn't need to. Daddy came out first, and his face was quite calm. Grandpa Des came out after, and his face was calm too, and she thought he looked older.

"Keep playing, Ike," says Grandpa Des at the door. "You hear? Keep going off."

"I will."

"And Pearlie." He looks at her. He looks at her for a long moment. Then he says something she doesn't expect at all. "Both," he says. "You have both. You tell Winnie I said so."

And he goes down the stairs, slow, on his cane. She hears him whistling *Stardust* all the way down. She goes to the window and watches him cross the road in the dark. This time, at the corner, under the streetlamp, he stops, and turns round, and lifts his hat.

---

*Christmas 1984. Grandpa Des. Came 5 p.m., went 8 p.m. (Half Way Tree). Cane now, silver top. Played with Ike. Four hands. He was with both of us at once. I did not know you could.*

*I told him I wasn't finished. He stopped and listened. That is the first time.*

*He said: Both. You tell Winnie I said so.*

*He went before supper. I knew he would. I sang anyway.*

*He looked back at the corner. That is also the first time.*

---

The spring she's fourteen, she starts going to the bank on Saturday mornings.

It's her idea. She tells Daddy at dinner that if she's going to run something, she wants to see how something's run. And Daddy, who's the assistant manager of the King Street branch now, with his own office and his name on the door in gold, looks at her across the table for a long time. Then he says *eight o'clock sharp, and wear your school shoes*.

So on Saturdays she sits in the corner of his office on a hard chair, with the blue book, the sixth one now, and watches.

She learns more in four Saturdays than in a year of school. She learns what a clearing is, and a draft, and how you reconcile the day's figures against the ledger, which is going down and up in a very big way. She learns that Daddy's good at it. He's very good. He's quiet and careful, and the girls in the back office like him, and the manager, who's an old man called Mr Lim, calls him *Carew* in a way that means he trusts him. She's never seen Daddy at work before. She's never seen him be good at something where she could watch.

On the fifth Saturday, just before twelve, he takes a form out of his drawer and fills it in.

He does it at his desk, with his own pen. He does it slowly, the way Grandma does a bill she doesn't like. Pearl's across the room on the hard chair, but she's got good eyes, and she's been reading Grandma's writing upside down across a desk since she was eight.

*Cancellation of Standing Order.*

*Beneficiary: D. A. Carew.*

*Account: First Federal, Miami, Fla.*

*With effect from: 1st June 1985.*

Daddy signs it. He blots it. He puts it in the tray on the corner of his desk marked *Out*. Then he sits back in his chair and looks at it for a long moment, in the tray, with his hands flat on the desk.

"Daddy."

He looks up. He sees her looking at the tray, and he sees her see. She watches him think about pretending she didn't. She watches him decide not to.

"It's a payment," he says. "I've been making it a long time. Since before you were born." He looks at the form. "I've decided I've made enough of it."

"It's to Grandpa Des."

"Yes."

Pearl sits on her hard chair with the blue book on her knee.

"Are you cross with him?"

"No." Daddy says it at once, and then again, slower, as though he were checking. "No. I'm not cross. I'll see him when he comes. I'll shake his hand. He can have my chair, and bring all the mangoes he likes, and Ike can play for him till eight o'clock." He looks at the tray. "I've just stopped paying."

"Why now?"

Daddy's quiet for a while. Out in the banking hall somebody's pulling down the shutters for Saturday afternoon, a long rattle.

"Because at Christmas he came in the kitchen," says Daddy. "When you and Ike were getting your coats. And he shut the door. I'd the envelope ready, in my pocket. I always have it ready." He turns his pen over. "And he stood in the kitchen with his cane, and he said, *Lloydie, those are the two best children in Jamaica, and you and Marcia made them.* And for a second I thought — I thought, that's all he's come in to say."

He stops.

"And then he said, *and speaking of the children, Lloydie, there's a man in Half Way Tree.*" Daddy's voice has gone very flat. "He'd just played with you for three hours. He'd just heard Ike go off in the second verse and gone off with him. And he came into my kitchen and used the two of you to get to the envelope. Like a lead-in. Like the first bars before the tune."

Pearl doesn't say anything. She can't.

"I gave it to him," says Daddy. "The envelope. It was Christmas. And I stood in the kitchen after, while you were all on the stairs, and I thought, *I'll not be paid in my own children.* Not in you and Ike. Anything else. Not that." He looks at the tray. "It took me till May to fill the form in. But I knew in the kitchen."

He's quiet for a moment.

"And I thought," says Daddy, "I can stop. And not stop —" He stops.

"Not stop loving him," says Pearl.

Daddy looks at her across the office. His face does something. Then he laughs, quite quietly, and rubs his eyes with the heel of his hand.

"You've been talking to your grandmother."

"She says you don't stop. You just stop paying."

"Yes," says Daddy. "She would. She'd know." He stands up. He gets his jacket off the back of the door. "Come on. Your mother'll have lunch on. And Ike's got Mrs Beckford at two and he'll want you to hear the scales."

At the door he stops, with his hand on it, and looks back at the tray.

"Don't tell Ike," he says. "Not yet. He's seven. He'll understand it one day. Not yet."

"I won't."

"And Pearl." He looks at her. "Thank you for not pretending you didn't see."

---

In the blue book, on the bus home, with Daddy asleep beside her against the window, she writes:

*Sat. 18 May 1985. Daddy cancelled a standing order. To Grandpa Des. First Federal, Miami. Since before I was born.*

She looks at it. Then she does what Grandma taught her. She goes up as well as down. She turns back through the blue book, and the one before, and the one before that. She turns right back to the first one, from when she was five, and the letters in the shoebox, and all the things she's written down and didn't understand.

*Machine in cupboard. No parcels. Daddy laughed at the radio. Do not know why.*

*R.: P.S. I'm very glad about the radio.*

*Your grandmother stopped paying him in 1968,* Auntie Joy wrote once, in the middle of a letter about something else, *and Mum says it took her a whole month.*

*Since before you were born*, Daddy said. Pearl was born in 1971.

She takes her pencil and writes, very small, at the bottom of the page:

*1968. Grandma stopped.*

*1968. Daddy started?*

She sits with it on the bus all the way to Barbican. Daddy's asleep against the window with his mouth a little open. He looks lighter. He looks younger than she's ever seen him, as though he'd put down something she didn't know he was carrying.

She doesn't ask him. She doesn't need to. She can see the column. It goes down, and it goes up, and it comes out the same both ways.

She rubs out the question mark.

*Dear Reggie,*

*I think I have worked something out. I think you knew it already. I think everybody did but me and Ike.*

*Daddy was paying Grandpa Des since 1968. That was when Grandma stopped. He picked it up. He stopped this Saturday. I saw him. He said he is not cross and he will still shake his hand. He said Grandpa Des came into the kitchen at Christmas and said lovely things about me and Ike and then asked for money straight after, and that was the last straw.*

*I think that is why Mummy used to sew at night.*

*I think that is why you were glad about the radio.*

*Am I right? You don't have to say if it's not yours. But I think I'm right.*

*Love from Pearl.*

The envelope that comes back is one page. It's never been one page before. It's in the small neat hand, and it's a little shakier than it used to be, because Reggie's sixty-six now and his hands aren't what they were.

*My dear Pearl,*

*Yes.*

*Your father has done a very hard thing. It took your grandmother a month, in 1968, with the letter in her apron. It took him from Christmas to May. I shouldn't think either of them would tell you it was easy.*

*I was glad about the radio because I'd sat on a harbour wall with your father once, a long time ago, and told him he could stop if he wanted to, and that it wasn't the same as not loving his father. It was the only useful thing I've ever said to him. I've been waiting eleven years to hear whether it was any use. Thank you for telling me. You're the first one who has.*

*You went up as well as down. Your grandmother will be very proud. So am I.*

*Your loving*

*Reggie.*

*P.S. Don't tell Ike yet. You know that. But you might tell him how good his scales are. He writes to me too, you know, since last year. Mostly about the generator.*

---

The summer she's fifteen, Grandma gives her the day book.

Not for good. For a week. She hands it over on the first Monday in August at the office desk. She puts it down in front of Pearl, open at the page, with the pencil on top, square. And she says, "The week's yours. Rooms, meals, laundry, the Ministry, the cay. I'll be in the kitchen. If you want me, you'll come and find me. I'll not come and look."

Pearl stares at it.

"Grandma, I can't —"

"You can. You've done every column in this house since you were eight. You found the Ministry's bill. You've been to your father's bank on Saturdays for a year." Grandma folds her arms. "Down, then up. If it doesn't come out, you find it. You know how."

"What if I get it wrong?"

"Then you'll find it on the way up," says Grandma. "Or I will, on Sunday, when I check it. And then you'll know for next time." She goes to the door. At the door she stops. "I was twenty-two when I kept my first book. It was your grandfather's band's. I'd never been taught a thing. I got it wrong for a year." She looks back. "You're fifteen and you've been taught by the best bookkeeper on the north coast. You'll not get it wrong for a year."

And she goes out, and shuts the door, and doesn't come back.

Pearl keeps the book for a week. It's the hardest week of her life. She gets the laundry wrong on the Tuesday and finds it on the Wednesday, going up. The Ministry sends a man who wants a room that's taken, and she has to tell him no, in Grandma's guest voice. He goes red, and says he'll speak to Mrs Carew, and Pearl says Mrs Carew is in the kitchen and has asked not to be disturbed. It's true; Grandma asked. He goes away. She finds her hands are shaking, and she writes it in the book anyway: *Rm 3 refused, Min. of Agriculture, taken*.

On the Sunday Grandma checks it. She goes down every column and up every column, at the office desk, with Pearl standing behind her chair not breathing. At the bottom of each one she puts a tick, tiny, like a bird's foot. There are nine columns.

There are nine ticks.

Grandma puts the pencil down. She doesn't say anything. She turns round in the chair and looks at Pearl, and her face has gone the way it went in Grandad Hinds's church when Pearl was five, and in the bar door when Ike found the piano.

Then she writes, at the bottom of the week, in her own hand: *Kept P.C. Checked W.T.* And she shuts the book.

---

It's the Saturday after that the Kilburns ask for a song.

They've come up to Harbour House for the end of the season. They do it every year, the whole family, from old Mrs Kilburn in purple down to Winston's grandchildren. Uncle Pascual's come across from the cay, and Auntie Marguerite. Dr Leslie's there in a hat. Mr Wilmot's there, retired now, with the colleague who's also retired. They come every August and take rooms three and five and sit in the bar very correctly and are very kind to Ike. Reggie's in the corner chair by the window. He's got his feet up on a stool, because Auntie Marguerite has decreed it, and a glass of the one small rum he's allowed on a Saturday.

And old Kilburn, who's been told by Winston, who's been told by Alleyne's boy, stands up. He says he hears the little Carews make music and he'd like to hear some, if it's no trouble to the house.

Ike's at the piano before anybody can say anything. He's eight. He's been waiting all summer.

They do *O Little Town*, because it's the one they started with, even though it's August. They do *Abide With Me*, because old Mrs Kilburn asks for it, with the going-off bit in the second verse. Pearl lets Ike have it and doesn't stop in the middle to tell him he's wrong. The bar's gone quiet by the end, and old Mrs Kilburn's got her handkerchief out.

"*Stardust*," says Uncle Pascual, from the bar. "Do *Stardust*."

Pearl looks at Ike. Ike looks at Pearl. Then Ike puts his hands on the keys, and plays the start of it, slow, in the key Grandpa Des taught him at Christmas. It's the key Grandpa Des said was *hers*. It's a bit lower than you'd think.

She comes in on the second line.

She's sung it a hundred times now. In the bath, and at the window in Barbican, and with Ike in the church hall on Saturdays after Mrs Beckford. She knows every word. She knows where it goes up and where Ike's going to go off, and she goes with him, and it's good. It's the best they've done it. She can feel it come back to her off the far wall of the bar, over the heads of the Kilburns and the Ministry and Dr Leslie's hat.

She's halfway through the second verse when she hears it.

Another voice. Under hers. Low.

It's coming from the kitchen door.

Pearl doesn't stop. She doesn't know how she doesn't stop. She keeps singing, and she turns her head, and there's Grandma.

She's in the kitchen door with a tea towel over her shoulder, in her apron, with her sleeves rolled up. She's standing quite still, with one hand on the door frame. And she's singing. She's singing the second verse of *Stardust* under Pearl, a third below. She isn't loud. She isn't trying to be heard. But it's there, it's the same voice as Pearl's, only older and darker and lower, like the same tune played on a bigger string.

Ike's head comes round. He looks at Grandma. His hands don't stop. He's eight and he's been playing since he was four, and he knows you don't stop. But he looks at Grandma with his whole face open, and then he looks at Pearl, and then he bends over the keys and makes room. He makes room for both of them. Nobody's following. It isn't a parade.

The three of them go to the end of the verse like that. Pearl and Grandma and Ike.

The bar's so quiet Pearl can hear the sea.

At the end, Ike plays two more bars, soft, the way Grandpa Des does. And stops.

Nobody says anything.

Pearl looks at Grandma in the kitchen door. Grandma's face is wet. She's not wiping it. She's looking past Pearl, across the bar, to the corner chair by the window.

Reggie's in the corner chair. He's got his glass in his hand, and he hasn't drunk from it. His eyes are shut. He's sitting very still, with his head back against the wall, not moving at all. And Pearl knows that look. She's seen it at the rail of the north pen, when the calf's been down a long time and hasn't come up yet. He's not breathing. He's waiting.

He opens his eyes and looks at Grandma. Just looks. Across the whole bar, over everybody's heads, the way he looks at her across the fish.

Then Uncle Pascual starts to clap, and cry, both at once, very loudly. Then everybody's clapping, and old Mrs Kilburn is saying *Lord, Win, Lord, Lord*, and Mr Wilmot's standing up, and Ike's gone pink to the ears and hidden his face in his arms on top of the piano.

And Grandma takes the tea towel off her shoulder, and folds it, very square, and says, "Well. Somebody has to see to the puddings," and goes back into the kitchen.

---

Pearl finds her there afterwards, when the Kilburns have gone and the bar's empty.

She's at the sink with her back to the door. She's not doing anything. She's just standing there with her hands flat either side of the sink, looking out of the window at the harbour.

Pearl stands in the doorway.

"You sang," she says.

"I sang." Grandma doesn't turn round. "Don't tell anybody. They'll all want it again."

"*Everybody* heard, Grandma. The whole *Bay*."

"Then don't tell anybody *else*."

Pearl comes and stands beside her at the sink. She looks at Grandma's face in the dark window. It's still wet.

"With Ike," she says. "You sang with Ike. Playing."

"Yes."

"He plays like Grandpa Des."

Grandma's quiet for a long time.

"He does," she says. "He plays exactly like him. He goes off in the same places." She wipes her face with the back of her wrist. "I've heard it all summer through the wall, love. Every day. I've stood at this sink and heard your grandfather's hands on that piano, in that boy, and I've not been able to go through that door."

Pearl doesn't say anything.

"And tonight I did," says Grandma. "And it was Ike. It was only Ike. It was Ike's hands, and Ike's going-off. And it was you, singing. It wasn't your grandfather at all." She turns round, at last, and looks at Pearl. "It was *mine*. It was ours. It was just us, and it was good, and it was mine."

She puts her hand on Pearl's face, the way she did in the vestry when Pearl was five.

"Both," says Grandma. "You'll have both. I had to wait thirty-eight years for mine. You'll not have to wait at all."

In the blue book that night, the eighth one, Pearl turns right back to the first. There's the page with the heading, in big careful letters, from when she was nine: *Things That Come Back The Same Twice*.

She reads down the list.

*The tide.*

*A column, if you go down and then up.*

*The calf, after 31.*

*The back wall at church.*

*Reggie.*

*Grandpa Des. Only in the music.*

She sits with it for a long time. Then she takes her pencil and adds, underneath, in her fifteen-year-old hand, which is getting small and neat now, like somebody else's:

*Daddy. Stopped paying. Still shook his hand.*

*Ike. Goes off, comes back.*

*Grandma. 38 years. But she came back.*

And then, on the next line, after thinking about it a while:

*Me.*

She shuts the book. Through the window she can see the cay, long and dark, with the lamp at the near end. And in the far corner of the bar below, she can hear Reggie and Grandma. They're talking very low, and then they're not talking, and then Grandma's laughing, quietly, the way she only laughs at him.

And then, so softly that Pearl isn't sure she hasn't made it up, somebody humming. Low. A third below the tune.
