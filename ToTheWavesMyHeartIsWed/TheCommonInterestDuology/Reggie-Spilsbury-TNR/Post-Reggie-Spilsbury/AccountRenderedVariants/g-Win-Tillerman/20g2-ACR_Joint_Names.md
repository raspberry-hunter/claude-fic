# Joint Names

The piano is flat at the top. That's the first thing Maybeth knows about Harbour House, before she knows where her room is or which of the grey-haired women on the verandah is Pearl's grandmother.

She knows it because somebody's playing it when the boat comes in. They've come on the Kingston boat, the six of them. Gram's in the stern with her hat tied on and both hands on her pocketbook. She's not said a word since the airport, because she's decided flying was a thing she'd done once and would not discuss. Dicey's in the bow watching how the boy handles the tiller. Jeff has his guitar between his knees. James is reading the tide table he bought in Kingston. Sammy's standing up, which the boy has told him twice not to, because he can see Pearl on the harbour wall.

And Maybeth's listening. Because across the water, through an open window in the big pale house above the harbour, somebody's playing *Stardust* on an old upright. It's slow and a bit careful, and at the top of the tune, where it goes up, the notes go flat. Not wrong. Tired. As if the piano had been played a very long time and had earned it.

Maybeth doesn't know yet that it's Ike. She only knows she likes the piano already, the way you like a dog that comes up to you in a strange yard.

---

Pearl's grandmother is the tall one, in navy, with her arms folded, standing at the top of the harbour steps. She doesn't come down. She waits. She watches them all come up the steps, one by one, and Maybeth can see her look at each of them. It's not unkind. It's the look Gram gives a field before she decides what's to go in it.

Beside her there's a small old man in a white shirt, sitting on a bench. He's very thin, with very white hair, and he's got his hands folded on top of a stick. He doesn't get up, and Maybeth can see from how he's sitting that he's been told not to. But when Pearl runs down the steps to Sammy and Sammy picks her up clear off the bottom step, the old man's face does a thing. Maybeth sees it. She's always seen things like that. It's one of the things she's good at, Gram says, being slow: she has time to look.

His face goes all at once, and then quite still. As though there were nothing else on the harbour.

That's Reggie, Maybeth thinks. Pearl's told her. Pearl told her at Easter, at the farm, at the piano, for an hour, while Maybeth played and Pearl sang and then they stopped and just talked. Reggie counts things. Reggie can't carry a tune. Reggie never once told Pearl what to do, and Pearl wanted him to so badly she cried on a verandah, and he still didn't, and she said it was the best thing anybody ever did for her.

Maybeth comes up the steps last, because she always does. When she gets to the top, the old man on the bench looks up at her.

"You'll be Maybeth," he says. "The one who plays."

"Yes."

"Pearl says you're slow at everything but that." He says it quite simply, the way you'd say somebody was tall. "She says it's the best thing she's heard in her life, you playing *Stardust* in your front room."

Maybeth feels her face go hot. People have said *slow* to her all her life. They've said it at school, at the bank, in the shop in Crisfield when she counts the change twice. They say it in a voice that's sorry for her, or cross, or careful.

He doesn't. He says it as though it were a fact about the weather, and a good one.

"I'm slow," she says. "Yes."

"So's the tide," says Reggie. "Nobody's ever called the tide stupid. It gets everywhere in the end, and it's never once been wrong about where." He looks at her over his glasses. "The slow things are the ones that last, in my experience. I've watched a great many things in the sea for fifty years. The quick ones are very pretty. The slow ones are still there in the morning."

Maybeth stands at the top of the harbour steps at the Bay with her suitcase in her hand and can't say anything at all.

"There's a piano in the bar," says Reggie. "It's flat at the top. My wife won't have it tuned. Ike's on it now. He'll give you the bench, if you ask. He's been waiting all week to hear you."

---

She plays for him that night.

It's late, after the supper, which was fish, and a great deal of it. Gram said *well* at the fish in a voice that meant it was very good and she'd not say so. Ike gives her the bench without her asking. He's twenty, and he's home from London for the wedding, and he's round and serious and doesn't say much, like her. He just gets up and makes a little movement with his hand, *go on*, and goes and leans on the wall.

She plays *Stardust*. In Ike's key, which is Pearl's. Slowly, the way she plays everything.

Reggie's in a chair by the window, with his feet up on a stool because somebody told him to. He doesn't move while she plays. He's got his hands folded. He's not looking at her; he's looking at the floor, about a yard in front of his feet. And at the top of the tune, where the notes go flat, she sees him stop breathing.

When she's done nobody says anything for a while.

Then Reggie says, very quietly, "Again. If you would. The last eight bars."

She plays the last eight bars again.

"You don't hurry it," he says. "At the top. Where it's flat. Ike hurries a little, there, because he knows it's flat and he wants to get past it. Pearl goes up over it, because she can. You don't do either. You let it be flat." He looks up at her. "I've heard that piano every day for twenty-four years, and nobody's ever let it be flat before. It sounded as if it had been waiting."

Maybeth sits on the bench with her hands in her lap.

She'll remember that. She'll remember it at the farm, in the front room, for years. Two winters later, when she's playing *Stardust* in the evening on her own piano, she'll suddenly understand what he meant, all the way down, and sit down and write to him about it. It'll take her three days to write the letter, because she's slow. And he'll write back four pages.

Right now she only says, "Thank you."

"No," says Reggie. "Thank you. I'd not known it could sound like that."

---

Win's in the kitchen at half past five on the Friday, because she's always in the kitchen at half past five. And at twenty to six the old woman from Maryland comes in, fully dressed, with her hair pinned, and stands in the doorway.

Win looks at her. She looks back.

"You'll be up for the hens," says Win.

"There aren't any hens."

"No. But you'll be up for them anyway."

The old woman considers this. Then something happens at the corner of her mouth, and she comes in and sits down at the kitchen table without being asked, the way Win would in another woman's kitchen if she'd decided she liked it.

"Abigail Tillerman," she says. "They call me Gram. You needn't."

"Winifred Tar. They call me Win. You may."

Win puts the kettle on. She puts out two cups, the good ones, and then thinks better of it and puts out two of the kitchen ones, thick, with the chip on the handle. Mrs Tillerman looks at them and nods, once. Win knew she would.

They drink their tea. Neither of them says anything for some time. Outside the harbour's grey and flat, and there's a boat going out past the head, and a cock crowing somewhere up the hill behind Marguerite's.

"That's the first time I've been in an aeroplane," says Mrs Tillerman at last.

"How did you find it?"

"I didn't care for it." She puts her cup down. "I'll do it again, though. If I've got to."

"You'll have to," says Win. "If she's going to Baltimore. They'll be back and forth. We'll all of us be back and forth."

"I know it." Mrs Tillerman looks at her across the table. "You don't want her to go."

Win doesn't answer that for a while.

"No," she says. "I don't. I'll not say so to her. She's done her columns and they came out, and she's the one to live in them." She turns her cup round. "But no. I don't want her to go. I'd have her in that room upstairs doing my books till she was sixty, if I had my way."

"And you'll not have your way."

"No."

"No more will I," says Mrs Tillerman. "I'd have had Sammy on the farm since he was eighteen. He went and hit tennis balls in Jamaica." She says *tennis balls* the way she might say *moonshine*. "And now he's bringing a wife home who can do accounts, and I'm to be grateful. I am grateful. Dicey keeps her receipts in a coffee tin."

"Pearl told me."

"Pearl did the whole year for her at Easter. Two nights. Dicey stood behind her the whole time with her arms folded." Mrs Tillerman looks at Win's arms, which are folded. "Like that."

Win unfolds them. Then she folds them again.

"I taught her," she says. "Pearl. The accounts. She was eight. She went up as well as down the first time I showed her."

"Then you're the one to thank." Mrs Tillerman nods, as if a matter had been settled. "I'll say it once. Thank you."

"You're welcome," says Win. "I'll not need it said twice."

"No. I didn't think you would."

---

They have a second cup. Mrs Tillerman asks about the house, and Win tells her: 1958, and the loan from her mother, and the rooms, and the day book. And Mrs Tillerman asks to see the day book, which nobody's asked before in forty years. Win fetches it from the office, this year's. Mrs Tillerman turns the pages very slowly, with a licked finger, reading the columns the way you'd read a letter, and at the bottom of each page she touches the tick.

"You go up as well," she says.

"Every time."

"My husband did the farm books," says Mrs Tillerman. "He went down. Never up. He was a hard man and he was sure of everything, and half of it was wrong, and I never said." She closes the book. "I've been doing them myself since he died. Badly. I'd not tell anybody that but you."

Win looks at her.

"Mine was a soft man," she says. "Charming. He never did a book in his life. I did his. I paid for him for twenty-one years." She takes the day book back. "Came to the same thing, in the end. Yours hard and mine soft."

"It did," says Mrs Tillerman. "It comes to the same thing." She's quiet a moment. "And we both of us ended up with the children in the end. Mine were my daughter's. Yours were your own. And now the two of them are marrying."

"The two of them are marrying," agrees Win.

They sit with that.

"Your husband," says Mrs Tillerman. "The one on the bench. With the stick."

"Reggie."

"He's not well."

"He's very well," says Win, "for a man of seventy-nine with a heart the doctor's been counting since 1970. He does every single thing he's told. He'll be at the wedding and he'll stand for the vows and he'll sit for the hymns." She looks at the harbour. "He's the other kind. Not hard and not soft. The kind that stays."

"Sammy's that kind," says Mrs Tillerman.

"I know he is. I watched him pick her up off the bottom step."

"He picked me up once," says Mrs Tillerman. "Off the kitchen floor. When I fell. He was eleven and I was twice his weight. Dicey said he couldn't. He did it anyway." She stands up, slowly, with a hand on the table. "He's the one that turns up. The others think, and worry, and build boats, and play. Sammy turns up. He always has."

She stands in the kitchen door in the grey light, very small and very straight.

"She'll do," says Mrs Tillerman. "Your Pearl. I said so at Easter. I'll say it to you now, so you've heard it. She'll do."

"So will your Sammy," says Win.

"I know he will. I raised him." Mrs Tillerman puts her hand on the door frame. "Do they sing, in this house?"

"Not much. They play. Ike plays. Pearl sings."

"And you?"

Win doesn't answer for a moment.

"I did," she says. "Once. A long time ago. And once or twice lately."

"So did I," says Mrs Tillerman. "A long time ago." She looks at Win. "We sing at the table, at home. All of us. It's how they came back to me, the children. They came singing. I'd forgotten how." She's quiet. "You'll hear us tonight. You'll have to put up with it."

"I'll put up with it," says Win.

"You might join in," says Mrs Tillerman, and goes out to look at the harbour before Win can answer.

---

James has been seated beside the old man on purpose. He knows this because Pearl told him so, on the Friday, with a look he's learned to be wary of. *You'll sit by Reggie at the breakfast. He'll want to talk to you. Try to let him eat something.*

So at three o'clock on the Saturday, with his brother married an hour and a half and the whole yard of Harbour House full of tables under the almond trees, James is sitting at the top table with a plate of fish. On his left is a man of seventy-nine with a stick hooked over the back of his chair. On his right is Pearl's uncle, or not-uncle, a large old Spaniard called Dr Pobil, who's cried through the whole of the ceremony and shows no sign of stopping.

James has prepared, a little, because he always prepares. He doesn't know much about the old man. Pearl said *marine biologist*, and *fifty years on the cay*, and *he counts things*, and then laughed and wouldn't say any more. But James has been looking at the cay since Thursday, from the boat and then from the harbour wall, and it's been bothering him. He's got three questions about it ready. He's been turning them over since the boat.

He asks the first one before he's eaten anything.

"The cay," he says. "Across the channel. I was looking at it from the boat on Thursday. It's very low. How does it drain, in the rains? It can't be more than four feet above high water at the highest point, and there's no —"

"Five feet two," says Dr Tar.

James stops.

"At the highest point. By the north pen." The old man's put his fork down. "It drains to the south, through the sand, very slowly, and in a bad year it doesn't drain at all and we pump the long room. Why do you ask?"

"Because I couldn't see how it could," says James. "There's no gradient. I worked it out from the boat."

"From the *boat*?"

"From the angle of the long-room steps against the waterline. I estimated —"

"Show me," says Dr Tar.

And James finds that he's drawing on the paper tablecloth with the end of a fork. The steps, the waterline, the angle, his estimate. The old man leans over and watches him do it, very close, with his glasses pushed up. At the end he takes the fork out of James's hand and corrects the angle by about four degrees. Then he looks at James.

"That's very good," he says. "That's very good indeed, from a boat. You were four degrees out because you didn't allow for the tide; it was making, on Thursday, you'd have been looking at it a foot higher up the steps than the mean. What else?"

James stares at him.

"The cistern," he says. "Behind the long room. It's too small. For forty people in a dry season, it's —"

"It's too small by about a third," says Dr Tar. "I told the Board so in 1961. I told them again in 1968. Jean told them in 1979. They built a second one in 1983, and it's in the wrong place. Go on."

"The jetty," says James. He can't stop. He can feel himself not stopping, the way he does in the library at two in the morning. "The Institute's jetty. It's on the north side of the cay. But the prevailing wind's from the east, and the swell comes round the north point, and in any kind of weather you'd get a surge on that side that'd make it very hard to bring a boat alongside. It ought to be on the south, in the lee. Why's it on the north?"

The old man doesn't answer at once.

He sits back in his chair. He looks across the channel at the cay, low and green in the afternoon, with the jetty sticking out from the north side like a finger. He looks at it for a long time.

"Because I put it there," he says. "In 1953. Because it was nearer the pens, and I didn't want to carry buckets further than I had to. I've been bringing boats alongside it in a surge for forty-five years." He turns his head and looks at James. "You're right. It ought to be on the south. I've never admitted that to anybody. Jean's been telling me since 1956."

On James's right, Dr Pobil makes a sound into his napkin like a kettle.

"Forty-five *years*," says Dr Pobil. "*Hombre*. Forty-five years I've been bringing the launch round that point in a swell, and you tell a boy you met on Thursday, in the middle of the fish —"

"He asked me properly," says Dr Tar. "You only ever shouted."

"I *shouted* because you'd not *listen* —"

"*Pascual*," says a very small, very old woman on Dr Pobil's other side. She's got white hair and a black bag at her feet. "Eat your fish."

Dr Pobil eats his fish.

---

"What do you do?" says Dr Tar.

James blinks. He'd been going to ask what the old man actually did on the cay, all those fifty years. He's lost his place.

"I'm a — I'm in medicine. A resident. At Hopkins. Internal medicine. I want to do cardiology."

"Do you."

The old man's face has changed. James has seen that change before, but only in his own family: in Maybeth, when you put a piano in front of her; in Dicey, when there's a boat. It's the face of somebody who's been handed the thing.

"Tell me," says Dr Tar, "how you count a pulse."

"I — the usual way. Radial. Fifteen seconds and multiply by four, or thirty seconds if it's irregular, or a full minute if —"

"No. How do *you* count it. Your fingers. Where do you put them. How hard. Do you look at your watch, or at the patient?"

James opens his mouth, and shuts it.

Nobody has ever asked him that. Not in medical school, not in three years of wards. They've asked him what the rate was. They've never asked him how he got it.

"At the watch," he says slowly. "I look at the watch."

"Why?"

"Because — because that's where the time is."

"The time's in the pulse," says Dr Tar. "The watch only tells you what it's doing. If you look at the patient, you'll see the breath. You'll see where the beat comes, against the breath. That's where you'll see it go wrong, before it's gone wrong. Here." And he holds out his wrist across the tablecloth, across James's plate of fish, over the fork-drawing of the cay. "Count it. Look at me, not your watch. Tell me the figure."

James puts his fingers on the old man's wrist.

He doesn't look at his watch. It's the hardest thing he's done in months. He looks at the old man's face, and at his chest under the white shirt, going up and down, very steadily. He feels the beat under his fingers, thin and quite regular, and he counts. He counts it against the breath, the way he's been told, and something happens that's never happened on the wards. He can *feel* the shape of it. The beat slows, a little, on the out-breath, and comes up again on the in, like a tide under a jetty.

"Sixty-eight," he says, when he's got it.

"Marguerite had seventy-two this morning," says Dr Tar. "At rest. You've a light touch. You're missing one after the pause, on the out-breath; it's a small one, it's always been small. You'll feel it next time." He takes his wrist back. "It's the out-breath. That's where the heart tells you things. Everybody counts on the in."

At the end of the table, the very small old woman with the black bag has turned round in her chair and is looking at James with an expression he can't read.

"Sixty-eight's not bad," she says. "For a first go. I missed that one for two years."

---

After that James asks him what he works on, and it takes the rest of the afternoon.

He asks about the pens, and the animals in them, and the Institute. The old man tells him. Then James asks how you'd weigh an animal the size of a kitchen, and the old man tells him that, with the sling, and the line, and Pascual at the other end. James asks what they eat, and how you'd know if they were ill, and why they come into the pens at all when the reef's open. Every answer's got a figure in it. Every figure makes James ask another question. And every question James asks, the old man answers, and then asks one back. *Why do you want to know that? What would you do with it? How would you measure it, on a ward?* Sometimes the questions come back so fast that James has to stop and think, with his fork in the air, and the old man waits, and watches him think, and doesn't help.

Across the table James's sister Dicey catches his eye once. She raises her eyebrows so far they disappear under her hair. Then she turns back to Pearl's father with every sign of relief. Further down, Pearl's grandmother is talking to Gram about hens in a low, contented voice, and nobody at either end of the top table is being asked anything at all. Dr Pobil, beside James, has stopped crying and started eating, steadily, with the air of a man who's been let off something.

"Leave them," James hears Mrs Tar say, at one point, to somebody who's come up with a question for her husband. "It's the quietest the rest of us have been all week."


---

He gets to the question he's been saving all afternoon much later, when the fish is gone and the cake's been cut, and Sammy and Pearl are going round the tables.

"The calf," says James. "In 1956. You said earlier you'd sit at the rail and you could tell she was going to come up before she came up. Before there was anything to see. How did you know?"

Dr Tar's quiet for a moment.

"I didn't know," he says. "Not the way you mean. I'd only counted it enough times. So many times that I could tell when a figure was about to be wrong, before it was." He looks at James. "That's all. That's the whole of it. You count the thing so many times that the next one surprises you, if it's going to be different. It's not knowing. It's having counted."

James looks at him for a long time.

Then he takes the pen out of his breast pocket, and pulls a paper napkin towards him, and writes it down. *Counted it enough times to tell when a figure's about to be wrong.* He folds the napkin in four and puts it in his wallet.

"You'll quote me," says Dr Tar, watching him do it.

"I might."

"Ask first."

"I will," says James, and means it, and finds, four years later, that he's very nearly forgotten to.

---

Pearl doesn't remember the vows. She'd been warned she wouldn't. Grandma said she didn't remember a word of hers except *contralto*, which wasn't in them.

She remembers pieces.

She remembers Daddy's arm on the walk up the aisle of the Methodist chapel at the Bay. It's the same aisle, Grandma says, that Daddy walked Grandma up in 1974. Daddy's arm was shaking, and then it stopped. She remembers Grandad Hinds, who's eighty-nine and came from Kingston in the back of Lloyd's car with a cushion, standing up at the front beside the Bay's own minister. He has his big cello voice gone thin and still perfectly clear. She remembers Ike at the chapel piano, playing her in, very slow. She remembers Maybeth in the second pew on the groom's side, crying without making any sound. And Mrs Tillerman beside her with her hat tied on and her hands folded on her pocketbook, not crying at all, watching everything.

She remembers Sammy's face.

He's standing at the front in the suit Mummy made him, and he doesn't smile the smile, because he hasn't got one. He just watches her come up the aisle. He watches her the whole way, the way Reggie watches the calf's calf come up, as though there were nothing else in the chapel. When she gets to the front and Daddy puts her hand in his, he doesn't say anything. He holds it. Very carefully. Like a man holding a racket he's going to use for the rest of his life.

And she remembers, when the minister says *who gives this woman*, a sound from the front pew on her side. She looks, and it's Reggie. He's stood up. He was told he could sit, and he's stood up anyway, with his stick in one hand and Grandma's arm in the other. He's not saying anything. He's only standing. And Daddy, who's said *I do*, looks round at him, and Reggie nods, once, and sits down again.

It wasn't in the order of service. Nobody had asked him. He just stood up.

---

The breakfast is in the yard under the almond trees, because where else. Mummy and Grandma did it between them, with Mrs Joseph's granddaughter, and there's a great deal of fish, and a cake that took four days. Somebody's put a garland on the generator again. It's a new generator, and Ike says it doesn't stick, and Grandma hits it anyway, every morning, for luck.

Daddy makes a speech. It's very short. It's all about her going up as well as down, and at the end he looks at Sammy and says, *she'll check you, you know*. And Sammy says, loud enough for the whole yard, *I know, sir, she already has*, and everybody laughs.

James makes a speech, because Sammy asked him to. It's much too long and has three quotations in it, and nobody minds, because at the end he looks at his brother and says, *He's the one who turns up. I'm the one who worries. I've been worrying for twenty years. I don't need to worry about this.* Then he sits down very fast and puts his face in his napkin, and Dicey pats him on the back without looking.

And then Pearl asks Reggie.

She'd asked him on the Monday, on the verandah, whether he'd say something at the breakfast. He'd said no. He said he couldn't make a speech without the voice, and he'd not do the voice at her wedding. He said Pascual would have to do it, and Pascual said he'd cry, and Reggie said *yes, that's why*. And they left it.

But now she looks down the top table at him, over the ruins of the cake, and he's looking back at her. He's looked at her the whole afternoon, whenever she's glanced his way. He's got his hands folded on his stick.

"Reggie," she says. "Please."

He looks at her for a long moment. Then he gets up. He does it slowly, with his hand on the back of his chair, and Auntie Marguerite half gets up too and then sits back down. The yard goes quiet.

He doesn't do the voice. Pearl's listening for it. She's heard it only twice in her life, and she knows exactly what it sounds like. It doesn't come.

"I've known Pearl," he says, in his own voice, which is small and a bit hoarse and doesn't carry, so that the back tables lean forward, "since she was three. She put her arms up at me in that kitchen and decided I'd do. I've been trying to deserve it ever since." He stops. "She's never once let me off anything. Not a figure, not a question, not a promise. She held me to eighty when she was four."

Laughter, a little.

"I've watched her do her columns," he says. "I've watched her go up as well as down, for twenty years. I've watched her find the thing that was wrong and put it right, and I've watched her find the thing that was right and not be talked out of it." He looks at Sammy. "She found you. She checked. You came back the same twice. Every time. I've read it in her letters, a year of them. I'd like it on the record that I knew before she asked me."

He picks up his glass. It's water; Auntie Marguerite has seen to that.

"To Pearl and Sammy," he says. "Wherever they are."

"*Wherever they are*," says the yard, and the Bay, and the Tillermans, and a large old Spaniard at the end of the top table who's crying into his napkin again.

Reggie sits down. His hand's shaking a little on the glass. Grandma puts hers over it, on the tablecloth, and leaves it there.

---

At nine the Tillermans sing.

Pearl had told everybody they would. She'd told Grandma, and Mummy, and Ike. And still nobody at Harbour House is ready for it: the whole of Sammy's family, in the bar, round the flat-topped piano, just starting. There isn't a signal. Maybeth's at the keys, and Jeff has his guitar on his knee, and somebody, Dicey she thinks, starts a line of something about a sailor. And then they're all in it, Sammy and James and Dicey and Jeff and Maybeth, and old Mrs Tillerman in the corner chair with her eyes shut. They sing it all the way through, in parts, without looking at each other, as though it were the most ordinary thing in the world, like drinking water.

The Kilburns stop talking. The Ministry men in rooms three and five come down in their dressing gowns and stand on the stairs.

Then Maybeth plays the start of something Pearl knows. She plays it slow. It's *Amazing Grace*, because Mrs Tillerman asked, at Easter, in writing, for Pearl to sing it at the wedding. And Pearl comes in.

And then, on the second verse, she hears a voice come in under hers. Low. A third below.

She turns her head. It's Grandma, in the kitchen door, with a tea towel over her shoulder.

And in the corner chair, very thin and very true, a third below Grandma, Mrs Tillerman opens her eyes and sings too.

Pearl sings over both of them. Maybeth plays under all three, slow, letting the top be flat. Nobody's following. It isn't a parade. The two old women sing, one in the kitchen door and one in the corner chair, looking at each other across the bar at Harbour House. It's as though they'd been doing it for years and had simply not got round to meeting.

In the chair by the window, Reggie has his eyes shut and isn't breathing.
