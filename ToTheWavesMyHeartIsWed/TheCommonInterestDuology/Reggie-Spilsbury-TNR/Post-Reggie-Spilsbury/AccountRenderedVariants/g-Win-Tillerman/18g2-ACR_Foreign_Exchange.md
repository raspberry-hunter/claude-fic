# Foreign Exchange

Auntie Marguerite has him on the verandah with her fingers on his wrist when Pearl comes up the harbour steps.

It's the Monday before the concert. Pearl's come on the early boat, the long way round from Kingston, to fetch them. She's taken three days off from the Pegasus, which the general manager said he'd not survive. Pearl said he'd survive very well, since she'd done the month-end on Friday night till two, and left it on his desk reconciled, with a tick.

She stops at the top of the steps and watches. Auntie Marguerite is eighty-one and has given up the surgery. She hasn't given this up. She sits in Grandma's chair with her little black book on her knee, looking at her watch, while Reggie sits in his and looks at the harbour and lets her.

"Well?" says Grandma, from the door.

Auntie Marguerite doesn't answer for a whole minute. Then she lets go of his wrist and writes in the book.

"Seventy-two," she says. "At rest. At seventy-eight years of age." She shuts the book. "He'll go to Kingston. On the boat and in Lloyd's car, not the bus. He'll take one before the chapel steps, and one before he stands for the anthem, and he'll not stand for the anthem if it's longer than four verses. And he'll be home by Wednesday, and I'll have it again." She looks at Reggie over her spectacles. "I've been taking this pulse since 1970, Reggie Tar. You're the only patient I've ever had who did every single thing I told him. It's very nearly insulting."

"I had reasons," says Reggie.

"You had reasons," agrees Auntie Marguerite. "And you'll see eighty on them. Eighty-five, I shouldn't wonder, if Win keeps feeding you." She gets up, slowly, with a hand on the arm of the chair. She's small and white-haired and quite straight. She looks at Pearl at the top of the steps, and something happens at the corner of her mouth. "You'll be wanting him. Go on. I've done."

She goes in past Grandma. Reggie turns his head and sees Pearl.

He's old. She knows that, and she always knows it, but it comes at her new every time she comes up these steps. He's small in the chair, and thin, thinner than ever, and his hair's gone all the way white and very fine. His hands on the arms of the chair are all bones and spots. But his eyes are the same. They're very blue, behind the same kind of glasses, and when they see her they do the thing they've always done. They go all at once, and then quite still. As though there were nothing else on the verandah.

"Pearl," he says. "You came on the early boat."

"I came to fetch you."

"I know. I've been told." He holds out his hand. "Your aunt says I may go. She's written it down."

---

She asks him on the Monday night.

She's had it in her for a month, since June, since Sammy asked. She's had it in the blue book, which is the twenty-first blue book now, on a page by itself. She's had it on the boat all morning, and on the harbour steps, and all through dinner, which was fish, because it's always fish. She's waited till Grandma's done the day book and gone in, and the Ministry men are in the bar, and it's just the two of them. Her in Grandma's chair with her feet up on the rail, him in his with his feet on the stool, with the cay across the channel, long and dark, and the lamp at the near end.

"Reggie."

"Mm."

"There's somebody I want to marry."

He doesn't move. He doesn't take his feet off the stool. But she sees his hands go still on the arms of the chair.

"The tennis professional," he says. "The American. You've written."

"I've written about him since last year."

"Since last April," says Reggie. "The fourteenth. You said the Pegasus had a new man on the courts, from Maryland, on a two-year contract. He came to the Starlight Lounge on the last Friday and sat at the back and didn't talk through a song. Not one. You asked him why, and he said his family sings, and in his family you don't talk while somebody's singing, you'd get a look from his grandmother that'd take the paint off a boat." He turns his head. "You said you liked him for that before you liked him for anything else."

Pearl stares at him.

"You *remember* that."

"I've got it in a drawer," says Reggie. "With the others. I've got four hundred and twelve of them now. I've got them in order." He's quiet a moment. "He asked you. In June."

"On the court. At six in the morning, before it gets hot. It's the only hour it's his." She hears herself laugh, a little. "He'd swept it. He'd swept the whole court, Reggie. He said he wanted it to be tidy."

"And you said yes."

"I said yes." She pulls her knees up. "Daddy's said yes. Mummy said yes before I'd finished the sentence. She says she knew in February. Grandma likes him; she says he's the only man she's ever met who carries a tray the right way. Auntie Joy's written from London in capitals, all good ones." She stops. "And I went to Maryland at Easter. To meet them."

"You wrote."

"I wrote four pages. I didn't write the half of it." She's looking at the cay. "There's a farm, on the Chesapeake, on a point. There's a boat shed where his sister builds boats, by hand, and a kitchen with a table that seats twelve if they squeeze. And his grandmother's very old, and very small, and says exactly what she thinks. And the first thing she said to me was, *you'll be the one who sings*. And the second thing was, *can you do accounts, because nobody in this family can*." Pearl laughs. "She meant Dicey. That's the one who builds boats. Her books are — Reggie, they're a *crime*. Receipts in a coffee tin. I sat up two nights at Easter in the boat shed and did the whole of last year for her, and she stood behind me the whole time with her arms folded, like Grandma. And at the end she said, *where have you been all my life*." She laughs again. "And she *meant* it, which is more than Julian ever did. She's asked me to do this year's as well." She stops. "Reggie, she's *Grandma*. She's Grandma, only white, and from Maryland, and she keeps hens."

Reggie's smiling.

"And his sister," says Pearl. "Maybeth. The middle one. She plays the piano. Not like Ike. Different. Slower. She's not quick at anything, Sammy says, except that. We sat at the piano in the front room the second night, and she played, and I sang. And the whole family just — stopped. Stopped eating, stopped talking. They all stood in the doorway." Her voice goes. "They *sing*, Reggie. All of them. At the table, washing up, in the car. They sang me a song about a sailor on the way to the airport. They're a *singing* family. I've never been in a house that sings."

"You've been in one your whole life," says Reggie. "Only it sang through the walls."

Pearl can't say anything to that for a moment.

"Tell me the other thing," says Reggie. "The one you're not telling me."

She looks at him.

"Baltimore," she says at last. "His contract's up next June. He's got an offer from a club in Baltimore, and it's near enough he could get down to the farm at weekends. Maybeth's running it now, mostly, and Gram can't do what she did, and Sammy wants to be there to help. He says Maybeth never asks for anything, so somebody has to just turn up. He wants to go home. He wants me to come." She pulls her knees up tighter. "He's not asking me to choose. He says so. He says we'll do it however I want."

"Has he thought about how?"

"He has." She laughs, a little. "Not columns. Sammy doesn't do columns. He thinks about a thing for a long time, walking up and down the court, and then he knows. But he's thought of things. And then he went and asked James, because James thinks of the things he doesn't."

"James?"

"His brother. The clever one." Pearl's smiling now, in the dark. "Reggie, you should hear him about James. Every time James comes up. *James says. James thinks. James worked out.* Like other men talk about a brother who's rich, or plays for the West Indies. James is the smartest person Sammy's ever met and he'll tell you so in the first five minutes. And James said —" She stops, and her voice goes. "James said people do it long distance. For a while. That we could. He said it's four hours on a plane, and there are pianos in Baltimore, and hotels that need their books done, and nothing says it has to be all at once. Sammy'd not thought of that. He came back from the telephone looking like somebody'd handed him a whole new court." She swallows. "So he's not saying *we'll work it out*, the way Julian did. He's saying *James says we could, and I think James is right, and what do you think?* And then he waits for me to say."

"But."

"But it's *here*." She puts her hand flat on the arm of Grandma's chair. "It's Harbour House, and the Pegasus, and the church hall, and Ike on Saturdays, and Mummy at the machine, and you. It's you, Reggie. You're seventy-eight."

She stops. Then she says the thing she hasn't said to anybody, not Sammy, not Mummy, not the blue book.

"And if I'm in Baltimore," she says, "and something happens. To you. I'll be four hours away on a plane and a boat and Lloyd's car. And I'll get the telephone call at a club in Maryland. And by the time I get to the harbour steps —" She can't finish it.

Reggie doesn't say anything for a moment.

"Yes," he says. "That's true. It could happen like that."

She'd wanted him to say it couldn't. She knows he won't. He never has, about anything.

"I'm seventy-eight," says Reggie, "with a heart your aunt's been counting since 1970. I'll not pretend to you. It could be next week. It could be at eighty-five, if Win has her way. Nobody knows, Pearl. Not Marguerite, not me." He turns his head and looks at her. "But it'll happen whether you're in Baltimore or Barbican or sitting in that chair. You'd not stop it by staying. You'd only have stayed."

Pearl can't answer.

"And if it does," he says, more gently, "I'll still be there. Not the way you want. I know that. But there are four hundred and twelve letters in a drawer on the cay, and your grandmother knows which drawer. There's a list in your blue book with me on it. You'll count between breaths at the rail of something, and that's me. You'll ask a question and then wait for the whole of the answer. You do it already. I've watched you do it to Sammy." He's quiet. "That doesn't go anywhere, whatever Marguerite's book says. It's yours. I gave it you. It's not a thing I can take back by dying."

Pearl puts her face down on her knees.

After a while she feels his hand on the top of her head. It stays there a long time.

He doesn't say anything for a long time. Across the channel there's the lamp on the long-room verandah. At the far end of the row, in the hut that was his, there's a light, small and yellow.

"I want your blessing," says Pearl, very quietly. "Everybody else has given theirs. But you're the one who never told me what to do. So I want to know what you see. And then, if it's all right, I'd like you to say it's all right."

Reggie's quiet for a while.

"Tell me what he does," he says. "Not what he is."

"He comes back," says Pearl. "He went home at Christmas for three weeks and wrote me every day. Every single day, Reggie. Twenty-one letters. Some of them were four lines. One was just the score of a tennis match and *I miss you*. But every day." She laughs, wet. "He said his family got left, once. By people who were supposed to stay. He said he's not going to be one of the ones who leaves. He said it like it was a fact about the weather."

She stops.

"He comes back the same twice," she says. "Every time. I've checked, up as well as down. I put him on the list in March."

Reggie takes his glasses off. He holds them in his hand and looks at the cay without them.

"I left England," he says, "in 1952. I got on a boat at Southampton and came here, and there weren't planes, much, not for people like us. I didn't go back for a very long time." He turns the glasses over. "And there was somebody I loved, once, on the other side of the North Sea. For fourteen years. With a lamp." He's quiet a moment. "I know what distance is, Pearl. It's a great deal. It's not everything."

Pearl doesn't breathe.

"You asked me what I see," says Reggie. "I see a girl who's done her columns. And a young man who doesn't do columns, and knows he doesn't, and went and asked his brother. That's rarer. I see a family that sings at the table, and a grandmother who asked in the first minute whether you can do accounts, and a sister who needs you to." His mouth goes crooked. "And I'll tell you the thing I've seen for a year, in every letter. It's the way you talk about him. Not the way you talked about Julian, what he could do, what he played. You talk about him the way he talks about his people. His sister who's slow at everything but the piano. His brother who's cleverer than anybody. The one with the boats. Gram." He looks at her. "It's the way you talk about Ike, Pearl. And your mother. You've always talked about your people like that. Since you were ten and wrote me that your brother had come in to the tide by a different door. He talks about his the same way. I could hear it through the letters. That's how I knew, before you asked me."

Pearl can't say anything.

"And I see your face," says Reggie. I've been waiting a long time to see it look like that about somebody who'll be there at supper. Even if the supper's in Baltimore."

"And the rest? The distance?"

"The rest's yours to work out," says Reggie. "Yours and his. I'll not tell you where to live. I'd not know how." He puts his glasses back on. "But I'll tell you this, because it's true. If you go, we'll be here. I'll be here. At supper, whenever you come. Your grandmother will keep your room. And there are planes now. Your aunt Joy's been flying across the Atlantic for thirty years and she's only ever complained about the food."

Pearl laughs, and it breaks in the middle.

"I don't know that a blessing's mine to give," says Reggie. "I've never given one." He holds out his hand across the gap between the chairs. "But I'm glad. I'm gladder than I've got any way of saying. And it's all right. Wherever you are. If that's a blessing, you've got it."

She takes his hand. It's dry and cool, all bones, and it holds hers the way it did on the jetty steps when she was four.

"It's worth everything," says Pearl.

"Then that's settled," says Reggie. "And I'll want to meet him properly. I'll want to ask him about the tide on the Chesapeake. It's very small, I believe. Like Kingston's."

"About a foot and a half at the farm," says Pearl. "He told me. He grew up sailing on it. His sister taught him."

"*Did* she," says Reggie, and his whole face changes.

---

He takes one on the chapel steps. Before, not after. There are eleven steps up to the University Chapel at Mona, grey stone, and he counts them on the way up, with Win on one side and Lloyd on the other, and stops on the sixth and the tenth. Nobody says anything about it. Win's hand is under his elbow, not holding, only there. At the top he stands in the doorway with the evening coming in behind him and gets his breath. He looks at the chapel.

It's old. Older than anything on the cay, older than anything at the Bay. It was a sugar warehouse, Lloyd told him in the car, brought over stone by stone from Trelawny and put up again here. It's got a high timber roof like the inside of a boat, and it's full. There are three hundred people in it, perhaps more. He can't count them from the door. He's seventy-eight and his eyes aren't what they were.

He can count the front pew.

There's Marcia at the far end, with her handbag on her knee and her back very straight, in a grey dress she's made herself, beautifully. There's Lloyd beside her, going grey himself now, at forty-nine, with his hand already on the back of the pew in front, gripping. There's Joy, flown in from London on Thursday to take Ike back with her in September. She's forty-six now, and a nursing officer at the Hammersmith, and she's been watching Reggie's colour since the car. Next to Joy there's the Reverend Hinds, who's eighty-eight and deaf and has come anyway, and Great-Aunt Merle.

And at the near end, on the aisle, where he can stretch his leg, there are two places left. And a young man standing up beside them, waiting.

He's not tall. He's middling, and square, and very brown from the courts, with fair hair gone almost white in the sun and cut short. He's in a suit that fits him, which Reggie suspects is Marcia's doing, and he's holding a hymnbook. He sees Reggie in the doorway and doesn't come forward, and doesn't smile the smile. He waits, very still, the way you'd wait at a net. When Reggie and Win get to the end of the pew he moves back to let them in and holds out his hand.

"Dr Tar," he says. "Mrs Tar. Sam Tillerman. Pearl said you'd want the aisle."

His voice is flat and low, American, with the vowels of somewhere Reggie can't place, and there's nothing in it but what it says.

Reggie shakes his hand. It's hard across the palm, in a line, where a racket goes.

"Mr Tillerman."

"Sammy," says the young man. "Everybody at home does. Pearl says you'll call me whatever you like, and I should let you."

Reggie sits down, carefully, on the aisle. Win sits beside him. Sammy sits beside Win and puts the hymnbook on his knee, and looks at the front, where there's a grand piano with the lid up and nothing else.

After a moment he says, quite quietly, without turning his head, "Pearl said you'd ask me about the tide. I don't know Kingston's. I'm sorry. I asked at the harbour office and the man laughed at me." He's still looking at the front. "At home, on our point, it's a foot and a half, near enough. My sister'd know to the inch. Gram says it's the only thing on the whole Eastern Shore that's never once let her down."

Reggie turns his head and looks at him.

Win makes a sound beside him that Reggie knows very well. It's the one she makes when she's trying not to laugh in church. Reggie finds, sitting on the aisle at seventy-eight, with one under his tongue and his heart going carefully, that he likes the young man so much he can't speak.

"After," he manages. "I'd like to hear about your grandmother."

"You'd like her," says Sammy. "She'd like you. She doesn't like anybody."

---

Then Ike comes out and sits down at the piano, and Pearl comes out and stands beside it, and he stops being able to think about anything else.

He's heard them before. He's heard them since Ike was four and Pearl was eleven, in the bar at Harbour House, quarrelling about tempo. He's heard them every summer since, and every Christmas, and down the telephone at the harbour office, crackling. He heard them in the bar the night Win came in from the kitchen. He knows every piece they do. He knows where Pearl will go up and where Ike will go off. He knows, because he's been told at length in letters, which of them won which argument about the second verse of *Abide With Me*.

He's never heard them like this.

It's the chapel, partly. The stone, and the high wooden roof. Pearl told him once, when she was six, that the church on Constant Spring Road had a good back wall. This has a better one. When she sings the first note it goes up into the roof and comes back down on everybody like something falling out of the air.

But it's them. It's the two of them. They've grown up. Ike's nineteen, and tall now, taller than Lloyd, with Marcia's round serious face and Desmond's hands. He sits at the grand piano as though it had been built for him. Pearl's twenty-six, in the blue, the same blue, let out again by Marcia. She stands with one hand on the curve of the piano and sings as though there were nobody in the chapel but her brother.

Reggie can't carry a tune in a bucket. He's never been able to. But he's sat at the rail of the north pen for fifty years listening for things coming up. He knows what it sounds like when two things breathe together.

They go together. Nobody follows. It isn't a parade. When Ike goes off, in the places he's always gone off, Pearl goes with him, a hair ahead, as if she'd known before he did. When Pearl goes up, Ike's there already, the chord waiting. They don't look at each other. They don't need to. They've been doing it since the bar at Harbour House, since the flat-topped upright, since *We are better TOGETHER, do not tell him I said so*. Pearl wrote that to him, underlined, when she was thirteen. It's in the drawer.

He sits on the aisle and listens, and doesn't breathe.

Win's hand finds his on the pew between them. She holds it. He doesn't look at her. He knows what her face is doing without looking; he's known for twenty-four years. Her thumb moves once across his knuckles and stays.

---

They do the hymns, and then something by Handel, and then a thing of Ike's own, the *Improvisation*. It's grown. It's twice as long as it was at eleven and goes off in a great many more places. And at the end it still goes all the way up to the top of the piano, where the notes on this one aren't flat at all, and Ike plays them anyway, as though they were.

Then there's a pause. Pearl steps forward from the piano.

"This is the last one," she says. She says it to the whole chapel, but she's looking at the front pew. "It's for our grandmother. She sang it first, before any of us. In 1947. At the Myrtle Bank." She stops. "And it's for our grandfather, who taught it to us. He couldn't be here. He's in Miami. He sent a card." Something happens at the corner of her mouth. "It says *Both*."

Win's hand goes tight on his.

"And it's for Reggie," says Pearl, "who can't carry a tune in a bucket. Who taught me to count between breaths when I was four, and never once told me what to do." Her voice goes, and she brings it back. "*Stardust*. Ike's key."

Ike plays the start of it. Slow.

Pearl comes in on the second line.

Reggie hears Win beside him draw in her breath. He feels it through her hand. He knows what's coming before it comes. He's known her for twenty-four years and he knows every sound she makes.

And in the front pew of the University Chapel at Mona, in the second verse, a third below the tune, low, so low that only the front pew can hear it, Win Tar sings.

Pearl hears it. He sees her hear it. Her eyes go to the front pew, and she doesn't stop, and her whole face opens. At the piano Ike doesn't look round. You don't. But his shoulders change. He makes room. He makes room for all three of them, the voice at the front and the voice in the pew and his own hands, and nobody's following.

Reggie sits on the aisle with one under his tongue and his wife singing beside him and his grandchildren at the front of the chapel. He's not breathing. He hasn't been breathing for some time, and his physician would have something to say about it, but she's on the verandah at the Bay with her black book, a hundred miles off.

He thinks, quite clearly, the way he'd think a figure: *this is the last time I'll come this far for anything.*

It isn't sad. It's only true. It's a thing you'd write down.

And then he thinks, with his wife's hand in his and her voice under Pearl's: *it's enough. It's more than enough. If it's the last, I'd have picked this one.*

Beside Win, Sammy Tillerman has his hymnbook shut on his knee. He comes from a family that sings at the table, and he isn't singing. He's listening, with his whole face, the way Reggie's watched him listen for the last hour. He's not waiting for his turn. He hasn't got one. He just wants to hear.

Reggie watches him listen, and breathes out, at last, all the way.

---

The girl who turns his pages is called Andrea Chung, and Ike has been trying for three weeks not to think about her.

She's a violinist. She's at the Edna Manley School, second year, and she's eighteen. Mrs Beckford, who's retired and still comes to every concert Ike plays, found her for him. She said a pianist who'd been playing twenty years ought to be able to turn his own pages, and a pianist with any sense would let somebody else do it on the night. Andrea came to the church hall three times to practise. She sat at his left on a little stool and turned exactly when he nodded, never early, never late. On the third time she said, *you go off in the second verse*, and he said *yes*, and she said *good*.

That's all. That's the whole of it. He's going to London in September.

She's on the stool at his left now, in a black dress, with her hands folded, waiting for the nod.

He's got the card from Miami in his music case, under the Handel, because Pearl gave it him in the vestry. It came to the flat on Thursday, addressed to *Master Isaac and Miss Pearl Carew, c/o the Revd. Hinds*, in the big looping writing gone very shaky now. It's got a picture of the pink hotel on it, the same one as always. On the back it says only *Both. Keep going off. D.* Pearl read it and went into the vestry lavatory for ten minutes. Ike read it and put it in the case, under the Handel, where he can feel it.

He doesn't look at the front pew. You don't. But he knows exactly who's in it, because he looked through the door from the vestry before they came out. Mummy at the far end, very straight. Daddy with his hand gripping the pew in front. Auntie Joy, watching Reggie. Grandad Hinds with his hand cupped round his good ear. Grandma, in the navy, with her handbag. Reggie on the aisle, very small, very white, with his hands folded, the way he folds them at the rail of the north pen.

And Sammy, on the end, with his hymnbook.

Ike likes Sammy. He decided to in January, on the Pegasus courts, at six in the morning, when Pearl sent him down to learn to serve because he'd been bad-tempered all Christmas. Sammy didn't say *how's the piano, little man*. He said *show me your hands*. He looked at them a long time, and then he said *you'll want to be careful with those*, and taught Ike a serve that wouldn't hurt his wrist. And at the end, sitting on the bench with a towel round his neck, he said, *my sister plays. You'd like her. She's slow at everything but that*. And then he sang something under his breath, a sea song, in a light true voice, as though singing were a thing you did when you'd finished something, like drinking water.

He comes back, Sammy. Every Tuesday to supper, every last Friday to the Starlight Lounge, and every day at Christmas, by post, from Maryland. Pearl said it to Ike in March, in the church hall, and then she wrote it in the blue book and showed him: *Sammy. Every last Friday, and a letter every day when he's away. Twenty-one at Christmas.* She'd put it right under the line for Mummy and Daddy and Grandad Hinds.

Ike nods. Andrea turns the page.

---

In *Stardust*, in the second verse, he hears Grandma.

He doesn't look round. He doesn't need to. He's been hearing Grandma through walls since he was four, at the sink, in the bar, humming when she thought nobody was there. He knows her voice the way he knows Pearl's, only lower, and darker, and further off. It's coming from the front pew, a third below, so quiet only the front pew and the piano can hear it.

He makes room.

He doesn't have to think about it. It's what Grandpa Des taught him on the bench at Christmas when he was six: *nobody follows*. It's what Julian did, whatever else Julian was. It's what he's been doing for Pearl since he was four with one finger. He makes room for Grandma's voice under Pearl's, and his own hands under both. For a verse and a half the three of them go together in the University Chapel, over three hundred people's heads. And the roof sends it back down on them like rain.

He plays two bars after, soft. He stops.

Beside him, on the little stool, Andrea Chung has forgotten to turn the page. There isn't one to turn. She's sitting with her hand in the air, and she's crying, and she doesn't seem to know it.

He looks at her. He can't help it. You're allowed to, at the end.

She looks back at him. And then she takes her hand down and wipes her face with the back of her wrist, very fast, and says, under the noise of three hundred people getting up off the pews, "*Write* to me. From London."

Ike's gone hot to his ears. He knows it.

"I'll write," he says. "I've been taught by the best. Four hundred and twelve letters. Reggie's got them all in a drawer."

"I don't want four hundred and twelve," says Andrea. "I want one. And then another one." She gets up off the stool. "About the going-off bits."

She goes off the side of the stage into the vestry without looking back. He sits at the piano with three hundred people standing up in front of him and can't think of anything at all.

Then he looks at the front pew.

Reggie's looking at him. Not at Pearl, not at Grandma. At him. And Reggie's face is doing the thing, the thing Ike's seen it do at the rail when the calf's calf comes up after a long time down. Reggie raises one hand off the pew, just a little, and points, very slightly, at the vestry door, where Andrea went.

And then Reggie smiles. It's small and crooked, and it goes up further on one side than the other.

---

Supper's at the flat in Barbican, because where else.

There are fourteen of them. Pearl counts, because she always counts. It's a front room built for four, and there were nine in it the Christmas she was five, and there are fourteen tonight. There's Mummy and Daddy. There's Grandma and Reggie, Auntie Joy, and Grandad Hinds and Great-Aunt Merle. There's Mrs Chin from downstairs, who's ninety and still comes. There's Mrs Beckford. There's Ike, and Pearl, and Sammy. There's Andrea Chung, whom Mummy asked on the chapel steps in front of everybody before Ike could stop her.

That's thirteen. She counts Grandpa Des as well, because of the card. Fourteen.

Mummy and Grandma are in the kitchen. Pearl can hear them through the wall. They're side by side at the stove with their sleeves rolled, and at one point she hears Grandma say, *no, love, your way*. And Mummy says, *Win, we've been doing it my way since 1976*, and they both laugh.

Ike's in the corner with Andrea. They're not saying anything. They're looking at the Handel together, as if it had something in it they'd missed. Every so often Andrea says something, very low, and Ike goes red to the ears, and Auntie Joy, across the room, catches Pearl's eye and raises both eyebrows so high they disappear.

And Reggie and Sammy are on the balcony.

They've been there an hour. Pearl can see them through the door. Reggie's in the good chair, which Daddy carried out for him, with his feet on the upturned laundry basket. Sammy's sitting on the balcony floor beside him with his back against the rail. He's got a photograph out of his wallet, and then another, and then the whole wallet's out on the concrete between them. There's the farm, and the point, and the boat shed. There's a small upright old woman with her arms folded standing among hens. And there's a fair, round-faced young woman at a piano, looking up, smiling, slow.

She can't hear what they're saying. She doesn't need to. She can see Sammy's hand on the photograph of the point, following the line of the water. She can see Reggie leaning forward with his glasses pushed up, and his finger on the same line. She can see his face doing the thing where you can see him think. Every so often Reggie asks something. And Sammy waits till he's finished, and then answers, and then Reggie asks about the bit Sammy wasn't sure of.

Once she sees Reggie say something, and Sammy go quite still. Then Sammy says something back, low, looking at the concrete, and Reggie puts his hand on the young man's shoulder and leaves it there. She'll find out later, much later, from Sammy, what it was. Reggie asked about his father. And Sammy said, *he left*. And Reggie said, *my wife's first husband left too. Before supper, every time. Pearl's grandfather.* And Sammy said, *then she knows*. And Reggie said, *she knows. It's why she can tell the difference.*

They're both listening. Neither of them is waiting for a turn.

Pearl stands in the balcony door with a cup of tea going cold in her hand and watches her whole life sit on the balcony floor in Barbican, looking at a photograph of a farm on the other side of the Gulf Stream.

---

At ten Reggie looks up and sees her.

"Pearl."

"Mm?"

"Come here." He holds out his hand. She comes and kneels down beside them on the concrete, in the blue dress. He's got the photograph of the point in his other hand. "Sammy's been telling me about the tide on the Chesapeake. It's a foot and a half at the farm, springs. His sister taught him to read it off the pilings when he was nine." He looks at Sammy. "And his grandmother's asked him to ask you to sing *Amazing Grace* at the wedding, and to do her accounts while you're there."

"I *told* her I would," says Pearl.

"She wants it in writing," says Sammy. "She says she's been left before."

"Have you got your book?" says Reggie.

She's always got her book. She takes it out of her handbag, the twenty-first blue book, and opens it on her knee.

"Write it down," says Reggie. "The tide at the farm. A foot and a half, at springs. Off the pilings." He sits back in the chair, carefully, and puts his hand on his chest, where the tin is, not taking one, only resting. "And next Easter, when you're there, go down to the pilings with him at high water and look at the weed. And see if it's right."

"It'll be right," says Pearl.

"I know it will," says Reggie. "Check anyway. Up as well as down." He looks at her, and then at Sammy, and something happens at the corner of his mouth. "And write and tell me. From Maryland. I'd like a letter with a Baltimore postmark. I've never had one."

Pearl can't answer that. She puts her hand over his, on the arm of the chair, and Sammy puts his over hers, and nobody says anything for a while.

---

That night, very late, when everybody's gone and Sammy's gone down the stairs saying *Tuesday*, as he always does, and whistling something from Maryland, Pearl sits up in her old bed in her old room with the torch. Ike's asleep across the passage, or pretending to be. She can hear him writing something, very slowly, on the other side of the wall. The flat's quiet. Mummy's machine is under its cloth in the corner of the front room.

She turns back to the front of the first blue book, which she's brought from the shoebox, because she always brings it to Kingston. There's the page with the big careful heading from when she was nine.

*Things That Come Back The Same Twice.*

She reads down it. It's longer now. It's gone over onto the next page. There's the tide, and the column, and the calf, and the back wall. There's Reggie, and Grandpa Des only in the music, and Daddy, and Ike, and Grandma, and *Me*. There's Julian only in the music, that's all right. There's Mummy and Daddy and Grandad Hinds, every single time, underlined twice, where they should have been first. And there's Sammy, every last Friday, and a letter every day when he's away, written in March.

She takes her pencil. She writes, underneath, in her twenty-six-year-old hand, which is small and neat now, like Grandma's and like Reggie's and like nobody else's:

*Grandma. In the front pew, a third below. The second time.*

*Ike and me. Together. Every time. I told him so tonight, in the vestry. He said he knew. He said he'd read it in 1984.*
She looks at it for a long time. Then she turns to a fresh page, not the list, because this isn't a thing that comes back, it's a thing that happened once. It's true, though, and the blue book's for true things.

*21 July 1997. Blessing: given. On the verandah. He said he didn't know how. He did.*

*Reggie at 78, on the aisle, not breathing. Auntie Marguerite says he'll see 80. He said so first, on the jetty steps, when I was four. Nobody can know a thing like that. I'm going to hold him to it anyway.*

She puts the torch out.

Across the passage, Ike's pencil stops. After a moment she hears him get up, and cross the passage, and push her door open a crack.

"Pearl."

"Mm."

"How do you start a letter?" His voice is very quiet. "To somebody. When it's the first one."

Pearl lies in the dark and smiles so hard it hurts.

"You say what you saw," she says. "And then you ask them a question. And then you wait for the answer."

There's a silence at the door.

"That's how Reggie does it," says Ike.

"That's how Reggie does it," says Pearl. "Go to bed. Write it in the morning. And go up as well as down."
