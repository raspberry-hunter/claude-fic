# Beneficiary

The calf's mother dies in March 1983, and Pearl doesn't cry.

Marcia watches her not cry for a week. Pearl's twelve. She reads Reggie's letter at the kitchen table on the Tuesday it comes, the thin one, one page, and puts it back in its envelope very carefully. She says *the calf's mother died*, in the voice she uses for a figure. Then she goes and does her homework.

She doesn't cry at school, or at church, or at the window where she sings. She does her columns. She eats her supper. On Thursday she asks Lloyd, at the table, how long an animal like that would usually live, and Lloyd says he doesn't know, sweetheart, and Pearl says *Reggie will*, and goes quiet. On Saturday she doesn't go to the manse.

That's what tells Marcia. Pearl's been to the manse every Saturday since she was seven, through the measles and the 1980 election and a hurricane warning. On Saturday she sits on her bed with the shoebox of letters on her knees, not reading them. She's just holding the box.

On the Sunday night she writes. It takes her two hours and she won't let anybody see it.

On the Monday she posts it on the way to school. She doesn't say what's in it. And on the Saturday after, when the answer comes, fat, four extra stamps crooked, she reads it at the kitchen table. Then she gets up and comes to the front room where Marcia's at the machine with the windows open, and holds it out.

"Will you read it?" says Pearl. "I want to know what you think."

Marcia takes her foot off the treadle.

Pearl hasn't asked her to read a letter since she was seven. Not one. They're Pearl's, the letters, the way the blue book's Pearl's; Marcia's never once asked. She takes it and looks at her daughter. Pearl's standing by the machine with her arms folded, very straight, the way Win stands, the way Marcia's mother used to. Her face is doing nothing at all.

Marcia unfolds it.

---

*My dear Pearl,*

*Thank you for your letter. I've read it a good many times. I'll try to answer it properly, because you asked properly, and you'd know if I didn't.*

*You asked whether she's gone anywhere.*

*I don't think so. I think she's stopped. I sat with her at the rail on the last morning, with Baptiste, and I counted, and she came up three times more, slower each time, and then she didn't. I don't think there was anywhere for her to go after that. I think she was a very large, very old, very good animal who had lived for thirty-one years, which is a long time for her kind, and her heart stopped, and she stopped with it.*

*Then you asked whether people do. Go anywhere. And whether I believe they do, since I don't go to church except to hear you.*

*I'll tell you the truth, because you'd want it. No. I don't believe it. I haven't since I was fourteen, and I've had a good deal of reason since to think about it, and I've not changed my mind.*

*I'll tell you one of the reasons, which I've not told you before. I loved somebody very much, for a very long time. They died the year you were born. And I've sat on a good many verandahs since, and looked at a good many harbours, and I don't think there's anywhere that person has gone. I think what's left is what was done while they were alive, which was a great deal, and what I remember, which is more than I can always bear. And whatever I do because of them, which I hope is something. That's not nothing, Pearl. I don't want you to think I think it's nothing. It's very nearly everything I've got.*

*But I want to say this next part very clearly, so you'll read it twice. Your grandfather believes otherwise. He's a better man than I am, and he's thought about it longer, and harder, and with a great deal more at stake. He's buried more people than I've ever met. I'd not want you to take my answer as the answer any more than you'd take his only because he gave it. You asked me what I think, and I've told you. It's not a thing I can find for you. It's one of the things you'll have to go up as well as down on, for yourself. Perhaps for a long time.*

*Ask him. I know you'll have meant to. Ask him properly, the way you asked me, and listen to the whole of the answer. And then you'll have two, and you can do what you like with them.*

*She was a good calf's mother. Baptiste cried. So did I. You may, if you like. It's allowed. It's not a thing you do because you've decided something. It's only a thing you do.*

*Your loving*

*Reggie.*

*P.S. The calf is very well. She came to the pen on Thursday on her own and stayed an hour, with her nose against the rail, where her mother used to be. Then she went out again. I've written it down.*

---

Marcia reads it twice, because she's told to.

Then she sits at the machine with it in her lap and doesn't say anything for a while, and Pearl lets her.

She's reading it the way she reads a garment somebody's brought her to alter. Not for what it says on the outside. For how it's made. She's been reading this man's hand for eight years, out loud, at this table. She knows how it goes. She knows he says *he* and *she* about everybody, plainly, the way he says *calf* and *tide*. *Your grandmother says. Pascual says. Baptiste cried.*

*I loved somebody very much. They died the year you were born. Anywhere that person has gone.*

It's beautifully done. You'd not see it unless you were looking. It's a seam that's been picked out and done again so close you'd need a glass. Not one *he*. Not one *she*. In a page and a half from a man who's never in his life wasted a word on going round the side of anything.

She thinks about the Bay, in June 1974, the week of the wedding. The Kilburns in the bar. Old Mrs Kilburn, who'd talk to a post, telling Marcia about the Doctor's Light over a plate of fried fish, while Marcia did Pearl's sash. About the lamp at the end of the row on the cay that burnt every night for years, and the fishermen steered home on it. How it burnt all night in the spring of 1971. How everybody at the Bay said that was when the man in Norway died, the one whose specimen's in the deposit, Mr Purdie drew the deed. How it went out after, and never came on again.

The spring of 1971. The year Pearl was born.

Marcia sits at the machine with the letter in her lap.

She doesn't know. She'd like that on the record, to herself, if nobody else. She doesn't *know* anything, and she's not going to sit in her own front room and decide a thing about a man from a seam. But she's a minister's daughter in Kingston. She knows what a seam like that is for, and what it would cost a man if it came undone. It's not hers. He's made very sure it's not hers. He's gone to all that trouble, at a desk on the cay, so that a child of twelve can have the truth he's got without having the one thing he's kept.

And she thinks, quite clearly: *and that's his, then. That's his and Win's.* Because Win will know. Win, who keeps the day book and reads every bed on the cay the way her mother did. Win will have known since before the stairs. And married him.

She folds the letter.

"Well?" says Pearl.

Marcia looks up at her daughter.

"I think he's told you the truth," she says. "All of it he's got. I think that cost him something." She holds out the letter. "And I think he's right about one thing for certain. You're allowed."

"Allowed what?"

"To cry, darling. About the calf's mother. You've not, all week."

Pearl stands by the machine with her arms folded.

"I don't want to," she says. "If I cry it means she's — it means it's that. What he said. Stopped." Her chin's going. "And if I don't, it means I think she's somewhere. And I don't *know*. I don't know which, Mummy. I went down and up and it won't come out."

Marcia puts the letter on the machine. She holds out her arms. Pearl's twelve and nearly as tall as she is, and hasn't come and sat on her knee since she was eight. She comes anyway. She stands between Marcia's knees at the machine with her face in Marcia's shoulder, and cries for the calf's mother for twenty minutes, very hard, the way she did at three off Port Maria.

"What do *you* think?" she says, at the end, into Marcia's collar. "You've not said."

Marcia holds her.

She thinks about her mother on the bench in the vestry with a lily in her lap. She thinks about twelve years of Sundays, standing in the front pew where her mother stood, saying the words. She thinks about the thing she's said to nobody, not her father, not Lloyd: that she says them every Sunday and means them, and doesn't know them. Not the way her father knows them. Not the way Reggie knows the other thing.

"I think I'll see my mother again," she says. "Your Grandma Lucille. I think it every day. I don't *know* it, darling. Your grandfather knows it. Reggie knows the other way." She strokes Pearl's hair. "I hope. I've hoped every day since 1971. Some days it's more like knowing and some days it's less. That's all I've got. It's not a column. It doesn't come out."

Pearl's quiet against her shoulder.

"Is that allowed?" she says. "Just hoping?"

"I've no idea," says Marcia. "Ask your grandfather. He'll tell you."

---

He tells her.

Marcia walks up with her on the Saturday, because Pearl asks her to, which Pearl hasn't done since she was seven either. She sits in the hall on the chair by the hatstand with her handbag on her knee, and the study door's open, and she doesn't pretend not to listen. Nobody asks her to.

Pearl gives him the letter. He reads it at his desk with his glasses down his nose. He reads it twice, the way Reggie told Pearl to read the middle of it, and then he puts it down on the blotter and smooths it flat with his big hand.

He's seventy-three. He's slower than he was, and his hearing's started to go on the left. But he's still the tallest man on Constant Spring Road and his voice is still a cello, and when he takes his glasses off and looks at Pearl over the desk she stands up straighter without being told.

"He's an honest man," he says.

"Yes, Grandad."

"He's wrong. And he's honest. Those can go together, child. They go together a good deal less often than you'd think." He taps the letter. "He's told you the truth as he has it. He's told you he could be wrong without saying so, which is the most an honest unbeliever can do. And he's sent you to me. A man who thought he had the answer wouldn't have sent you to me." He puts his glasses down. "You'll write and thank him."

"I will."

"Now. Your mother says you want to know about hoping."

Pearl looks round at the hall, at Marcia, and goes red.

"Don't look at your mother. Look at me." He folds his hands on the desk. "Your mother hopes. She's told you so. She's a better Christian than I am, your mother, and always has been, and she'd not believe you if you told her. Hoping's not less than knowing, Pearl. It's harder. Any fool can know a thing on a Sunday. It takes a good deal more to hope it on a Tuesday when the washing's in and the rent's due and nothing's come out." He looks at her. "Paul says *now abideth faith, hope, charity*. You'll have done *hope* in your concordance."

"Over a hundred."

"Then you'll know it's in the same sentence as the other two. Not underneath them. *Beside*." He picks up his pen. "Here's what you'll do. You'll cry for the animal; you've done that, I can see it on you. You'll go on to church on Sundays and sing to the back pew, and you'll say the words, and you'll mean the ones you can. And you'll pray for that man, every night, the way I do. Not to make him believe. He'll not, and he'd box your ears. Because it's yours to do. It's the one thing in all this that's yours for certain." He looks at the letter. "And for the person he loved. You'll pray for them too. Whoever they were."

Marcia, in the hall, goes very still.

"I don't know who they were," says Pearl.

"No more do I," says her father. "The Lord does. That'll do."

Pearl stands in front of the desk a long moment. Then she says, "Grandad. Reggie says *it's not mine to say*. And you say *it's the Lord's*. And Mummy says *I hope*. And it's three different things. And I don't know which is mine."

Her father looks at her for a long time.

"No," he says. "You don't. You're twelve." He puts his glasses back on. "You'll find out. Get the concordance down. Do me *abide*. I want it for Sunday."

Pearl gets the concordance down.

Marcia sits in the hall with her handbag on her knee and her eyes gone, and thinks: *he told her what to do.* Every bit of it. *You'll write. You'll cry. You'll pray. You'll do* abide. He's never once in his life not told anybody what to do.

And it's what Pearl needed. She can see it on her daughter's back, through the study door, kneeling on the floor over the concordance. Something's gone down out of her shoulders that's been up all week. Reggie gave her the truth and the space to find her own. Her father gave her a thing to *do* in the meantime, with her hands, every night, till she finds it.

Marcia thinks she'd not have known how to give her either. She thinks she's been lucky, her whole life, in the men round her daughter. She thinks she's never once been lucky in anything else.

That's not true. She knows it isn't, even as she thinks it. She's sitting in her father's hall. Lloyd's at home with Ike, who's five and has the mumps. It's not true at all.

---

On the bus home Pearl says, looking out of the window, "Grandad said I'm to pray for Reggie's person. The one who died."

"I heard."

"Do you think Reggie would mind?"

Marcia thinks about the seam. About the care in it. About a man at a desk on the cay picking out every *he* and every *she* so a child could have the truth without having the rest.

"No," she says. "I think he'd mind if you told him. A little. Not because he'd mind the praying." She looks out of the window at Half Way Tree going by. "Because he'd not know what to say. He's a man who likes to know what to say."

"Then I'll not tell him."

"That's up to you, darling."

Pearl's quiet for a stop.

"I'll tell him one day," she decides. "When I'm grown up. When he'll know what to say."

She does, though Marcia doesn't find out about it for a long time.

---

Mr Carew comes the Christmas after next, at five, with a cane. Marcia knows about the cane before she opens the door, from the sound of it on Mrs Chin's stairs, a third footstep, slow.

She knows what's coming. She doesn't know it the way Pearl knows the tide; she knows it the way she knows how a bias-cut skirt will hang before she's let it down. She watches him at her door with his mangoes and his white hair, and the beautiful smile, and she watches him see Ike.

She watches the second of nothing go across his face when Ike says *the piano*.

And she watches him walk the two of them down Constant Spring Road in the dusk, slow on his cane, with her and Lloyd behind, and Pearl saying *I'm not finished*, and him stopping on the pavement. And in the church hall she watches him play with both her children at once for three hours, four hands and a voice, *with* them, the way he's never once been with anybody off a piano bench in his life. And she stands at the back with her father and the key and is so grateful she could hit him.

She doesn't hear what's said in the kitchen. She's on the stairs with the coats. She sees Lloyd come out first, very calm, and his father after, looking older. Then *there's a man in Half Way Tree*, and the cane on the stairs going down, and *Stardust*, whistled.

Lloyd tells her in bed. He lies on his back and tells the ceiling, the way he tells important things.

"He said they were the two best children in Jamaica," says Lloyd. "And that we'd made them. You and me." His voice is quite level. "And then he said, *and speaking of the children*."

Marcia lies still.

"He used them," says Lloyd. "Like the first bars before the tune. He'd just played with them three hours. And he came in my kitchen and —" He stops. "I gave him the envelope, Marce. It was Christmas. I had it in my pocket. I always have it in my pocket."

She doesn't say anything. She finds his hand on the sheet.

"I'll not be paid in my own children," says Lloyd, to the ceiling. "Anything else. Not that."

She's waited sixteen years. Since 1968, and a counterfoil she didn't find till 1972, and a stranger on a harbour wall in 1974 saying *you can*. She's never once said *stop*. She's never said it at the table or at the machine or in this bed. She's not going to say it now. She can feel it in her mouth, the whole of it, *stop, Lloyd, stop, for God's sake*. She holds his hand and doesn't say it, because it's his, and because if she says it he'll do it for her, and it has to be his.

"I know," she says instead. "I know you'll not."

He turns his head on the pillow and looks at her in the dark.

"You've never once asked me to," he says. "Sixteen years."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because it was yours," says Marcia. "And because if I'd asked, you'd have stopped. And you'd have been a man whose wife made him stop. And you'd have gone on paying it some other way, in your head, every first of the month, for the rest of your life." She holds his hand tighter. "I wanted you to be a man who stopped."

Lloyd doesn't say anything for a long time.

"I'm not yet," he says.

"I know."

"I will be."

"I know that too," says Marcia, and means it, and goes to sleep holding his hand. He doesn't.

---

Pearl's confirmed in May 1985, by her grandfather, in his church, with seven other children from the class, in white.

She very nearly isn't.

She comes home from the class in March, the fifth Thursday, and sits down at the kitchen table, and says, "I'm not going to do it."

Marcia's at the stove. She turns round.

"Why not?"

"Because you have to stand up and say *I believe*." Pearl's very pale. "All of it. *The resurrection of the body and the life everlasting.* In front of everybody. And Grandad puts his hands on your head. And I don't *know* it, Mummy. I've been trying since the calf's mother. I've done every column. I hope it. Most days. But I don't *know* it, and I'm not going to stand up in front of Grandad and say I do when I don't. That's lying. Reggie wouldn't. He told Grandad so in the vestry. He'd not read the lesson because he'd be saying it as if he meant it."

Marcia puts the spoon down.

She thinks about saying a great many things. That everybody in that class is lying, the half of them, and the other half don't know what the words mean. That she said them herself at thirteen, in the same church, in a dress her mother made, and didn't know them then and doesn't now. That it would break her father's heart.

She doesn't say any of them. It's not hers.

"Tell your grandfather," she says.

"I *can't*."

"You can. You've told him harder things." She picks the spoon up again. "And he'll tell you what to do. That's what he's for."

---

She doesn't go up with her this time. Pearl doesn't ask. She goes on the Saturday on her own, with the blue book under her arm, and comes back at one with her face washed and her eyes red and an expression Marcia can't read. She sits down at the kitchen table.

"Well?"

"He said I'm to be confirmed," says Pearl.

Marcia waits.

"He said —" Pearl stops. She opens the blue book. She's written it down. Of course she has. "He said, *it's not knowing, child. It's promising. They're not the same thing. When you stand up there, you're not telling me you know. You're telling the Lord you'll come back.*" She looks at the page. "*Every Sunday. Whether you feel it or not. Whether it comes out or not. That's all I've ever done. Fifty years. I've not known it every Sunday either.*"

Marcia sits down across from her.

"He said that?"

"He said he didn't know it every Sunday." Pearl's voice has gone odd. "*Grandad*. He said there were years. After Grandma Lucille. He said he'd stand up in the pulpit and say *the life everlasting* and not believe a word of it, and come back the next Sunday and say it again. And one Sunday he did believe it again, he doesn't know which one. He said that's what faith is. It's not a thing you've got. It's a thing you keep turning up to." She looks up. "Like the tide. He said that. He said *your Englishman will have told you about the tide*. He said it goes out every day and nobody thinks it's gone for good, because it's always come back. He said, *you're not promising you'll always feel it. You're promising you'll be there when it comes in.*"

Marcia doesn't say anything. She can't.

She's thinking about her father in 1971, after the funeral, sitting in his study with the door open, not reading. She'd thought he was grieving. He was. She'd not known he was also standing up on Sundays and saying words he didn't believe, for years, and coming back.

He never said. Not to her. Not to anybody, she'd have thought.

He's said it to Pearl.

"He said one more thing," says Pearl. "He said I'm to put it on my list."

"What list?"

Pearl goes red. She turns back through the blue book, a long way, and Marcia sees the page before Pearl can turn it away, and doesn't let on. *Things That Come Back The Same Twice*, in big careful letters from when she was nine.

"He knows about the list?"

"He's known since I was nine. He asked what I was writing and I showed him." Pearl's looking at the page. "He said, *you've got the tide on there and the calf and the Englishman, and you've not got the Lord. And He's come back more times than any of them.*"

Marcia laughs. She can't help it. It comes out of her all at once, in the kitchen, and Pearl looks up, startled, and then starts laughing too.

"Did you put it?"

"No," says Pearl. "Not yet." She closes the book. "I'll put it when I've seen it. Twice. That's the rule. Grandad said that was fair. He said the Lord's not afraid of a rule." She stands up. "I'm going to be confirmed. And I'll say the words. And I'll mean that I'll come back. That's the bit I can mean. I can mean that all the way."

---

Win and Reggie come for it. They come on the boat and the bus and Lloyd's car, and Reggie takes the aisle, and stands for the hymns, and sits for the rest. He doesn't say the creed. He doesn't pretend to. He stands with the book open and his mouth shut, and every so often he looks at Pearl at the front in her white dress, and looks away.

Marcia's beside him. She's watching Pearl, and she's watching her father in his robes at the rail, and she's watching Reggie.

Her father comes along the line of children with his hands. He puts them on each head and says the words, the same words, seven times, in the cello voice. And he comes to Pearl, at the end, because she's the tallest. He stops in front of her a moment longer than the others, and puts his big hands on her head, and says it.

And then he does a thing he didn't do for the other seven. He looks up, over Pearl's head, down the church, to the aisle seat.

Marcia sees Reggie see it. She sees him look back at her father, across the whole church, with nothing on his face at all. The two old men look at each other over Pearl's head, the one who believes and the one who doesn't. Marcia can't tell what passes between them. She thinks perhaps it's only that they're both there.

Then her father looks down again and moves on to the end of the rail, and it's done.

---

After, at the flat, there are eleven of them and a cake Win brought on her knee from the Bay. Marcia's in the kitchen, and the balcony door's open, and Pearl's out there with Reggie.

She's not listening. She's cutting cake. It's a very small flat.

"Do you mind?" Pearl's saying. "That I've been confirmed. And you don't believe."

"Mind?" Reggie sounds genuinely surprised. "No. Why would I mind?"

"Because you think it isn't true."

"I think it isn't true," Reggie agrees. "I'd have minded if you'd done it because your grandfather told you to. Or not done it because of me. You'd not have been doing it then; one of us would." He's quiet. "But you went and asked him, and you went up and down, and you found the bit you could mean. Your mother told me. You're promising to come back." Something in his voice. "I'd never mind that. I've spent a good part of my life on a verandah watching for things that come back. I'd be a hypocrite to mind it in you."

Marcia stops cutting.

"Reggie."

"Mm."

"The person who died. The one you loved." Pearl's voice is very careful. "Do you still think about them? Every day?"

There's a pause. Long.

"Not every day," says Reggie. "Not now. I did for a long time. Most days, now. Less than I did. More than I'd have thought, at the start, that I could manage."

"Does it make you sad? That you think they're not anywhere?"

"Yes." He says it quite simply. "It did, a great deal. It does still. But being sad's not a reason to believe a thing, Pearl. Your grandfather would tell you that. He'd tell you it the other way round as well: that being sad's no reason *not* to." Another pause. "I'll tell you what I do think. I think I'm what's left, a bit. Of them. Of what they did. And then your grandmother. And then, God help you, you. I've told you things they told me, without your knowing. You count between breaths because somebody counted to me." He's quiet. "So in a way you've got some of them as well. I'd not want you to think it was nothing."

Marcia stands in the kitchen with the knife in her hand.

*You count between breaths because somebody counted to me.*

"Then I've been doing it right," says Pearl.

"Doing what right?"

"Grandad told me to pray for them. Your person. When the calf's mother died. Every night." Pearl's voice is steady. "I've been doing it for two years. I wasn't going to tell you till I was grown up. But I've been confirmed now, and Grandad says that's grown up in the Church's eyes, so I'm telling you."

The balcony is very quiet.

Marcia puts the knife down. She doesn't go to the door. She stands at the counter with her hands flat on either side of the cake, the way Win stands at the sink, and waits.

"Every night," says Reggie. His voice isn't quite right. "For two years."

"After you and Grandma and Grandpa Des. Before Grandma Lucille." Pearl hesitates. "I didn't know their name. I just said *Reggie's person*. Grandad said the Lord would know who I meant."

It's a long time before Reggie says anything. When he does it's very low, and it's not the voice, and it's not quite his ordinary one either.

"Thank you, Pearl."

"You don't mind?"

"I don't know what to say," says Reggie. "That's all. I'd not have known what to say about that at any age. I'm glad you waited till you were grown up. I'd have made a fool of myself at twelve." There's a sound that might be a laugh, or might not. "I'm making one now."

"You're not."

"I am a bit. Give me a minute."

Marcia picks up the knife again and cuts the cake into eleven, very evenly, and puts it on the plates. When she takes it out to the front room, Reggie's still on the balcony on his own, with his back to the room and his glasses off. Pearl's beside Win on the sofa with her shoes kicked off, telling her about Mrs Beckford's hat. Win's looking past her at the balcony door, and her face has gone very soft.

Marcia gives Win her cake. Win takes it and looks up at her.

"She told him," says Win. Quite low.

"She told him."

Win doesn't say anything else. She doesn't need to. Marcia sees it in her face: that Win knows exactly who Pearl's been praying for, by name, and has done for a long time. And she's never going to say it, any more than Marcia is. It's his.

Marcia sits down beside her mother-in-law and eats her cake and doesn't ask.

---

Lloyd cancels it on the eighteenth.

He doesn't tell her he's going to. He takes Pearl to the bank that Saturday the way he has every Saturday since March, eight o'clock sharp in her school shoes. They come home at one on the bus, and Pearl's very quiet and Lloyd's very light. He comes in and puts his briefcase down by the door and stands in the front room looking at the curtains. The blue ones, the first ones, still up, gone pale at the edges now where the sun gets them.

"I've stopped it," he says. "From the first of June."

Marcia's at the machine. She takes her foot off the treadle.

She doesn't get up. She doesn't say *thank God* or *at last* or any of the things she's been saying in her head for seventeen years. She looks at her husband standing in the front room in his Saturday suit with his hands at his sides, and she sees he's not wound up at all. Not one turn. She's never seen him stand like that. Not since the church steps in 1969, and not then.

"Good," she says.

That's all. He nods. He goes into the kitchen and puts the kettle on.

Pearl doesn't say anything that day, or that week. She writes to the Bay; Marcia sees the envelope go. And when the answer comes back, one page, which has never happened, Pearl reads it at the kitchen table and then comes and stands by the machine. She doesn't say anything for a while. Then she says, very low, "Mummy. Did you know? About the standing order?"

Marcia looks at her daughter. Fourteen, with the one page in her hand and her blue book under her arm, and the look she gets when a column's come out and somebody's checked it.

"Yes," she says.

"Since when?"

"Since 1972. When you needed shoes."

Pearl's quiet. Marcia watches her go up the column. She watches her get to the machine, three nights a week, the parcels in brown paper on the table in the mornings. She watches her get to the spring before Ike.

"That's why you sewed at night," says Pearl.

"That's why."

"You never said."

"No," says Marcia. "It was your father's."

Pearl looks at her for a long time. Then she does a thing she's not done since she was small. She puts her hand on Marcia's right hand, on the machine, and turns it over, and looks at the tips of the first two fingers, where the skin's gone hard.

She doesn't say anything. She doesn't have to.

---

The form comes in July. Marcia finds it on the kitchen table one evening, when Lloyd's gone up Mrs Chin's stairs to fix the old lady's fuse. It's on the bank's paper, filled in, in his small careful hand, signed. Not posted. Left on the table, square to the edge, where she'd see it.

*Standing Order. Monthly.*

*Beneficiary: M. E. Carew.*

*Account: Savings, King Street Branch.*

*Reference: PIN MONEY.*

*With effect from: 1st August 1985.*

The amount is twice what the other one was, at the end.

She sits down at the kitchen table with it.

When he comes back down she's still sitting there. He stops in the kitchen doorway with the screwdriver in his hand.

"It's the first of the month," he says. He's not looking at her. He's looking at the form. "I didn't want the first of the month to be his any more. I've been watching it go out on the first for seventeen years. I'd like to watch something go out on the first that's — that's for you." He turns the screwdriver over. "You're not to spend it on the house. Or the children. Or me. It's yours. It's for whatever you like. Cloth. Another machine. A shop. I don't know. Whatever you'd have bought if you'd not been sitting up at eleven at night paying my father's debts."

Marcia looks at him.

"*Pin money*," she says.

"I couldn't think what else to call it." He's gone red to the ears. He's thirty-seven and he's gone red to the ears like Ike. "It's what you'd call it. A wife's own money. For pins."

She gets up. She goes across the kitchen and puts her arms round him, screwdriver and all, and holds on, and doesn't say anything at all.

He holds her back.

They're a family that doesn't say. She's known that since she was twenty-three. But they do things. Her father with the brides. Win with the French seams, and the letter to the manse, and soup for a frightened girl in 1968. Lloyd stacking the chairs in his wedding suit. Lloyd measuring the flat in his socks. Lloyd with a form on the kitchen table, square to the edge, that says *Beneficiary* and her name.

She thinks she'd not swap it. Not for all the saying in the world.

---

Win rings in August 1986, three days after the Kilburns' night in the bar. Except Marcia doesn't know yet that it's the Kilburns' night. She only knows that Pearl's at the Bay for the summer, and the telephone's ringing in Mrs Chin's hall at nine in the evening on a Tuesday, and Win never rings on a Tuesday.

She runs down. It's Win. She says Pearl's well. She says Ike's well. She says the Ministry's sent a man who wanted room three and Pearl refused him, in the guest voice, while Win was in the kitchen. And then she stops.

Marcia waits. Mrs Chin's radio's going in the back. Long distance costs the earth.

"I've to go," says Win. "It's long distance."

"Win."

"Mm."

"Is everything all right?"

There's a pause on the line. A long one, crackling.

"Pearl will tell you," says Win at last. "When she's home. She'll write it in her book first. I expect she's done that." Another pause. "I only wanted to hear your voice. That's all. I'll ring off."

She rings off.

Pearl comes home on the first of September and tells her. In the kitchen, at the table, with her case still in the passage. The Kilburns, and Uncle Pascual saying *Stardust*, and Ike in Grandpa Des's key. And Grandma in the kitchen door with a tea towel over her shoulder, singing the second verse a third below. Reggie in the corner with his eyes shut and his drink untouched. Grandma at the sink afterwards: *I had to wait thirty-eight years for mine.*

Marcia sits at the kitchen table and listens to all of it.

She thinks about the church steps in 1969. A woman in navy with her back to the door and the piano coming through it, faint. *I like it very much. That's the trouble.*

She gets up. She goes down Mrs Chin's stairs to the hall and puts forty cents in the tin, which is a great deal, and asks for the harbour office at the Bay. She waits twenty minutes while Alleyne's boy runs up the road to fetch Win.

"Marcia? What's happened? Is it the boy?"

"Nothing's happened," says Marcia. "Pearl told me."

There's a silence on the line. Crackling.

"I'm glad," says Marcia. "That's all. I'm so glad, Win."

Another silence. She can hear the harbour office round it, somebody's radio, the sea.

"Thank you, love," says Win, very low.

That's all either of them says. Marcia puts the receiver back on the hook and goes back up the stairs. Pearl's standing at the top of them with her arms folded, exactly like her grandmother, with her eyebrows up.

"You rang *Grandma*? Long distance? From *Mrs Chin's*?"

"I did."

"What did you say?"

"Not much," says Marcia, and goes past her into the kitchen to put the kettle on.

---

It builds. That's the thing about a standing order, Lloyd says; it's dull, and it builds. She doesn't touch it for four years. Then, in the autumn of 1989, she takes the room above the haberdasher's on Constant Spring Road. It's one room with a good north window and a sink, two hundred yards from her father's church. She has a sign painted for the door. *M. Carew. Dressmaking & Bridal.* Not *Reasonable*. She's not reasonable any more. She's very good, and she charges for it.

But that's after Julian. Julian comes first.

---

Her father finds out in February 1989. Somebody tells him; somebody always does on Constant Spring Road. She thinks it's Mrs Beckford, who's seen Pearl coming out of the church hall with a young man in a white shirt three Saturdays running. And the young man's not Ike.

He's eighty. He's handed the church on to a young man from Montego Bay the Christmas before, who preaches very well and is frightened of him. He still sits in the front pew on the aisle and says *amen* at the wrong places because of his ear. The church has let him keep the manse; the young man has a wife with money and a house in Red Hills. He still has his study, and the concordance, and Pearl on Saturdays when she comes, which isn't every Saturday any more.

He sends for her. He's never sent for anybody in Marcia's memory but the choir.

Pearl comes home at four. She comes up Mrs Chin's stairs at a speed Marcia hasn't heard since she was eleven and found the piano. She comes into the front room, where Marcia's on her knees pinning the blue dress on a dummy, and stands in the middle of the floor.

"Grandad," says Pearl, "says I'm not to see Julian."

Marcia takes a pin out of her mouth.

"He said it like *that*. Like the choir. *You'll not see that young man again, Pearl.* He's never *met* him. He's never heard him *play*. He's heard from Mrs Beckford that he's at the Pegasus on Fridays and has a room in Half Way Tree, and he sat me down in the study and told me." She's white with fury. "He *told* me, Mummy. Like I was eight and counting *mercy*. He said I'm too young and he's too charming and I'll not see him. And I said I'm eighteen and he's the best musician I've ever sung with, and Grandad said, *I've no doubt*. Like that was the *point*."

Marcia puts the pin in the hem.

She knows exactly what she wants to say. She wants to say, *he said the same about your father*. She wants to tell her about the Port Maria boat and being sick three times and the doorstep at the Bay. She wants to say, *I went anyway. And I was right. And you might be too.* And she wants to say the other thing as well, the thing she's been saying in her head since January, at the machine: *he's right. Your grandfather's right, and he's not even met him, and he's right.*

She doesn't say either.

It's not hers. If she says *I went anyway* it'll be permission. If she says *he's right* it'll be a second wall, and Pearl will go over it as she'd go over the first. And it'll be Marcia's answer and not Pearl's, and Pearl will spend her life wondering.

"Turn," she says.

Pearl stares at her.

"The dress. It's pinned. I want to see the hem on you, not the dummy." Marcia sits back on her heels. "Get on the chair."

Pearl gets on the chair. She stands on it in her school blouse with the blue dress held up against her, furious, and Marcia pins.

"You're not going to *say* anything?"

"I'm pinning."

"Mummy."

"I'll say one thing," says Marcia, round a pin. "Then I'll not say any more about it. Then or after." She puts the pin in. "Does he listen to you? Off the stand?"

Pearl stands on the chair and doesn't answer for a long time.

"Sometimes," she says.

Marcia nods. She takes another pin out of her mouth.

She doesn't say anything else about it. Not that day, and not all spring, while the grown-ups go mad round her through the board wall. She lets the hem down half an inch because Pearl's grown. She takes the waist in. She makes the dress fit.

---

She goes to see her father on the Monday.

She goes with his shirts, the way she does on Mondays, and puts them on the hook behind the study door, and then she doesn't go. She sits down in the chair across the desk where Pearl sits on Saturdays, and folds her hands, and looks at him.

He looks at her over his glasses.

"You told her," says Marcia.

"I told her."

"You told me. In 1968. *A bank clerk with a father who owes half of Kingston.*"

"I remember."

"And did it work?" says Marcia. "When you told me?"

Her father puts his pen down.

"No," he says. "You got on the Port Maria boat. On your own. And were sick three times. And came back with a letter." Something happens at the corner of his mouth. "Which I've still got. In there." He nods at the drawer of things he means to pray about. "I take it out once a year. On your anniversary."

Marcia goes quite still.

She's never seen that letter. She asked her mother once. *That's your father's and Mrs Carew's.* She's not going to ask now.

"Then why tell her," she says, "if you knew it wouldn't work?"

Her father leans back in his chair. It creaks. He's eighty and he's very tired, and his left ear's gone, and he looks at her with the face he had in the pulpit for forty years, before he said the thing he'd been working on all week.

"Because she needs to have been told," he says.

Marcia waits.

"Your Englishman won't tell her. He writes her about cisterns; she told me so. Your mother-in-law won't; she's frightened. And you won't, because you've decided it's not yours, and you're right, God help you, you're your mother's daughter." He folds his hands on the desk. "So nobody's told her. And that child sings, Marcia. You know how she sings. She sends it to the back wall and waits for it to come back. That's how she knows where she is. She told me so at six. If there's no wall, it doesn't come back. It just goes out. And she doesn't know where she is."

Marcia sits very still.

"I'm the back wall," says her father. "I'm eighty and deaf and I've never met the boy, and I'm wrong, very likely, about half of it. She'll go over me as you went over me. I'd think less of her if she didn't. But she'll have *had* something to go over. Something solid, that said no, that she could put her voice against and hear it come back." He picks his pen up again. "And when she decides, whichever way she decides, she'll know it was hers. Because she'll have decided it against somebody. Not into nothing."

Marcia looks at her father for a long time.

"That's not what Reggie thinks," she says. "Reggie thinks if anybody tells her, it'll be their answer, not hers."

"Reggie's right," says her father. "And so am I. We're both right. That's why she's got both of us." He looks at her over his glasses. "You should know that by now. You married into a family that does it one way and were born into one that does it the other. And you've stood in the middle twenty years with your mouth full of pins, saying nothing, and loving all of us. It's the hardest place in the church. I've stood in it myself."

Marcia can't say anything.

"Go home," says her father, quite gently. "And let the dress out, if she wants it let out. And don't say anything. You're good at it."

---

The night of Ike's recital, she's at the machine at a quarter to midnight.

She knew. She knew from the empty seat. She sat beside Pearl in the second pew in the church hall and watched her look at her watch at a quarter past six, and again at half past. She watched her take the handbag off the seat and put it on her lap. She thought: *there*. The way she thought it in 1980, in this hall, when Desmond Carew laughed at the Ministry's bill. *There. She's seen it.*

And then Ike played, and Marcia forgot everything for twenty minutes but her son. Her son, with his round serious face and his grandfather's hands, going off and coming back and going all the way up to the flat notes at the top. Playing them anyway.

Afterwards, under the streetlamp, the roses. She saw Pearl not take them. She took Ike's hand and walked him up the road with Lloyd, and didn't look back, because it wasn't hers to see.

At home she didn't go to bed. She took the cloth off the machine and got out the grey cotton for Mona and the pattern for the jacket. She sat down at the machine at a quarter to eleven and put her foot on the treadle, and the needle went, like rain on a tin roof. The sound Pearl went to sleep to every night of her life till she was seven.

She wanted Pearl to hear it, coming up the stairs. She wanted it to be the first thing she heard.

Pearl comes in at ten past twelve. Marcia hears her on Mrs Chin's stairs, alone, slowly, one at a time. And she knows, from the step, before the door's open.

She finishes the seam. She cuts the thread.

"Well?" she says.

Pearl sits down on the floor beside the machine, the way she did at four, at five, at six, with her back against Marcia's legs.

"I said no."

Marcia puts her hand on her daughter's hair.

"Good girl," she says.

And then she says the other thing, because it's true, and she's been holding it since 1980.

"I'm sorry, darling. I'm so sorry."

She's not sorry Pearl said no. She'll never be sorry about that. She's sorry because she stood at the back of her father's church hall when Pearl was nine and heard what that kind of piano does to her daughter's voice. She knows what Pearl's just put down on Constant Spring Road under the streetlamp. Nobody else in this family does. Lloyd doesn't, who can't hold a tune. Her father doesn't, who leads and has never once been led. Reggie doesn't, God love him, who can't carry a tune in a bucket. Win does. Win's the only other one. Win, who stood on the church steps in 1969 with her back to the door.

Marcia knows because she watched. She's the only one who watched, both times, from the back, with her arms folded. Who saw Pearl's face being carried and knew it for what it was, and knew it would come with a man who left before supper. Both times. And couldn't do anything either time but stand at the back and watch, and then be at the machine when Pearl came home.

"Both?" says Pearl, into her knee.

"Both," says Marcia. "It's allowed to be both."

She puts her foot back on the treadle.

---

In the morning Pearl goes to the manse. It's Sunday; she goes before church, at eight, on her own.

Marcia doesn't go. She hears about it from her father, at Sunday dinner, which he still has at the flat every week with Aunt Merle. He says it to the yam, quite comfortably, in front of everybody.

"Pearl came to see me this morning."

Pearl, across the table, goes red to the ears.

"She told me she'd sent the young man to Miami. Without her." Her father helps himself to more yam. "I said *good*. She said, *you'd have liked it better if I'd done it in February when you told me to.*" He chews. "And I said I'd have liked it a great deal better. And she said, *I'd have done it in March without you. I kept seeing him through April to spite you.*"

Lloyd puts his fork down. Ike chokes on his water. Aunt Merle says *Pearl Carew* in her Sunday voice.

And her father laughs. It's the big laugh, the cello one, that Marcia's heard perhaps five times in her life. He sits at the head of her table with his napkin tucked in and laughs until Aunt Merle has to thump his back.

"Your mother," he says to Pearl, when he can, "kept seeing your father through a whole summer to spite me. *And* went to the Bay. You've a long way to go."

Pearl stares at him. Then at Marcia.

"Mummy. You *never*."

Marcia looks at her father down the table. He looks back at her, perfectly bland, and helps himself to more yam.

"Eat your dinner," says Marcia. "All of you."

---

Her father stops coming to Sunday dinner in the winter of 1995. He's eighty-six. It's not his heart; it's his knees, and the stairs, and Mrs Chin's stairs are steep. So they go to him instead. Lloyd carries the dinner up Constant Spring Road in a basket every Sunday, under a cloth, and they eat it in the manse kitchen. Pearl's twenty-four now and keeps the Pegasus's books, all of them, in an office behind the reception desk with her name on the door in gold, like her father's. She sings in the Starlight Lounge on the last Friday of the month. Ike's seventeen, and plays the manse piano after dinner while her father sleeps in his chair with his hand cupped round his good ear, not asleep at all.

He tells Pearl what to do every single Sunday. *You're working too late. That manager's taking advantage. You'll not sing in that lounge in a dress that colour.* And Pearl argues with him, every single Sunday, and does about half of what he says. He seems to find that a very satisfactory proportion.

In April 1996, at the manse kitchen table, over the fish, Pearl says, to nobody in particular, "There's a new man on the courts at the Pegasus. A tennis professional, from America. On a two-year contract. He came to the Starlight on Friday and sat at the back."

Marcia, at the stove, doesn't turn round.

"He didn't talk through the songs," says Pearl. "Not one. Everybody talks through the songs. The Ministry wives talk through *Stormy Weather*. He just sat at the back with a lemonade and listened. So I asked him after why. And he said his family sings. In Maryland. At the table, and in the car, and washing up. And in his family you don't talk while somebody's singing, or you get a look from his grandmother that'd take the paint off a boat." She stops. "He said *the paint off a boat*. Like it was a thing people said."

Marcia stands at the stove with her back to the table.

She's heard Pearl talk about a man before. In 1989, all spring, through the board wall: *he's the best musician I've ever sung with, he's with me every bar*. What he could do. What he played. Like a list of things on a stand.

This isn't that. It's not what he does. It's who he comes from. *His family sings. His grandmother. The paint off a boat.* Pearl's telling it the way she tells about Ike coming in to the tide by a different door. The way she tells about her grandfather and *mercy: enough*. The way she tells about her own people.

Behind her, at the head of the table, she hears her father put his fork down.

"Is he a churchgoing man?" says her father. "This tennis player."

"I don't know, Grandad. I've only talked to him once."

"Find out," says her father. "And bring him to dinner. I want to look at him."

"*Grandad*."

"You heard me." Her father picks his fork up. "You'll bring him to dinner. I'm eighty-seven. I've not got time to wait for you to make up your mind."

Marcia turns round from the stove.

Pearl's sitting at the manse kitchen table, twenty-five years old, with the Pegasus's books in her handbag. She's gone red to the ears. She's looking at her grandfather with her mouth open and her eyes very bright. She's not furious. Not this time. She doesn't look at all like a girl who's going to go over a wall to spite anybody.

She looks, Marcia thinks, like somebody who's been told to do a thing she was going to do anyway, and is glad of the excuse.

Marcia meets Lloyd's eyes across the table. Lloyd's eyebrows go up, very slightly. Hers go up back.

She turns round to the stove again so Pearl won't see her face.

That night, in the flat, Lloyd lies on his back and says, to the ceiling, "Marce."

"Mm."

"She's going to bring him to dinner."

"I know."

"You knew in the kitchen. I saw your face."

Marcia lies in the dark and thinks about it. She thinks about her mother in the vestry in 1969 with her cherry hat in her lap, counting who stays for the washing-up.

"I don't know anything," she says. "I've not met him."

"But."

"But she talked about his grandmother," says Marcia. "Before she talked about him. That's all."

Lloyd's quiet for a while. Then he laughs, very softly, in the dark. The radio laugh.

"Your mother," he says. "She'd have counted him."

"She'd have counted him," says Marcia. "We'll see if he stays for the washing-up."

She turns over and goes to sleep. In the morning, on her way to the room above the haberdasher's, she stops at the bank on King Street. She doesn't go in to see Lloyd. She goes to the counter and asks the girl for the balance on a savings account with the reference *PIN MONEY*.

It's a great deal. Eleven years of the first of the month. It's enough for a good deal of very good cloth.

She doesn't spend it. She writes the figure in the back of her pattern book, and goes to work. And at the north window, with somebody else's bride's bodice on the dummy, she catches herself thinking about white silk, and how much of it that figure would buy.

She puts the thought away. It's April. She's not met him. That's not counting, that's hoping.

She's allowed. Her father said so.
