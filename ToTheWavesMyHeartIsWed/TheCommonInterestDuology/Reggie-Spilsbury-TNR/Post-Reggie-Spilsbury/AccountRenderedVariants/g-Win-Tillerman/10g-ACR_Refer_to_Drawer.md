# Refer to Drawer

The Kingston boat is late, because the Kingston boat is always late, and Win is on the harbour wall waiting for her bread.

It's the sixteenth of August, a Thursday, and the heat's come down on the Bay like a lid. She's got the day book under her arm because she's going on to the fishmarket after. She's thinking about the Ministry party coming on Thursday week, and whether the second cistern will hold if they all want baths, and about Reggie, a little, the way she thinks about him most of the day now. Underneath everything else, like the hum of the generator.

The boat comes round the head at half past ten, low in the water. There's the bread, in its sacks. There's the mail. There's a woman from Port Maria with chickens. And there's a man in the stern in a cream linen suit and a panama hat, with one leather case, sitting the way a man sits who has sat in a great many sterns and expects to be looked at when he gets out.

Win knows him before she can see his face. She knows him by the hat, and the angle of it, and the hand on his knee. She's known that hand since she was twenty-two.

She stands on the harbour wall with the day book under her arm and doesn't move.

The boat comes in. Alleyne's boy throws the line. The man in the cream suit stands up, and takes his hat off, and looks up at the wall, and sees her. He smiles. It's the smile that got a whole dance floor at the Myrtle Bank up off its chairs in 1946, and it comes at her across fifteen years and twelve feet of water as though it had never been away.

"Winnie," says Desmond Carew. "Oh, *Winnie*. Look at you."

---

He's older. That's the first thing, and she's ashamed of how glad she is of it. He's fifty-seven. The hair's gone grey at the sides and thin on top, and he's thicker through the middle than the suit was cut for. The suit's good, and it's been good for a long time, and it's going at the cuffs. There are little broken veins across his nose that weren't there in 'fifty-eight. His hands shake, very slightly, when he lights a cigarette on her verandah. He sees her see it and turns it into a flourish with the match.

And he's still the handsomest man she has ever seen in her life. That's the second thing. She'd hoped it wouldn't be.

"You've done well," he says. He's leaning on the rail of the top verandah with the cigarette, looking down at the harbour, at the yard, at the new cistern, at all of it. "You've done *very* well, Winnie. I always said you would. Didn't I say? I said to Lloyd — I said, your mother's got a head for figures like a cash register, she'll own the Bay by the time she's fifty."

"I'm forty-eight."

"There you are, then. Two years." He turns round and smiles at her. "How are the children?"

"You'd know, if you wrote to them."

"I write to them."

"You write to them at Christmas, Des, when you want Lloyd to ask me for something."

He laughs. He isn't offended. He was never offended; it was one of the loveliest things about him. You could say anything to Desmond and he'd laugh, and turn it round, and hand it back to you as a joke you'd both made.

"Well," he says. "I'll not pretend. You'd see through it anyway. You always could." He stubs the cigarette out on her rail, which she'll have to scrub. "I'm in a bit of a hole, Win. Nothing I can't get out of. A man in Miami, and a card game that went on too long. You know how it is."

"I know how it is."

"Eight hundred dollars. American." He says it lightly, the way he always said it, as though it were the price of a hat. "He's a patient man, but he's not *that* patient. I'd not ask if there was anybody else." He spreads his hands. "There isn't anybody else, Winnie. There never was."

She stands in the doorway of her own verandah with her arms folded and looks at him.

It took her a month, in 1968. It took her a month with a letter in her apron pocket, and she thought when it was done that it was done. That she'd paid off the last of it by not paying, and closed the book.

And here he is on her verandah with his hat on the chair, and her hands are cold.

"No," she says.

He looks at her with his head on one side.

"You've not heard it all yet."

"I don't need to hear it all, Des. I've heard it all. I heard it all from 1947 to 1968, every month, in an envelope." She keeps her voice level. It isn't easy. "I stopped. You know I stopped. I didn't answer the last one and I'm not answering this one either, just because you've brought it yourself."

"Win —"

"You'll have room nine," she says. "Tonight and tomorrow. I'll not charge you. On Saturday the boat goes back to Kingston and you'll be on it."

She goes down to the kitchen before he can smile at her again, and stands at the sink with her hands flat either side of it until they stop shaking.

---

He isn't on it.

He's charming to the kitchen boy on Friday, and charming to the girl who does the rooms. He sits in the bar on Friday night and plays the old upright she keeps for the guests, which nobody's played properly since 1965. He plays the whole of the Myrtle Bank set from memory, and the Bay comes in off the street to listen. She stands in the kitchen doorway with a tea towel and hears *Stardust* go through her house like a draught and can't make herself go in and stop it.

On Saturday the boat goes back to Kingston and he isn't on it. He's on the verandah, in her chair, with a rum punch he's made himself behind her bar, reading the *Gleaner*.

"Monday," he says, when she stands over him. "I'll go Monday, Winnie. Promise. It's a long way from Miami and my back's not what it was." He smiles up at her. "Sit down. You never sit down. Tell me about this place. Tell me about *you*."

And that's when the kitchen boy, who is fourteen and a fool and adores her, comes out on to the verandah with the Monday provisions list and says, "Mrs Carew, ma'am, will Dr Tar be wanting his breakfast in the kitchen Monday as usual, or up here with the Ministry gentlemen?"

Desmond lowers the *Gleaner*.

Win watches his face. She was married to it for eleven years. She knows it better than her own. She watches it take in *Dr Tar*, and *Monday*, and *as usual*, and she watches something go across it that she's never seen there before in her life.

It isn't jealousy. She'd have known what to do about jealousy. It's the face of a man at a card table who's just seen the dealer turn over a card he'd been sure was still in the deck.

"Dr Tar," says Desmond. He's smiling. "Well, now. Who's Dr Tar?"

---

Win tells him at Marguerite's on the Sunday, on the back verandah before dinner, with Pascual pretending to do something to a lamp at the other end.

"He's at the house," she says. "Desmond. Since Thursday. He wants eight hundred dollars, and I've said no. He said he'd go on the Saturday boat, and he didn't. And on Saturday the boy asked about your breakfast in front of him." She's got her arms folded tight. "So now he knows."

"Knows what?"

She looks at him.

"Oh," says Reggie. "*That*." He considers it. "Well, he'd have found out. The fishermen know. Alleyne's boy knows. Lady Mallory knows, and she's told half of Kingston. He'd only to ask at the fishmarket."

"Reggie, he's my *husband*."

"Yes. I know." He does know. He's thought about it a good deal since May, generally at about two in the morning. "Is he unkind to you?"

"No. He's never unkind. That's not —" She stops. "He's *charming*. That's what he is. He's charming at everybody, and he plays the piano, and the whole Bay came in on Friday to listen. And he's sitting in my chair drinking my rum, and he's not going, and I don't know how to make him."

Reggie looks at her. She's gone grey under the brown, and there are marks under her eyes, and she's holding her own elbows as though she were cold on an August evening.

He feels something come up in him, quite slowly, from a long way down. He doesn't recognise it for a moment, because it isn't a thing he's had occasion to feel about anybody in some time.

"I'll come down tomorrow," he says. "As usual."

"Reggie —"

"As usual, Win. I'll have my breakfast in the kitchen. And I'd like to meet him."

---

He meets him at six on the Monday, on the top verandah, where Desmond Carew is sitting in Win's chair with his feet on the rail and a rum punch at his elbow.

He gets up when Reggie comes out. He does it beautifully, all in one movement, the way a man gets up from a piano stool at the end of a set. He comes forward with his hand out and the smile on.

"You'll be Dr Tar," says Desmond. "The famous Dr Tar. Winnie's told me *nothing* about you, which is how I knew you must be important. Desmond Carew." He shakes Reggie's hand in both of his. "I've been wanting to meet you since Saturday. Sit down, sit down. Have a drink. I make a very fair rum punch."

"So I hear," says Reggie. "It's Win's rum."

"Isn't everything?" says Desmond, and laughs, and Reggie finds he's laughing too, a little, before he can stop himself.

He sits down.

He hadn't expected to like him. He'd thought about it on the walk down Marguerite's hill, and decided that he'd be civil and nothing else, and see what the man wanted, and see him off if he could. He'd reckoned without the fact that Desmond Carew is simply the most agreeable man to sit next to that he's met in years. He listens. He laughs in the right places. He asks about the Institute and actually wants to know, or makes it seem so. He does it the way a very good pianist accompanies a singer: right with you, every bar, listening for where you'll need room before you know it yourself.

Reggie knows exactly what he's watching. He's done it himself for twenty-five years.

"She sang, you know," says Desmond, about twenty minutes in. He's got his head tipped back against the chair, looking at the sky. "Did she tell you that? With the band. Nineteen forty-seven, forty-eight. At the Myrtle Bank on a Saturday. She'd a voice like —" He makes a shape in the air with his cigarette. "Contralto. Low. She'd stand up at the front in a green dress and sing *Stardust*, and you could have heard a pin drop on that floor, and there were four hundred people on it."

"*Win* sang?"

"Like an angel. She gave it up when Lloyd came." Desmond's watching him now, sidelong, through the smoke. "She'll not have told you. There's a good deal about Winnie she'll not have told you. You've got to know a woman a long time, Dr Tar. You've got to know her twenty-five years."

Reggie isn't listening to that part.

He's thinking about Win at twenty-two, in a green dress, singing to four hundred people in a hotel ballroom in Kingston. He's never heard her sing. He's never heard her so much as hum. He wants, suddenly and very badly, to know what key.

"What did she sing besides *Stardust*?"

Desmond blinks.

"I — the standards. *Stormy Weather*. *Sentimental Journey*, the band used to go wild for —" He stops. He looks at Reggie with his head on one side, as though Reggie had played a wrong note and he couldn't tell whether it was on purpose.

"Contralto," says Reggie. "I'd have guessed that. From her speaking voice. It sits low when she's tired." He finds he's smiling. "I'll ask her."

"She'll not sing for you."

"Very likely not. I'll ask her anyway."

Desmond looks at him for a moment longer. Then he laughs, and leans back, and the smile comes on again, a notch brighter.

---

Win comes out at a quarter to seven with a tray, and puts it down, and sits on the step of the verandah door, between them, where she can see both their faces. And that's when it starts.

Reggie doesn't know afterwards who begins it. He thinks, on the whole, that it's Desmond. Desmond says something about the Institute — what an extraordinary thing it must be, to be a man of science, a man of real learning, in a place like this, among people like Winnie's people. He says it as a compliment, in the voice, and it isn't one.

And Reggie feels his own come on.

It comes up the way it always did, easy and warm, the shoulders coming down, the lamp turned up. He leans back in his chair. He raises his eyebrows.

"Oh, but you'd know about that far better than I would, Mr Carew. A man who could hold four hundred people on a dance floor with a trumpet — I've read papers to forty at the Linnean and had half of them asleep by the discussion. You'll have to tell me how it's done. I've been trying to learn for thirty years."

"Ah, now." Desmond leans back too. He puts his hand flat on his chest. "You're too kind, Doctor, *far* too kind. It's nothing to what you people do. I read about you, you know. In the *Gleaner*. The man who knows everything there is to know about the — the —"

"Marine mammals."

"The *marine mammals*. I said to myself, there's a man with a real gift, and here's me with nothing but a trumpet and a good ear —"

"Oh, a *very* good ear, I'm told —"

"— and a little luck at the tables, now and then —"

"— and the loveliest singer in Kingston, from what you tell me, so I'd not call it *nothing* —"

"— and of course the Institute, with all its *resources* —"

"— which are, as you'll appreciate, entirely the Director's —"

They're both leaning back. They're both smiling. They've both got one hand spread on the chest. It's like looking in a glass in a fairground, the kind that makes you fatter or thinner; Reggie can see every single thing the man's doing, because he's doing it himself, a half-second behind. Desmond's selling him something. Reggie's selling it back. Neither of them is buying. Neither of them knows how to be the one who stops.

It would be the funniest thing he's ever seen, if he could stand outside it.

And somewhere under it, he's aware, is the thing he knows better than anybody on this coast. The voice comes on when there's something not to be seen. Desmond Carew has eight hundred dollars owing to a patient man in Miami. He's fifty-seven, and he's got a tremor in his hands, and he's sitting in the chair of the only woman who ever paid for him, and she's said no. And he's selling as hard as he can, because if he stops, there'll be nothing there but that.

Reggie knows. He's been that man at a table, more times than he could count, whenever somebody said a name.

"— and I will say, Doctor, a man of your *distinction* —"

"— oh, a man of *yours*, Mr Carew, surely —"

Something touches the back of his hand.

It's Win. She's still on the step between them. She hasn't said anything. She's just reached up, without looking at him, and put two fingers on the back of his hand where it lies on the arm of the chair, and left them there.

He looks down at her.

She's looking at Desmond. Her face is quite still. And he can see, from where he sits, what she's watching: the man she married, doing the thing, with all of it showing, at fifty-seven, on her verandah. And beside him, the other one. Doing it too.

The voice goes.

It goes the way a lamp goes when the oil's out: not all at once, but down, and down, and then it isn't there. His shoulders come up. He sits forward. Her fingers are still on the back of his hand. He doesn't move it.

"Oh, stop it, Carew," he says, in his own voice. "She can hear us both."

Desmond stops mid-word.

His eyes go to the arm of the chair. To Win's two fingers on the back of Reggie's hand, and Reggie's hand not moving. Win sees him see it. She takes her hand back, not quickly, and folds it in her lap.

For a second, no more, there's nothing on Desmond's face at all. Reggie sees it. It's a man at the rail when the animal's gone down and hasn't come up, not yet, and there's nothing to do but wait and see if it will.

Then the smile comes back on, brighter than ever, and he laughs.

"Oh, *Doctor*," says Desmond Carew. "You're a card. You're a *card*, you are. Winnie, he's a card." He reaches for his drink. "Where was I?"

He can't stop. Reggie watches him not be able to. He picks the whole thing up exactly where he left it, and goes on, alone, to nobody, for another ten minutes, about the Myrtle Bank and the men from Havana and a night in 1949. And Reggie sits beside Win on her own verandah, not touching her, and listens to him do it. It's the saddest ten minutes he's spent since a jetty in 1957.

---

It's nearly nine when Desmond plays his card. Reggie can see him decide to. He's been losing all evening, and knows it, and he's that kind of player. When the table goes against you, you don't get up. You double.

"You'll know, of course, Doctor," Desmond says pleasantly, "that Winnie and I are still married."

"I know."

"In the eyes of the law. And the Church. Jamaican law's very old-fashioned about these things, I'm told. There's a thing called adultery." He pronounces it with care, like the name of a wine. "And a thing called a co-respondent. That'd be you. And a thing called damages, which a wronged husband can claim, from the man. For the loss of his wife's — what's the word — her *society*." He smiles at Win. "I asked a lawyer in Kingston on Saturday. On the telephone at the harbour office. Isn't it wonderful what you can do from the harbour office."

Win's gone grey.

Reggie watches it happen. He watches it go into her face, and her shoulders, and her hand go up to the base of her throat and stay there. He knows what she's thinking, because she's told him on her own verandah what she's afraid of. The house. Fifteen years of it. The Board, and the Ministry party coming, and every family from Kingston who ever sent a girl here to be safe. *Mrs Carew of Harbour House, co-respondent Dr R. Tar of the William Martin Institute.* In the *Gleaner*.

"And then I suppose," Desmond goes on, "your Institute might have something to say. A man in your position. I might write to them. I might go and see them. I've always wanted to see the famous Institute."

He's looking at Reggie. He's waiting for it: the flinch, the jealousy, the fear. Something he can bet on.

Reggie isn't thinking about any of that. He's thinking, with enormous and very cold clarity, that this man has just made Win put her hand to her throat. That he's done it on purpose, on her own verandah, after she's fed him for four days. That he's had her twenty-six years, one way and another, and sat in her chair all week, and hasn't once looked at her the way you'd look at something you'd be sorry to lose. That he'll do it again.

It isn't jealousy. He doesn't know what it is. It comes up in him from somewhere a long way down, and it isn't generous, and it isn't anything he'd put in a paper. *Mine*, it says. *And you never even saw her.*

"Go and see them," Reggie says. "The Director's Dr Leslie. She's in the office from eight. Alleyne's boy will take you over."

Desmond looks at him.

"You're not worried."

"About the Institute? No." Reggie stands up. He does it slowly, because his chest is tight and he's not going to give the man that either. "Excuse us, Mr Carew. Win's been up since five. Goodnight."

He holds out his hand to her. In front of her husband, on her verandah, deliberately, and he knows it's deliberate while he does it. She looks at it, and at him, and at Desmond. Then she takes it, and gets up off the step. They go in together and leave Desmond on the verandah in her chair, with his rum and the dark and the lights of the harbour.

Behind them, after a minute, they hear him go down to the bar. And then, faintly, through two floors of Harbour House, the piano. *Stardust*.

---

She doesn't say anything on the stairs. She doesn't say anything in her room, with the door shut and the piano coming up through the boards. She stands at the verandah door with her back to him and looks at the harbour. Her hands are shaking, and she's furious with them for it.

She knows what she saw on the verandah.

She saw a man go cold. She saw his face shut like a door, and his voice go flat and hard, and his hand come out to her in front of Desmond as though he were laying down a card. And she's seen that before. Not on Desmond. Desmond was never jealous; you'd have to mind a thing to be jealous of it. But she's kept a guest house fifteen years. She's seen a husband come down to breakfast and find his wife laughing with a man at the next table. She knows the face.

*You've got to know a woman twenty-five years, Dr Tar.* And Reggie had gone cold, and got up, and taken her away.

She doesn't know what she'll do if he's that. She doesn't know what she'll do if he isn't. She doesn't know anything, except that her husband's playing *Stardust* on her piano in her bar at nine at night, and her name might be in the *Gleaner* by Christmas, and her hands won't stop.

"Win."

She doesn't turn round.

"Win. I'm going to pay him."

She turns round.

He's sitting on the end of her bed in his shirtsleeves with his elbows on his knees. His face isn't cold any more. It's white, and set, and quite calm, the way it was in the kitchen in May when he told her about the file.

"You're *what*?"

"The eight hundred. And whatever the lawyer in Kingston costs him, and the fare back to Miami, first class if he wants it. I'll write a cheque on the London account tonight and he can have it at breakfast, and be on the boat by noon." He looks up at her. "It's mine to pay. I'm the reason he's got a card to play. I can take it off the table."

She stares at him.

"No," she says.

"Win —"

"*No*." It comes out of her much louder than she means. She hears it go down through the floor and doesn't care. "You'll not. You'll not write him one penny, Reggie Tar. Not one."

"It's only money —"

"It's *my* money." She's crossing the room. She doesn't know she's doing it. "Do you understand? It's not about eight hundred dollars. I've *got* eight hundred dollars. I could pay him out of the drawer in the office tomorrow morning and not miss it by Christmas." She stops in front of him. "I paid him for twenty-one years. And then I stopped. It took me a month with a letter in my apron. It's the only thing I've done in my whole life that was just for me, and it *cost*, and it's *mine*. It's my account. I closed it." She's shaking all over now. "And you'll not go and open it again behind my back to make him go away, and call it being kind."

He looks up at her for a long moment.

Then he says, "No. I won't. You're right," and she sits down on the bed beside him, very suddenly, because her knees have gone.

He doesn't touch her. He waits. After a while she puts her hand on his arm, and he puts his over it.

"I thought," she says, and stops.

"What?"

"On the verandah. When he said — about knowing me twenty-five years. You went all cold. I thought you were jealous." She makes herself say it. "I've seen men go like that. I've seen it at breakfast. I didn't know what I'd do."

He turns his head and looks at her. He looks genuinely baffled, which is somehow worse and better at once.

"Of *him*?"

"He's my husband, Reggie."

"I know he's your husband." He thinks about it. She can see him do it honestly, the way he does. "I don't think it was jealous. Not the way you mean. I don't want what he had. He had you for eleven years and didn't know what he'd got. He sat in your chair tonight and still doesn't."

"Then what was it? Your face."

He's quiet for a second.

"Your hand went to your throat," he says. "When he said *damages*. I watched it go." His voice is very even, and she can hear what it costs him to keep it there. "He did that to you on purpose. On your own verandah. After you'd given him room nine for nothing and fed him four days and let him play your piano. He knew what he was doing. He looked at your face while he did it, to see it land." He stops. "I wanted to put him in the harbour, Win. I'm fifty-four and I've a heart Marguerite won't insure, and I wanted to put him off the end of the jetty in his good suit. That's what my face was."

She looks at him.

"And that's all?"

"No." He looks at his hands. "No, that's not all. And the rest doesn't do me any credit." He's quiet for a moment. "I took you to bed in front of him. On purpose. I held out my hand on your verandah in front of your husband, so he'd see you take it. I wanted him to see that somebody knows what you are. That somebody *looks*." He lets out a breath. "It was a cheap thing to do, and I'd do it again. I'm sorry. I'd not have done it if you'd been in any doubt, but I didn't ask you, and I should have."

It goes through her slowly, the way warmth goes back into cold hands. Not the breakfast face. Not a man minding what another man had had of her. A man minding that nobody had *looked*.

She starts to laugh. She can't help it. It comes up out of her in a great breathless heave, and she puts her face against his shoulder, and laughs until she's crying.

"You wanted to put him in the *harbour*."

"Off the end of the jetty. I'd worked out the angle."

"You'd have *drowned*. You can't go in the water, Marguerite said —"

"I'd have pushed him. I'd not have had to go in." He's got his arm round her now, and she can feel him beginning to laugh too, against her. "He's heavier than me. I'd have needed a run up. I thought about the sling."

"*Reggie*."

"Line to the shoulder side."

She laughs until her ribs hurt. When she's done she's lying half across him on the counterpane with her face in his neck. Downstairs the piano has stopped. The house is quiet, the way it goes at the end of a night, with the shutters ticking.

"I'm frightened," she says into his neck. "Of the other thing. Not you. The *Gleaner*. The house. The Ministry party's coming Thursday, Reggie, and the Board in November, and every mother in Kingston who's sent a daughter to me because it's a *respectable* house —"

"I know."

"You don't care. Do you. About the scandal."

He thinks about it honestly. She can feel him do it.

"No," he says. "Not for me. But that's easy for me, Win, and I know it. Jean won't sack me. She'd sack the Board first, and she'd enjoy it. I've been in a worse file than the *Gleaner*. It'd cost me nothing." His hand's moving on her back, slow. "It's not my name over the door. It's yours. You built it. I'll not have it cost you the house."

"Then what do we do?"

"I don't know yet." He turns his head and kisses her hair. "Let him go and see Jean. Jean's been waiting twenty years for somebody to come and complain about me to her face. It'll make her week."

---

She kisses him, after a while. It's slow. She's tired to the bone and still shaking a little, and she wants him with a kind of hunger that surprises her. It's not the way she wanted him in May, all wonder. It's something with an edge to it, as though she needs to put her hands on what's hers and make sure.

He seems to understand that. He lets her.

She undoes his shirt and pushes it off his shoulders and puts her mouth to the hollow of his throat, where his pulse is going. She bites him there, gently, and hears him catch his breath. She pushes him back onto the counterpane and he goes, and lies looking up at her with his glasses crooked. She takes them off and puts them on the chest, beside the children, who've been facing the wall since May because she's never turned them back.

"Win," he says. His voice has gone low. "Win, let me —"

"No. You lie there." She's kneeling over him with her hands flat on his chest. His heart's going hard under her palms, and she keeps them there, and watches his face. "You lie there and you let me. You've had your own way all evening."

"I've had *my own way* —"

"You told my husband to stop it on my verandah. You stood up and took me to bed in front of him." She bends down and kisses his mouth, hard. "*Your own way*."

He laughs against her mouth. Then he stops laughing, because she's moved her hand.

She takes her time. She finds she wants to. She wants to learn him the way he learned her in May, slowly, all over, as though she were writing it down. There's the place under his ear that makes him turn his head. There's the long line of his side where the ribs show, and the hollow of his hip, and the flat of his stomach, which jumps when she puts her mouth there. He's so thin. She thinks, *I'll feed you*, fiercely, and goes on. He's saying her name. He's got one hand in her hair and the other flat on the counterpane beside him, gripping it, and he isn't telling her what to do. He's just saying her name, over and over, in a voice she's never heard anybody use to her. It isn't charming at all.

"Is this —" she says. "Tell me."

"*Yes*. Win. Yes, God, you know it is —"

"I want to hear you say it."

"It's *good*. It's so good I can't — I can't *think* —"

"Good," says Win. "Don't."

And when she takes him into her, at last, and he goes still under her, with his eyes shut and his mouth open and his hands come up to hold her hips, she thinks that she's never been so sure of anything. She rocks against him, slowly, and watches him. She watches his face do everything it's doing with nothing behind it and nothing in front of it, and knows it's hers. All of it. Every bit of what he's showing her. There isn't a room in him she can't see into.

"*Win*," he says, and opens his eyes, and looks at her.

It's the jetty look. The look he gave the thermometer.

She bends down and puts her forehead on his. And they finish like that, together, with her hair coming down round both their faces like a curtain. Neither of them is quiet about it, quite, though they try. She bites down on the heel of her own hand. He turns his face into her wrist and she feels the sound he makes go into her skin instead of into the house. Below them the Ministry's rooms are empty till Thursday, and Desmond is in room nine at the back, and the piano has stopped.

---

Afterwards, when he's asleep, curled round her from behind with his face in the back of her neck the way he always ends up, she lies awake and thinks about Desmond.

She thinks about his face on the verandah when Reggie said *stop it*. That second when there was nothing on it. She's been married to that face twenty-six years and she's seen it with nothing on it perhaps three times. Once when Lloyd was born. Once at his mother's funeral. And once in 1958, on the platform at the Kingston station, when she got on the train with the children and the four cases and didn't look back.

She'd looked back, of course. She'd looked back at the last minute, through the window. And he was standing on the platform in his good suit with nothing on his face at all.

She puts her hand over Reggie's, where it's lying on her stomach, and holds it, and doesn't sleep for a long time.

Jean has been Director of the William Martin Institute for five years, and Director of Research's assistant, deputy, conscience and whipping-girl for fifteen before that. She's sat in this office through two hurricanes, a Colonial Office inspection, and a man with a card from an insurance agency in Nassau. She's very nearly buried Reggie, and she's read a letter from Ålesund at this desk that she'll remember on her deathbed.

So when Alleyne's boy knocks at ten on the Wednesday and says there's a Mr Carew from the Bay wishing to see the Director on a private and delicate matter, Jean says *show him in*, and puts her pen down, and settles back in her chair to enjoy herself.

He's handsome. She'll give him that. He's handsome and he knows how to come into a room, and he takes her hand and bows over it slightly, which nobody's done since about 1950. He sits down in the chair across the desk, the one Halloran sat in, and puts his panama on his knee.

"Dr Leslie," he says. "I'm so sorry to trouble you. I know how busy a woman in your position must be."

"Yes," says Jean.

He smiles. She watches him decide she's shy.

"It's a delicate matter," he says. "And I'd not bring it to you if I didn't feel — as a man of some standing myself, in Kingston, in Miami — that you'd want to hear it before anybody else. Before it became — well. *Public*."

"Go on."

"It concerns your Dr Tar." He lowers his voice. "And my wife."

Jean waits.

He tells her. He tells it well; she'll give him that too. He tells her that he's Mrs Carew's lawful husband, which she knows. That they've been living apart for some years by mutual arrangement, owing to the demands of his profession, which she very much doubts. That he's come home to find his wife in a situation. He picks the word with care. He's taken advice. The law of Jamaica is quite clear on these matters, and a man in his position would be within his rights to seek redress. And he'd hate — he'd *hate* — to see a respected institution like hers dragged into the kind of case that finds its way into the papers.

When he's done he sits back with his hat on his knee and looks at her with enormous sympathy.

Jean looks back.

She's thinking about Reggie at the long table in April 1971, not eating, and at the rail of the north pen with his hand flat on his chest. She's thinking about him in the hut at the end of the row at two in the morning with the photograph face down on the drawer. She's thinking about the Founders' Dinner in April this year, when she put Win Carew beside him and he turned his chair through the whole of her speech so his shoulder was against Win's. She'd had to stop in the middle of the paragraph about the Founder's bequest, because she'd looked down and seen his face, and hadn't been able to see her notes for a moment.

"Mr Carew," she says. "What is it you want me to do?"

He spreads his hands. "I'd not presume to tell the Director her business. But a man in Dr Tar's position — a *consultant*, I understand, retained at the Institute's pleasure — if the Institute felt that his conduct —"

"You'd like me to sack him."

"I'd not put it so —"

"You'd like me to threaten to sack him," says Jean, "so that he'll pay you not to go to the papers. Or so that Mrs Carew will. I'd imagine you're not very particular which."

Mr Carew's smile stays exactly where it is. She's impressed.

"Dr Leslie —"

"Mr Carew." She folds her hands on the blotter. "Let me tell you about Dr Tar's conduct. In 1957 a man from the Colonial Office sat in the chair you're sitting in and asked him questions a great deal worse than anything you've got."

"I'm sure —"

"In 1971 I read a letter at this desk that I'd not wish on anybody, and then I watched him get up off the floor." She looks at him. "He has been the despair of this Board for twenty years. Not because he had a private life. Because he hadn't got one. If you're telling me he's finally found one, at fifty-four, with a woman whose mother did the sheets on this cay for thirty years, then you've come a very long way across a very hot channel to give me the best news I've had all year."

Mr Carew doesn't say anything at all. For a second, just a second, the smile isn't there. Then it is.

"You're a remarkable woman, Dr Leslie."

"Yes," says Jean. "I am. Alleyne's boy will take you back. There's tea on the verandah while you wait, and Mrs Joseph's made a coconut cake. Do have some. You look as though you've not had a proper meal in a month."

She watches him realise she means it. She watches it land on him, oddly. He doesn't know what to do with a woman who's thrown him out and offered him cake in the same breath. He stands, and bows over her hand again, less smoothly, and goes out.

Through the window she sees him on the verandah, sitting in the cane chair with a slice of Mrs Joseph's coconut cake on a plate. He eats it very fast, the way a man eats who's hungrier than he'll admit. He looks, for a moment, when he thinks nobody's watching, about seventy.

She sits back in her chair and lets out a long breath.

---

She finds Reggie at the north pen at a quarter to twelve, doing the weights with Baptiste. She waits until Baptiste's gone up with the sheet and then stands beside him at the rail.

"Your lady's husband came to see me," she says.

"I know. I told him to."

"I gave him coconut cake."

Reggie turns his head and looks at her.

"He was hungry," says Jean. "He's broke, Reggie. Properly broke. Not the way a man's broke who's had a bad month. His cuffs have been turned twice, and his shoes have been resoled, and he ate that cake like a boy at school." She leans on the rail. "He's not a villain. He's a man who's been living on his charm for thirty years and it's running out, and he knows it, and he's frightened."

"I know," says Reggie quietly. "I sat across from him on Monday."

"And he thinks he loves her."

"He does love her." Reggie's looking at the calf's mother, coming up. "In his way. He'd not know what else to call it. She's the one thing in his life that was always there when he turned the card over." He's quiet for a moment. "I can't blame him for that, Jean. Anybody would. I'd have loved her too, in his shoes. I'd just have *noticed*."

Jean looks at him sidelong.

She's known him since 1953. She fought for four years to get him to see her at all, and she remembers exactly what it was like to be in the room with him before he did. She remembers what it was like after. She's watched him look at exactly two people the way he's talking about Win Carew now. One of them's dead, and she read the letter.

"Reggie," she says. "Marry her."

"She's married, Jean."

"Then do something about that." She pushes herself off the rail. "You're supposed to be clever. And she's cleverer than you, from what I've seen of her books. Between you, you ought to be able to manage one Kingston bandleader."

She goes back up the path to the office. Behind her she can hear him not moving at all. Then, faintly, over the sound of the calf's mother blowing, she hears him laugh.

---

Marguerite does her rounds on Thursday and Friday with her bag, as she always does, and on this particular Thursday and Friday she does them slowly.

She's not going to tell anybody anything. She's a doctor; she doesn't tell. But she's been a doctor at the Bay for thirty years. She knows perfectly well that there's no more efficient instrument for finding out what a village thinks than a woman with a stethoscope and her whole afternoon. People tell the doctor things. They tell her things while she's got her fingers on their wrists and her eyes on her watch. Even Reggie does, on her verandah on Sundays. So she takes her time over every blood pressure between the church and the fishmarket, and she listens.

She starts with Mrs Kilburn, because Mrs Kilburn's ankles are bad and she's the greatest talker on the north coast.

"You'll have seen Win's husband's back," says Marguerite, pumping the cuff.

"Oh, *that* one." Mrs Kilburn sniffs. "In the fishmarket yesterday in his white suit, telling everybody Win's carrying on with the little doctor off the cay. Like it was *news*." She rolls her eyes. "Everybody know that since the storm. My Winston saw the little doctor carry her bucket up the stair."

"And what did people say? When he told them."

"Say?" Mrs Kilburn looks at her as though she were simple. "What they going to say, Doctor? They're *fifty*. Win's a grandmother. What's he want, a sermon?" She shifts her ankle on the stool. "Kilburn say to him, Mr Carew, sir, that little doctor been steering the whole Bay home for fifteen year with his lamp, and now Win's got the light in her top window going every night and we come in on that instead. And good luck to the both of them. And then all the men laugh, and your Mr Carew he go off up the street with a face."

"He's not my Mr Carew."

"He's not *Win's* either," says Mrs Kilburn, "not since 'fifty-eight. What's my pressure?"

"High," says Marguerite, writing it down with great contentment. "Less salt."

---

The minister she catches at the Friday surgery, where he's come about his knee.

He's a Methodist, and seventy, and a widower, and he's been at Sunday dinner at her house perhaps forty times since 1971. He sits on the couch with his trouser leg rolled up and says, before she's asked anything at all, "I understand there's been some talk. About Mrs Carew and our friend Dr Tar."

"Has there?"

"Mr Carew was good enough to call on me on Wednesday evening." The minister looks at his knee. "He seemed to feel I ought to preach on it. On Sunday. Against it."

Marguerite keeps her fingers exactly where they are on his kneecap.

"And will you?"

"My dear Dr Sealy." He looks up at her mildly over his spectacles. "I've sat at your table beside that man for two years. I watched him come back from the dead, a little at a time, over the fish. I shall be preaching on Sunday on the parable of the lost sheep, as it happens, which was already on the calendar." He considers. "I suppose Mr Carew may take it as he likes. At their age, I should think the Lord has rather larger concerns. And if He hasn't, I've a great many parishioners I'd sooner He looked at first." He rolls his trouser leg down. "Is it the cartilage?"

"It's the cartilage."

"I thought it was." He stands up, carefully. "Give Mrs Carew my regards. And tell Dr Tar that if he intends to make an honest woman of her, I'm available on most Saturdays, subject to the usual formalities."

---

The last one she doesn't mean to canvass at all.

She's at Harbour House on the Friday evening to see Win, and Win's in the kitchen with the Ministry party's dinner, so Marguerite waits in the bar. There's a man in there already, alone, with a whisky. He's the older of the two from the Ministry, grey and neat and quiet. She knows him slightly; he's stayed at Harbour House two or three times a year for as long as she can remember. Mr Wilmot.

He stands up when she comes in, and they talk about the weather. And then, because she's been canvassing for two days and can't stop, she says, "I suppose you'll have heard some talk this week, Mr Wilmot. About Mrs Carew."

Mr Wilmot is quiet for a moment. He turns his glass round on the bar.

"I've heard a man in a white suit say some things in this bar," he says. "Last night. To anybody who'd listen." He looks at the glass. "I've stayed in this house since 1964, Dr Sealy. Two or three times a year, with a colleague. Mrs Carew keeps a very good house. A very discreet house. She has never once, in nine years, made either of us feel —" He stops, and chooses the word. "*Remarked upon*."

Marguerite looks at him. He doesn't look back. He's looking at his glass, very steadily, with his whole face held still. It's a face she recognises, after a moment. She's seen it across her own table, on Sundays in 1971, whenever somebody said a name.

"I should be very sorry," says Mr Wilmot, "to see anybody try to make it otherwise. I've told the Ministry's travel office that we'll be continuing with Harbour House. For as long as Mrs Carew will have us." He finishes his whisky. "You might tell her that, if it's any use to her. I'd rather not say it myself. She'd only thank me."

He nods to her, very correctly, and goes up.

Marguerite stands in Win's bar with her bag in her hand for some time.

---

"*Nobody*?" says Win.

It's nine o'clock. They're on the top verandah, with the Ministry fed and gone to bed, and Desmond out somewhere in the Bay, and the light in the top window lit. Across the channel the cay's a long dark shape with the long-room lamp at the near end and nothing at the far one.

"Nobody," says Marguerite. "Not a soul. Mrs Kilburn says you're fifty and it's not news. The minister's preaching on the lost sheep and says the Lord has larger concerns. Kilburn told Desmond in the fishmarket that the Bay's been coming in on your window since the Doctor's Light went out, and good luck to the both of you, and all the men laughed. Alleyne says his boy's been expecting it since the storm and has money on Christmas." She takes a breath. "And the Ministry's staying."

Win stares at her.

"The *Ministry*?"

"Somebody in the Ministry's travel office has been told you keep a very good house. I'm not to say who. I'm told you'd only thank him."

Win's face does something. Marguerite watches it. She watches her friend work out who, and then something else go across her face that Marguerite can't read at all. Win puts her hand over her mouth.

"Oh," says Win, behind it. "Oh, Mr *Wilmot*."

"I didn't say —"

"No. You didn't." Win takes her hand away. Her eyes are wet. "It's his business. I do the sheets."

Marguerite doesn't ask. She's a doctor. She knows when a thing isn't hers.

"So you see," she says instead, "you've nothing to be frightened of. Not from the Bay. They've decided you're sweet. It'd be a scandal if you were twenty, and I dare say it'd have been one in 'fifty-five. But you're forty-eight and he's fifty-four and he's got a heart like a leaky cistern, and the whole coast has watched him carry your buckets. They think it's the nicest thing that's happened since Independence."

Win laughs. It's shaky, and wet, and it goes on longer than the joke.

"Then what's Desmond got?" she says.

Marguerite looks out across the channel at the dark end of the row.

"The law," she says. "And not much else. And I should think he's beginning to find that out."

---

"Counsel's opinion," says Reggie. "That's the first thing."

It's Saturday afternoon, on the step of the hut at the end of the row, and he's got a pad on his knee and he's been writing on it for an hour. Pascual's on the step below with a bottle of beer, listening, which he's been doing for most of the hour.

"There's a man in Kingston Jean knows. Chambers in Duke Street. I'll go on Tuesday's boat and take it to him. Whether damages against a co-respondent still lie in this jurisdiction at all — they've gone in England, since 'seventy, and I'd put money on the Jamaican courts following, but I want it in writing. Then what a court would award if they do, which on the facts would be nominal. There's no loss of society to speak of after fifteen years apart. Then —"

"*Hombre*."

"— then the question of costs. If he petitions and she doesn't defend, he'll carry his own, and he can't. So he'll not petition unless he thinks there's money at the end of it. Which means the real lever is —"

"Reggie."

"What?"

Pascual's looking up at him from the lower step with the beer bottle held against his knee.

"Does she still love him?"

Reggie stops writing.

"What?"

"Win. Does she still love him. The husband."

"No. She left him. In 'fifty-eight. She stopped paying him in —"

"That's not what I asked." Pascual says it quite gently. "You told me in May. She said it to you in her kitchen. You told me on this step. *You don't stop, you just stop paying.* Your words, *hombre*. Her words." He turns the bottle round. "And now he's in her house. Four days. Playing her the songs from when she was twenty-two. And you've been sitting here an hour with a pad, working out the law of damages."

Reggie looks at the pad.

It's covered, both sides, in his small neat hand. Duke Street. Costs. *Matrimonial Causes — check whether '70 Act extended?* A diagram, God help him, with arrows, of how you'd get a man to petition who couldn't afford to. It's very good. It's the best piece of work he's done since the joists.

There isn't a single word on it about Win.

"She's frightened for the house," he says slowly. "She told me. On Monday."

"I'm sure she is. And what else?"

He thinks about Monday night. He thinks about her lying awake afterwards, when he thought she was asleep. He was half asleep himself, curled round her, and she'd put her hand over his where it lay on her stomach. She'd held it hard, and for a long time, and he'd thought it was the *Gleaner*.

He thinks about a piano coming up through two floors. *Stardust*. And Win on the verandah step, between them, watching her husband do the voice at fifty-seven, with her face quite still. He'd thought that was fear. He'd been so busy with the man making her hand cold that he'd not once looked at what else was in her face.

He knows what else. He's worn it. He wore it at other people's tables for years whenever somebody said a name, and for fourteen years with a lamp.

"Oh, God," says Reggie.

"Yes," says Pascual.

"I've been — Pascual, I've been doing *sums*. She's been watching a man she loved for twenty-six years come apart on her verandah, and I've been drawing arrows about costs."

"You've been furious," says Pascual. "On her behalf. It's a good thing to be. It's the first thing a man should be." He puts the beer down on the step. "It's not the only thing, that's all. She doesn't need you to win. She's got Marguerite for that, and Jean, and half the Bay. She needs somebody to know what it is to see him."

Reggie puts the pad down on the step, face down.

"She'd not have said," he says. "She'd not have wanted to — she'd have thought it wasn't fair. To me. To grieve him in front of me."

"And would you have minded?"

"*No*." It comes out of him so hard that Pascual blinks. "No. I'd not have minded. I'd have — God, Pascual, I of all the men on this coast."

"Yes," says Pascual. "You of all the men on this coast. That's why I said."

---

He goes down on the Sunday night instead of the Monday. He doesn't stay for Marguerite's dinner. He walks down her hill at eight in the dusk, not fast, because he can hear Marguerite in his head, and goes round to the back of Harbour House. The kitchen's dark. There's a light in the little office off the passage, where the fuse board is.

She's at the desk with the cheque book open in front of her and the pen in her hand.

She doesn't hear him. She's sitting quite still, with the pen over the page, not writing. He stands in the doorway and looks at her, and knows before he's close enough to read it what she's written. *Pay D. Carew. Eight hundred dollars.* The amount's done. The words are done. There's nothing left but the signature.

She's been sitting there, he thinks, some time.

"Win," he says.

She jumps. She puts her hand flat over the cheque book, the way you'd put your hand over a letter. Then she sees who it is, and her face does something terrible, and she takes the hand away.

"I wasn't going to," she says. "I wasn't. I was just — I wanted to see what it looked like. Written down."

"I know."

"It's only eight hundred. It's nothing. It's the Ministry's bill for a fortnight. And he's frightened, Reggie, he's so frightened, I've never seen him frightened, not once, not in twenty-six years, and there's a man in Miami —"

"I know."

"— and he played *Stardust* last night in the bar, all through, the whole arrangement, the one we did at the Myrtle Bank, he remembered every bar of it, and I stood in the kitchen and —" She stops. Her face goes. "And I thought, I'll just write it out. I'll just *write* it. And then he'll go, and he'll be all right, and I'll not have to look at him being — being *old*, and frightened, and still *him* —"

He crosses the office and kneels down beside her chair. He does it carefully, one knee at a time, because of his chest, and because the floor's tiled. He takes the pen out of her hand. He doesn't take the cheque book. He puts the pen down on the desk beside it, square.

"You stopped," he says. "In 'sixty-eight. It took you a month. You told me in your kitchen it was the hardest thing you've done."

"Yes."

"Then don't undo it on a Sunday night because he played you a song." He keeps his hand over hers on the desk. "You'd have had to stop again. Next year. Or the year after. The first time it cost you a month. I don't know what it'd cost you the second time. I don't want to find out, and I don't think you do."

She's crying now, without any sound, with her other hand over her eyes.

"I still love him," she says. "I told you. You don't stop."

"I know. I know you do."

"I thought you'd *mind*."

"Win." He waits till she takes her hand away from her eyes and looks at him. "I sat at other people's tables for years and heard a man's name and had to put the voice on to get through it. I kept a lamp lit fourteen years for a man I'd sent to Norway. You don't stop." He hears his voice go, and lets it. "I of all people. I'm so sorry. I should've seen it on Monday. I was so angry at him for making you put your hand to your throat that I didn't look at the rest of your face. Pascual had to tell me. On the step. With a beer." He stops. "And I told you on Sunday I was coming down. I didn't ask whether you wanted me here, with him in the house. I just came. I should have asked."

"I did want you."

"I know. You'd a right to be asked all the same."

She stares at him.

Then she laughs, wet, and puts both arms round his neck, and holds on, with her face in his shoulder, and cries properly. He kneels on the tiles of her office with his knee complaining and holds her, and lets her.

After a while she tears the cheque out of the book. She doesn't sign it. She folds it in half, and in half again, and puts it in the drawer.

"I'll keep that," she says. "Like your letter."

"Yes."

"Not to send. Just to know I wrote it and didn't."

"Yes," he says. "That's what it's for."

---

It's her idea. He's quite sure of that, afterwards. He's the one who says it out loud, but she's the one who gets there.

They're in the kitchen at ten, with tea, and she's stopped crying, and he's got his foot up on a chair because she made him. She says, turning her cup round, "He'll not stop, you know. Now the Bay's laughed at him and Jean's given him cake. He'll not get on a boat with nothing. He's never got up from a table with nothing in his life. When he's losing he doubles."

"I saw him double. On Monday."

"He'll go to law." She says it flatly. "He'll do it out of spite now, if he can't do it for money. He'll petition, and name you, just to have *won* something. Just so he can go back to Miami and tell them at the bar that he threw me over. That the wronged husband walked away with his head up."

Reggie puts his cup down.

He looks at her across the kitchen table, and she looks at him, and he watches her hear what she's just said.

"If he petitions," Reggie says slowly, "you're free."

"Yes."

"You can't petition. You left him. You've no grounds that'd stand, not after fifteen years. But *he* can. On your adultery. With me." He can feel the thing coming on in the back of his head, the way it does, where everything else goes quiet. But this time she's in the middle of it. "And you don't defend. And in a year, or less, it's done."

"And he'll not do it," says Win, "if he thinks I *want* it. He's Desmond. If he thinks it's what I want, he'll sit in my chair till Christmas to spite me."

"Then he mustn't think you want it."

"No."

"He must think," says Reggie, "that it's the worst thing he could possibly do to us. To *me*."

Win's looking at him. There's something starting at the corner of her mouth.

"You'd have to be frightened," she says. "Properly frightened. Of the law, and the Institute, and the papers. He'd have to see you frightened, Reggie. He'd not do it otherwise. He'd have to see it land."

"I know."

"And you're not frightened of any of it."

"No." He finds that he's smiling. "But I've got a voice for exactly this. I've been told. It's like a man selling a car."

Win puts her hand over her mouth.

"You'd do the *voice*. At *Desmond*."

"Not badly. Not at first." Reggie's thinking it through as he speaks. "I'll come up those stairs like we're the best of friends. Like Monday never happened. Old man, just the fellow. And then I'll let it slip. A little at a time. So he sees it going, and thinks he's the one who's seen through it." He looks up. "He'll like that better than anything. He'll think he's caught me." He turns his cup round. "He's a card player, Win. You said. He's not been let win a hand in fifteen years. Let him win this one."

She takes her hand away from her mouth. Her eyes are wet again, but it's different.

"No money," she says.

"No money."

"Not a penny. Not from you, not from me. He goes home on his own fare."

"Not a penny." He holds out his hand across the table, the way Pascual would, the way you'd shake on a bet. "Just the one hand. Let him win it and walk away with his head up. It's the only thing he's got that he'll want to keep."

She looks at his hand for a moment. Then she takes it.

"He'll ask about damages," she says. "He'll ask you to your face."

"Then I'll tell him the truth." Reggie's smile widens. "That I've no idea what a Jamaican court would award. And that I'm *terrified* to find out."

---

Desmond's had a bad day. Win can see it on him when he comes up onto the verandah at six.

He's been to the harbour office twice on the telephone, to Kingston, and come back both times with the smile on too bright. He's had no breakfast, because he was in bed till ten, and no lunch, because he was at the harbour office. At four she saw him from the kitchen window standing on the harbour wall looking at the Kingston boat as it went out, with his hands in his pockets, like a man at a rail.

He sits down in her chair. He doesn't put his feet up. He lights a cigarette and his hand shakes, and he doesn't turn it into anything.

"Winnie," he says. "Let's not quarrel. I'm too old to quarrel. Sit down and let's talk like sensible people."

She sits down on the step.

She's not going to be able to do this. She knew that at breakfast, and at lunch, and at four o'clock at the kitchen window. She knows it now, looking at him in her chair with his cuffs turned twice, trying to smile at her. She's going to open her mouth and say *Des, I've a cheque in the drawer*, and it'll all be for nothing.

Then she hears the back door, and Reggie's step on the stairs.

It's his ordinary step. Not fast, not slow, the way he comes up to his breakfast. He comes out onto the verandah in his shirtsleeves with his jacket over his arm, and sees Desmond, and his face lights up as though Desmond were the one man on the north coast he'd been hoping to find.

"Carew! There you are." The voice is on. It's on beautifully, easy and warm, the shoulders down, the lamp turned all the way up. "I hoped I'd catch you before the Ministry lot come down and want the verandah. May I?" He drops into the other chair and crosses his legs. "Win tells me you make a very fair rum punch. I've been thinking about it all the way down the hill."

Desmond looks at him. Then he laughs, and gets up, and goes to the tray, and makes him one. Win watches him do it. She watches him be pleased to be asked.

"Here's to you, Doctor."

"And to you." Reggie drinks, and closes his eyes, and says *oh, that's very good*, and Win has to look at the harbour. "I hear you went across to see Jean. She liked you, you know. She said so. She gave you the good cake, and she doesn't give that to the Board."

"A charming woman."

"Isn't she? Terrifying. I've worked for her for five years and I'm frightened of her every day." He laughs. "She did mention the Board, of course. They sit in November." He says it lightly, in the middle of the laugh, as if it were the weather. His hand goes to his chest pocket, and stops, and comes away again, and he picks his glass up. "But that's November."

Win sees Desmond see the hand.

It's so small. That's what she can't get over, afterwards. She'd thought he'd do it with a great show, the way he did it in the kitchen when he was describing it to her. But it's nothing. A hand to a pocket. A laugh that goes on a beat too long. He talks about the Ministry, and the calf's mother, and a man from Miami he met once at a conference who played the trumpet, and did Desmond know him? And every so often, not often, something slips. The voice catches on a word. On *proceedings*, once, when he's saying something about the Board's proceedings in November. On *position*. It catches the way a sleeve catches on a nail, and he pulls it free and goes on, a little brighter, and it's a quarter of an inch off. Nobody would hear it who hadn't lain awake with him. She hears every bit of it. And across the verandah she watches her husband begin to lean forward in her chair.

"Between ourselves, old man," says Reggie, after twenty minutes. He's still smiling. The smile's held a fraction too long. "Man to man. I'm sure there's no need for anything — *unpleasant*. I'm sure we can come to some sort of arrangement."

Desmond sits up.

Win watches it happen. She's watched it happen at a hundred card tables in Kingston. She watches her husband's whole body change, the way it did when the dealer turned over the card he wanted. The shoulders go back. The tremor goes out of the hand. The smile comes on — not the one for the dance floor, the other one, the one she hasn't seen since 1952. The winning one.

"An arrangement," says Desmond. "Well, now, Doctor. What sort of arrangement did you have in mind?"

Reggie looks at her.

She's ready for it. She lifts her head and looks straight at Desmond, and says, in the flattest voice she has, "He has no money for you, Des."

"Come, Winnie. A doctor."

"He's got none for *you*." She's not acting now. She doesn't have to. "He gives it away. He pays students' fares. He's paying a solicitor in London every quarter to look for a man who went missing in 1957, and they'll never find him, and he goes on paying. You'll not see a penny of it if you sit in my chair till Christmas." She looks back down at the step. "Nor of mine. I told you."

Every word of it is true. She's never made the truth do a liar's work before, and it does it beautifully.

Desmond looks from her to Reggie. Reggie's sitting very still with his glass in his hand, looking at the rail.

"Then I suppose," says Desmond slowly, "it'll have to be the law."

And Reggie puts the voice on again.

She watches him do it. She watches him reach for it, the way you'd reach for a rail in the dark, and get it, and have it not hold.

"Now, Mr Carew." The shoulders come down. The eyebrows go up. "Now, you'll appreciate — a man in my *position* —"

It goes. It goes in the middle of *position*, like a record when somebody knocks the table. He stops. He takes his glasses off and looks at them and starts to clean them on his shirt, carefully, not looking at anybody.

She has to look at the harbour again. She bites the inside of her cheek until she tastes it.

"I'm sorry, Doctor. I am. I like you." Desmond's standing now. He's taller than Reggie by four inches and he uses every one. "But a man has his pride. A man's wife — his *wife* — and you, carrying on under his nose, in his own house —"

"It's my house," says Win, to the harbour.

"— under his nose, and the whole Bay laughing at him in the fishmarket. A man can't just walk away from that, Doctor. Not and hold his head up." He takes a long pull on his cigarette. "No. I'll go to law. I'll petition. I'll name you. And the court can decide what you owe me."

Reggie puts his glasses back on. He looks up.

"And what does a court think it's worth?" he says. Quite quietly. In his own voice. "Win's society. For fifteen years you didn't have it."

For a moment Win thinks he's overplayed it. That it's too true, and Desmond will hear it.

But Desmond only smiles down at him.

"Oh, I couldn't say, Doctor. That's for the judge." He taps the ash off his cigarette onto her rail. "But I'd not sleep, if I were you."

Win makes a noise that she's afraid is going to turn into a laugh. She turns it into something else so fast it hurts, and puts her hand over her mouth, and hopes he'll think it's weeping.

"Des," she says. It's the last line. They agreed it in the kitchen. She makes herself look up at him. "Des. Please don't."

And she watches it land.

She watches her husband look down at her on the step, the only woman who ever paid for him, begging. And she sees on his face exactly what she knew she'd see. It isn't cruelty. He was never cruel. It's relief. Enormous, shaking relief, like a man who's been falling for a month and has just felt his feet touch the floor. He's won. At last, at fifty-seven, on her verandah, after fifteen years of losing, he's won a hand. And she's handed it to him, and he doesn't know.

"I'm sorry, Winnie," he says, very gently, and means it. "It's done. I'll write to the man in Kingston tonight. You'll hear from his office."

---

He writes it at the kitchen table, at nine, on the Harbour House paper, with her pen. She makes him an omelette while he does it, because he's not eaten all day. Reggie sits across the table from him with his hands folded in front of him, watching him write.

She's folding the omelette when Reggie gets up.

"I'll leave you to it," he says, quite pleasantly, to nobody in particular. And he goes out of the kitchen door and up the back stairs, not fast, not slow.

It isn't in the script. Win stands at the stove with the pan in her hand and thinks, *oh, that's good*. The co-respondent who can't bear to sit and watch his name go down in ink. She'd not have thought of it. She glances at Desmond, and Desmond is looking after him with an expression of enormous, quiet satisfaction, and she thinks, *yes, he's loving that*, and slides the omelette onto the plate.

Desmond eats it with one hand and writes with the other. He reads the letter aloud to her when he's done, in the voice. *Instructs you to commence proceedings forthwith. Adultery. The co-respondent being one Dr Reginald Tar.* He spells it out, with relish. *T-A-R.*

He seals it and gives it to the kitchen boy for the morning post, and sits back, and lights a cigarette. His hand is perfectly steady.

"No hard feelings, Winnie," he says.

"None, Des," says Win.

---

She washes up. She sees him to room nine, and he kisses her cheek at the door and says *goodnight, Mrs Carew*, as though it were a joke they'd always had. She turns the lamps down in the bar. She goes up the front stairs at ten with her shoes in her hand, grinning in the dark like a girl, rehearsing it. *Reggie, your face. When he spelt your name. I nearly dropped the pan.*

Her room's dark. He's standing at the verandah door with his back to her, looking at the harbour.

"That was clever," she says, shutting the door behind her. "Going up. He *loved* it. You should have seen his —"

He turns round, and crosses the room, and kisses her.

It isn't like him. That's the first thing, and she hardly has time to think it. He puts her back against the door, hard enough that the handle catches her hip. He's got both hands in her hair, pulling it down out of its pins, and his mouth on hers as though he were starving. Then on her jaw. Then on her throat, below her ear, where he knows it undoes her, not asking, not waiting to see. His whole body's pressed against hers from knee to shoulder, and he's saying her name into her neck in a voice like something tearing.

For a moment it's glorious. She's never been wanted like this. Not with this kind of force, as though there were something in him that had to go somewhere. She gets her hands into the back of his shirt and holds on, and her heart's going like a drum.

And then she notices that he's shaking.

Not the way he shakes when he wants her. Something else. His hands in her hair are trembling. He hasn't looked at her face once. He always looks at her face. And his heart's going against her chest so hard and so fast that it frightens her.

She puts both her hands flat on his chest and pushes. Gently.

"Reggie," she says. "Stop. Look at me."

He stops at once. He doesn't look at her. He puts his forehead against the door beside her head and stands there, breathing as though he'd run up from the harbour.

"I'm sorry," he says into the wood. "I'm sorry. I didn't — Win, I'm sorry."

"Hush. I'm not hurt." She keeps her hands on his chest. She can feel his heart under them, much too fast. "What is it? Is it your chest?"

"No. It's not my chest."

"Then what?"

He doesn't answer.

She waits. She's learned that from him, on her own verandah, nine weeks and a day. She stands against the door with her hair down round her shoulders and her hands on him, and waits, and doesn't say anything. After a while his breathing slows a little.

"You said *please*," he says.

She doesn't understand him for a second.

"At the end. On the verandah. *Des, please don't.*" He lifts his head off the door, but he still doesn't look at her. He's looking at the wall. "It was your line. I wrote it down. We laughed about it in the kitchen. I thought it'd be nothing. A line." He swallows. "And you said it. And he looked right at you, and he said *I'm sorry, Winnie*, and meant it, and did it anyway. He didn't even stop."

"Reggie —"

"He didn't *hesitate*, Win." It comes out of him low and hard. "Not for a second. Not for the length of a breath. If you'd said *please* to me like that — about anything, I don't care what, I'd not care what it cost — I'd have stopped. I'd at least have *stopped*. And not just you." He shuts his eyes. "If Jean said please to me like that. *Jean*, who'd cut anybody dead who said she and I were friends. If Jean ever said it like that I'd put down whatever it was and think. And he's had you twenty-six years, and you asked him, and he didn't miss a beat."

Win stands very still against the door.

"You were enjoying it," she says slowly. "The whole evening. I watched you. You were having the time of your life."

"I was." He laughs, and it cracks down the middle. "I was. God help me, it was the best fun I've had since the storm. Right up till then."

"You laughed. When we wrote it. In the kitchen."

"I know. I didn't know." He opens his eyes and looks at her at last. "I didn't know what it'd be to hear it. You, saying please. And him."

She takes her hands off his chest and puts them on his face instead, either side.

"And then what?"

"Then I wanted to hit him." He says it quite simply. "I wanted to put him through your kitchen wall. I sat at that table with my hands under it while he wrote my name down, and I couldn't do anything, because it'd have spoiled the whole thing. So I came up here. I thought I'd stand at the window till it went." He looks at her, and his face is wretched. "And then you came in, and it all went at you. Every bit of it. That's not what you're for. I'm sorry."

She thinks about it. She thinks about the handle of the door in her hip, and his hands in her hair, and the glory of it for a moment before she felt him shake.

"If you'd hit him," she says, "mind, it'd have been very convincing."

He stares at her.

Then he laughs. It's wet, and it breaks in the middle, but it's a laugh. He puts his forehead down on her shoulder and laughs into it.

"The wronged co-respondent," he says, muffled. "Oh, God. He'd have *loved* it."

"He'd have been writing to the *Gleaner* himself." She holds the back of his head. "Reggie. Listen to me. I'm not sorry."

He lifts his head.

"I'm not." She feels her face go hot and doesn't care. "I liked it. The door, and your hands, and — I liked it. I'd like it again. I didn't know you had it in you and I'd like it *again*." She holds his face so he has to look at her. "But not tonight. Not with Des asleep in room nine, and not with it being about him. I'll not have him in this room. Not like that."

"No," he says. "No. You're right."

"So could we do it slow? Tonight. The way we do." She strokes her thumbs along his cheekbones. "And the other. Later. When he's gone and it's not about him at all. When it's only about us."

He looks at her for a long moment.

"Yes," he says. "Please."

And she hears him hear himself say it. She watches his face change. She takes her hands away and kisses him, very gently, before he can think about it any more.

---

She takes his glasses off first. She folds them and puts them on the chest beside the children, who are still facing the wall. She undoes his shirt, one button at a time, the way he undid her dress in May. At each one she puts her mouth on the place it uncovers, because it's what he did, and she wants him to have it back. By the fourth his breathing has changed. By the last he's stopped shaking.

"Lie down," she says.

He lies down on Iris's counterpane in the dark, with the verandah door open and the harbour lights coming in, and she lies down beside him and holds him.

That's all, for a while. She holds him. She lies on her side with her arm across his chest and her cheek on his shoulder, and his heart comes down under her hand, slowly, from much too fast to only fast, and then to something she doesn't have to count. He's got one hand in her hair, not pulling now, only holding. After a long time he turns his face into her hair and breathes out, all the way.

"I didn't expect him to stop," she says, into his shoulder. "You know. I've known him twenty-six years. I knew he'd not stop. That's why it was my line. It had to be a line he'd not stop for."

He's quiet.

"That's worse," he says.

"I know it is."

He turns on his side to face her, then, and puts his hand on her cheek. He looks at her the way he does, very close, because he can't see her otherwise, with his whole face.

"I'd stop," he says.

"I know you would."

He kisses her. It's slow. It's the slowest he's ever kissed her, as though they had all the time there was, and she supposes they have. She finds the hem of her dress has come up somehow, and his hand's on her thigh, and he's waiting there, the way he does, until she moves against it to say yes. Then his hand goes on, slow, and she closes her eyes.

They take a long time over it. She's glad of it. She wants him to have every bit of the time. She gets out of her dress, eventually, and he helps her. He puts his mouth to her collarbone, and the soft place below her ribs, and the curve of her side where she's always been ashamed of the width of it. He stays there, with his cheek against her, as if that were the thing he'd come for. She holds his head against her and feels his breath on her skin. She thinks, *I'll never be ashamed of it again, not after this*, and is surprised to find she means it.

When she draws him over her, and into her, he stops, and holds very still, and looks down at her in the dark.

"All right?"

"All right," she says. "Better than all right. Stay there a minute."

He stays. They lie joined, not moving, with his forehead against hers and his weight on his elbows. She can feel his heart against her, and her own going back at it. She thinks she's never been so close to anybody in her life, not with Desmond, not with anyone.

Then she moves, a little, and he moves with her.

It's very slow. It's so slow she can feel everything, every bit of it, as though she were learning it for the first time. The hollow of his back under her hands. The catch of his breath when she lifts her hips. The damp of his hair where she's got her fingers in it. The sound he makes, low, against her ear, that he bites off, because room nine is at the back and the house is old and the walls are thin.

"Win," he breathes. "Win, I can't —"

"Hush. You can. Quietly. I've got you."

She has. She holds him, and he lets her. At the end he shudders all the way through and turns his face into her neck to keep the sound in. She holds his head there with both hands and feels his mouth open against her throat, and she comes apart under him, quietly, with her own teeth in her lip, in the dark.

Afterwards he doesn't let go of her. He stays where he is, heavy and warm, with his face in her neck. She lies under him and strokes his back, slowly, up and down, till he's nearly asleep.

"I'm sorry," he says, very indistinctly, into her skin. "About the door."

"I told you," says Win. "I'm not."

"Later, then."

"Later," she says. "When he's gone."

He falls asleep before she's finished saying it. She lies awake a while longer, with his weight on her and the harbour lights on the ceiling. She thinks about a man at her kitchen table who didn't stop, and a man in her arms who would have, and she knows perfectly well which one she's crying for. She lets herself, a little, because nobody can see.

---

The Kingston boat's late on the Tuesday, because the Kingston boat is always late, and Reggie's on the harbour wall in the sun with Desmond Carew, waiting for it.

He hadn't meant to be. He'd come down to see the man off because Win asked him to, and then Win went back up to the house for something she'd forgotten. Now there are just the two of them, and the case, and the mail sacks, and half a mile of flat bright water with no boat on it.

Desmond's in the cream suit. He's shaved very close and he smells of Win's good soap, and he's standing on the edge of the wall with his hat in his hand, looking out at the head. He looks well this morning. He looks, Reggie thinks, like a man who slept.

"You'll be hearing from the man in Kingston," says Desmond, without turning round.

"So I understand."

"I'd get yourself a good lawyer, Doctor. I would. He's a hard man, the one I've got."

"I'll bear it in mind."

Desmond laughs, softly, at the water.

Then he's quiet for a while. When he speaks again it's in a different voice. Reggie's been waiting a week to hear it, and hadn't been sure it existed. It's lower, and a bit hoarse, and there's nothing in it at all, no lamp, no shoulders. It's an old man's voice on a harbour wall.

"You'll look after her."

Reggie looks at him. Desmond doesn't look back. He's watching the head.

"She'd not let me," Reggie says. "She looks after everybody. I'll let her look after me, and I'll carry the buckets up the stairs when she lets me do that, and I'll mend the fuses. That's about as much as she'll stand."

"That's about as much as she ever stood." Desmond turns his hat round in his hands. "I never carried a bucket in my life. She never asked."

"No. She wouldn't."

"She sang, you know. I told you."

"You told me."

"She'll not sing for you."

"Very likely not," says Reggie. "I'll ask her anyway."

Desmond turns his head then and looks at him. It's the first time all week he's looked at Reggie without anything in front of his face. Reggie can see him properly for a moment, a man of fifty-seven with a tremor and a debt in Miami and a letter going to Kingston in the morning post. He's won the only hand he's been let win in fifteen years. Somewhere underneath the winning, Reggie thinks, he knows exactly what he's won, and what it cost, and who paid.

"You're a strange one, Doctor," says Desmond.

"So I'm told."

"I'd have hated you, if you'd been jealous. I tried very hard to make you." He shakes his head. "You'd not play."

"No," says Reggie. "I'm sorry. There wasn't anything to play *with*." He hesitates, and then says it, because it's true, and it costs him nothing. "You loved her. I'd have thought less of you if you hadn't. Anybody would love her. You were just the first."

Desmond doesn't say anything at all. After a moment he puts his hat on, very carefully, at the angle, and looks back out at the head.

The boat comes round it at twenty past eleven.

---

Win comes down the harbour steps with a parcel in brown paper tied with string, just as Alleyne's boy throws the line. She gives it to Desmond without a word. He looks at it.

"Winnie, you've not —"

"It's lunch." She says it very firmly. "Chicken, and bread, and two pieces of the guava cake. And a bottle of ginger beer. It's a long way to Kingston and you'll not eat on the boat." She folds her arms. "That's not paying, Des. That's lunch."

He looks at her for a long moment. Then he laughs, the real one, the one from before the voice, and bends down and kisses her on the cheek.

"Goodbye, Winnie."

"Goodbye, Des."

He gets into the boat. He sits in the stern with his case and his hat and his parcel on his knee, the way he sat when he came, and Alleyne's boy casts off. He doesn't look back. He sits with his face to the head and his shoulders square, and the boat goes out through the harbour mouth into the bright water.

Reggie stands on the wall beside Win and watches it go. She doesn't take his arm. She stands with her own arms folded, very straight, watching, the way she watched the end of the row for fifteen years.

At the head, just before it goes round, Desmond turns and lifts his hat.

Then it's gone.

Win lets out a long breath. She doesn't cry. He'd thought she might, and she doesn't. She stands there on the harbour wall in the noon sun with her arms folded, looking at the empty water. Then, very quietly, so quietly he almost doesn't catch it, she begins to hum.

It's low. It sits low, where her voice goes when she's tired. It's a tune he knows, because he heard it come up through two floors of Harbour House on a Monday night. She hums the first line of it, and the second, not for him and not for anybody, looking at the place where the boat went round the head.

Contralto. He'd guessed that.

He doesn't say anything. He stands beside her on the wall and listens, and she gets to the end of the first verse, and stops.

"You're not to tell anybody I did that," says Win.

"No."

"Not Marguerite. Not Pascual. *Especially* not Pascual."

"No."

She takes his arm then. They turn and go up the harbour steps together, at his pace, towards the house.

"The minister does Saturdays," he says, at the top.

Win stops on the step.

"Subject," she says, "to the usual formalities."

"Subject to the usual formalities. I'll wait." He looks at her. "I'm told I'm good at it. I waited nine weeks and a day on your verandah."

"You *did*," says Win, and begins to laugh.

She's still laughing when they get to the top of the road, by the harbour office, where the Kilburns are mending nets and pretending not to watch. She stops there. She doesn't let go of his arm.

"Reggie," she says. "About last night."

He looks at her. She watches him remember. She watches it go right across his face, and his ears go red, which she didn't know they could.

"I said later," says Win. "When he'd gone."

"You did."

"Well." She looks at the empty water beyond the harbour mouth, and back at him. "He's gone."

For a moment he doesn't say anything at all. Then he looks at his watch. It's twenty-five past eleven in the morning. The Kilburns are ten feet away. There are two girls turning the rooms at Harbour House, and the laundry's due, and the kitchen boy's somewhere about with the provisions list.

"*Win*," he says, in a voice she's never heard him use in public.

"Tonight," says Win, very serenely. "I've the laundry this afternoon, and the rooms, and the Ministry's bill to do. You'll stay. Jean can spare you." She pats his arm. "You're good at waiting. You said."

She walks on up the road towards the house. After a moment she hears him come after her, not fast, because of his chest. When he catches her up he doesn't say anything. He just walks beside her, very straight, with his ears still red. And every time she glances at him he's looking at the side of her face with an expression she's going to think about all afternoon, over the laundry.

Behind them, one of the Kilburns laughs out loud, and gets a net-needle thrown at him by his mother.
