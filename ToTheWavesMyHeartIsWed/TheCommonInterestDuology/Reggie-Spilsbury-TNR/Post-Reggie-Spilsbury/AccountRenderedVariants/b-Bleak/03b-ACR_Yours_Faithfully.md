# Yours Faithfully

The first thing is that he doesn't sleep.

He'd say he did, if anybody asked. Nobody asks. He lies in the hut at the end of the row with the lamp lit and his hands behind his head, and at some point it's light. On the bad nights he gets up at about three and goes down to the north pen and sits on the rail, and comes back up at six and shaves and goes to the long room. Twice in April he doesn't go back to the hut at all. He sleeps for an hour on the camp bed at the back of the long room in his clothes, and tells Jean, when she finds him, that he'd been watching a run through the night. It's true. He had been.

He can't be in the bed. That's the thing he'd tell nobody on earth. It's his bed, and since the end of last May it's been theirs, and it's the width it's always been. Iris Greaves stripped one side of it on the first Monday and the other on the second, and it still smells of tobacco, and it's the wrong width. He lies on his own side of it, from habit, and there's nothing on the other side, and that's worse, somehow, than anything he'd said in the office.

Pascual has started filling the lamp for him on Saturdays without being asked, the way he'd top up a trough, and neither of them mentions it.

The second thing is that the work is good.

It's better than good. He does it the way he's done it since the Labs, when he'd work until two and eat whatever had been left on the end of the bench and not notice either. It turns out that's still available to him, and that it's the one place he can stand for eight hours at a time. The paper goes to the printer at the end of April with the decline in figure one and two names at the top, and the pairing condition goes into the schedule in May in Hester's words, and the calf comes off her dressings and starts taking fish from the hand. In June the Colonial Office writes to say that the Institute's licence is renewed for three years without further condition. Hester reads it out at breakfast and then goes and stands on the verandah for a while by herself.

What people notice, if they notice anything, is that the Director has gone quiet. He doesn't talk at dinner the way he did. In May he snaps at a student over a mislabelled jar in front of the whole long room. An hour later he goes and finds the boy at the pens and apologises, carefully and at length, and the boy is so astonished that he tells everybody, which is how Hester hears. Hester notices that he's stopped finishing sentences in the office, and starts finishing them for him on her pad without looking up.

Pascual doesn't notice anything. Pascual knows. He puts a plate in front of him at breakfast and stands there.

And some time in August, without any warning at all, it lets go. He lies down on a Thursday meaning to read and wakes at six with the lamp still burning and the book on the floor, and lies there for a while, astonished, the way you'd be astonished to find a tooth had stopped aching. After that it's most nights. By the end of August he'd say, if anybody asked, that he's all right. He's Director of Research of a licensed station with a paper out and a calf taking fish, and on paper this is the best year he's ever had. He'd be very clear that he isn't unhappy. It would be very nearly true.

He has one thing to watch himself about, and he knows it by June. It's Alec's name.

It isn't the name as such. He can read it at the top of a page, and he can say *Spilsbury's sections* at the bench to Jean without anything happening at all. It's the name when it comes at him from the side, from somebody who doesn't know, in a friendly voice, as a thing to talk about. Then something happens that he doesn't decide. He can feel it the way you'd feel a boat come off a wave: the lift in the chest and the face settling, and the warmth. Ten minutes later the person who said the name is talking about their own boat, or their wife, or the price of cod, and thinks Dr Tar the most charming man in the colony, and doesn't know what's been done to them.

He watches it happen the first time, at the end of May, with a man from the Fisheries Department at dinner who says *your co-author, the tall fellow — what's become of him?* He watches the whole of it from about a foot behind his own eyes, and he can't stop it. That's what he learns. He'd thought, for thirty years, that the coat was a thing he put on. It turns out that on this one subject it puts itself on, and all he can do is stand inside it and wait for it to finish.

He finds that he doesn't mind that as much as he should.

---

The offprints come on the second Tuesday in June, a stiff brown packet of a hundred.

He opens it in the long room at four with Jean and Pascual there, because that's what you do with offprints, and they each take one and turn it over. Jean says the plates have come up better than she'd feared, and Pascual reads the acknowledgements twice and goes a bit red, because his name is in them for the pen drawings. Reggie reads the title and the names and the address of the Institute underneath, and then he turns to figure one and looks at the curve for a while.

Fifty-one days. He'd drawn the axes himself.

"Fifty for the Institute," Hester says from the door. She has the packing list. "And fifty to the first author, under the terms. He'll want them sent to Ålesund. Shall I do the label?"

"I'll do it."

He does it at his own desk in the office, with the door open, in the square capitals he uses for specimen labels, because it's a specimen label and there's no reason to use anything else.

*DR A. SPILSBURY*
*STATENS HAVFORSKNINGSSTASJON*
*ÅLESUND*
*NORWAY*

He has to look up where the ring goes on the *Å*. He's never written it before. He puts it in and looks at it, and then he ties the string round the packet himself with a reef knot and gives it to Hester, and goes down to the north pen for the evening feed a quarter of an hour early.

---

The first letter comes in September.

Hester had set the terms on the jetty. The depositor is to write to the Institute in March and September and state where he is, and the Institute is to acknowledge. She'd made it a condition of custody, and Mr Purdie had read it aloud, and it's in the deed. Letters from the depositor are to be addressed to the Director of Research, as custodian. Reggie has read that clause perhaps a dozen times since March and has never once been able to decide whether Hester did it on purpose.

It comes up from the Bay in the ordinary post. Hester opens the Institute's post, as she has done since 1951, and she brings it to his desk opened and flat, with the envelope pinned to the back.

"The depositor," she says, "as required," and goes out, and shuts the door behind her, which she does not generally do.

> *Statens Havforskningsstasjon*
> *Ålesund*
> *2nd September 1957*
>
> *The Director of Research*
> *William Martin Institute for Marine Studies*
>
> *Dear Sir,*
>
> *In accordance with the terms of deposit, I write to inform the Institute that the depositor of accession 197 is resident at the above address and may be reached there.*
>
> *I have received the offprints, for which I am obliged.*
>
> *Yours faithfully,*
>
> *A. Spilsbury*

He reads it twice.

He's had letters from this man every third Tuesday for six years, on fixative paper, in the form they'd worked out in 1950 so that anybody could read them and nobody would. He could read those. He knew where to look. A sentence about the rate of penetration of formalin into a block was about formalin, and was accurate to the hour, and he could read three other things off it as well. The weather in the last line was the weather, and it was also Alec at a window, telling him what he was looking at.

This is a letter to an institution. It's in the form you use to an institution, and there's nothing in the form to read, because the form is the same for everybody. *Dear Sir* is what you write to a Director of Research. *Yours faithfully* is what you close with when you've begun *Dear Sir*. It's in any handbook of correspondence. The whole thing could have come from a man he's never met.

He reads it a third time anyway, looking for anything, the way he'd look at a tank for an animal he knows is in there, and there is nothing, because Alec has made quite certain there's nothing. That's the point. He understands the point completely.

The handwriting's the same.

He sits with it for a while. Then he takes a sheet of the Institute's paper out of the drawer and writes the acknowledgement himself, by hand, in the form.

> *Dear Sir,*
>
> *I acknowledge with thanks your letter of the 2nd instant, and have noted the address.*
>
> *Yours faithfully,*
>
> *R. Tar*
> *Director of Research*

It takes him a quarter of an hour. Most of it is the signature, which he writes four times on the blotter before he writes it on the sheet, because he finds his hand wants to put the *R.* on its own, with the full stop, the way it has gone at the bottom of every letter he's sent that man since 1950, and he has to make it add the rest.

He takes it through to Hester for the post.

She reads it, because she reads everything that goes out. It takes her about four seconds. She folds it in three and puts it in the envelope and writes the address in her own hand, not looking at him, and says, "The Tuesday boat," and he says, "Yes."

---

That night he doesn't sleep, which is the first time since August.

He lies in the hut at the end of the row with the lamp lit and his hands behind his head and does something he's been very careful not to do for six months, which is think about it in order.

He thinks about the Monday boat and the bollard. He thinks about the office and the voice, and the corner of his own mouth going up. He thinks about Frank, who is somewhere. The Institute has a microscope in the long room with an eyepiece that came from Ashby's of Southport in 1955. He'd ordered it himself. It has the firm's name on the barrel in small capitals, and he has looked through it every working day for two years without once reading the name, and in June he read it, and now he can't not.

He thinks about the spare hut with the wrong roof. Hester has had the pitch put right over the summer, and Iris Greaves makes the bed up on Mondays for visiting workers.

And then he thinks, very clearly, the thing he has been not thinking since March, which is that he did it. Alec did the sum and handed it to him, and he took it and did it in about a second in a chair in the office, and he'd do it again. That's the thing he can't get round. If Halloran came up the path tomorrow and asked him again with Pascual by the window, he'd do it again, in the voice, and get the same result, and Frank would still be somewhere.

At about four he gets up and goes down to the north pen and sits on the rail in the dark and watches the calf come up for air every forty seconds, and counts it, because it's a thing he can count.

---

Pascual has learnt the pattern by the spring of 1958, and so has Hester, and it takes everybody else about two years.

It goes like this. Somebody says the name — a visitor, generally, or one of the Colonel's friends at dinner, or a new student who has read the paper and is excited about it — and says it the way you'd say any name, as a thing to talk about. And Reggie's shoulders come down about half an inch, and his face opens, and his voice goes warm. Then for about ten minutes he's the most charming man on the island. It's a rare, delightful thing, and Pascual would enjoy it a great deal if he didn't know what it was. At the end of the ten minutes the subject has gone, and nobody in the room could tell you where. The man who said the name goes away delighted.

The first time Pascual really watches it all the way through is in March 1958, at dinner, with a man called Allerton from the Plymouth laboratory. Allerton is a nice man with a beard who has come out for a fortnight to look at the pairing figures. On the second night he puts his fork down and says, *I must say, Tar, that decline curve of Spilsbury's is the most remarkable thing I've read in ten years — is he still in Norway? Odd sort of chap, of course. I met him once at the Linnean, before the war, and he told me my paper was wrong in front of about forty people, and it was.*

And Pascual watches Reggie lean back.

Across the table Hester's hand stops on its way to the water jug, just for a second, and goes on.

Reggie laughs. It's a good laugh, easy, from the chest. "*Before* the war," he says. "That's nothing. He did it to me in '46 in a corridor, about a curve I'd been up two nights on, and I went and did it again and he was right. I think you'll find half the men at the Linnean have a story like that. Were you at the Linnean long? You'll know old Hartley, then. Is it true he keeps a skate in his bath?"

And it's done. Pascual watches the name go down like a stone into the conversation and not come up. Allerton talks about Hartley's bath for a quarter of an hour, and about his own boat after that, and about his daughter at Girton, and goes up to bed at half past ten saying what a delightful evening. Reggie sits at the end of the table with his glass and smiles at the door after him until it's shut.

Then his shoulders come up the half-inch they came down, and he's just a tired man at a table.

Hester gets up and starts clearing, which is not her job and which she does not do. Pascual watches her carry three plates to the sideboard with her back very straight.

---

He tries once. Only once. He's thought about whether to for a month, and he decides in the end that a man who never tries at all isn't being kind, he's being a coward about it, and Pascual Pobil has been a great many things but he'd like not to be that.

He does it at the north pen at the evening feed in April, when there's nobody about but the animals, and he does it the plainest way he knows.

"You do a thing when his name comes up."

Reggie's got the bucket. He doesn't stop throwing. He doesn't look round.

"Do I?"

And there it is. Pascual hears it arrive, the lamp being turned up behind the voice. Reggie's doing it to him. He's got his back to him at the rail, and he's about to be charming about the weather.

"Don't," Pascual says. "Not with me, *hombre*. I told you that the morning after."

The bucket stops.

Reggie stands at the rail with it hanging from his hand. For a moment Pascual can see him try to take it off, the way you'd see a man try to get a wet jersey over his head, and not manage.

"Please don't, Pascual," Reggie says.

It isn't the voice. It isn't anything. It's a man asking.

Pascual looks at the back of his neck for a long time, where the hair wants cutting again.

"All right," he says.

"I'm sorry."

"No. It's all right. I heard you." He picks up the other bucket, because the cow is looking at him, and throws her a fish, and she takes it. "But if it ever starts coming on when I'm the one saying it, I'll say it anyway, and you'll have to put up with me. You understand? It's not a bargain. It's a warning."

"Understood."

"Good."

They finish the feed. They don't talk about it again, and it doesn't come on with Pascual, and after a while Pascual understands that that's the arrangement. Reggie will let him see it and won't let him touch it, and that's the most there is.

---

He has his own reasons for leaving it alone, and he's honest with himself about them, because there's nobody else to be honest with.

He was in love with this man for about four years. It started in the spring of 1953. He knows the date roughly, because it was a Tuesday feed and Reggie had laughed at something he'd said about Alpha and he'd gone back up to his hut and sat on the bed and said *ay, no* to the wall. Reggie had noticed something before that, in the first six months, a new man's crush on the Director, and had been decent about it, and had decided some time in 1953 that it had passed. Pascual had let him. He's told nobody else, ever.

It ended while Spilsbury was on the island, which he hadn't expected. He'd expected that to be the worst of it. For nine months he watched the two of them at the bench and at the long table and coming up the row at night, and he saw what Reggie looked like when he was being given what he wanted. Pascual saw, quite plainly, that it wasn't anything he'd have known how to give. Some time around Christmas he noticed that he'd stopped minding, the way you notice a noise has stopped. What was left was what's there now, which he doesn't have a name for and doesn't need one.

So when the man went to Norway and Reggie was left on the other side of the wall on his own, Pascual looked at that squarely, once, to make sure, and found nothing there but the thing without a name. He was glad. A man who has lost a thing that size doesn't have room in him for anybody else's arithmetic, and Pascual would rather be shot than be the kind of man who counts it. Reggie comes down to the north pen at four in the morning and counts the calf's breathing. It's the only thing Pascual has ever seen him do that he'd call praying.

And there's the other thing, which he's worked out slowly, over a year, in the way he works things out.

Nobody has ever told him anything.

That's not like Reggie. Reggie tells him things. Reggie told him about Alpha the week he arrived, and about Beta, and a good deal more about England than Pascual had wanted to hear. Reggie tells him what he thinks of his pen drawings whether he wants to hear it or not. And on the morning after the solicitors' letter, with the buckets, Reggie had said *I can't tell you. Not won't. Can't,* and had meant it. Pascual has turned that over a great deal since.

A man doesn't say *can't* about his own business. He says it when the knowledge is dangerous to the man he's not telling.

Pascual doesn't know what it was. He'd been by the window in the office with a veterinary schedule on his knee, and the inspector had asked his question, and Reggie had said his sentence, and the inspector had written it down and said *that'll do*. And the inspector had not asked Pascual anything at all, which Pascual had put down at the time to his being the vet.

He doesn't put it down to that any more. He can't prove anything else, and he isn't going to try, because there's only one man who could tell him and that man has asked him, plainly, not to.

He does what he can do instead. He fills the lamp on Saturdays. He puts a plate in front of Reggie at breakfast when Reggie's come up from the pen without eating, which is about three mornings a week, and stands there until something's gone off it. In the summer of 1958 Reggie does four nights on the tank drawings for the new pen without going to bed, and on the fifth night Pascual walks into the long room at midnight and takes the pen out of his hand and says *no*. Reggie looks at him for a while and then goes to bed. That still works. It's always worked, since 1954, over the work, and it goes on working, and Pascual is more grateful for that than he'd be able to explain to anybody.

It's the other thing that doesn't. For that he's got nothing but a lamp and a plate.

And he watches the stairs. He's noticed, the way you'd notice a limp, that Reggie takes the verandah steps one at a time now. He used to take them two at a time when something had gone well. Pascual had never noticed that he did it until he stopped.

The March letter comes in 1960, and it's different.

Reggie knows it the moment Hester puts it on the desk. He knows because she doesn't go out.

She stands by the desk with her pad against her chest, and says, "He's moved. From the first of April. And he's put a line in that I can't account for. I've read it three times." She sounds almost cross. "It isn't a thing the Institute requires."

"Thank you, Hester."

She goes, then. She doesn't shut the door. He gets up and shuts it himself.

> *Statens Havforskningsstasjon*
> *Ålesund*
> *3rd March 1960*
>
> *The Director of Research*
> *William Martin Institute for Marine Studies*
>
> *Dear Sir,*
>
> *In accordance with the terms of deposit, I write to inform the Institute that from the 1st April the depositor will be resident at Kongens gate 18, Ålesund, the station's accommodation being required for a new appointment. Letters addressed to the station will continue to reach me.*
>
> *The sound was at four degrees this morning.*
>
> *Yours faithfully,*
>
> *A. Spilsbury*

He reads it once. He doesn't need to read it twice, and he does anyway, and then he puts it down flat on the desk and puts both his hands on either side of it, and sits.

*Four degrees.*

It's November 1946 and he's twenty-seven. He's coming up the stairs of the Labs two at a time with a clipboard, because the third subject's run is clean all the way down, and the whole building is at four degrees because the boiler's gone. Alec's standing by the fuse box in his overcoat with a card of wire in his fingers, and he's had a thermometer on his bench all evening, reading it on the hour, out of interest.

Alec has written thirty years of notebooks, and burnt two of them, and written five Institute letters since without one word in any of them that a clerk couldn't file. And this morning, in a cold town on a sound, he's gone down to the water with a thermometer, out of interest, and read it, and got four. He's written it down, and there was nowhere to write it but here.

It's the loudest thing anyone has ever written to Reggie in his life.

He sits at the desk with his hands flat. After a while he notices that they're shaking, and that he can't do anything about that either.

He can't answer it. He knows that as clearly as he knows the figure. Anything he writes back that isn't the form is a line, in his own hand, from the Director of Research of a licensed station to a man with *Spilsbury and Nicholson* in a drawer in Bedford Row and in a Colonial Office file that gets read every three years, with Reggie's sentence in it, by whoever the inspector is. Frank is somewhere because of that file. Reggie said what he said, in the voice, because of that file. If he answers this, all of it will have been for nothing, and it'll have been his hand that made it nothing.

Alec knows that. Alec knew it when he wrote it. Alec, who doesn't make errors of that kind, has written him one line that he knew perfectly well couldn't be answered, because he had nowhere else to put it.

Reggie takes a sheet of the Institute's paper and rolls it into Hester's typewriter, which is on the side table, because for some reason he can't do this one by hand.

> *Dear Sir,*
>
> *I acknowledge with thanks your letter of the 3rd instant, and have noted the change of address.*

He sits with his fingers on the keys.

The water off the jetty this morning was twenty-six degrees. He'd taken it himself at the feed, because he takes it every morning at the feed. It's on the sheet in the long room in his own hand.

He could put it in. Six words. *The water here was twenty-six.* Nobody on earth but one man would know what it meant, and Hester would read it and think the Director had caught the depositor's disease and say nothing, and it would go on the Tuesday boat.

He doesn't. He types the close.

> *Yours faithfully,*

And signs it, *R. Tar, Director of Research,* on the fourth attempt, and takes it through to Hester, who reads it in four seconds and folds it in three. She doesn't look at him. Then she does.

"Is that all?" she says.

It's the only question she's ever asked him about any of it.

"That's all it needs," says Reggie.

Hester looks at him a moment longer. Then she addresses the envelope in her own hand, and puts it on the pile for the Tuesday boat, and says, in exactly the voice she uses for the fish accounts, "I have always thought four degrees an extremely unpleasant temperature. I can't imagine why anybody would go and stand in it," and goes back to her pad.

Reggie goes down to the north pen and stands at the rail for an hour with no bucket.

The first thing Marguerite Sealy notices about Dr Tar is that he's lovely to her.

She's been waiting for this for a fortnight, and so, she gathers, has Pascual. He'd said so twice, in the tone of a man who has thought about it properly: *he'll be clever at you, and you'll think he's laughing at you.* It's November 1960. She's on the verandah of the house on the cay with a glass of lemonade, and Pascual is standing between the two chairs like a man waiting for a shell to land.

It isn't that at all. It takes about four minutes. She asks Dr Tar whether it's a habit of Pascual's to walk around for five days with a hand like a bag of pus and tell the doctor he's been busy. Dr Tar says *every time*, and starts on the notes — a torn calf in 1954, a tooth in 1957, a dressing gown on a jetty in 1959. She laughs out loud, and Pascual stands on his own verandah with his ears scarlet for an hour and twenty minutes and doesn't leave and doesn't want them stopped.

That's the first thing. The second thing she notices is at about a quarter to six, when the light has gone gold and she has had two glasses of Hester Bevan's lemonade and is enjoying herself more than she has for a year.

She says, because it's in her head, and because Pascual has talked about it on the boat — "Pascual says the best anatomist he ever worked beside sat at your bench. A Dr Spilsbury. He says he cut sections like a watchmaker. Did you train with him?"

Pascual goes still. She sees that first, from the corner of her eye.

And then she sees Dr Tar lean back in his chair, and every muscle in his face go easy at once. She's been a doctor since 1940, and she knows what it looks like when the pulse goes up in a man who's decided it isn't going to show.

"Train with him? God, no. He'd have eaten me. He was down the corridor and about seven years ahead and he used to correct my curves in the corridor, in pencil, on the backs of envelopes, and his sevens were appalling, you've never seen anything like them. Did Pascual tell you about the time he tried to strap his own ankle on the reef with a length of the Colonel's fishing line? No? Pascual. You didn't tell her about the fishing line?"

"There was no fishing line," says Pascual, "*ay*, Reggie —"

"There was a great deal of fishing line."

And off it goes, down the verandah and away, a lovely, funny story about a sprained ankle in 1955 and the Colonel's best trout cast, and Marguerite laughs in all the right places, because it is funny, and watches him. She watches his hands, which have gone flat on the arms of the chair, and his breathing, which is a little high in the chest. And after ten minutes by her watch, she sees the whole thing let go of him, all at once. He looks down at his glass as though he's surprised to find it there.

The name has gone. She'd have been hard put to say where. She'd said it, and ten minutes later it was nowhere, and she had been laughing.

She's seen that done before. She's seen it done in her surgery by women who come in with a bruise on the arm that they don't mention and a great deal to say about feeling run down, and who go out with a bottle of tonic, because a tonic is what they came in to be given. She's seen it done by men of a certain age who are there about their chests and would very much rather tell her about their runner beans than where the pain goes. She gets the proper thing out of rather more of them than most doctors would. She does it by making them come back for the tonic every week, and asking about the beans until there aren't any beans left to talk about. She doesn't get it out of all of them. She's never once seen it done so well. And she's never seen it done by a man who, when it was over, looked as though it had cost him a pint of blood.

"Don't ask him about that," Pascual says on the boat.

It's dark, and Alleyne's boy is at the tiller and can't hear over the engine, and Pascual is sitting beside her on the thwart with his elbows on his knees.

"I didn't ask him about that. I asked him about a microtome."

"I know. I know you did. It's my fault, I should've said." He rubs his face. "I should've told you on the way over. Everybody learns it in the end. I just — I hadn't thought you'd get there in an afternoon."

"Get where?"

He's quiet for a while. She lets him be. In the eight months she's known him she's learnt that he'll come to a thing in his own time and from a direction you wouldn't have picked, and that if you try to hurry him he'll go round again.

"There was a man," Pascual says eventually. "Here, on the island, for nine or ten months. Spilsbury. Before that, in England, for years. I don't know how many. More than I thought, I think." He looks at the water. "He went to Norway in the spring of '57. There was a business with some solicitors, and the licence, and an inspector. And Reggie said something about him to the inspector, in the office, in front of me and Hester, which was true, and which kept the licence, and which I'd have cut my tongue out before I said. And the man went, and Reggie hasn't —"

He stops.

"That's all I know. It's more than I should say. You're a doctor, so I'm saying it."

Marguerite sits beside him and thinks about the verandah. She thinks about a pint of blood.

"He's not unhappy," she says slowly. "That's what's odd. He isn't. I'd put money on it. He likes his work, and he likes you — God, he likes you, Pascual, he lit up like a lamp every time you opened your mouth — and he's very funny, and he eats, more or less, and he sleeps, more or less. I'd have signed him fit this afternoon on everything I saw."

"Yes."

"And then I said a man's name and he nearly fainted in a deckchair and made me laugh for ten minutes so I wouldn't see."

"Yes."

"Does he write to him?"

"The man writes to the Institute. Twice a year. Where he is. Hester makes him." Pascual's mouth goes sideways. "Reggie answers. *Dear Sir*. Hester showed me one, once, by accident, when she was filing. She didn't mean to. Or she did. You never know, with Hester."

Marguerite looks at the cay going away behind them in the dark. There are lamps in the row, three or four of them, and one at the far end, by itself, a little brighter than the others.

She'd been told the other thing in June. Pascual had told her on her own verandah, after supper, with the plates still on the table, before he had told her anything else of any importance: that he'd been in love with Reggie Tar for four years, from 1953, and that it had stopped in the winter of 1956, before any of the rest of it, while the other man was still on the island, and that she ought to know before she decided anything about him. He'd been scarlet to the collar and he hadn't looked away once.

She'd decided a good deal about him that evening, in fact, though she took another eight months to say so.

She doesn't raise it now. She puts her hand on his knee on the thwart and leaves it there, and he puts his own over it, and they watch the lamp at the end of the row go round the head.

---

They're married on the fourteenth of July 1962, in the morning, in the Anglican church at the Bay, which is small and hot and full.

Marguerite had not intended to cry and doesn't, very nearly. Mr Purdie gives her away and gets through it. Jean Leslie is on the Menai Strait with three students and a tide, and has sent flowers of such dimensions that Alleyne's boy had to make a second crossing. Hester cries from the second hymn, upright, without a sound. And Pascual Pobil stands at the front of the church in a grey suit with his hair flattened and can't stop his face doing what it's doing, and at his shoulder, with the ring in his waistcoat pocket, stands Reggie Tar. He looks, Marguerite thinks, coming up the aisle, like a man who is watching the best thing he has ever been allowed to see.

He hands over the ring without fumbling. He's the only person in the church who doesn't cry. He's too busy looking at Pascual.

---

At the schoolroom afterwards there is a long table and a man with a guitar, and the students get at the rum, and the best man is asked to speak.

He does it beautifully. Marguerite has been expecting that, and it's still better than she'd expected. He doesn't use a note. He tells ninety people how he met Pascual in November 1952 and understood inside two days that he'd been sent a man who'd tell him he was wrong, in public, to his face, once a week for the rest of his life. Pascual says *twice*, and Reggie says *twice a week*, and the room laughs. He tells them Pascual was right about an animal called Alpha on his second night on the island. He tells them he has watched that man work for nine and a half years and never once seen him choose the easy thing over the correct one.

Then he turns his glass round on the tablecloth.

"And I want to say, in front of his wife and her family and everybody on this island," says Reggie, "that whatever anybody else here thinks they know about him, I have had the best of him, and I've had it for nine years, and I'm handing it over today with extremely bad grace."

The room roars.

And Marguerite, at the top of the table, in her good blue, with her husband's hand gone very still on the cloth beside hers, looks at her husband's face.

She has known what she'll see there since June 1960. She sees it anyway. There's no pain in it. That was done with years before she met him, and she believes him about that, and she's watched him closely enough in two years to know. But there's something else, something she hadn't expected, and it takes her a moment to name. It's tenderness, for something that's over. A man hearing, at his own wedding, in front of ninety people, the whole of what he'd wanted for four years, handed over in public like a toast by a man who has no idea he's done it.

Reggie doesn't know. She watches him not know. He's standing up at the end of the table, delighted, flushed, with his glass up, absolutely unaware that there's anything in that sentence but what he put in it. He's saying she's far too good for Pascual and he'd like it minuted that he isn't being funny. He's telling the young men present to write down that she took fourteen months to decide. And not one muscle of his face has any idea.

He'd read her in four minutes on a verandah in 1960. He could read anybody, she's since learned, if he were pointed at them. He'd never once been pointed at Pascual.

Under the table she turns her hand over and takes hold of Pascual's, hard, and after a second he holds on back.

"Two people," Reggie is saying, "who've each been entirely formidable on their own for twenty years, and who are going to be a very great deal better than that together," and lifts his glass. Everybody stands, and Pascual gets up and goes round the table and hugs him in front of the whole schoolroom, in a way that would be entirely unremarkable in a man from Zaragoza at his own wedding, and says something in Spanish into his shoulder.

Reggie laughs and holds on and says, "*Yes*, all right, all *right*, you great —" and doesn't let go for rather longer than he means to.

---

It's Mr Purdie who does it, at about four, when the guitar has got louder and the cake is gone.

He's seventy-six and has known her since she was born, and he's had two glasses of the Colonel's champagne and has been placed next to the best man at the far end. Marguerite, going down the table to thank the Colonel for the champagne, hears him say it in his careful, courteous, courtroom voice.

"And how is Dr Spilsbury, Dr Tar? I have thought of him a good deal. He came to me with that deposit, you know, in 'fifty-seven, and asked me to read every word aloud, and sat through the whole of it. Every word. I have never had a client do that. I was very much struck by him. Do you hear from him at all?"

Marguerite stops beside the Colonel's chair with her hand on the back of it.

She watches it happen for the second time in her life, and this time she's ready for it, and knows what she's looking at. The shoulders come down half an inch. The face opens. The voice goes warm and easy and delightful, and there's a lamp behind it.

"Every March and every September, Mr Purdie, like clockwork, under the terms of that deed, and I'll tell you a thing about that deed that'll amuse you — did you know the Colonial Office sent a man out last year to see whether our custody met the standard? A man in a hat, from Kingston. And he read the whole of your deed and said he'd never seen one drafted so well in the colony, and asked who'd done it, and Hester told him, and he wrote your name down in his notebook. I thought you ought to know. You're famous in the West India Department."

"Good heavens," says Mr Purdie, going pink.

"Oh, entirely. Now — you'll know this, you know everything — is it true the old sugar wharf at the Bay was built with ballast stone from Bristol? Because Pascual swears it was, and I say it was Liverpool, and there's five shillings on it."

And Mr Purdie, who has been a solicitor for fifty years and knows the history of every stone at the Bay, puts his glass down and begins.

Marguerite looks at her watch.

Ten minutes, more or less, by the time Mr Purdie has got the wharf built and the five shillings awarded to Pascual. By then the name is nowhere. Then she watches the best man sit back in his chair and look at his own hands on the tablecloth for a moment, as though somebody had handed them to him, and then look up and find Pascual across the room, and smile. It's the plain smile. It's the one he'd had in the church.

She goes back up the table to her own chair and sits down and doesn't say anything to anybody for a little while.

She's a doctor. She'd say, if pressed, and professionally, that she's looking at a man who'd sign himself fit on every count and mean it, and who has one place in him that's been open for five years and that he walks round very carefully every day. He's walked round it so long that he's stopped knowing he does it. It's taking something out of him every time. She'd say he probably doesn't know how much.

She's been a wife since this morning, and she'd say something else, which is that she's going to be writing that man a great many letters.

---

They go back to her house above the harbour at ten, when the students have been put on the boat. It's his house now as well, as of this morning, though his books are still mostly on the cay, and will be for some time, because he won't be hurried.

She's on the verandah with her shoes off when he comes out with two glasses, and sits down beside her in the dark, and gives her one, and doesn't say anything. From her verandah you can see straight across the channel to the cay, a long low shape against the stars, and the lamps along the row.

Most of them go out as they sit there. The students' first, then Hester's, then the Colonel's.

One doesn't. The one at the far end, by itself.

"That's him," Pascual says.

"At this hour?"

"Most nights. Since the spring of '57." He turns his glass round. "He sleeps. I've looked. He goes out like a light with it lit and I go in on Saturdays and fill it. He doesn't put it out. I don't think he knows he doesn't."

Marguerite looks at the lamp for a while.

"He had the best of you for nine years," she says.

Pascual is very quiet beside her.

"He did," he says eventually. "He still has the best of me, *querida*. It's just a different best." He looks at the light across the water. "And he didn't know what he was saying. He'd have been mortified. He'd have gone white. I'm glad he didn't know."

"So am I."

"You heard it, though."

"I heard it."

"I thought you would," says Pascual, and takes her hand, and they sit on her verandah, on their wedding night, and look across the water at a man neither of them can do anything for yet, until the lamp at the end of the row is the only light on the cay.
