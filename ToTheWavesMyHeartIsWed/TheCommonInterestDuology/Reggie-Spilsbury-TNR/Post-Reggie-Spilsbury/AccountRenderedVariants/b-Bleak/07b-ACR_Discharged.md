# Discharged

John Weald comes back from home leave at the end of October with his wife and his child and a crate of photographic paper. On the first afternoon Reggie walks into the darkroom without knocking, which nobody on the station has done since 1962, and shuts the door behind him, and stands in the red light.

"Did you see him?" he says.

John is at the enlarger with a strip of negatives in his fingers. He puts it down very carefully on the bench before he turns round.

"Yes."

Reggie had known. He had known since September, when Ferris mentioned the symposium at dinner and John's home leave came into his head a second later. He'd spent six weeks not asking, and had found on the jetty this afternoon, watching John hand the child up the steps, that he was going to ask.

"How did he look?"

John thinks about it. He didn't, as a boy; he used to say the first thing that came into his head and go scarlet afterwards. He's learned since, somewhere between the darkroom and Nora, to stop and think before he speaks, and Reggie has watched him learn it and likes him for it more than for almost anything.

"Like himself," he says. "Older. He gave a good paper. There was a woman from Aberdeen who corrected him from the floor, twice, and he thanked her both times, and she went red." He hesitates. "He asked me not to tell you I'd seen him. He said if you asked I was to tell you the truth, because I would, and so would you. But I wasn't to raise it."

"No," says Reggie. "He wouldn't want it raised."

He stands in the red light with his hands in his pockets.

"Did you give him anything?"

"The station," says John. "Photographs. Thirty-one. The jetty and the row and the long room and the calf." He looks at the negatives on the bench and then back. "And one of you, at the north pen, in July. I didn't mean to take it. You walked into the frame. I told him he could decide, and he decided."

Reggie is quiet for a while.

"Show me."

John finds the strip without looking and puts it in the enlarger, and runs off a print in about four minutes without saying a word, and rocks it in the tray, and hangs it up dripping on the line. Reggie stands and looks at it in the red light.

It's himself at the rail of the north pen in his shirtsleeves, with his glasses up on his forehead. He's side-on, thinner than he'd thought. His hand's flat on his chest, on the left, over the pocket.

He knows what's in the pocket. John doesn't. Alec won't have known either. Alec will have looked at that hand for a long time and read it the other way, as a man with a hand on a pain.

And Alec will have measured him. Reggie knows that as surely as he knows his own name. He'll have looked at the rail posts, which Halloran taped in 1957 while Alec stood by and watched, and taken the width of the shoulders off the print with his thumb. And he'll have got a figure. Somewhere in Norway there's a man who knows to within half a stone how much weight Reggie Tar has lost since 1957. He won't have written it down, because he's got nothing to write it in.

"He'll have measured me," Reggie says. "Off the posts."

John looks at him and doesn't understand, and doesn't ask.

"It's all right, John." Reggie puts out his hand and touches the edge of the wet print on the line, very lightly, and takes his hand away. "Thank you. You did right. You did exactly right."

---

For about a week after that he's a model patient.

He eats breakfast. He takes the little white tablet in the morning with his tea, where Pascual can see him do it. He takes one of the other sort before he goes down the verandah steps and before the north pen steps and before the jetty, the way Marguerite told him to, before the tightness and not after it. He goes to bed at ten. He lets a student row the tide gauge. On the Sunday at the Bay, Marguerite takes his pulse on the verandah, and looks at her watch, and then at him. She says *seventy-two* in a voice of the most profound suspicion, and asks Pascual what he's done to him, and Pascual says he hasn't done anything, he swears to God.

Reggie doesn't tell either of them why. It isn't the kind of reason you can tell anybody. A man in Norway had measured him off a photograph and got a figure he didn't like, and couldn't say so. So for a week Reggie did the only thing he could think of that would answer it.

Then there's a student with a question about an oxygen curve at midnight. And an animal that won't take fish. And a Tuesday he forgets breakfast because the calf's mother is off her food. And it goes the way it always goes, the way it's gone since the Labs, a little at a time and then all at once. By Christmas Marguerite is saying *eighty-four* on the verandah in the voice she uses for her worst patients, the ones she's fond of.

---

The last of the beans runs out in August 1971, on a Sunday, on the back verandah under the vine.

Marguerite has known for some time that it was coming. That's what the weekly tonic is for. You don't get to the thing by asking for it. You get to it by coming back every week and asking about everything else, until there's nothing else left to ask about and the patient is so used to being asked that he answers before he's noticed.

Pascual has gone down to the harbour with Hester to see a boat about something. The table's cleared. Reggie is in the cane chair with his feet on the rail, which she has told him not to do because of the rail. She's in the chair beside him with his wrist in her fingers, and she's just said *seventy-eight*, and he's said *there, you see*, and she has let go.

His hand goes to the pocket.

He does it every Sunday, perhaps six or seven times in an evening. She's counted. She has watched it go there for ten months and never once said a word about it, because it wasn't time.

"What's in your pocket, Reggie?" she says.

He doesn't take his hand away. He looks at it, the way he does, as though somebody had put it there.

"Pascual asked me that," he says. "Last year. On the step."

"I know he did. He told me you told him. He didn't tell me what you said." She puts her feet up on the rail next to his, which she has told him not to do. "He said you'd said it was all there was to it. He said he didn't believe you, and that he'd been told not to ask, and wasn't going to. He said it was a thing he'd promised you in 1958."

"He's kept it."

"He's kept it. He's a better man than I am. I haven't promised you anything."

Reggie laughs. It isn't the laugh from the table. It's a short one, down in his chest, and it catches at the end of it, and she notes that too and doesn't say so.

For a while he doesn't say anything at all. The harbour's going gold below the verandah, and the cay is a long low line across the channel with the row on it. Somebody down at the quay is singing.

Then he takes the letter out.

He doesn't hand it to her. He holds it in both hands on his knee. It's gone soft at the folds from sixteen months against a man's chest, the way paper goes, and a little brown at the edges, and there's a mark on it at one corner that she thinks is salt.

"He wrote to me," Reggie says. "In April last year. You know that. Pascual knows that. He said he'd be in London and he'd like to see me, and if I didn't answer he'd read it as an answer. So I didn't answer. And I've had it in my pocket ever since, and I don't know why, and I know exactly why. I can't burn it. He's the one who burns things. That was him, not me."

"No."

"I'm not going to show it to you. It's his."

"I didn't ask to see it."

"No. You asked what's in my pocket." He turns it over on his knee. "It's the only thing he's written me in thirteen years that wasn't *Dear Sir*."

Marguerite waits. She's got the whole evening. She's always got the whole evening, on Sundays; that's what Sundays are for.

"Pascual told you," says Reggie at last, "that I said something to an inspector in 1957."

"He told me that, the first time we talked about it. In a boat, in 1960. He said it was true and it kept the licence, and that he'd have cut his tongue out before he said it."

"He was in the room. By the window."

"Yes."

"He doesn't know why I said it."

She looks at him.

"He thinks it was the licence," says Reggie. "It was the licence. That's true. I've told him that and it was true when I said it. But it wasn't all of it, and he knows it wasn't, and he's never asked, because I asked him not to." He looks out across the channel at the cay. "The inspector asked whether I'd known about Alec when I brought him onto the station. If I'd said no, it was a lie. If I'd said yes, that was the licence. And if I'd said it was none of his business, he'd have written that down, and then he'd have looked round the room for somebody else to ask. And he did look. I watched him do it. He looked at the window, where there was a man with no wife whose name was on the licence under mine, who'd been my friend for four years, who lived the other side of my wall."

Marguerite sits very still in the cane chair.

"So I said it," says Reggie. "The true thing. In the voice. And he stopped looking at the window."

The singing down at the quay has stopped. There's a gull somewhere, and the sound of the water at the foot of the hill.

"He's never known," Marguerite says.

"No. And he's not going to. You're not going to tell him."

"Reggie —"

"He'd carry it." Reggie's voice is very quiet. "He'd carry it round for the rest of his life, and he'd never be able to put it down, because there'd be nothing he could do about it and nobody he could tell. I know exactly what that's like. I'm not doing that to him. I did the whole thing so he wouldn't have to be anywhere near it. It'd be a poor sort of return to hand it him now."

She thinks about that for a long time. She thinks about her husband on a thwart in the dark in 1960 saying *more than I should say*. She thinks about a man with his arm round another man's shoulders on a step, on a promise from 1958. She thinks about the wedding, and the best of him for nine years, and a man at the end of a table who hadn't known what he was saying.

He had known this, though. All of it. He'd known it for fourteen years and had never once, as far as she can tell, said it aloud to anyone.

"All right," she says. "It isn't mine to tell. While you're alive."

He turns his head.

"While you're alive it's yours, and I'll keep it," says Marguerite. "If you die, it's mine. And I'll tell him the same week, the whole of it, the window and all. So you'd better not."

"That's blackmail, Marguerite."

"It's medicine. Take your tablets."

He looks at her for a long moment in the gold light. Then he laughs, short, and shakes his head.

"All right," he says. "Thank you. I think."

"I want it on record that I think you're wrong."

"You generally do."

"I'm generally right." She reaches over and takes his wrist again, without asking, and he lets her. "That's three, by the way. That's three times I've been right about you. You said at my wedding it was two."

"I said I hadn't forgiven you for two."

"Well, now you can not forgive me for three."

He laughs properly then. She feels it under her fingers, and counts through it, and doesn't like the count, and doesn't say so.

He puts the letter back in the pocket, and buttons the jacket over it, and puts his feet back up on the rail, which she has told him not to do.

"Is it better?" she says. "Having said it."

He thinks about it. That's the thing she likes best about him, of all the things. He always does think about it, whatever you ask. He never just gives you the answer you'd like.

"Yes," he says. "A bit. I didn't think it would be. It's a bit better."

---

He goes out in the boat on the twelfth of February 1972, at two in the morning.

He knows he's not meant to. He's known it since the Monday in her surgery when she said *not one, not for any animal*. He's kept to it for sixteen months, more or less, which for him is a very long time. He's let the students row. He's stood on the jetty with a lantern and his tablets in his pocket and shouted instructions across the water, and hated every minute of it, and done it.

But it's February, and there's a norther, and the animal's on the reef off the second head. She's a young female, and the tide's going out from under her. The two students who come hammering on his door at a quarter to two are Ansell and Baptiste. They're twenty-three and twenty-two and have been on the island since October and have never seen a stranding in their lives. Ansell's white to the mouth, and Baptiste is trying not to cry. Jean's across at Kingston for the Board, and won't be back until Sunday. Pascual's at the Bay, and there's no crossing in this until it's light.

And the animal will be dead by light. Reggie knows that as well as he knows anything. If she dries out on the coral, her own weight will crush her lungs by about five, and there'll be nothing for anybody to do in the morning but come out in a boat and look.

"I'll stay in the boat," he says. "I'll tell you what to do and you'll do it. I'm not going in the water."

He means it when he says it. He's pulling his boots on while he says it. He takes a tablet on the jetty steps, before the tightness, the way he's been told, and gets into the boat.

---

The reef is a horror. It's black and loud, with the wind coming across it, and the spray, and the lantern's no use at all past about six feet. They find her by the sound of her breathing. It's a long harsh sound, too fast, and he can hear the fear in it, and he'd know it anywhere.

She's high on the coral, lying half on her side, with the water draining out from under her. There's perhaps two hours before the tide turns and there's nothing left to float her on.

He stays in the boat for about twenty minutes.

He tells them where to put the sling, and how, and they do it wrong. It isn't their fault. It's dark and they're frightened and they've never seen one before. And she rolls, the way they do when they're frightened, and the sling slips, and she's further up the coral than she was, and her breath has changed.

He goes over the side.

The water's to his waist and very cold for the Caribbean. He feels it take hold of his chest like a hand. He feels the other thing too, the tightness, coming up under the cold from the other direction. He ignores it, because there isn't time for it, and he puts both hands on her. She's so frightened, and he talks to her, the way Pascual talks to them, low and steady and without stopping, in English because he hasn't got any Spanish. He tells Ansell where to put his shoulder and Baptiste where to put the line, and they get the sling back under her properly.

And on the third heave she comes off.

She goes off the coral into the black water with a sound he's going to hear for — he's going to hear for a while. She lies there in the swell for a long moment, with the three of them holding on to the line, and breathes. The breathing changes. It goes long, and slow, and right.

Then she goes. She takes the sling with her, which is a waste of a good sling, and nobody cares. She goes out past the edge of the reef into the dark on the falling tide, and they hear her blow once more, a long way off, and then there's only the wind.

Ansell is laughing and crying at the same time. Baptiste is being sick over the side of the boat.

Reggie stands in the water to his waist, holding on to the gunwale, and can't get his breath.

---

They get him into the boat. He doesn't know how. He knows that Baptiste rows and Ansell holds him upright on the thwart with both arms round him, and that he's got the tablets out of his pocket somehow and one under his tongue, and that it isn't doing anything. The tightness has gone past tightness into something else. It's in his left arm, and his jaw, and a place behind his breastbone the size of a fist that he's never felt before and knows exactly what it is.

"I'm all right," he tells Ansell. "Row."

"You're not all right, sir."

"No," Reggie agrees. "I'm not. Row anyway."

At the jetty he gets out of the boat by himself. He insists on that. He gets up four steps and sits down.

He sits on the fourth step from the bottom with the lantern on the step beside him and his hand flat on his chest over the pocket. He sends Ansell running up the path to the office for the radio-telephone the Colonel put in in 1965. He says the Bay, and Dr Sealy, and to tell her it's Dr Tar and she'll know. And he tells Baptiste to sit down on the step beside him and stop being sick, because he's done very well and so has she.

"She went out, sir," says Baptiste. "Didn't she? She's all right?"

"She's all right."

"You got her off."

"You got her off. I just talked." He can hardly hear his own voice over the wind. "You did very well, Baptiste. Write it up in the morning. Every bit of it. Times. The sling. What went wrong the first time. Write it all down."

"Yes, sir."

"Somebody's got to write it down," says Reggie, and closes his eyes.

---

Pascual is in the boat across the channel inside twenty minutes of the call, in his trousers and a coat over his pyjamas, with Marguerite in the bow with her bag on her knees. Alleyne's son is at the tiller, taking the norther on the bow in a way his father would have called suicide and would have done himself.

Pascual doesn't say anything the whole way across. Nor does Marguerite. He holds on to the gunwale with both hands, and watches the cay come up out of the dark, and the lamps on it. The office is lit, and the students' huts, and down at the jetty a lantern.

And the one at the end of the row. That's lit too, as it's lit every night. He'd have gone out to the boat and left it burning, the way he always leaves it.

They carry him up to it.

It takes four of them, Pascual and Ansell and Baptiste and Alleyne's son, with Marguerite walking alongside with her hand on his wrist, up the path in the wind. He's conscious. He tells them to mind the third stone, which is loose, and to put him down if they're tired, and not to drop him because it'd be undignified. Pascual tells him to shut up, in Spanish, and Reggie says *I heard that*.

They put him on his own bed in the hut at the end of the row, under his own lamp.

Marguerite does what there is to do. Pascual stands against the wall and watches her do it, and knows from her hands before she says anything, because he's been married to her for nearly ten years and has watched her hands. She's quick and very gentle and she doesn't hurry. When she's done what there is, she sits on the edge of the bed and takes his wrist and keeps it.

"Well," says Reggie. "Go on."

"You went in the water."

"I did."

"I told you not one. Not for any animal."

"She came off," says Reggie. "She's all right. She went out on the tide. You should have heard her breathe, Marguerite. At the end. It went right. You'd have liked it, you'd have written it down." He's quite grey, and there's sweat on his lip, and he's smiling at her. "You were right. That's four. I'm sorry."

"You bloody idiot."

"Yes."

"You bloody, bloody idiot, Reggie."

"I know." He moves his hand in hers, turns it over, so that he's holding her wrist too, round the bone. "I know. Four times. Tell the young men."

She doesn't say anything. She sits on the edge of the bed with his hand, and her face does something Pascual has seen it do once before, at a confinement at the Bay that went wrong in 1964. And then it stops doing it, because she won't let it, and she's a doctor, and he's her patient, and she's going to stay his doctor to the end of it.

"Pascual," says Reggie.

Pascual comes and kneels by the bed. It's a narrow bed. It's always been a narrow bed. He kneels on the boards with his coat still on over his pyjamas and takes the other hand, and it's cold.

"*Hombre*," he says. "*Ay, hombre*."

"I know. I'm sorry. Listen." Reggie's voice has gone very thin, and he stops, and gets his breath, and goes on. "When — afterwards. Write to him."

Pascual goes still.

"Not the Institute. Jean'll do the Institute, she'll do it properly. You. In your own hand. Put it on the bottom."

"What do I say?"

"Tell him the water was twenty-four degrees." Reggie's eyes are closed. "This morning. Yesterday morning. At the feed. I took it. Twenty-four degrees. Tell him that."

Pascual looks at him.

He doesn't know what it means. He's quite sure there's no reason on earth a man should want the water temperature from the feed sent to Norway with his dying breath. And he's quite sure, from Reggie's face, that it's the most important thing he's ever been asked to carry.

"That's all?" he says.

"That's all it needs," says Reggie. "He'll know."

"All right."

"And give him his letter back. It's in my pocket. It's his. He'll want —" He stops. "No. He won't want it. Give it him anyway. He should know I kept it. I don't know. You decide. Marguerite, you help him decide. You're better at it than I am."

"We'll decide," says Marguerite.

"And the other thing." His eyes go to her. "You said you would."

"I said I would."

"Tell him gently. Tell him it was —" He stops for breath. "Tell him that part first. That it was worth it. It was."

"I'll tell him."

"Good." He opens his eyes again and finds Pascual's face and looks at it for a long time, in the lamplight, the way he looks at anything he means to see properly. "You were right about Alpha. Your second night."

"*Ay*, Reggie, don't —"

"I'm saying it. You were. And about Beta. And the pairing. And the whole of it. Twice a week, twenty years." His mouth goes up at one corner. It isn't the voice. There isn't anything behind it but the man. "I've had the best of you. You know that? I said it at your wedding and I meant it. I've had the best of you, Pascual, the whole time, and not once did I say thank you properly."

Pascual puts his forehead down on the edge of the bed, on the blanket, beside the cold hand, and holds on.

"You said it," he says into the blanket. "At the wedding. That was properly."

"Was it?"

"It was very properly. Everybody cried. Hester cried."

"Hester cries at hymns."

"She cried at you."

He feels Reggie's fingers move in his. They tighten, very slightly, and then stay.

---

He dies at about a quarter past five, before it's light.

He doesn't say anything else that makes sense. He says something about the third subject, and something about the stairs. He says *two at a time*, once, quite clearly, and seems pleased about it. At the very end he says a name, not loudly, as though somebody had come in at the door and he'd looked up and seen who it was. Pascual, with his forehead on the blanket, hears it and doesn't look up. He doesn't need to look up to know whose.

Marguerite, who has had his wrist the whole time, takes her fingers away at last and sits back.

And the lamp on the table by the bed, which he had lit at dusk and left burning when he went out to the boat, as he had left it burning every night for fifteen years, gutters. The oil's low; nobody filled it yesterday, because yesterday was Friday and Pascual fills it on Saturdays. It burns down lower and blue, and goes out.

It's quite dark in the hut for a while. Then it isn't, because it's getting light outside, grey over the channel, and the norther has blown itself out in the night the way they do.

Across the water, if anybody at the Bay had been looking up at the cay, they'd have seen the last lamp at the end of the row go out just before dawn. They'd have thought nothing of it. It always did.

---

He finds the letter at seven, when Marguerite has gone up to the office to use the radio-telephone for the things that have to be done. He's alone in the hut with Reggie and the grey light.

He hadn't meant to look for it. He'd been told where it was. He stands by the bed for a long time with his hands at his sides, and then he undoes the jacket, which Reggie was still wearing, soaked through, because nobody had wanted to take it off him. He puts his hand into the inside pocket on the left side, the way you'd put your hand into a place in an animal you'd been told was hurt.

It's wet through. Sea water. The ink has run on the outside of the fold, and gone soft and brown at the edges, and it's been folded and unfolded so many times that it's coming apart at the creases. He takes it out very carefully, in both hands, and doesn't open it. It isn't his.

He sits down on the chair by the table where the lamp was, with the letter on his knee, and looks at the man on the bed.

He's fifty-nine. He's been on this island since 1952, and in that time he's been in love with this man, and got over it, and been married to somebody else, and sat by a window in an office while something was done that he still doesn't understand. He's watched the man carry it for fifteen years without being allowed to touch it. And he's been given, at the end, two things to do that he doesn't understand either: a number, and a letter that isn't his.

He likes Spilsbury. He has always liked him. He liked him at the long table in 1956 and on the jetty in 1957, when he took him by both shoulders and said *you could have asked somebody*. He's thought about him more than he'd admit to anyone but Marguerite. He's thought what it must be like in Norway for a man like that with nobody. He's thought, in his worst moments, that it served him right. And he's thought, in all his other moments, that no one alive deserved it, and that the man had very probably thought exactly the same, and done it anyway, for a reason Pascual has never been told.

He sits there until Marguerite comes back, and she stands in the doorway, and looks at him, and at the letter on his knee.

"I'll write it," Pascual says. "The postscript. What he said. I'll write it on the bottom of Jean's. And I'll put this in."

"Are you sure?"

"No." He looks at the letter. "He said he should know Reggie kept it. That he'd carried it. And I think — I think he'd want to know that more than he wouldn't. I think I would. If it was you."

Marguerite comes in and sits on the end of the bed, by Reggie's feet, and puts one hand on his ankle through the blanket, and leaves it there.

"Then send it," she says.

She tells him the rest on the Sunday night, in her own house, on the back verandah under the vine. From there the lamp at the end of the row across the channel is dark for the first time in fifteen years. She tells him because she said she would, and because the man told her to. She tells him the part about it being worth it first.

Pascual sits and listens to all of it, the inspector's question and the three answers and the look at the window. He doesn't say anything until she's finished. Then he gets up and goes to the rail and stands with his back to her for a long time.

He'd known half of it. He's known half of it since a morning at the north pen with two buckets, when a man said *not won't, can't*. He'd put it away in 1958 because he'd been asked to, and never taken it out. Now it's been taken out for him and put in his hands, and he finds that it's exactly as heavy as Reggie had said it would be. He finds, too, that he's glad to be holding it. He'd rather carry it than not know he'd been carried.

"He should have told me," he says to the dark.

"Yes," says Marguerite. "He should. He knew that. He said it was a poor sort of return."

"It's not a return. It's —" He stops. "*Ay*, Reggie. You stupid, stupid —"

He doesn't finish that either.

---

Jean writes the Institute's notice on the Monday, in the office, on Hester's old machine. It's the form, as it should be. She does it properly, as he'd said she would. It goes to the Society, and to the journals, and to the Colonial Office, and to the Linnean. And to one other address, under the terms of deposit, which require the Institute to notify the depositor of accession 197 of any change affecting its custodians.

Jean types that one herself too, last. Then she brings it across to Pascual in the long room and puts it on the bench by his elbow without a word, the way she used to bring the other letters to Reggie. There's a space left at the bottom.

Pascual picks up his pen.

He sits at the bench for a long time with the pen in his hand and the sheet in front of him. What he writes at the bottom of it, in his own hand, below Jean's signature, isn't long. He writes it once, without a draft, and signs it with his whole name. He folds the letter that isn't his inside it, soft and salt-stained and coming apart at the creases, and puts them both in the envelope.

Hester addresses it. She comes across from the Bay on the Tuesday boat to do it. Nobody asked her to, and nobody would have dared tell her not to. She writes the address in her own upright hand, the way she's written it twice a year since 1957, until Jean took it over. She writes it without looking at anybody, and seals it, and gives it to Alleyne's son for the post.

*Dr A. Spilsbury*
*Kongens gate 18*
*Ålesund*
*Norway*
