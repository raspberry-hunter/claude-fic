# Standing Orders

Jean brings it to the long room at four, as she's brought every letter in that hand since she became Director of Research, and puts it down on the bench by his elbow.

"That one's not mine," she says.

Reggie looks at it.

The depositor's letters have gone to Jean since April 1968. That was when Reggie went to the Colonel, and then to the Board, and said it was time the Institute had a younger Director of Research, and that there was nobody in the world better than Dr Leslie. It was all true. Jean had come back from the Menai Strait with her students and her transects and her doctorate and her way of not saying a thing twice. Reggie became Consultant, with a bench in the long room and the north pen and no signature on anything. The Colonel thought him very modest. Hester, who was retiring herself that summer, looked at him over her pad for a long moment and said *yes*, and nothing else.

The reason he didn't give was the licence file. It comes up for renewal every three years, and every three years a man in a hat sits down at the table in the office and reads it, all of it. And on the second morning, generally, he gets to a minute in an Ulsterman's square small hand from 1957, and reads it, and looks up at the Director of Research. Reggie had sat through that look in 1960, and in 1963, and in 1966. In 1966 the man in the hat was very young, and looked up at him, and then down again very quickly, and was kind to him for the rest of the day. Reggie had gone to the Colonel the following spring.

So the letters go to Jean now. They begin *Dear Madam*, which Alec changed to without comment the first time a J. M. Leslie signed for the Institute. Jean opens them in the office, and brings them to the long room, and puts them down by his elbow without a word. He reads them, and gives them back, and she answers them in the form and signs her own name. The first time she did it, in April 1968, he watched her write *J. M. Leslie* at the bottom. He thought, very clearly, that a man in Ålesund would open that and see a different signature and know exactly what it meant. He thought that it was the first thing he'd told Alec since 1957 that wasn't the form, and that he'd told it by not being there.

He hasn't let himself think very much about it since.

This one isn't in the form. He can see that without touching it. It's addressed in Alec's hand, and there's no *The Director of Research* on it at all.

*Dr R. Tar*
*William Martin Institute for Marine Studies*

"It came up with the Institute's post," Jean says. "I haven't opened it. It isn't addressed to the Institute." She stands there with her hands in the pockets of her coat. "Do you want me to go away?"

"Yes, please."

She goes. She pulls the long-room door to behind her, which nobody has done since 1957.

---

He takes it down to the hut at the end of the row and sits at the table and opens it there.

> *Statens Havforskningsstasjon*
> *Ålesund*
> *6th April 1970*
>
> *Dear Reggie,*
>
> *For two years the Institute's acknowledgements have been signed by Dr Leslie. I have looked at that signature four times now, and done the sum again each time. It comes out differently from the way it came out in 1957. The margin is one I can measure but have not been able to trust, and I have found that I have to ask you rather than go on measuring it.*
>
> *I shall be in London from the 14th to the 19th of September, for the symposium at the Zoological Society. I am to read a paper on the Tuesday. I shall be at the Charing Cross Hotel.*
>
> *I should like to see you. That is the whole of it. I have no other reason, and I shall not pretend to one.*
>
> *If you would rather not, you need do nothing at all. No answer is an answer, and I shall read it as one, and I shall not write again except in the form.*
>
> *I am sixty this year. I thought you should know that, too.*
>
> *Yours,*
>
> *A.*

He reads it twice. He reads it a third time only for the last two lines. There's a full stop after the *A*.

He sits at the table in the hut for a long time. The light goes round. The calf, who is thirteen now and the size of her mother and still called the calf by everybody on the island, comes up to breathe in the north pen, and he can hear her through the open window, and he counts it.

*I should like to see you.*

Alec doesn't say things he doesn't mean, and he doesn't say anything with more words than it needs. He's put that sentence in a letter, over his own name and address, to a man at a licensed station, having done the sum four times. He'd read the risk off Jean's signature the way he'd read a temperature, and decided it was small enough to spend. And he's spent it on six words.

And Reggie knows, sitting there, that he isn't going to answer it.

He doesn't decide it. He finds it, the way you'd find a stone in your shoe.

He tries, for Alec's sake, to find a better reason than the one he's got, and there isn't one. The licence is Jean's now. The file's thirteen years old. Nobody in Bedford Row has thought about accession 197 since about 1960, and the drawer has very likely been cleared. Alec is quite right. The margin is real. A man could go to London in September for a symposium at the Zoological Society, which is the most respectable thing a marine biologist can do, and sit in the bar of the Charing Cross Hotel with a colleague he hasn't seen in thirteen years. There isn't a man in a hat on earth who'd write a word of it down.

None of it's the reason. The reason's a minute in a file. It says *there has always been something about Dr S.* in an Ulsterman's hand, and it's true, and Reggie said it. He said it in the voice, leaning back, with his mouth going up at one corner, and got the licence for it, and Frank went somewhere because of it. And he can't be the man who said that and also go and sit in a hotel bar in London with his hand round Alec's wrist. He couldn't in 1957. He'd thought, for thirteen years, that that was because of the licence, and the file, and Pascual by the window. It turns out that it wasn't. It's because he'd know. Every minute in the bar he'd know. And Alec would know, and they'd both sit there knowing, and it wouldn't wear off, because it hasn't. It isn't the kind of thing that does.

He folds the letter along its folds.

He doesn't burn it. He's never burnt anything of Alec's in his life. Alec is the one who burns things. He sits with it in his hand. Then he puts it in the inside pocket of his jacket, on the left, where Alec had kept the green book for thirty years. He buttons the jacket over it and goes down to the north pen for the evening feed a quarter of an hour early.

---

By June Pascual has noticed the hand.

It goes to the inside pocket of the jacket, on the left side, perhaps a dozen times a day. It doesn't go in. It goes flat against the cloth, over whatever's in there, and rests, and comes away. He does it at the bench and at the pens and at breakfast, and on the verandah of the house above the harbour on Sundays, when he comes to lunch. He doesn't know he's doing it. Pascual is quite certain of that. It's like watching a man touch a sore tooth with his tongue.

Pascual has seen that gesture before, on another man, a long time ago, on this island. He'd noticed it that time too, and thought nothing about it. He thinks about it now.

He asks Jean, in June, at the pens, because Jean was at the bench that spring and Jean notices things.

"Has something come for him?"

Jean throws a fish to the calf's mother, who is getting old and has to be fed first. Jean has been Director of Research for two years and has changed nothing on the station that didn't need changing, and has changed that ruthlessly, and Pascual likes her more than he'd have thought possible in 1956.

"Something came in April," she says. "Addressed to him. Not in the form."

"From —"

"Yes."

Pascual says something in Spanish.

"I didn't open it." Jean throws another fish. "I've never opened one of them, Pascual, not even the ones addressed to me. I take them down to the long room and I put them by his elbow and I go away. And he gives them back and I write *Dear Sir*." Her mouth goes straight. "I've written it four times. I'm a scientist. I don't know what he thinks I'm reading when I sign it, but I'm not stupid, and I'd like it on record that I think it's the most dreadful thing I've ever been asked to do on this station, and I've buried animals here."

---

He asks Reggie in July, on the step of the hut at the end of the row, at night. It's the only place on the island anybody has ever managed to talk to Reggie Tar about anything that matters.

He doesn't lead up to it. He's fifty-seven. He's been married eight years to a woman who can get a history out of a stone, and he's learnt from her that you don't lead up to things. You ask.

"What's in your pocket?"

Reggie's hand goes to it. He looks down at his own hand as though it belonged to somebody else.

Pascual waits for the voice. He has warned this man, since 1958, that if the voice ever comes on him he'll say it anyway. It hasn't come on him once in twelve years, and he isn't sure it won't now.

It doesn't.

"He's written," Reggie says. "Not in the form. He's going to be in London in September. At a symposium. He's said he'd like to see me."

Pascual sits very still on the step.

"And?"

"And I'm not going to answer it."

"*Hombre*."

"He said no answer's an answer. He said he'd read it as one." Reggie's voice is perfectly level and very tired. "So that's the answer. That's all. I'm answering it."

"By carrying it round in your coat for three months."

Reggie doesn't say anything.

"Why?" Pascual says. "I'm asking. You don't have to tell me. Jean's got the licence, the whole thing's thirteen years —"

"I know how old it is."

"Then *why*?"

Reggie looks out at the dark where the sea is.

"Because I said a thing in 1957, in that office," he says. "You were there. It's in a file. It's still true. And I can't be the man who said it and go and sit in a hotel in London with him. I couldn't in '57 and I can't now. I thought for a long time it would wear off, and it hasn't, and I've stopped thinking it will. That's all. There isn't any more to it than that, Pascual. I'm sorry. I wish there were."

Pascual thinks that there's a great deal more to it than that. He thinks there's been more to it than that since the morning after the solicitors' letter, when a man stood at a rail with two buckets and said *not won't, can't*. He doesn't say it. He's been told plainly, once, not to go in there, and he hasn't. He isn't going to start on a step at midnight because the man is tired.

He puts his arm round Reggie's shoulders instead, for a moment, hard, the way you'd steady a man on a deck.

"You'd tell me," he says. "If you changed your mind. You'd tell me before September."

"Yes."

"And you'd go."

"I'm not going to change my mind."

"I know. I'm saying if."

"Yes," says Reggie. "If. I'd go."

---

He doesn't change his mind.

The fourteenth of September comes, and the fifteenth, which is a Tuesday. Pascual has looked at the programme, which the Institute has, because the Institute has everything. He knows what time the Tuesday paper is. He goes down to the long room at the hour, London time, which is early morning on the cay, and Reggie is there at the bench with the calf's weight sheets as he is every morning, and doesn't look up.

That evening there's a visitor at the long table.

He's a young man called Ferris, from a university in Canada, who has come for a month to look at the pairing figures. He's very clever and very nice and very excited, and he's just come through London, where he stayed three days at the beginning of the symposium before his connection. He sits down to dinner full of it. Pascual, at the end of the table with Marguerite beside him, because she comes over on Tuesdays now, sees it coming about ten seconds before it arrives, the way you'd see a wave.

"I heard Spilsbury this morning — well, your morning, our — well, it doesn't matter." Ferris laughs. "The seal paper. The hooded seals, the whole series from the West Ice, twenty years of it. It was extraordinary. You were colleagues, weren't you, Dr Tar? At the Labs? Someone said he'd been out here. Are you going over for the second week?"

And Pascual, at the end of the table, puts his knife down and waits for the shoulders to come down half an inch.

They don't.

Reggie sits at the head of the table with his hands either side of his plate, and looks at the young man from Canada. His face does nothing at all. It doesn't open. It doesn't go warm. There's no lamp behind it. It's the face of a man of fifty-one who has been up since five, and it stays that way, grey under the brown.

"No," he says. "I'm not going. I'm too tired, I think."

Ferris blinks.

"Oh — of course. Of course, sir. It's a long way."

"It is a long way," Reggie agrees. He picks up his fork, and looks at it, and puts it down again. "I'm glad it went well. He's the best anatomist I've worked with. You should read everything he's written. Excuse me."

And he gets up and goes out, at a quarter past eight, with his dinner half eaten. He goes past the students' end of the table and out of the door, and Pascual hears him on the verandah steps.

He's taking them one at a time. That's not news. He's taken them one at a time since 1957, and Pascual has watched him do it for thirteen years and put it down to the only thing he could think of.

Then the steps stop. Halfway down, Pascual thinks, from the sound. There's a pause, and it's too long.

Marguerite is up before he is.

---

Reggie is sitting on the fourth step from the bottom with one hand flat on the rail and the other flat on his chest, over the left side, over the pocket.

He's the wrong colour. Marguerite can see that by the verandah lamp from the top of the steps. It's the colour of the underside of a fish, and there's a sheen of sweat on his top lip in the evening that has nothing to do with the heat. He looks up as she comes down, and she watches him think about the voice, and discard it, and decide not to bother.

"Don't," he says. "I'm all right. I stood up too fast."

"You did not stand up too fast. I watched you. You stood up like a man of ninety." She sits down on the step above him, and takes his wrist, round the bone, and gets her watch up into the light. "Be quiet. I'm counting."

He's quiet.

It's fast, and it's not quite regular, and she doesn't like either of those things. She counts a full minute and then another, with Pascual standing above them on the verandah with the lamp behind him and his hands in fists at his sides. By the second minute it has slowed a little and the colour's coming back into the man's face.

"Has that happened before?" she says.

"What?"

"*Dr Tar*."

He looks at her. She looks back. It's the look she gives men in her surgery who would like to talk about their gardens, and she's been giving it for thirty years, and it works on about two in three.

"Sometimes," Reggie says. "On the steps. It goes off."

"How long is sometimes?"

He doesn't answer.

"Since Christmas? Since last summer? Since the stranding?" She means the stranding in 1969, when he'd been out in the boat all night in the rain with Pascual and two students, and had come back at six in the morning grey to the lips. He had said he'd got cold, and she'd believed him, and she's been angry with herself about that for about twenty seconds now.

"Since about the stranding," says Reggie. "Yes."

"Eighteen months."

"About that."

"And you told nobody."

"It goes off."

Marguerite lets go of his wrist.

"Tomorrow," she says, "Pascual is going to bring you over on the eight o'clock boat, and you are going to come to my surgery, and I am going to have your shirt off and listen to you for as long as I like. On Thursday you are going to the General to have a tracing made of your heart, because I haven't got the machine and they have. And you're not going to tell me you're busy, because I know exactly how busy you are, I have your whole history in my files. You wrote most of it."

She waits for him to argue. She has, in eight years, seen Reggie Tar argue with her about his own health exactly twice, and lose both times, and not forgive her.

"All right," he says.

She looks at him, and then up the steps at Pascual, and sees her husband's face.

That, Marguerite thinks, is what has frightened him most. Not the colour, and not the pulse. It's that he said *all right*.

---

She has him in her surgery at nine, with his shirt off, on the couch under the window with the blind half down.

He's thin. She had known he was thin. She hadn't known how thin until she saw the ribs. He has the kind of long spare frame that looks well in a jacket and has had nothing added to it in twenty years. There's an old scar on the left forearm from the lip in 1954. There's another on the shin that he says is from the Labs and won't explain. His blood pressure is higher than she'd like. His heart, under the stethoscope, is doing something she doesn't care for.

She listens for a long time. He lies still and lets her, with one arm behind his head, looking at the ceiling.

She's good at this part. She's always been good at it. The trick with men like this isn't to ask about the chest. It's to ask about the beans, and go on asking about the beans, until there are no beans left and they've got nothing to talk about but the chest.

So she asks him about the stranding. She asks about the nights in the boat, and the tank drawings in 1958 that Pascual took the pen away from, and what he eats on a day he's working. He tells her all of it, because it's all the history of the Institute and he's never minded telling that. She sits on the stool with her notes on her knee and writes it down. What she's writing down, underneath what he's telling her, is a man who's spent himself like water for thirty years. He's worked till two, and eaten whatever's left on the end of the bench, and walked out into the cold in his shirtsleeves because he had something to say and his jacket was on the other chair. And in about the last thirteen years he's done it with a great deal less reason to stop than he had before.

She doesn't write down that last part. It isn't a clinical finding. She thinks it anyway.

"How often," she says, "do you go out in the boat at night?"

"When there's an animal."

"How often is there an animal?"

"More often than there used to be." He's still looking at the ceiling. "There are more of them out there than there were, and they come in on the reef in the winter, and somebody's got to go."

"And the somebody's got to be you."

"Pascual's fifty-seven."

"You're fifty-one."

"Yes," says Reggie, "but I haven't got a wife."

She sits on the stool and looks at him.

The tracing comes back from the General on the Friday. She reads it in her surgery with the door shut, and then reads it again. Then she sends for him on the Monday and tells him, in the plainest words she has, what it says.

His heart isn't getting all the blood it wants. That's what the steps are. It's been that way for some time, and it's getting worse. On the tracing, some time last year, at about the time of the stranding, there's what she's fairly sure is a small piece of heart muscle that stopped getting any blood at all and died. It healed, more or less, and he walked about on it and told nobody. He'll have thought it was cold, or cramp, or the rain.

"You had a heart attack," she says, "in a boat, in the rain, and rowed home, and went to breakfast."

"It didn't seem much at the time."

"It wasn't much. That's why you're here." She puts the tracing down. "The next one might not be."

He takes that. She watches him take it. He's very good at taking things; he takes them like a man catching something thrown at him hard, and doesn't let his hands move.

"What do I do?"

She tells him. There are tablets, little ones, to go under the tongue when the steps come. He's to carry them everywhere and take one before the tightness, not after it, not when he's decided it's bad enough. There's another tablet for the blood pressure, every morning. He's to eat breakfast. He's to eat lunch. He's to sleep at night, in a bed. There are to be no more nights in the boat, not one, not for any animal, not if the whole of the West Indies comes in on the reef at once. Pascual's fifty-seven, and he has a wife, and the wife is going to have something to say to anybody who lets Pascual go out on his own. There are three students on the station who are twenty-four and who can row.

"And you're to stop," says Marguerite, "spending yourself as though there were an unlimited supply. There isn't. There never was. You've been extraordinarily wasteful with yourself your whole life, Dr Tar, and until about two years ago you were young enough to get away with it. You aren't any more."

He's quiet for a long moment.

"How long?" he says. "If I don't."

"I don't know."

"You know roughly."

"I know roughly, and I'm not telling a man who'd use a number as a target." She looks at him steadily. "If you do what I say, a good many years. You'll be an old man on that verandah telling students they're wrong. If you don't, I don't know. Less. It's in your hands, which I'd rather it weren't, knowing whose hands they are."

He almost laughs.

"And one more thing," says Marguerite, "which isn't medical, and which you're going to do anyway."

"What?"

"You're coming to dinner. At the house. Every Sunday. Not lunch, dinner, and you'll stay the night in the spare room, because Alleyne's boy doesn't run on Sunday night and you're not going across in the dark in anybody's boat. Every Sunday, from this one. And I'm going to put food in front of you and watch you eat it, and I'm going to take your pulse on the verandah afterwards, and you are going to let me."

Reggie looks at her for a long time from the couch.

"That's the tonic," he says slowly. "Isn't it."

She's never told him about the tonic. She's quite sure she hasn't. Then she thinks of a verandah in November 1960, and a man who read her in four minutes from a standing start. She thinks she had better stop being surprised by him.

"Yes," she says. "That's the tonic. You come back every week and I ask you about the beans."

"And one day there aren't any beans."

"That's the idea."

He sits up on the couch, slowly, and reaches for his shirt. She watches him put it on and button it. His hand goes, when the jacket's on, to the left side, over the pocket, flat, and rests there, and comes away.

She doesn't ask about the pocket. There are a great many beans left yet.

---

The first Sunday is the fourth of October.

He comes over on the afternoon boat with a bag. Pascual meets him on the quay and carries it up the hill, over his protests, as though it were a case of specimens. Marguerite's house stands above the harbour with a verandah on three sides. There's a long table on the back one under a vine that she says her grandmother planted and that Pascual says is trying to kill him.

Hester's there.

Hester has lived at the Bay since 1968, in a white house by the church with the Colonel and a cat. She reads the whole of the *Gazette* every week and writes letters to the Colonial Secretary that he answers by return. She sits at Marguerite's table in a grey dress with her back straight, and when Reggie comes up the verandah steps she looks at him over her glasses and says, "Dr Tar. You're late. You're never late."

"The boat was late, Hester."

"The boat is always late. You used to allow for it." She looks at him a moment longer. "Sit down. Here. Next to me."

He sits down next to her.

Marguerite feeds them fish, and rice, and a great many things her cook has done with plantain, and a pudding. Reggie watches Pascual eat Marguerite's pudding with every appearance of pleasure, and thinks about Hester's pudding in 1957, and a man who went red to the ears when he said he'd enjoyed it. He laughs out loud at the table, suddenly, and has to explain. Pascual threatens litigation and Hester says she'll give evidence, and Marguerite says she's had the notes on Pascual's pudding perjury since 1960 and they're very thorough.

He eats all of it. He hadn't meant to. It's just that every time he puts his fork down somebody is looking at him. There are three of them, and they're very good at it, and he's outnumbered.

Afterwards, on the verandah, in the dark, with the lamps lit, Marguerite takes his wrist without asking and counts, and he lets her. Pascual sits on the rail with his back to a post, and Hester sits very upright in a cane chair with a glass of the Colonel's port and says nothing and misses nothing.

"Seventy-eight," says Marguerite, and lets go. "Better."

"Is it?"

"It's better than the steps." She sits back. "It's not good. It's better."

He sits in Marguerite's cane chair with his hands on the arms and looks out across the harbour.

From Marguerite's verandah you can see straight across the channel to the cay. It's a long low shape against the stars, with the lamps along the row. The students' lamps are lit, and the lamp in the office where Jean works late, and the one in Pascual's old hut, where a student lives now.

And the one at the far end, by itself, a little brighter than the others.

He looks at it for some time before he understands what he's looking at.

He'd left it lit. He always leaves it lit. He'd gone out of the door this afternoon with his bag and not thought about it, because he never thinks about it. He's lit that lamp at dusk every evening for thirteen years and gone to sleep with it burning, and Pascual fills it on Saturdays. And he's never once in thirteen years seen it from outside.

It's quite bright, from here. You can see it across the whole channel. Anybody at the Bay who looked up the hill at night would see it: one lamp, at the end of the row, lit.

"Is it always like that?" he says.

Nobody says anything for a moment.

"Most nights," Pascual says eventually, from the rail. "Since '57."

"You can see it from here."

"Yes."

"You can see it from your *bedroom*."

"Yes," says Marguerite. "We can."

Reggie sits with his hands on the arms of the chair and looks at his own lamp across the water. His hand goes, while he isn't watching it, to the pocket, and rests there. Hester watches it go, over the rim of her glass, and looks away.

"I should put it out," he says. "When I go to sleep. I should get into the habit."

"Yes," says Marguerite.

He doesn't say he will, and none of them ask him to. They sit on the verandah, the four of them, until the Colonel's port is finished, and then Hester says she's an old woman and goes home by the path with Pascual walking her to the church. Marguerite goes in to see about the spare room. Reggie sits on by himself in the dark for a while and looks at the lamp across the channel, burning on in the hut at the end of the row, with nobody in it.

It will go out, some time before morning, when the oil does. It always has.
