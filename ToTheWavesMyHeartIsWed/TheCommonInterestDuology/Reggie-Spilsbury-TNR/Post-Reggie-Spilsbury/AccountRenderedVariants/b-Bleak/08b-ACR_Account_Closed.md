# Account Closed

He takes the water at a quarter to seven, as he does every morning.

It is a grey still morning at the beginning of March, with the ice just off the inner harbour and the first of the sealers fitting out along the far quay for the West Ice. He lets the thermometer down on its string from the end of the quay into the Brosund, and waits three minutes, and brings it up.

Four point one.

He looks at it for a moment in the grey light. Then he shakes the water off the bulb and winds the string round his fingers and puts it in his coat, and keeps the figure, since there is nowhere else to keep it. He walks back up the hill to Kongens gate for his coffee before the station.

Fru Brekke has the post on the hall table. She is standing beside it, which she does not generally do, with her hands folded in front of her apron.

"There is one from the West Indies," she says. "A thick one. I have not touched it. I thought you would like to have it yourself." And then, because she is a woman who has had lodgers for thirty years and knows when to leave a hall: "I will bring the coffee up."

He looks at the envelope on the table.

It is addressed in Hester Bevan's hand.

He has not seen that hand on an envelope since the spring of 1968. From then on the Institute's acknowledgements had come in Dr Leslie's, and he had understood what that meant, and done the sum on it four times, and spent it on six words in April 1970. He knows Hester's hand as well as he knows his own. It is upright and even, with the capitals made the way she was taught at school in about 1910. The address is exactly as she wrote it every spring and autumn for ten years. *Dr A. Spilsbury. Kongens gate 18. Ålesund. Norway.*

Hester retired in 1968. John Weald told him so on a towpath. She lives at the Bay.

She came over to write this.

He stands in the hall with his hand on the table beside it and does not pick it up for some time. He knows. He has known since he saw the hand. He knew, if he is honest, in September 1970, sitting on a bench by a canal with a print on his knee. He has taken the measurement off that print with his thumb every morning since, and got the same result every morning, and known what the result meant. He has not needed a letter to tell him what his own figures said.

He finds that he would have given a great deal, standing in Fru Brekke's hall, to have been wrong.

He picks it up and takes it upstairs.

---

It is thick because there are three things in it.

The first is a typed sheet on the Institute's paper. It is Hester's old machine; the *e* still sits a hair high. It is Dr Leslie's typing, which is faster than Reggie's was and makes no overstrikes at all. It is in the form, as it should be.

> *William Martin Institute for Marine Studies*
> *14th February 1972*
>
> *Dr A. Spilsbury*
> *Kongens gate 18*
> *Ålesund*
>
> *Dear Sir,*
>
> *In accordance with the terms of deposit relating to accession 197, I write to inform you of a change affecting the Institute's custodians of that material.*
>
> *Dr Reginald Tar, Director of Research of this Institute until 1968 and thereafter its Consultant, died suddenly at the Institute on the 12th of February 1972, in his fifty-fourth year.*
>
> *The material remains in the Institute's custody under the terms of the deed, and is available on application as before.*
>
> *Yours faithfully,*
>
> *J. M. Leslie*
> *Director of Research*

He reads it twice. The first reading is for what it says. The second is for how it has been built.

It has been built very well. Jean Leslie has always done things properly. *Died suddenly* is what one says. *In his fifty-fourth year* is what one says of a man of fifty-three. He had turned fifty-three in December, and Spilsbury has known the date since 1945 and has not once let it pass without marking it, in his head, where no one else could read it. The deed is referred to by its proper name. Nothing on the sheet could not be put in front of any man in any hat in the world.

It is the kindest letter he has ever been sent by an institution, and it has taken, he thinks, a very great deal of care to make it say nothing, and he knows exactly how much.

At the bottom, below the signature, there is a gap of perhaps three inches. It has been left on purpose. In it, in black ink, in a hand he has seen perhaps a dozen times in his life, on pen drawings and veterinary schedules and once on a Christmas card in 1956, somebody has written a postscript.

---

> *P.S. — In my own hand, because he asked for it.*
>
> *Dr Spilsbury,*
>
> *Jean has written what the Institute writes. This is the rest.*
>
> *He went out in the boat at two in the morning on the twelfth, in a norther, for a young female stranded on the reef off the second head. There were two students with him who had never seen a stranding. Jean was in Kingston and I was at the Bay and there was no crossing until light, and she would have been dead by light. My wife had told him not to go in the water for any animal whatever. He had kept to it for sixteen months, which for him was a very long time. He went in to the waist. They got her off on the third heave, and she went out past the reef on the falling tide, and they heard her blow, a long way off. The student who wrote it up says her breathing had gone right before she went. He was very pleased about that.*
>
> *His heart had been bad for about two years. My wife found it in 1970 and he did what she told him about half the time, which she says was better than she expected. He died in his own bed at the end of the row at a quarter past five in the morning, with my wife holding one wrist and me holding the other hand. He knew. He was not frightened. He told my wife she had been right four times, and he told me some things I will keep.*
>
> *He asked me to tell you that the water was twenty-four degrees. At the feed, the morning before. He took it himself. He said you would know what it meant. I don't. I have written it as he said it.*
>
> *He asked me to send you your letter. It is enclosed. He carried it in his coat from April 1970 until he died. It was on him in the water. He said you should know that he kept it, and then he said he didn't know whether you would want it, and he told me and my wife to decide. We have decided you should have it. If we are wrong, I am sorry.*
>
> *My wife has told me what he did in the office in March 1957, and why, and what I was, sitting by the window. He asked her to tell me gently. He asked her to tell me first that it was worth it. I am telling you the same thing, in the same order, because I think it is yours as much as mine, and because nobody else is going to tell you.*
>
> *I liked you. God help me, I still do. I don't know if I forgive you. I don't think it is mine to forgive. I think there is only one man who could have done that, and I think he did it a long time ago and never told either of us.*
>
> *Pascual Pobil*

---

The third thing is his own letter.

He knows it before he has it out of the envelope. He knows it from the weight and the feel, and from the fold, which is his own. He has folded letters in three the same way since he was nineteen, with the top third a quarter of an inch short. It comes out into his hand soft, the way paper goes when it has been carried against a body for a long time. It has gone brown along the creases, and it is coming apart at them. There is a tidemark across one corner where the sea got into it and dried. The ink on the outside of the fold has run, so that his own handwriting on the back of the last page has gone into a blue cloud, and he can read only *Yours* in it, and nothing after.

He does not open it. He knows what is inside. He wrote it at this table.

He sits on the edge of the bed with it in his hands.

And he looks up, because he cannot help it, at the photograph propped against the wall on the table, where it has been since October 1970. A man stands at the rail of the north pen in his shirtsleeves, side-on, with his spectacles pushed up on his forehead. His hand is flat against his chest, on the left, over the breast pocket of his jacket.

*July 1970*, in pencil, on the back, in John Weald's small upright hand.

He had looked at that hand every morning for seventeen months. He had read it as a man resting his fingers on the place where it hurt, to see whether it had stopped. He had taken it for a finding, and he had been right. Pascual's postscript says so in plain words, and so does Jean Leslie's *suddenly*: the heart, two years, the steps, the tablets taken about half the time. He had read it correctly. It was a measurement, and it had been accurate.

It had also been this.

It had been a hand on a letter. It was the letter he is holding, three months after it was written, in a pocket over a heart, in July. It had not been answered because it could not be answered. It had not been burnt because the man had never burnt anything of his in his life. It had been carried, in the only place there was to carry it, against the one thing in the man that was already failing. It had been carried into the water at two in the morning in February, to the waist. And it had come out again, here, in Fru Brekke's upstairs room, with a tidemark on the corner and his own *Yours* gone to a blue cloud.

Both readings were right. That is the thing he sits with. He had read the hand as heart and it was heart. He had never once thought to read it as the other, because he had not believed there was an other left to read. He had written *no answer is an answer, and I shall read it as one*, and he had. He had read the silence as a closed door. He had not read it as a man with his hand flat on the door from the other side for twenty-two months.

He had got the measurement right and the meaning wrong. He has never done that before in his life. He had not known it was possible.

---

The coffee has come and gone cold on the table, and he has not heard Fru Brekke bring it.

He reads the postscript again, twice, the way he reads everything. The first reading is for what it says, and the second for how it has been built.

It has not been built at all. That is the second thing he learns from it. There is no draft behind it. A man wrote it once, straight down, at a bench, in the hand he uses for veterinary schedules, and did not cross anything out, and signed it with both his names. It is the least careful letter Spilsbury has ever received, and every word of it is exactly where it should be.

*The water was twenty-four degrees.*

He had written *four degrees* in March 1960 because there was nowhere else to put it. It had come back typed, with the signature pressed through the paper and two overstrikes in *acknowledge*, and he had read in the typewriter that the man could not trust his own hand. He had been right about that as well. He had guessed, even then, that the man had sat at the machine with his fingers on the keys for a long time, deciding not to type something. He had not guessed what. He had not guessed that it was a figure, taken that morning at the feed, or that it would be kept for twelve years and sent at the last, through a man who didn't know what it meant and wrote it down anyway because he'd been asked.

It is the answer. Twelve years late, it is the answer, and it came in a dead man's words, in a living man's hand, below a typed notice. It is also the first line from that island in fifteen years that has not been in the form.

*Twenty-four degrees. At the feed, the morning before.*

The man had gone down to the jetty on his last morning with a thermometer, as he always did, and let it down into the water, and waited the three minutes, and read it. And he had kept the figure, the way Spilsbury keeps his, because he had nowhere to write it either. Then he had gone out in the boat that night and given it to Pobil at a quarter to five in the morning, in a narrow bed, with his breath going.

Spilsbury sits very still.

And then the last paragraph but one, which he had read on the first reading for what it said, and which he reads now for what it has cost.

*My wife has told me what he did in the office in March 1957, and why, and what I was, sitting by the window.*

He had costed that too, at a table in lamplight on the ninth of February. *There is another man on this row.* He had put it in front of Reggie in so many words, and Reggie had said *leave Pascual out of it*. And he had said *it is you who will have to*. And Reggie had. He had kept Pobil out of it for fifteen years, in an office and at a rail and on a step, and would not tell him. He'd told the man *can't, not won't* and let him think it was the licence. He'd carried it for both of them, so that the man by the window would never have to know that he was the reason.

Now the man knows. He knows because Reggie died. And the first thing he has done with it, in the same week, in his own hand, is give it to Spilsbury. *Because I think it is yours as much as mine.*

*He asked her to tell me first that it was worth it.*

Spilsbury had done the sum on the ninth of February 1957. He had set it out across a table: on one side a man alive, on the licence, in post, with his work and his animals and his friend behind the wall; on the other, the cost. He had taken the cost, and had it deferred until Easter, and found he could pay it, just, if he did not look at it too long. He has looked at it every day for fifteen years. He has been paying it every day. He has never once in all that time had any way to know whether the thing on the other side of the page had been worth what it cost, because the only man who could have told him had been put out of reach by the sum itself.

The man had told Marguerite Sealy, at the end. And she had told Pobil. And Pobil has told him. It has come to him the only way it could have come, through two other people, after it was too late to answer, in the same order the man had asked for.

*Worth it.*

He had not costed this. He had costed Frank and the bench and the room in Southport. He had costed the canteen sentence, and Easter, and his own fifteen years on a quay in Norway with a thermometer. He had costed every piece of it but one, which was the order. He had never once, in any version of the sum, put the man on the other side of the page as the one who went first. There were nine years between them, near enough. The arithmetic had always assumed he would be the one to go and the man would be the one left, with his work and his animals and a lamp at the end of the row.

He had been wrong about that. It had not occurred to him that he could be.

The man is dead at fifty-three of a heart he had spent like water since he was twenty-seven. He died in the water for an animal, which is what he had become. Spilsbury is sixty-one, with a thermometer in his pocket and a photograph on a table and a letter he wrote himself, coming back to him apart at the creases. He has, for the first time in his life, nothing on the other side of any page at all.

---

At about half past ten he takes a pencil out of his coat.

He has not written a figure down since the seventh of February 1957. He had burnt the books in a brick incinerator behind a sick bay, so that there should be no paper, and there has been no paper since. There has been the form and the stove, and his head, which is very good. And there has been a column in a bank in London with fifteen pounds going into it every quarter day for a man who has gone away, and nothing on the other side.

He turns his own letter over, very carefully, so as not to tear it along the creases. The back of the last page has gone to a blue cloud where the sea got into it, but the back of the first page is clean. It is the only paper from that island he has ever been allowed to keep, and it is his own.

He writes on it, in pencil, small, in the hand he used for thirty years in two green books:

*Martin Inst., jetty, 11.ii.72, feed. 24°. R. T.*

He looks at it.

Then underneath, because it is his practice and because there is now somewhere to put it:

*Ålesund, Brosund, quay, 2.iii.72, 06.45. 4.1°.*

He looks at the two figures one above the other, in pencil, on the back of a letter that was carried into the sea. It is a column of two readings, taken four thousand miles apart by two men who each went down to the water every morning with a thermometer, out of interest, and had nowhere to write it down.

He does not write *Result of arithmetic*. There is no arithmetic. There is nothing to add them to and nothing to take them from. They are two readings, and they are accurate, and they are the only entry in the book.

He folds the letter again along its own folds, top third a quarter of an inch short. He puts it in the inside pocket of his coat, on the left side, where the green book used to go and where there has been nothing for fifteen years. He buttons the coat over it.

His hand stays there, flat against the cloth, for some time.

---

In the afternoon he goes to the station and writes two letters.

The first is to Pobil, by hand, on the station's paper. It is short. He writes it once, without a draft, the way the postscript was written, because he has learnt that morning that it can be done.

> *Dear Pobil,*
>
> *Thank you. You were not wrong to send it.*
>
> *In March 1960 I wrote to him, in a letter to the Institute, that the sound here was at four degrees that morning. It was. He could not answer it, and did not, and I know now that he kept the answer. Twenty-four degrees is the answer to that. I should like you to know what you carried.*
>
> *It was worth it. I have your word for it and his, and I shall have to take them, since I have nothing else. I do not forgive myself, and I don't ask you to. I think you are right about who could have, and when, and that he did.*
>
> *Please thank Dr Sealy. Please tell her she was right a fifth time, about the order of the telling.*
>
> *A. Spilsbury*

The second is to the Institute, in the form, because it is March, and the terms of deposit require it.

> *Dear Madam,*
>
> *In accordance with the terms of deposit, I write to inform the Institute that the depositor of accession 197 remains resident at Kongens gate 18, Ålesund, and may be reached there.*
>
> *I acknowledge with thanks your letter of the 14th ultimo.*

He sits with the pen above the paper for a long time after that. There is a great deal he could write below it, and nobody left to be harmed by any of it. Jean Leslie would read it and file it and say nothing, and the man in the hat would never see it, and it would not matter now if he did. It is the first letter to that island in fifteen years in which he could write anything at all.

He writes nothing. The form is the right form. It had been the right form for fifteen years, and the man at the other end had kept it with him, and it does not become wrong because there is nobody left to keep it.

He writes the close, as the handbooks give it, as he has written it nearly thirty times to that address, twice a year, in March and September, since 1957. For the first time he knows exactly what it says.

> *Yours faithfully,*
>
> *A. Spilsbury*
