# Gone Away

The first thing that comes back is the money.

It reaches him at Ålesund at the beginning of May 1957, forwarded from the Bay by Hester in a plain Institute envelope with nothing in it but the enclosure. The enclosure is a long envelope from his bank in London. Inside is a short letter regretting that the draft for fifteen pounds issued on the first of March had been returned undelivered. Inside that is the draft, and the envelope it had gone out in, addressed in a bank clerk's hand to Southport. The envelope carries a Post Office endorsement, in violet ink, in capitals:

*GONE AWAY.*

He sits at the table in the room the station has given him, which has a window on the harbour and a stove and a bed, and looks at the violet capitals for some time.

It is the Post Office's phrase for a person who has left an address and not said where. He has seen it on returned letters all his life. It is a very exact phrase, and he has never before been in a position to admire it.

He does not write to the bank to ask it to try again. There is nowhere to try. He does not write to Southport, or to Ashby, who was his pupil, or to anybody who might know. Every one of those is a line. The only kindness left to him is to draw no more of them, and he has spent a great deal to be able to afford that one.

He writes instead to the bank. He directs that the sum of fifteen pounds be transferred on each quarter day, beginning at midsummer, from his current account to a deposit account in his own name. He is to be sent no statement of the second account, and it is to accrue until further instruction.

He knows exactly what that is. It is a column with nothing on the other side of it. He does it anyway, because the quarter days come round whether or not there is anybody at the end of them, and it has turned out that he does not know how to stop.

Then he puts the draft and the letter and the violet envelope into the stove, one at a time, and watches them go.

---

The station is a good station.

It is a grey stone building on the quay at the mouth of the Brosund, with a laboratory on the first floor and a cold room in the cellar and a slipway at the back. Its Director is a broad slow man called Rasmussen, who was at Tromsø before the war and in Sweden during it, and who met him off the coastal steamer on the second of April in a snowstorm. Rasmussen shook his hand and said, in careful English, "We have waited two years for somebody who can cut a hooded seal properly. I hope you are not too good for us," and took him to a hotel, and gave him dinner, and asked him not one question about anything but seals. Spilsbury sat across the table from him in the steam of the dining room with snow melting off his coat and thought that it had been a long time since anybody had given him exactly what he needed without being told.

The work is the sealing catch.

The Ålesund fleet goes out to the West Ice in March and comes back in May, and it is one of the largest sealing fleets in the country. It brings back, besides the skins and the blubber, whatever the station has asked for from the carcasses. They come in salt, in ice, in formalin, in barrels, with labels in the skippers' own hands, some of them excellent and some of them in a spelling that takes an afternoon. Harp seals, mostly, and hooded seals, and once in 1958 a bearded seal that the whole station comes down to the cellar to look at.

He is very good at it. He had known he would be. The animals are dead before they reach him, all of them. Nobody asks him to keep anything alive, or to decide whether it should be, and nobody asks him to write down what it cost. He takes them apart, very carefully, and writes down what they were. It is the cleanest work he has done in thirty years, and he is aware of exactly why, and he does not look at the reason often.

He learns Norwegian, by the end of the first winter, well enough to lecture in it badly and to be corrected by the students without minding. He finds, as he found at the Labs, that he likes students who correct him. He has two assistants, a young man called Vik who will be very good and a woman called Fru Aarseth who already is, and who runs the cold room with a thoroughness that he recognises. He stands in the cellar door watching her check the barrel labels against the manifest, and thinks of Hester Bevan with a pain so precise he has to go upstairs.

Rasmussen's wife, Solveig, feeds him on Sundays in a wooden house on Aspøya with a view of the islands, and has done since the first Sunday, without discussion. She puts more on his plate than he eats. He eats more of it than he would elsewhere. By the second winter he has understood that this is being done on purpose, and that she and her husband have discussed him, and have decided that there is something the matter with the Englishman that is not their business, and that the Englishman will be fed. He finds that he is grateful for this in a way he could not easily have said in any language.

He is not unhappy by any measure he could give. He sleeps, eats, works, lectures, walks out to the end of the quay in the evenings in all weathers and looks at the water. His hands are perfectly steady. His papers go out on time and are well received. He is good company at the Rasmussens' table, in his way, which is a way they have got used to. He has one expression and uses it for everything, and nobody in Ålesund has ever seen it used for anything else, and so nobody in Ålesund knows there is anything behind it.

That is the difference, and he knows it very exactly. On a cay in the West Indies there is a man who has to walk round a thing every day, because everybody on that island knows where it is. Spilsbury does not have to walk round anything. There is nobody here who knows it is there. He is steadier than Reggie Tar, and he knows that too. It is the steadiness of a man who has taken one thing out of a building that had very little else in it, and finds the building still stands. He goes on living in it. He has costed that. It is an adequate building.

---

The offprints come in July, forwarded by sea, a stiff brown packet.

He knows the hand on the label before he has it out of the station's pigeonhole. It is not Reggie's writing hand. It is the other one, the square capitals Reggie uses for specimen labels and has done since the Labs, so that a jar can be read across a room by anybody.

*DR A. SPILSBURY*
*STATENS HAVFORSKNINGSSTASJON*
*ÅLESUND*
*NORWAY*

He stands in the passage with it and reads it several times.

The ring on the *Å* is a fraction too careful. It is too round and sits too squarely. It has been put on by a man who has never written the letter before and has had to look up where it goes, and has then put it there as though labelling a jar that someone else would have to read. The string is tied with a reef knot. Reggie ties everything with a reef knot and always has, and Spilsbury used to cut them, when it was his turn to open things, because he could never be bothered to undo them.

He undoes this one. It takes him some minutes in the passage.

The paper is well printed. The plates have come up better than he had feared, and figure one is exactly as they drew it, the decline at fifty-one days with the axes in Reggie's hand. There are two names at the top, and he looks at them side by side for a while. It is the only place in the world they will now appear that way, and he knows the paper will be cited for about thirty years, and he finds that a great deal of consolation and no consolation whatever.

He gives forty-eight of them away, to people who ought to read them. He keeps two. The brown paper and the label go into the stove in his room that evening.

He stands in front of the stove with the iron door open for longer than it takes the label to burn.

---

He writes the first letter at the end of August, for September, as the deed requires.

It takes him an evening. That is not because it is long. It is because every sentence has to be weighed for emptiness, and he has never before in his life written a letter to that man with the object of saying nothing. He finds that it is very difficult, and he respects anyone who does it for a living. In 1950 they had worked out a form in which anybody could read a letter and nobody would, and he had written every third Tuesday in it for six years. Every word of it was true. A sentence about formalin was about formalin, and the weather was the weather. But he had always known that the man at the other end would read three other things off them as well, and had written them to be read. That was the whole pleasure of the form.

This letter must be read by the man at the other end and give him nothing. It must be a letter he could have got from anybody. Spilsbury writes it four times before it is empty enough.

> *Dear Sir,*
>
> *In accordance with the terms of deposit, I write to inform the Institute that the depositor of accession 197 is resident at the above address and may be reached there.*
>
> *I have received the offprints, for which I am obliged.*
>
> *Yours faithfully,*
>
> *A. Spilsbury*

*Dear Sir* is what one writes to a Director of Research. *Yours faithfully* follows *Dear Sir*, as the handbooks say. He has closed a great many letters that way to a great many institutions, and never once thought about what the phrase says. He thinks about it now, with the pen in his hand, for some time.

Then he signs it and seals it and takes it down to the post office on the quay himself.

---

The acknowledgement comes back in October.

It is on the Institute's paper, in Reggie's own hand, the writing hand, not the capitals. It is three lines long and there is nothing in it. He had known there would be nothing in it. He had spent an evening making certain there would be nothing in his own for it to answer.

He reads it standing at the window with the harbour behind it.

The signature is last. *R. Tar, Director of Research.* He looks at it for a long while. There is a gap after the *R.*, a very slight one, a fraction wider than the gap Reggie leaves between an initial and a name on anything else. The downstroke of the *T* is heavier than the rest, as though the pen had been set down and lifted and set down again.

For seven years every letter that came to him in that hand had ended *R.*, and stopped there, with the full stop. The hand had wanted to stop there again. The man had made it go on.

He reads that off the page as easily as he would read a temperature. And he understands that this, too, has been left there to be read, whether or not the man knew he was leaving it.

He puts the letter in the stove.

It goes on like that. March and September, in the form, from him; April and October, in the form, from the Martin, in Reggie's hand. Each time the signature has the gap. Each time he reads it and burns it, and each time he walks out afterwards to the end of the quay and stands there until the cold is somewhere he can locate.

He does not think that it is getting better. He thinks it is getting familiar, which is a different thing, and he has always been careful not to mistake one for the other.

---

In February 1960 the station needs his room.

Rasmussen is apologetic. There is a new appointment, a physiologist from Oslo with a wife and a child, and the station's two rooms on the quay are needed. Spilsbury is found a room in the town instead, at Kongens gate 18, from the first of April, with a Fru Brekke who keeps a very clean house and has a canary. So in March there is a change of address to report, and on the morning he is to write it he goes down to the quay before breakfast with a thermometer.

He has done this most mornings since 1957. It is not for the station. The station has its own instruments and a boy who reads them. He does it because he has done it since he was nineteen: he takes the temperature of wherever he happens to be, and writes it down, out of interest. He has no book to write it in now. He keeps them in his head, where he has found he can keep a surprising amount if he is careful.

It is a grey still morning at the beginning of March, with the ice not quite off the inner harbour. He lets the thermometer down on its string from the end of the quay into the Brosund, and waits the three minutes, and brings it up, and reads it.

Four degrees.

He stands at the end of the quay with the wet string in his fingers and the thermometer in his palm.

It is November, and the whole of the building is at four degrees because the boiler has gone. He has had a thermometer on his bench all evening, reading it on the hour, out of interest. The long tank on the ground floor is at four degrees to within a tenth, and he has noted the coincidence, and decided that it is not a significant one. And there are footsteps on the stairs, taken two at a time.

He had been wrong about the coincidence. It had been the most significant thing he ever measured.

He goes back to his room and writes the change of address. He writes it in the form, and it is perfectly empty, and it takes him ten minutes, because he has done this five times now and has become quite good at emptiness. Then he sits with the pen above the paper, below the paragraph and above *Yours faithfully*, for a long time.

He knows what it will do. He knows that better than anybody alive.

He knows that the man cannot answer it. Any answer that is not the form is a line, in the Director of Research's own hand, from a licensed station to a name in a drawer in Bedford Row and in a Colonial Office file that is read every three years. He knows that the whole of 1957 was spent, with some precision, to prevent that line from ever being drawn, and that Frank is somewhere because of it. He knows that the man at the other end will read the line, and understand it at once, and not be able to do anything whatever with it. That is the cruellest thing he could send.

And he knows that it is the only true sentence he has, and that there is nowhere else to put it.

He has no book. He burnt the books in a brick incinerator in February 1957, in order that there should be no paper. There has been no paper since. There has been the form, and the stove, and a column in a bank in London with nothing on the other side of it. He has kept every measurement since in his head, and his head is very good. But it has turned out not to be the same as writing a thing down. A measurement you cannot put anywhere does not stay a measurement. It stays a weight.

He writes it.

> *The sound was at four degrees this morning.*

He looks at it for some time. He does not strike it through. He signs the letter, and seals it, and takes it down to the post office on the quay, and puts it in the slot himself, and stands in the post office afterwards for a minute or so with his hand still on the brass. The clerk asks him whether he is quite well.

He says that he is obliged to him. That is true. It is not what he was asked.

---

The acknowledgement comes in April, to Kongens gate 18, the day after he has moved in.

Fru Brekke brings it up to his room with his coffee, because it has a foreign stamp, and she is interested in foreign stamps, and asks whether she may have it when he is done. He says that she may. She puts the coffee down beside it and goes out, and the canary sings in the room below.

It is typed.

He sits on the edge of the bed with it and does not open it for some minutes, because he has seen that through the envelope. The address is in Hester's hand, as it always is. But the paper inside is folded in three, and a typed sheet takes a fold differently from a written one, and he can see the ghost of the lines through the envelope against the light from the window.

Every acknowledgement for three years has been written by hand. Five of them. He has burnt all five, and could reproduce each of them, down to the gap after the *R*.

He opens it.

> *Dear Sir,*
>
> *I acknowledge with thanks your letter of the 3rd instant, and have noted the change of address.*
>
> *Yours faithfully,*
>
> *R. Tar*
> *Director of Research*

It is Hester's machine. He would know it anywhere. The lower-case *e* sits a hair high, and he read a great many Institute letters typed on it in the autumn of 1956. It is Reggie who has typed it, not Hester. The spacing after the full stops is doubled, which Hester does not do and Reggie always does. There are two very slight overstrikes in *acknowledge*, where a finger has gone down and come off and gone down again.

The signature is pressed so hard into the paper that he can feel it from the back.

He sits on the bed and reads it, and there is nothing in it, and it is the loudest thing he has ever been sent.

The typewriter is the answer. The man could not trust his own hand with this one. He had sat down at Hester's machine because a hand writes what it wants, and he could not afford for it to write what it wanted. He had typed the form, and nothing else. And somewhere between *noted the change of address* and *Yours faithfully* he had very probably sat for a long time with his fingers on the keys, deciding not to type something, and then decided, and typed the close.

It is *no*. It is *I cannot*. It is also, since the man has never been able to do anything without leaving a mark on it for somebody to read, the plainest possible statement that he had wanted to.

Spilsbury sits on the edge of the bed in Fru Brekke's clean room with the canary singing underneath, and does the arithmetic, since he cannot stop doing it.

He had wanted to put one true sentence somewhere. He had put it where the only man who could read it could do nothing with it but refuse. And the refusal had cost that man something, exactly how much Spilsbury can read off the overstrikes. He had not needed to know how that man was. He had needed to be read once, and he had spent the other man to get it, as he had spent him in the office in 1957. The difference is that this time there was nothing to buy with it. Nobody's licence was saved by the sound at four degrees. It was for himself.

*Result of arithmetic*, he thinks. And he knows what the entry would be, and he does not compose it. It is the first entry in his life he has declined to compose, even in his head, where no one else can read it.

He gives Fru Brekke the envelope, for the stamp.

He keeps the letter until the evening, which he has not done with any of the others. Then he puts it in Fru Brekke's kitchen stove, with her permission, since there is no stove in his room. She watches him do it with an expression that suggests she has had lodgers before. He thanks her for the coffee, and goes out to the end of the quay in a fine rain, and stands there until he cannot feel his hands.

---

The September letter goes in the form.

So does the March letter after it. He goes down to the quay on the first morning of March 1961 with the thermometer, because he goes down every morning, and lets it down into the Brosund on its string, and waits three minutes, and brings it up.

Three point eight.

He looks at it for a moment in the grey light, with the gulls going over and the first of the sealers fitting out along the far quay for the West Ice.

Then he shakes the water off the bulb, and winds the string round his fingers, and puts the thermometer in his coat pocket, and goes up to the station to write the letter. It says that the depositor of accession 197 remains resident at Kongens gate 18, Ålesund, and may be reached there.

It takes him ten minutes. He has become very good at it.
