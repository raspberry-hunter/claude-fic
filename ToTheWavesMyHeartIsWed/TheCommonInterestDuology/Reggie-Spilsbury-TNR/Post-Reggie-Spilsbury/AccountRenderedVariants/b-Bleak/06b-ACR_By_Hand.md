# By Hand

He takes a room at the Charing Cross Hotel for the six nights, as he had said he would.

He had not needed to. The Zoological Society had offered him a room at a college in Bloomsbury, with the other speakers from abroad, at a third of the price. He had declined it by letter in May, on the grounds that he had already made arrangements. That was true, in the sense that he had made them in a letter in April to a man who had not answered.

He had written *no answer is an answer, and I shall read it as one*. He had meant it. He has read it as one since about the middle of June, which is when a letter from the West Indies, had one been sent in April, would reasonably have arrived. He had gone on reading the post at Kongens gate 18 until the end of August anyway, and knew exactly what he was doing and why, and did not stop. He has not found that knowing why a man does a thing is much help in stopping.

So he is at the Charing Cross Hotel. He arrives on the Monday evening in rain and is given a room on the fourth floor with a view of the station roof, and he unpacks his two cases. He goes down to the bar at nine and sits in a corner with a whisky and water that he does not drink, facing the door.

He sits there until half past ten.

He notes, while he is sitting, that this is precisely the kind of thing he would once have written down, with the date and the hour and the result. He notes that he has no book. He notes that he had told the man, in April, *I shall not pretend to any other reason*, and that the man had not asked for one, and that he is sitting here anyway, without one, pretending to nothing. It is a curious sensation to be entirely honest with nobody watching. He has not had it very often.

At half past ten he goes up to bed.

---

The paper is on the Tuesday at half past ten, in the meeting room at Regent's Park. The room is fuller than he had expected, since he is not a man people come to hear for the pleasure of it.

It goes well. He had known it would. It is twenty years of hooded seals from the West Ice, taken apart very carefully by a man who has had nothing much else to do in the evenings, and it is good work, and the room knows it. He speaks for forty minutes without notes. The lanternist is very competent, and the slides come up in the right order, and he is corrected once from the floor, by a young woman from Aberdeen, on a point of nomenclature, and she is right. He says so, and thanks her, and she goes pink. A man in the third row he has never seen before asks a question so intelligent that Spilsbury has to think for a moment before he answers it. That does not happen to him often either, and he enjoys it.

Once, while the lanternist is changing the plate before the vascular series, he looks at the room.

He does it row by row, from the front, as he would read a column. He knows he is doing it and does it thoroughly, because if one is going to do a thing one should do it properly. Twenty rows. Perhaps two hundred faces. He knows about thirty of them, and at least ten of those would be glad to see him fail. None of them is the one.

He had known that none of them would be. He had read the answer in June. He reads it again now, in two hundred faces, in about the time it takes to change a glass plate, and finds that it comes out the same.

"The vascular series," he says, when the plate comes up. "I shall take this rather slowly, since it is the part that is new."

---

The young man is waiting at the back of the room when it empties.

Spilsbury does not see him at first. He is at the lectern putting his slides back in their box in order, and dealing with the young woman from Aberdeen, who has a second point and is right about that as well. Then he looks up and sees a man standing by the door with a flat case under his arm and his hat in his hand, waiting, the way someone waits who has decided to wait as long as it takes.

He is not so young now. He had been very young in the spring of 1957, with a darkroom and three pencils in a row at the end of a table. He is heavier. There are lines at the corners of his eyes from squinting through a viewfinder in the sun. He has the face of a man who has been happily married for some years and has a child who gets up at five.

"Weald," says Spilsbury.

"Dr Spilsbury."

They shake hands. John Weald's hand is dry and square and does not hold on longer than it should. Spilsbury is aware, as he takes it, that it is the first hand he has shaken in thirteen years that has been on that island, and he notes that, and puts it away.

"I didn't know you'd be here," he says. "I did not see the Institute on the list."

"I'm not with the Institute. Not this month. I'm on home leave." John looks at his hat. "Nora and I have got the child at my mother's in Islington for six weeks, so the grandparents can get a good look before we're all too grand for them. I saw the programme in the *Times*. Nora saw it, actually. She put it in front of me at breakfast and said *go*." He hesitates. "She's very sorry not to have come herself. She's taken the child to the Tower. She said it was that or the Tower, and the child won, and I was to say so."

"Please tell Mrs Weald that the Tower is a much better choice than a hooded seal."

"I'll tell her. She won't believe you."

There is a small silence. The young woman from Aberdeen has gone. Somebody at the front is stacking chairs.

"I've got something for you," says John Weald. "If you've got a quarter of an hour. It isn't — nobody's sent it. It's mine. I just thought — " He stops, and does what he always did at the long table when a sentence would not come out right, which was to stop trying to make the sentence and say the useful thing. "It's photographs of the station. I took them this summer. I thought you'd want to see the station."

Spilsbury looks at the flat case under the man's arm.

"Yes," he says. "I should like that very much."

---

They walk down through the gardens to the canal, because the meeting room is full of people who would like to talk about seals, and because John says he can't do this with anybody listening. They sit on a bench on the towpath under the plane trees with the water going past very slowly, brown and still, with a leaf on it.

John opens the case on his knees.

The prints are ten by eight, on glossy paper, and there are about thirty of them. They have been printed by a man who knows exactly what he is doing and has taken a great deal of trouble. Spilsbury takes them one at a time, as they are handed to him, and looks at each for as long as he wants, and John does not hurry him.

The jetty, from the water, at low tide, with the weed on the piles.

The row, from the top of the path, in the evening, with the lamps not yet lit. The spare hut at the top has its roof pitched the right way now, and he looks at that for some time.

The long room, empty, with the shutters half open and the light coming in across the benches in bars. There are new benches, and a new microscope on the far one. The old one is still on the near one, the one with the eyepiece from Ashby's of Southport. He can see the name on the barrel if he holds the print to the light, because John has focused on it. He does not know whether John has done that on purpose.

The office, with a young woman at Hester's desk. It isn't Hester's desk any more. Dr Leslie is at the window with a sheaf of papers, not looking at the camera, with her mouth set in a way he recognises exactly.

The north pen, with the whole of the rail and the water beyond it, and two animals in the shallows.

"That's the calf," says John, "on the left. She's thirteen. She's the size of her mother now, but everybody still calls her the calf. Her mother's getting old. Pobil worries about her teeth."

Spilsbury looks at the calf for a long time. She had been a few weeks old, with a dressing on her flipper, when Halloran stood at that rail and said *well, that's what it's for, isn't it*.

He hands it back.

John does not give him the next one straight away. He holds it on his knee, face down, and looks at the canal.

"There's one with him in it," he says. "I didn't mean him to be in any of them. I tried not to. I did the whole set at times I knew he'd be in the long room or down at the pens with the students. But he walked into that one while I had the shutter open, and I'd have had to lose the frame. When I printed it I thought I'd leave it out. Then I thought that wasn't mine to decide." He looks at the print on his knee, face down. "So you can decide. I won't mind which. I'll take it home and put it in the drawer if you'd rather."

Spilsbury looks at the back of the print, which is white, with *July 1970* on it in pencil in John's small upright hand.

He had once told this man that his hatching was better than his own, on a jetty, in March 1957. It had not been true, and John had known it was not true, and had gone scarlet anyway. Spilsbury has thought of that since, now and then, with something that is not quite regret.

"I should like to see it," he says.

John turns it over and hands it to him.

---

It is the north pen again, from the other end, from the steps, with the light behind.

There is a man at the rail. He is side-on to the camera, about twelve feet off, in his shirtsleeves, with a jacket on. He is looking down into the water at something out of frame, and does not know he is being photographed. His spectacles are pushed up on his forehead, the way he pushes them up when he wants to see something close without them. His left hand is flat against his chest, on the left side, over the breast pocket of the jacket, and resting there.

Spilsbury looks at it.

He looks at it for a long time. John sits beside him on the bench and does not say anything, and the canal goes by.

He had not seen this face since the nineteenth of March 1957, when it came down a jetty smiling with its hat in its hand and gave him a colleague's handshake. He had known he would not see it again. He had costed that on a Saturday in February, at a table, in lamplight, and found that he could afford it, just, if he did not look at it too long.

He is looking at it now. It is a photograph, and it cannot look back, and he finds that that makes no difference whatever.

He looks at it, after a while, the way he looks at anything, because it is the only way he knows how to look.

The man is thinner. That is the first thing. The shirt is loose across the shoulders in a way it was not in 1957. The jacket hangs from the shoulder points and not from the chest. The posts of the north pen rail are four feet apart; Halloran measured them with a tape in 1957, and Spilsbury had watched him. He knows the width of those shoulders as exactly as he knows anything he has ever measured. So the proportions can be taken off the print, roughly, with a thumb, against the posts, and Spilsbury takes them. He makes it something over a stone. A stone and a half, perhaps. The hair at the temples is grey, which is not news and is not a finding. The line of the jaw is sharper. The colour cannot be told from a monochrome print, but the skin under the eyes is darker than the skin of the cheek. That can be told, and he tells it.

And the hand.

It is flat on the left breast, over the pocket, over the heart. It is not holding anything. It is not reaching for anything. It is resting there, with the fingers a little spread, the way a man rests a hand on a place that has been hurting and has stopped, to make sure it has stopped.

He has seen men stand like that. He has seen them on the second floor at the Labs, on the stairs, in the cold, men of fifty who had done too many nights, stopping on the landing with a hand just there. And on the stairs the man in the print used to take two at a time.

"How is he," says Spilsbury.

It comes out with no question mark on it. He hears it go.

John Weald is quiet for a moment, looking at the water.

"I told Nora in 1957 that I'd never say another word about any of it to anybody," he says. "I said it on a bench in Clerkenwell. I meant it. I've kept it for thirteen years." He turns his hat round in his hands. "She said this morning that there's a difference between keeping a promise and hiding behind one, and that I'd know which this was when I got here. She was right. She usually is."

"You needn't —"

"He's not well," says John. "I don't know what it is. Nobody's told me and I haven't asked. But he's not well. He takes the verandah steps one at a time now. He's done that since — for years — but this last year he stops halfway. He came in from a stranding last winter at six in the morning, grey. I mean grey, the colour of that." He nods at the canal. "Dr Sealy is on at him, Pobil's wife. She's the doctor at the Bay. She comes over on Tuesdays now, and I don't think she comes over on Tuesdays for Pobil. He says he's all right. He says it to everybody. He'd say it to you."

"Yes," says Spilsbury. "He would."

"And he still goes out in the boat at night when there's an animal on the reef, and nobody can stop him. Not Pobil, not anybody. He says somebody's got to go." John's voice is quite level. "That's all I know. I'm the photographer. I just see things. I don't know what they are."

Spilsbury sits on the bench with the print on his knee.

He knows what they are.

---

"Is there anything," John says, after a while, "you'd want me to say to him? When we go back. I won't say where I got it. I'll just say it."

The canal goes past. A boy on the far bank is throwing bread to a pair of swans, and they are ignoring him with great dignity.

He thinks about it properly, because the question deserves it.

He could say *the paper went well*. That is true, and it is the sort of thing a colleague says, and it would cost nothing to say. He could say *I have seen the photographs*. He could say *four degrees*, and John would carry it without knowing what it was, as he carries things. He could say *take the tablets, whatever they are; do not go out in the boat; I have done the arithmetic on you from a photograph with my thumb, and I do not like the result*. He could say the thing he said in the spare hut with the wrong roof, with his hand on the back of the man's neck. There is nowhere else to put that either.

And every one of those, if John carried it across the Atlantic and said it on the verandah, would be a thing the man would have to decide what to do with. He would have to not answer it, or answer it. He would have to carry it round with him and put his hand on it. He would have to walk round it every day for the rest of his life, however long that is, on an island where everybody knows where it is.

He had put one sentence in a letter in 1960 and watched it come back to him typed, with the signature pressed through the paper. He had put six words in a letter in April, and they have come back to him as nothing at all, which is harder to read than the typing and means the same. He has spent the man twice now for his own sake. He is not going to do it a third time on a towpath through a man who takes photographs.

"No," he says. "Nothing. Don't tell him anything. Don't tell him you saw me."

John looks at him.

"That isn't unkindness," says Spilsbury, and he hears his own voice not be level, and does not try to make it level. "He has had letters from me that he could not answer. I will not send him another by hand. Tell him nothing. If he asks you whether you saw me, tell him the truth, because you will, and so would he. But don't raise it."

"All right."

"And don't — " He stops.

John waits.

"Nothing," says Spilsbury. "Thank you. That is all."

John Weald nods, and starts to put the prints back in the case, in order, one at a time, square. When he gets to the last one, the one on Spilsbury's knee, he stops with his hand out and does not take it.

"Those are for you," he says. "All of them. That's what I brought them for. I've got the negatives." He closes the case on the rest and holds it out. "I'd like you to have that one as well. I'd rather it was with you than in my drawer."

Spilsbury takes the case, and puts the print on the top, face up, and closes the lid on it.

"Thank you, Weald."

"John," says John. "It's been thirteen years."

"John," says Spilsbury, and they shake hands again on the towpath, and John goes off up the steps to the bridge with his hat on to find his wife and his child at the Tower. Spilsbury sits on the bench with the case on his knees until the plane trees have begun to throw their shadows right across the water.

---

He sits in the bar at the Charing Cross that evening, and the next, and the next, until the Saturday, facing the door. He is entirely aware of what he is doing and entirely aware that it is irrational, and does it anyway, and goes up to bed at half past ten each night. On the Sunday he takes the boat train from King's Cross to Newcastle, for the Bergen boat, with his two cases and the flat case under his arm, and does not let the porter take it.

At Kongens gate 18, Fru Brekke's canary has died in his absence, and she is very low about it. He sits with her in the kitchen for an hour on his first evening back and lets her tell him the whole of its history. He finds that he is good at this, and that he minds about the canary more than he would have expected, and that he does not know which of several things he is minding about.

He puts the prints away in the drawer of the table in his room, all of them but one.

The one he props on the table against the wall, where he can see it from the bed, where he has not had anything propped in ten years. It is the first thing from that island he has kept. For thirteen years he has burnt everything that came, so that there should be no paper, and he looks at this and understands that it is not paper in that sense. It is a photograph of a licensed station, taken by its own photographer, of its own staff at work. It could be put in front of any man in any hat in the world and he would see nothing in it but a man at a rail.

So he keeps it. He has not been allowed to keep anything since 1957, and he has not allowed himself anything either, and he is going to keep this.

In the mornings he goes down to the quay with the thermometer, as he always does, and lets it down on its string into the Brosund, and waits three minutes, and brings it up. It is ten degrees in the second week of October, and falling. He reads it, and keeps it, since he has nowhere to write it, and puts the thermometer back in his coat.

Then he goes back up the hill to Kongens gate and stands at the table for a moment before he goes to the station, and looks at the man at the rail with his hand on his chest.

He does it every morning. He takes the measurement again with his thumb, from the rail, and gets the same result, and knows it is a photograph and that the result cannot change. He does it anyway. It is the only instrument he has.
