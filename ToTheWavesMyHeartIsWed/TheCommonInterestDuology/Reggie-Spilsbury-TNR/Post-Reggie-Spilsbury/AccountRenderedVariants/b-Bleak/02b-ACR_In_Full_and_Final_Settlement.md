# In Full and Final Settlement

*Not without asking him.*

Spilsbury has the answer to it before the man has finished the sentence. It has been ready since Thursday, and this time he gives it.

"No," he says.

Tar takes his hands down from his face.

"Asking him is a line," Spilsbury says. "It is worse than a line on paper. It is a line in a railway timetable, with a hotel register at one end of it. A letter to Southport from this island is a letter from the West Indies to a man with a conviction, and you told me yourself six years ago what that looks like. A visit is the Director of Research of a licensed institute, in the one month in which an enquiry agent may be anywhere in the north of England, sitting in that man's front room. There is no way to ask him that does not put you in Southport. And he cannot be protected by being consulted. He can only be protected by being left alone, and I am not able to leave him alone. So the least I can do is not also make him a party to it."

"The *least*."

"Yes."

"And the arithmetic comes out the same whether he's asked or not."

"It does."

"So why trouble him with a question when the answer won't change the result." Tar says it flatly, with no question in it, and Spilsbury hears his own sentence come back across the table in the other man's voice. "That's what you were going to say next."

"It was."

"It's the reason you'd give a committee."

"It is the reason."

Tar looks at him for a while. Then he says, very quietly, "You said you'd tell me what you'd have written. On Thursday. When you'd told me the rest."

That is true. Spilsbury had said it, and has told him the rest, and there is nothing left to put in front of it.

"*Result of arithmetic: F. N. Accepted.*" He does not look away. "There are three before it. Each says *declined*, and each time the reason was you, and each time the cost of declining was mine. I should have written underneath it, if I had been honest with the page, *fourth time. First time the cost is not mine.*"

He hears it.

A written sentence stays where it was put. A spoken one goes out into the room and comes back as though somebody else had said it, and he hears a man at a station table say *the cost is not mine* in a level voice about fifteen pounds a quarter and a house in Southport and a stranger on a doorstep. He knows precisely what kind of man says that. He has sat across tables from several of them and written letters about them to firms.

Tar is watching him.

"You heard that," Tar says.

"I heard it."

And having heard it, he does what he does with anything that has come out wrong, which is run it again with everything in.

He runs Tar forward to the inspector's table in March. Tar says the canteen's sentence, or doesn't; either way he is alive, and on the licence, and in post. And then he is gone from him by Easter. Not from the Martin; from the hut at the end of the row. Not in a temper. Tar does not leave people in a temper. He goes quietly, the way he left the Labs, having seen that if he stayed he would go on doing it.

So the sentence was wrong after all. The cost is his. It has only been deferred until Easter.

He looks at that for some time, sitting in the lamplight with the envelope squared to the edge of the table and the bread and cheese beside it. He finds that he can pay it. He has been paying for things since he was nineteen, and has a very exact idea of what he can afford, and this he can afford, just, provided he does not look at it too long. That is the whole of the difference between the sentence he said and the sentence he now believes. It has made the thing possible. He notes that he is not proud of it, and that the note changes nothing.

He does not say it.

He finds that he cannot. He has said everything else at this table tonight, the notebooks and the money and the six years, and he would say this too if it were a thing about himself. It is not. It is a thing about the man across the table, what he will do, and when, and why. Spilsbury will not put that in front of him as though it had already been decided. It is the first thing tonight he has kept back, and he notes it as that, and keeps it.

"It does not change the result," he says.

Tar stares at him.

"You heard it," he says, "and it doesn't change the result."

"No."

"*Christ*, Alec." Tar is on his feet. He doesn't seem to have decided to be. "Then I'll go. I'll go to Southport myself."

"And be the line."

"I'll go before the agent does. I'll find a reason. Hester's wanted somebody at the Colonial Office for months, and there's — I'll find one, it isn't hard, and I'll go out there after dark and sit in his front room and tell him."

"And the agent will come a fortnight after you," says Spilsbury, "because he cannot help it, because there is fifteen pounds a quarter pointing at that door whatever either of us does. And when he asks the landlady who else has called, she will describe you. You do not have a face that people forget, Reggie. Frank will have been exposed, and you will have gone to him first, and between the two of you there will be precisely the thing the whole business is meant to prevent. You will have made it cost him and bought nothing with it."

Tar stands with his hands on the back of the chair.

"And the inspector," Spilsbury says, since he is saying all of it. "If he asks you whether you knew, and you say the true thing, it holds. If you give him anything less, he will write down that the Director of Research declined to say what he knew. And then he will look round for somebody else to ask, and there is another man on this row. He has no wife. His name is on the licence under yours. He has been your closest friend for four years and he lives the other side of that wall."

"Leave Pascual out of it."

"I should be very glad to," says Spilsbury. "It is you who will have to."

---

"Don't do this," Tar says.

He says it standing up, with his hands on the chair, and his voice is not the voice. Spilsbury has heard the voice turned on a great many people and has never once had it turned on him, and it is not being turned on him now. This is only the man.

"I'm asking you. Not him. I'm asking *you*. Don't."

Spilsbury has given him everything he has asked for since the fifteenth of November 1946. He has kept count, because he keeps count of everything, and there has been one refusal in ten years, in February of last year, over a firm he was dismissed from four days later. He had not known until this moment that the one refusal had been practice.

"I know you are asking," he says. "No."

Tar doesn't move for a moment.

"When?"

"I will not tell you when."

"That's —" Tar stops. His mouth does something. "That's withholding."

"Yes. I have told you what I am going to do, and that I will not say when. I have told you to your face. It is the most I can do and still do it."

"It's the same sentence with more words in it."

"Yes."

"You've never once —"

"No," Spilsbury agrees. "I have not."

Tar takes his jacket off the hook on the door. He doesn't put it on. He stands holding it by the collar as if it were a thing he'd been handed and hadn't decided what to do with.

"I'm going out," he says. "I'm not going anywhere. I'm going out."

"Yes."

He goes. Spilsbury hears him on the path, not going to Pobil's, where the lamp is still out, and not up the row, but down past the sick bay towards the pens, walking fast and then not.

He sits at the table.

After a while he eats the bread and cheese, all of it, slowly, because a man who is going to the Bay on Monday afternoon and writing two letters there will make errors if he has not eaten. He notes that that is the reason. He notes that Pobil told him to, and that it had not occurred to him to do anything Pobil told him until now.

---

Reggie is at the north pen rail at six when Pascual comes down with the buckets.

He's been there since about one. He's sat on the rail and stood at the rail and at one point, around four, lain down on the planking with his jacket rolled up under his head and watched the cow and the calf breathe in the shallows by the lamp on the post. The calf comes up every forty seconds or so. The cow comes up when she does, and turns her head to look, and goes down again. He counted it for an hour, because it was a thing he could count.

Pascual stops at the top of the steps with a bucket in each hand.

"Did he tell you?"

"He told me."

"And?"

And Reggie feels it arrive before he's decided anything. It comes up from wherever it lives, the lift in the chest and the face settling, the warmth coming into the voice like a lamp being turned up.

"*And* we've had a very tiresome letter from some solicitors in Bedford Row," he says, "who'd like 197 back, which they're not getting, and Alec's going to write them something withering. You know what he's like with a solicitor. I should think they'll need a lie-down. Is that the calf's fish? She had a go at my bootlace about half an hour ago, I think she's feeling better —"

Pascual puts both buckets down.

"Don't do that," he says.

"Do what?"

"That. The voice. Don't do it with me, *hombre*. I've heard you do it to the Colonel and a man from Agriculture and a Commander with a moustache. You don't do it with me."

Reggie stops.

He stands at the rail in the grey light with his jacket over his arm, and he thinks, quite clearly, the way he'd think at a bench: *anything I tell him now is a thing he knows in March*. If Pascual knows, then when a man with a leather pen case asks Pascual a question, Pascual has two choices, and one of them is a lie and the other is the file. And Pascual won't lie. He's the worst liar on the island. He goes red to the ears when he says he's enjoyed Hester's pudding.

"I can't tell you," he says, without the voice. "Not won't. Can't."

Pascual looks at him for a long moment.

"Is he going to do something stupid?"

"He's going to do something correct."

That lands. Reggie watches it. Pascual's face does what Pascual's face does, which is everything at once and hide none of it, and then it closes, quite slowly, like a man shutting a door on a room he's been told not to go into. He picks up one bucket.

"You'll tell me when you can," he says.

"Yes."

"You'll *tell* me. Not that." He jerks his chin at nothing, at the air where the voice had been. "Tell me."

He goes down the steps to the water with the bucket, and the calf comes over to him at once, and he talks to her in Spanish in a low steady voice for a long time with his back to the rail.

---

He works out the Southport arrangement on the Sunday afternoon, in the office, with Hester's correspondence file open in front of him as a reason to be in the office.

It's good. It isn't hard. Hester has wanted somebody at the West India Department before the inspection since November; that's London. Port Erin has had a standing invitation out to this Institute since 'fifty-four to come and see their tank room; that's the Isle of Man, which is Liverpool, which is eighteen miles and twenty minutes on the electric from Southport. He could be at the Adelphi on Friday week as the Director of Research of the William Martin Institute, going to the Isle of Man, and be out on the electric after dark and back on the last one.

He goes and finds Hester and tells her he'll take the Tuesday boat and do the Colonial Office for her after all. Hester says, "Tuesday," and writes it on her pad, and looks at him over it for rather longer than the word needs.

He sees very well what Alec had said to him at the table. He has it all, the landlady and the face and the fortnight, and he's decided to do it anyway. There's a way to go that isn't as bad as Alec thinks. There's a way to be seen in Liverpool and not in Southport. He'll find it on the aeroplane. He's good at finding things on aeroplanes.

Alec is at the bench in the long room all Sunday, doing the figure legends for the paper with Jean, and when Reggie comes in at five they talk about the scale bar on figure three for twenty minutes, in front of Jean, perfectly civilly. Alec says the bar should be a centimetre and Reggie says two, and Jean says she doesn't care as long as somebody decides, and Alec says two. He gives way at once, without any argument at all. Reggie notices that and doesn't know what to do with it.

He sleeps that night, a little, on his own side of the bed. When he wakes at three Alec is lying flat on his back with his eyes open, looking at the ceiling, and neither of them says anything. After a while Reggie puts out a hand in the dark and finds Alec's wrist on the blanket and holds it, round the bone, and Alec lets him, and they lie like that until it gets light.

---

On Monday, Mrs Greaves comes over on the eight o'clock boat and does the row.

Reggie is in the long room at a quarter past three with the calf's sheets when he hears the engine. It's the boat going back to the Bay with Mrs Greaves and the linen, as it goes every Monday. He doesn't look up, because it's the Monday boat and he doesn't look up for it.

Then he does.

From the long-room window you can see the jetty and about a hundred yards of water. The boat is off the end of the jetty, turning, and Alleyne's boy is at the tiller, and Mrs Greaves is in the waist with the big canvas bundle of the week's sheets at her feet. And beside her, in the stern, with his hat on his knee and a long envelope on top of the hat, sits Alec.

Reggie gets to the jetty. He doesn't know how. He knows that he's standing at the end of it with the calf's sheet still in his hand and the boat a quarter of a mile off and going away, and that Alec does not look back, and that Mrs Greaves does. She lifts a hand to him, the way she would to any of the doctors, and he lifts his.

*I will not tell you when.*

It hadn't been Tuesday. Alec hadn't said Tuesday. Alec had never said anything that wasn't true, and Reggie had filled in *Tuesday* himself, because Tuesday was the boat he'd have gone on, and he'd thought there was time.

He sits down on the bollard at the end of the jetty.

He goes through it once, because he can't stop himself going through it. The letters go from the Bay tonight. They'll be in London by the end of the week. The agent will be in Southport before Reggie's even through the Colonial Office. And if the Director of Research then turns up in Southport a week behind a letter in Alec's hand that points there, he's the face the landlady describes. He's the thing that turns *Spilsbury and Nicholson* into *Spilsbury and Nicholson and Tar*. He's what Frank will have been spent for nothing to prevent.

There's nothing left to go and do. That's what Alec bought on the Monday boat. It isn't time. It's the absence of anything useful to do with it.

He sits on the bollard until the boat's round the head. Then he goes up to the office and tells Hester that he won't want the Tuesday boat after all, that it's not necessary, that the Colonial Office can have a letter. Hester writes it on her pad under the other. She doesn't ask. She has never in six years asked him a question she didn't want the answer to on paper.

"Iris Greaves," she says instead, not looking up, "tells me Dr Spilsbury was very kind to her on the crossing. He carried the bundle up the steps at the Bay for her. She said she'd never had a doctor do that."

Reggie goes out of the office and stands in the passage with his back against the wall for a minute or so before he can do anything else.

---

Alec comes back on the Tuesday boat, the one Reggie would have been on.

He comes up the row with a case in each hand and goes into the hut at the end and comes out again with the other two, and carries them up past the house to the spare hut with the wrong roof, the one the Institute offered him in May. Reggie watches him do it from the door of the long room. Pascual carried those cases down the row in May without slowing, past the spare hut and on to the last one. Alec carries them back up by himself, two trips, and it takes him about eight minutes, and nobody on the station helps him, because Reggie doesn't move, and everybody else is being tactful.

He comes to the long room at four, as he'd come every day of the last nine months, and stands at the other end of the bench.

"The letters went from the Bay at six yesterday evening," he says. "To Bedford Row, and to my bank. I cabled the bank as well, so that there should be no doubt about the first of March."

"What did you tell Bedford Row?"

"That I decline paragraph two. That since November 1950 I have made a gift of fifteen pounds a quarter to Mr F. Nicholson, a former colleague, of Southport, in Lancashire; that I have done so freely and without solicitation; and that this is the whole of my private affairs in which their client could conceivably have an interest, and that none of it touches any member of this Institute's staff."

"You gave them his name."

"They had his name. It was on a list in October 1950. I gave them the reason."

"You gave them the *town*."

"Yes."

Reggie puts the calf's sheet down on the bench, very carefully, square, the way Alec squares things.

"Mrs Greaves'll do the beds on Monday," he says.

"Yes."

"She'll find two slept in. Mine and yours. One at each end."

"That was the purpose."

"I know what the purpose was, Alec." He hears his voice go and lets it. "I sat on that bollard for an hour yesterday, and I know exactly what the purpose was, I could draw you the diagram. You told me the truth at every single stage and I couldn't do a thing with any of it."

"No."

"Did you *know* I'd think Tuesday?"

Alec is quiet for a moment.

"I thought it likely," he says. "It was the boat you would have gone on."

---

They work.

That's the part Reggie won't be able to explain to anybody afterwards, if there were anybody to explain it to. They have a paper going out in June, and figure one is the decline, and figure three needs its scale bar, and Jean has to have the legends by the twentieth. So for three weeks they stand at the same bench in the long room from nine until six and do the work, and it's good work. It's some of the best they've done. Alec is precise and patient and gives way on every single thing Reggie raises, and after the first week Reggie stops raising things to see whether he will.

At night Alec goes up the row to the spare hut and Reggie goes down to the end one. The bed smells of Alec's tobacco and then, after about ten days, of nothing much.

On the first of March, a Friday, Reggie wakes at five and lies in the dark and thinks: *it's gone*. Fifteen pounds, from an account in Alec's own name, in Alec's own hand, to a room in Southport where a man with a bench and a canal on the wall is either still living or isn't. He doesn't know which. There isn't any way to know.

On the fifth there's a letter from Bedford Row.

Alec brings it to the long room at four. He lays it face down on the bench in front of Reggie, the way he laid the first one on the table in the hut, and goes back to his own end, and picks up his pen, and does not write anything with it.

It's short.

> *WITHOUT PREJUDICE*
>
> *Dear Sir,*
>
> *We acknowledge receipt of your letter of last month, the contents of which are noted.*
>
> *Our client's enquiries, which extended to the person named in your letter and to his employer and his landlady at the address you gave, are now concluded, the person concerned having since left both his employment and his lodging. Our client sees no further purpose in them.*
>
> *Our client has thought it proper to place the results before the licensing authority.*
>
> *Our client reserves its position as to the material.*
>
> *Yours faithfully,*

Reggie reads it twice, because he has the kind of mind that rereads a thing it wishes it had misunderstood, and the second time he reads only the second paragraph.

"Left," he says.

"Yes."

"Both."

"Yes."

"Where's he gone?"

"They do not say. I should imagine they do not know."

"Is there anybody —" He stops. "Has he got anybody? Anywhere?"

"I do not know," says Alec. "There was nobody in 1950, after Margaret went to her mother's. There may be somebody now. I have no means of finding out that would not be a line to him, and I shall not draw one."

Reggie looks at the letter. *The person concerned.* He thinks of a frozen canal on a wall, which he's never seen, and a bench, which he's never seen either, and a man in a cardigan under his white coat in a lift saying *mine watches the door, you know*.

"You said he'd still have the bench and the room at the end of it."

"I said I did not know."

Reggie looks up.

"No," he says, after a moment. "That's right. You did say that. You said you didn't know."

He watches that reach Alec. It goes through the shoulders and the hand that holds the pen, and it's worse than anything Reggie could have said on purpose, and he hadn't said it on purpose. He'd said it because it was fair. It seems very important to him, for some reason, to go on being fair to Alec, now that it's the only thing left on the bench that costs him nothing.

---

The inspector's name is Halloran, and he comes on the twelfth.

He's a tall stooping Ulsterman who has been in the Colonial Service since 1931, and he spends three days on the island looking at everything and writing very little. He weighs the fish himself. He measures the north pen with a tape he's brought from Kingston in a leather case. On the second afternoon he stands at the rail for twenty minutes watching the calf sleep against her mother's flank in the shallows, and says to nobody in particular, "Well, that's what it's for, isn't it," and goes back up to the office. Reggie, who is at the rail beside him, likes him very much, and is aware of liking him, and aware of what it means that he's aware.

He asks his question on the third morning, in the office, with the licence schedule on the table and Hester at the side with her pad, and Pascual by the window with the veterinary schedule on his knee, because he holds that part of it and can't decently be sent out.

"Dr Tar. I'm obliged to put a matter to you, and I'll put it plainly, and you'll forgive me." Halloran has a sheet in front of him with a paper clip at the corner, and under the clip, Reggie can see, a carbon, and under the carbon a letter on good paper with a firm's name printed small at the top. "Representations have reached the office concerning Dr Spilsbury. They've been put in more detail than I'd like, and one of the documents is in his own hand. He's been supporting a man called Nicholson since 1950. You'll know the name."

"I know the name."

"Then you'll know what Nicholson was convicted of that year, and I needn't say it in front of Mrs Bevan." Halloran doesn't look at Hester. He is careful not to. "Dr Spilsbury doesn't say anything more than that in his letter, and he doesn't need to. You brought him onto a licensed station."

"I did."

"Were you aware of Dr Spilsbury's tendencies," says Halloran, "when you did?"

---

It takes him about a second.

He has the whole of it in that second, laid out flat, the way he has a curve once somebody's drawn the axes in. That's the part he'll hate most, later, when he's able to hate things in order. It isn't difficult. It isn't even slow.

*No* is a lie, in words, and he doesn't tell those. He's told Alec one lie in ten years, on the concrete at the lip in 1954, and there wasn't an untrue word in that either; he handed the man a thing with the wrong shape and let him carry it. He isn't going to tell one about him in words, in front of Hester's pad.

*Yes* is the Director of Research of a licensed institute admitting on the record that he brought a man he knew to be an invert onto the station and kept him there. Then the licence goes, and the paper, and every animal in the north pen goes to whoever the Colonial Office thinks fit.

*I'm not the person to ask* is a man declining to say what he knew. Halloran will write it down, because he's fair, and fairness writes things down. And then he'll look for somebody else to ask.

He's already looking. Reggie watches it happen. Halloran's eyes move, not far and not for long, from Reggie's face to the window, where a man of forty-three sits with a veterinary schedule on his knee. He has no wife, and his name is on the licence under Reggie's, and he's lived on the other side of a wall from Reggie for four years. The eyes come back again. It isn't unkind. It's a man doing his job and seeing where the next question is.

*There is another man on this row.*

And there's Frank, who's paid already. The agent went to his employer and to his landlady, and he's left both of them, and whatever he had in Southport is gone and can't be got back. The only thing that turns what he lost into something other than waste is the file closing where it was meant to close, on *Spilsbury and Nicholson*. If Reggie won't put the distance in, the file doesn't close. Frank will have lost the bench and the room for nothing.

Alec had told him all of it. At the table, on the Saturday, down to the wall and the man behind it. Reggie had said *leave Pascual out of it*, and Alec had said *it is you who will have to*.

He sits in the chair across from Halloran, and does Alec's sum, and gets Alec's answer.

And the sentence is true. That's the last thing and the worst. It's the only sentence available that is true, and keeps the licence, and keeps Pascual on the far side of the wall. When the men in the canteen said it about Frank they were lying after the fact. Reggie won't be. He'll be doing what he did at the lip: every word true, and the shape of it aimed. There has always been something about Alec Spilsbury. He noticed it the first week he knew him, before he knew what it was, and he's gone on noticing it every day since, and it's the thing he loves best in the world.

He feels the coat come.

He doesn't reach for it this time. It comes on its own, the way it came at thirteen in a school corridor, the way it came in the lift. There's the lift in the chest and the face settling and the warmth coming up into the voice like a lamp being turned up, and he lets it, because it's the only way he's going to get the sentence out of his mouth.

"There's always been something about him, Mr Halloran," he says.

He says it easily, with a little rueful lift at the corner of his mouth, leaning back in the chair, one colleague to another. It's the voice that likes you.

"Anybody at the Labs would tell you the same. I didn't bring him out here for his conversation. I brought him for his hands. He's the finest anatomist I've worked with, and the Institute's had the use of them for nine months, and the paper that comes out in June is the better for it."

Halloran looks at him.

Then Reggie watches relief come into the man's face. It's the decent man's relief, the relief of a man who didn't want to have to do the other thing and now won't, and it's worse than anything Halloran could have written down.

Halloran writes something short on the sheet. Then he screws the cap on his pen.

"That'll do," he says. "Mrs Bevan, I'll want the pairing figures again, if you have them to hand."

He doesn't look at the window.

---

Reggie keeps the coat on for the pairing figures. It takes twenty minutes. He is, he knows, extremely good. He makes Halloran laugh twice, once about Port Erin and once about the calf and the bootlace. Hester's pen moves steadily down her pad and she doesn't look up once. Pascual sits by the window with the veterinary schedule on his knee and doesn't say anything at all, and Reggie doesn't look at him, and it's the hardest thing he's ever not done.

Then Halloran says he'll see Dr Spilsbury after lunch, as a matter of form, and gathers his papers, and Reggie says *of course*, warmly, and goes out, and walks down the passage and out of the side door and round the back of the sick bay, not fast.

The incinerator is a squat brick thing with an iron door. It's cold. Nobody's fired it since February.

He gets one hand flat on the brick before he's sick.

It isn't much. He hasn't eaten since last night. He stays bent over with his hand on the brick and his eyes shut until it stops, and then a bit longer. The brick is rough and cool under his palm, and it's the brick Alec stood with his back to at three in the morning with the ash still warm, watching the sea, and Reggie thinks of that and is sick again, which is absurd, and he knows it's absurd, and it doesn't help.

He hears Pascual come round the corner of the sick bay and stop.

Pascual doesn't say anything for a while. Then he comes and stands beside him, not touching, and after a moment holds out a handkerchief. It's clean and ironed. Iris Greaves does Pascual's handkerchiefs, with the sheets, on Mondays.

Reggie takes it.

"I heard," Pascual says.

"It was true."

"I know it was true, *hombre*." Pascual's voice is very low. "That's what I heard."

Reggie wipes his mouth. He straightens up, slowly, with his hand still on the brick, and doesn't look round.

"Why?" Pascual says. "I'm asking. You said you'd tell me when you could."

*Because the next question was you.* It's right there. He could say it. He could turn round and say it to the one man on the island who'd understand it, and hand him the knowledge that he'd been sitting by the window while it was done for him. Pascual would carry that for the rest of his life, and never once be able to say to anybody that he was carrying it.

"Because it was that or the licence," he says, which is also true.

Pascual is quiet.

"That's not all of it."

"No."

"All right," says Pascual. He takes the handkerchief back, folds it inside out, and puts it in his pocket. "All right. Come and wash your face. You can't go up that path like that. Hester'll see."

"Hester's seen."

"Hester's seen *that*," says Pascual. "She hasn't seen this. Come on."

---

Halloran sees him at two, in the office, with the door shut and nobody else in the room.

Spilsbury has sat in a great many rooms of this kind, on both sides of the table, and he knows the form. There will be a statement of the representations. There will be an invitation to comment. There will be a note of whether he did. Halloran does all three with a courtesy that is not in the least put on. Spilsbury declines to comment on the representations, since he has already done so in writing and has nothing to add to what he wrote. Halloran nods, and makes his note, and then does a thing that Spilsbury had not expected of him, which is the fair thing.

"There's one more matter. It touches you, so you ought to see it." He turns the sheet round on the table and slides it across, with his finger at a line two-thirds of the way down. "That's what's been said. You may comment on it or not, as you like."

Spilsbury had read the sheet upside down some minutes ago, while Halloran was reading him the representations. He has read letters upside down on other men's desks for twenty years, and it is a habit, and he had not been able to stop it. He reads it now the right way up, because it has been offered, and because a man should read a thing properly when he is going to be asked about it.

It is in Halloran's hand, which is square and small and the hand of a man who was taught at a board school and has never forgotten it.

> *Dir. of Research (Dr R. Tar) states there has always been something about Dr S.; brought him on account of his anatomical work; valuable to the Institute. Satisfied no member of staff implicated. Dr S. to remove from the Institute's premises. Recommend: no further action.*

He reads it a second time, because that is his practice. The first reading is for what it says. The second is for how it has been built.

The first thing he feels, and he will not pretend otherwise, is relief.

It comes up through the whole of him like warmth. It held. It held exactly. *Satisfied no member of staff implicated.* The licence stands. The paper stands. The calf and the cow and the whole north pen stand. Pobil was not asked. That clause is not on the sheet, and it is the most important clause on it, and Spilsbury reads it in the white space between *implicated* and *Dr S.* where Halloran did not write it. Reggie's name is in this file once, as the man who said the sentence, and nowhere else.

He had put all of this on a table in lamplight on the ninth of February. He had put in the question, and the answer, and the glance at the window. It has come out to the line.

The second thing arrives more slowly, and it does not come up through him. It settles on him from above, like a weight on a bench.

He can hear it. That is the thing he had not budgeted for. He can hear, in the square small hand of an Ulsterman taught at a board school, the voice in which it was said. It is the warm one, the one that likes you, the one that got four days in a lift. It is the voice Reggie has never once used on him and has now used about him, in a room with a pad in it. He knows exactly how that sentence sounded, and how Reggie's mouth would have gone at one corner, and what it would have cost to make it go. He knows because he wrote the sum that required it.

He had known Reggie would say it. He had not known what it would be to read it.

*Result of arithmetic,* he thinks, and stops, because his hand has gone to his inside pocket for a thing that is not there. He has noticed it doing that since the seventh of February, the way a man's tongue goes to a gap in his teeth.

*F. N., R. T. Accepted.*

It is the first entry in thirty years with those two initials in the column where the cost goes. There were several hundred *R. T.*s in the two green books and he had never counted them, and not one was in that column. And there is nothing to write it in. He composes it anyway, exactly, with the full stops, and puts it in the place behind the eyes where he has found he can keep a surprising amount if he is careful.

"Dr Spilsbury?"

"I beg your pardon."

"Anything you'd wish to add?"

"No." He slides the sheet back across the table, square, with the line towards Halloran. "It is accurate. Dr Tar is always very careful to be accurate. I should not wish to add anything to it."

Halloran looks at him for a moment, with an expression Spilsbury has seen on referees who have understood rather more of a paper than the author intended.

"You'll understand," Halloran says, "that I'd expect you not to be resident here beyond the month."

"I leave on the nineteenth."

"Where to, if you don't mind my asking?"

"Norway," says Spilsbury. "There is a place at the state station at Ålesund. It is a long way from anybody's drawer."

---

Reggie comes to the spare hut at ten.

Spilsbury has the lamp lit and is sitting at the table with nothing on it. The spare hut is called the spare hut with the wrong roof because a man in 1948 put the roof on with the pitch the wrong way, so that it drains onto the step, and there is a place in the corner by the door where the boards have shrunk and you can see a strip of sky. He can see three stars through it.

Reggie stands in the doorway and does not come in.

"Halloran showed you," he says.

"Yes."

"Of course he did. He's a fair man. I liked him. I liked him on the second afternoon at the rail, and I knew I was going to have to —" He stops. "You read it."

"Twice."

"What did it say? Exactly."

Spilsbury tells him, exactly, with the full stops. Reggie listens with his hand on the doorframe.

"*Always*," he says. "He put *always*."

"You said *always*."

"I did. I said *there's always been something about him*." Reggie's voice is quite steady. "I said it in the voice. I leant back in the chair, Alec. I did the thing with my mouth."

"I know. I could hear it in his handwriting."

That goes into Reggie visibly, and he takes it, and stands there with it.

"You told me I'd say it," he says. "On the Saturday. You told me the whole thing, down to the window. And I sat in that chair and did your sum in about a second and got your answer. I didn't even have to look for it. It was just there, the way it's there for you."

"Yes."

"It held."

"It held."

"Yes," says Reggie. "It held. Frank's gone God knows where, and it held."

He comes in then, finally, and shuts the door behind him, and doesn't bolt it. He doesn't sit on the chair. He sits on the end of the bed, which is made up tight the way Mrs Greaves makes them, with the blanket folded back square, and puts his elbows on his knees and looks at the floor.

"I can't come up here," he says. "After tonight. I can't come up this path again."

Spilsbury had known that was the sentence since about half past two. He had known it in outline since the ninth of February, and in detail since the office, and he finds that knowing it in detail has not prepared him in the least to hear it. He had thought it would. It is the first time in his life a thing he has fully costed has arrived and been larger than the figure.

"No," he says.

Reggie looks up.

"*No*? That's all?"

"It is correct."

"Argue with me."

"I have nothing to argue with."

"You've argued with me about everything for ten years. You argued about the scale bar on figure three until last week, and then you *stopped*, and I knew then. I knew you'd stopped arguing because you'd — because you'd costed this." His voice cracks down the middle and he lets it. "Argue with *this*, Alec. Just once. Tell me I'm wrong."

"You are not wrong."

"*Tell* me —"

"You cannot say that sentence in the morning," says Spilsbury, "and come up this path at night. One of the two would be a lie, and we should both know which, every night, for as long as you kept coming. I have been handed a lie in the right words by you once before, on the concrete in 1954, and I knew it inside a minute. I should know this one in less, and so would you." He hears his own voice and it is not level. He does not try to make it level. "And you would be up this path every night on what Frank paid. You would be having the thing he lost his bench for, with your arms round it. You could not do that, and I could not ask you to, and I have spent a month finding out that I could not even want you to, which I had not expected."

Reggie puts both hands over his face.

"Which one's the lie?" he says through them. "The morning or the path?"

"I should not presume to say."

"*Say*."

Spilsbury looks at him for a long time.

"The morning was true," he says. "That is what makes it unbearable."

---

After a while Reggie takes his hands away.

"Did you cost this?" he says. "Me. Going. Tonight. Was that in it?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"On the Saturday. At the table." He does not look away. The man did not look away from him in the lift, in the story told him on the ninth, and it seems to him still the least that can be managed. "When I said the cost was not mine, I heard it. You heard it. And then I ran it forward and saw that you would go, and the cost was mine after all. That is what made the sum come out. I told myself I was paying. It made it possible to do."

"You didn't say."

"No. I could not say it to your face. I found I could say everything else to your face, and not that."

Reggie stares at him.

"That's the worst thing you've ever said to me."

"Yes."

"You spent me. You spent Frank and you spent me, and you counted *losing* me as the thing that made it all right."

"Yes. I should like it recorded —"

He stops.

Reggie laughs. It is a dreadful sound, short and wet, out through his nose.

"Recorded *where*, Alec?"

"Nowhere," says Spilsbury. "There is nowhere."

---

He does not know which of them moves. He finds that he is standing, and that Reggie is standing, and that they are about a foot apart in the middle of the spare hut with the wrong roof, under the strip of sky. Reggie puts out his hand and takes hold of his wrist, round the bone, the way he would take hold of an animal to count it.

"Your pulse is up," Reggie says.

"I expect it is."

"I'm counting it."

"I know you are."

Reggie counts. His lips move very slightly. He has done this in the dark at three in the morning on the blanket since November 1946, and in the hut at the end of the row every night since the thirtieth of May, and Spilsbury has never once asked him what the figure was.

"Hundred and four," says Reggie.

"That seems high."

"It's very high. You should see somebody."

"I shall see Rasmussen," says Spilsbury. "He is said to be very sound on seals."

Reggie makes a noise that isn't a laugh either, and lets go of his wrist, and puts his forehead down on Spilsbury's shoulder, hard, the bone of his brow against the collarbone. He stays there. Spilsbury puts his hand up to the back of the man's neck, where the hair has grown too long this last month because nobody has told him to get it cut, and holds on.

"I love you," Reggie says into his shirt. "That's true as well. I'm not ever going to say it in a room with a pad in it."

"No."

"I'm not ever going to say it in *any* room. Am I."

"No," says Spilsbury. "I do not think you are."

He can feel Reggie's breath through the cotton, warm and uneven. He can feel the whole length of him, the hip against his own, the hand that has let go of his wrist now gripping the back of his shirt in a fist. He thinks that he has been held by this man a great many times and has never once been held as though the man were trying to keep his footing on a deck.

"Say something," Reggie says. "Say something true. You've got nowhere to put it. Put it here."

Spilsbury stands in the lamplight with his hand on the back of the man's neck, and thinks for a moment, because it should be right.

"I have loved you since a passage at four degrees," he says. "I have not once done a sum since then without you on the other side of it. That is the whole of the trouble. It was never going to be anything else. I am sorry for Frank. I am sorry for this. And I should do it again, which means I am not allowed to say that I am sorry, and I am saying it anyway, because there is no book to keep me honest."

Reggie's hand tightens in his shirt.

Then, very slowly, it lets go.

He steps back. His face is wet and he doesn't wipe it. He picks his glasses up off the table, where he must have put them down at some point, and puts them on.

"I'll see you off," he says. "On the nineteenth. Everybody will. It'd look odd if I didn't."

"Yes."

"I'll do the voice."

"I know. I shall know that it is the voice."

Reggie stands at the door with his hand on the latch.

"That's the only thing that makes it bearable," he says, and goes out.

Spilsbury stands where he was left. After a while he sits down at the table. He does not turn the lamp down. Through the gap in the boards in the corner he can see the three stars, and then, as the night goes round, two, and then one, and then the sky going grey behind it. At some point in that time he composes the entry again, from the beginning, with the full stops. He has nowhere to put it, and so he goes on holding it.

---

The boat goes at half past nine on the nineteenth, and the whole station is on the jetty, because that is what the station does.

Pascual stands a little apart from the others with his hands in his pockets.

Spilsbury has two cases, as he came with. He deposited the material with Mr Purdie at the Bay yesterday, who read every word of it aloud, including *available on application to any qualified person*, and Hester has given him an envelope with the receipt in it. She has told him, in the voice she uses for the fish accounts, that he will write to the Institute twice a year and let it know where the depositor of that material is at all times. Spilsbury said that he would. Hester said *good*, and wrote it down.

Jean shakes his hand and says the legends went off on Friday and she'll send him the proofs. The Colonel says something about Norway and cod. Alleyne's boy loads the cases.

And Reggie comes down the jetty last, with his hat in his hand, smiling.

Pascual has watched him do it perhaps a dozen times in four years. He did it to the Colonel, and to a man from Agriculture, and to a Commander with a moustache. Five days ago he did it to an inspector from Kingston, with Pascual sitting by the window. Pascual knows it the way you'd know an animal favouring a flipper, by the set of it, before you've seen the wound. The shoulders are easy. The face is warm and open. The voice, when it comes, has a lamp behind it.

"Dr Spilsbury. Well." Reggie holds out his hand. "Bergen's been warned, I hope? You'll write to Hester, she'll want the address. And do tell Rasmussen from me his tanks are a disgrace, I've seen the drawings. He'll be delighted, nobody ever tells him."

Spilsbury takes the hand.

It is a colleague's handshake, warm and firm, one, two, and done. Pascual watches Spilsbury receive it. He watches the man who knows that voice better than anybody alive stand on a jetty and be given it, and take it, and not let a single thing show on a face that has one expression and uses it for everything.

"Dr Tar," says Spilsbury.

"Safe crossing."

"Thank you."

And that is all, and Reggie steps back, and turns to Hester and says something about the fish weights that makes her mouth go straight in the way it does when she won't laugh.

Pascual goes forward.

He has thought about what he'll do here since the office. He hasn't decided. He likes this man. He liked him on four years' evidence that he kept Reggie company, and then twenty minutes at the long table last May gave him the rest of the evidence, and he went on liking him anyway, which surprised him. He has heard a name read out in an office by a man from Kingston, and seen what happened to a face when it was asked a question, and held a handkerchief behind the sick bay. He doesn't know the whole of it and knows he never will. He knows enough.

He takes Spilsbury by both shoulders, briefly and hard, the way he'd steady a man on a deck.

"You could have asked somebody," he says, low, in the man's ear.

Spilsbury does not pretend not to understand him.

"Yes," he says. "I could."

"*Ay*, hombre." Pascual lets go. "Eat something in Norway. You look like the ash."

Spilsbury gets into the boat. Alleyne's boy casts off. And Spilsbury sits in the stern with his two cases and his hat on his knee and does not look back until they are nearly round the head.

Then he does, once.

Pascual sees him do it. He turns and looks where the man is looking, at the end of the jetty, where anybody would stand to see a boat round the head.

There is nobody there. Reggie is halfway up the path with Hester, talking, with his hands in his pockets, and as Pascual watches, Hester says something and Reggie laughs.

When Pascual looks back at the water, the boat is round the head and gone.

---

He wakes at two, because he has gone to sleep with the detective story on his chest and the pages have fallen out onto the floor.

He lies in the dark for a minute before he knows what's different, and then he knows. There's light on the wall. It's coming through the gap under the eaves between his hut and the next, a thin yellow line on the boards above the washstand. It's the lamp in the last hut in the row.

That lamp has not been lit at two in the morning since the thirtieth of May. Pascual knows, because he has lived on this side of the wall for the whole of that time and has been glad, every night, without once saying so to anybody, of the dark.

He gets up and puts his trousers on and goes out onto the path in his bare feet, and stands outside Reggie's door. It isn't bolted. He can hear nothing inside, not a page, not a chair.

He knocks.

"I'm reading, Pascual," Reggie's voice says through the door, easy, warm, with a smile in it. "Something frightfully dull on sponges. Go back to bed."

It's the voice.

Pascual stands on the path in the dark with his hand still up, the way you'd stand at a rail when an animal has gone down and you're waiting to see if it'll come up again. The frogs are going in the cistern. Down at the north pen the calf blows, once, and her mother answers.

He doesn't knock again.

He goes back into his own hut, and doesn't take his trousers off. He picks the pages of the detective story up off the floor and puts them back in, more or less in order, and turns his own lamp up as high as it'll go, so that there'll be a line of it on the other side of the wall as well. Then he sits up in bed with the book open at a page, and doesn't read it, and keeps him company through the boards until it gets light.
