# On Demand

Pascual's got a shilling on Thursday.

Reggie's got a shilling on the Tuesday after. He's had it since the middle of February, and he's been saying *early* about everything since, as though the word could be got to work backwards. The Colonel's got half a crown on the full moon, which falls on the Sunday, and won't be argued out of it, because he had a mare at Poona in 1921 who did everything by the moon. Jean's declined on the grounds that she's the Director. Baptiste has declined on the grounds that she keeps the book, and a person who keeps the book mustn't have an interest in what goes into it.

The book's a green one. She ruled it herself in the first week of March, *Date, Time, Resp., Posture, Remarks*, and there's been a line in it every quarter of an hour since, day and night, in her upright hand. Nobody asked her to. Pascual came into the long room on the second of the month and found it on the bench and stood over it for some time. Then he went and found Reggie at the rail and told him the Spilsbury Student had ruled a book for the calf. Reggie said *yes, I've seen*, and his face did something small and pleased that it wouldn't have done two years ago.

The calf is sixteen, and enormous, and has been *the calf* since the morning in 1957 when Pascual had his hand on her mother as she came up. She'll be the calf until she dies. Nobody on the cay has managed to call her anything else. She's lain in the shallows at the north end of the pen since the end of February, with her great grey back just breaking the water, blowing slow, and a look in her eye that Pascual calls *patient* and Reggie calls *bored stiff*.

And Pascual's still next door. He's been next door since 1952, ten yards up the row from the hut at the end. He's thought about moving over to the Bay now and then, over the years, the way you'd think about a boat you'd no real intention of buying, and hasn't done it. On Saturdays he still goes along to the end of the row with the oil can. The lamp hasn't needed it since November 1971, when it went out and stayed out, and Reggie started coming in to breakfast looking as if he'd slept. Pascual fills it anyway. He sits on the bed and says *well?*, and Reggie tells him the week, and that's Saturday. Nobody's seen any reason to change Saturday.

---

At a quarter past nine on the Thursday there's a knock at his door, and it's Baptiste, in her oilskin, with the book under her arm and a torch.

"Dr Pobil. She's gone to eighteen." She's quite calm. Her voice isn't. "And she's turned over twice since nine."

"Thursday," says Pascual, and reaches for his boots.

Reggie's at the rail already. Of course he is. He's in his shirtsleeves with his glasses pushed up on his forehead and his hands in his pockets, and he looks round as Pascual comes down the path. He has the face of a man who's about to lose a shilling and would pay a pound for the privilege.

"Don't," says Reggie.

"I haven't said anything."

"You're going to."

"It's Thursday."

"It's Thursday for another two hours and three quarters. She could take all night."

"Then you'll have lost on a technicality," says Pascual, "and it'll serve you right for saying *early* for a month," and swings his leg over the wall.

The water's warm. She's lying half on her side at the north end, and she turns her eye on him as he wades up, and knows him, and doesn't mind. He puts his hand flat on her flank, low down, and waits. In a minute he feels it go through her, long and slow, from a long way in.

"Well?" says Reggie from the rail.

"It's started."

"I can *see* it's started. I asked you *well*."

"*Well*, it's started," says Pascual. "Get the lamps. Baptiste, every five minutes now. And somebody go and tell Jean, she'll never —"

"Jean knows," says Jean, from the top of the path, in her dressing gown with a lantern in each hand. "Jean's been at her window since the second time she turned over. I'm not waking the Colonel."

"He'll never forgive you."

"He's eighty-three and it's going to be all night. I'll wake him for the end. Take these, Reggie, before I drop them on your head."

---

It's all night.

Most of it's waiting. Pascual's good at waiting; it's most of what a vet does. Reggie isn't. He sits on the wall above with a lamp in each hand, holding them out over the water where they're wanted, and fidgets in a way that makes the light jump.

"Hold them still."

"I am holding them still."

"You're holding them like a man signalling a ship. Hold them like lamps."

Reggie holds them like lamps. It lasts about ten minutes.

At one o'clock Baptiste asks, from the far end of the wall, what she's to call it.

"She's *C*," she says. "In the posture column. I can't have them both as *C*, Dr Tar. Nobody'll be able to follow it."

"*C-two*," says Reggie.

"It's not a *tank*, Reggie, it's a calf."

"It's a column heading. Column headings are allowed to be dull."

"Then *the calf's calf*."

"That's not a heading, Dr Pobil," says Baptiste. "That's a sentence."

"It'll name itself," says Pascual. "They do. Put a dash for now."

Baptiste puts a dash. He hears the pen scratch it in, very firmly, as if it were being held in reserve for something.

At two Jean goes up and brings tea down in a can. At four the calf stops.

He feels her go still under his hand. The long pull that's been coming every few minutes since nine doesn't come. He waits for it, and it doesn't come. He keeps his hand where it is and counts her breathing. Twelve. Steady. Her eye's half shut.

Above him, the lamps begin to jump.

"Pascual."

"She's resting."

"She's *stopped*. That's not resting, that's —"

"It's resting. They rest. I've seen a mare rest an hour. Sit *down*, *hombre*, you're dripping oil on her."

Reggie looks at the lamps. There is, in fact, a small dark drip on the wall below the left one. He sits down.

"How long do we give her?"

"As long as she wants."

"And if that's —"

"Reggie." Pascual looks up at him. From down here, with a lamp either side of his face, Reggie looks about twelve, and frightened, and trying not to show it, which is a thing he does so badly it's almost a comfort. "She's been lying in this pen sixteen years. Every one of those years she's known more about what she's doing than we have. Let her."

Reggie lets her.

At twenty to five she starts again.

---

At a quarter to six the sky's going grey behind the second head, and she's in earnest now. Pascual gets his hand to where it needs to be, and feels what he's been afraid of since February.

"It's come round," he says. "The flipper's back. It's caught."

"Can you turn it?"

"I can turn it. I can't turn it with her going like this. She'll roll. She'll roll on it, or on me." He can feel her gathering to do it, the whole vast weight shifting in the shallows. "Somebody's got to have her *head*. Jean, give Baptiste the lamps and —"

Both lamps come down on the wall at once, with a clank. And Reggie's over the wall and into the pen in his shirt and trousers, and the water's to his waist.

"*No*," says Pascual. "*Get out*. Reggie. Get out of the water."

"You need her head."

"I need *Jean* —"

"Jean's got the lamps and Baptiste's got the book, and they're both half your weight, and you've got your arm in a calf." Reggie's wading up the length of her, one hand on her flank, not hurrying, talking to her under his breath. He gets to her head and puts both arms round it, the way you'd take hold of a frightened horse, with his cheek against her. "Shout at me afterwards."

"I *will* shout at you afterwards."

"I know. I'll tell you when."

What happens after that, Pascual doesn't see, because he's got his eyes shut and his arm in the calf. He hears it. It's Reggie's voice, the length of her away, perfectly level, the voice from strandings, the one with no lamp in it at all. *She's gathering. Wait. Wait. Not yet. Now. Now, Pascual.* And his own hands pushing, and the thing under them moving, and not moving, and moving. *Wait. She's going to roll. I've got her. She's not going to. Now.*

It comes round.

He feels it come round under his palm, all at once, easily, like a line coming free of a cleat. He hears himself saying *ya, ya, ya*. She heaves. And there's a calf.

It comes up through the grey water between his arms, small and dark and furious, and blows, high and thin. Its mother has it under her before Pascual's got his hands out of the way. Behind the second head the grey's going gold.

"Six-twelve," says Baptiste on the wall, and her voice goes in the middle. "*Friday*."

Nobody says anything for a second.

"Nobody had Friday," says Jean.

"Then it goes to the book," says Pascual, and starts to laugh. He finds he can't stop. He finds his legs have gone, and he's sitting down in the water to his chest with a calf blowing beside him. And Reggie's hand has come down through the water and got hold of his wrist, and is holding it, hard, the way you'd hold a rail.

He looks up.

Reggie's still at the head, with one arm round her, and the other down in the water to Pascual. He's looking at the calf. He's grey.

Pascual stops laughing.

"Bottle," he says.

Reggie lets go of his wrist. He gets the little brown bottle out of his shirt pocket, which is soaked through, and shakes a tablet out on his palm and puts it under his tongue. He does it in the pen, at dawn, in front of Jean and Baptiste and the calf and God, without being asked. He doesn't turn away to do it.

Pascual sits in the water and watches him.

"Out," says Pascual.

"In a minute."

"*Now*, Reggie."

Reggie gets out. He sits on the wall, and Jean takes off her dressing gown and puts it round him. That leaves her standing in her nightdress in front of the Institute's only Spilsbury Student, and she doesn't seem to mind. After a minute his colour starts to come back. Pascual watches it come, from the water, and doesn't take his eyes off it until it has.

Then Jean goes up to wake the Colonel, and the Colonel comes down the path in his pyjamas and sees the calf and weeps.

---

Pascual's shoulder seizes at about four in the afternoon.

He's doing the new calf's weights. They aren't weights yet, because nobody's fool enough to lift a calf off its mother on the first day; they're guesses, written down carefully. He reaches up for the reference book on the shelf and his right arm stops at the level of his ear and won't go any further, as if somebody had put a bolt through it.

He says a word in Aragonese and takes the book down with his left.

It's nine hours in the water with his arm in her and her whole weight closing on it every few minutes. He knows exactly what it is. He'll be a week getting it back, if he's sensible. He tells nobody.

And he's going to have it out with Reggie, about the pen. He's had it ready since six this morning, sitting in the water, watching a man put a tablet under his tongue. He's going to do it properly, on Saturday, at the end of the row, where it's Saturday and he can.

---

Reggie opens the door at eight and knows at once.

"Your shoulder," he says.

"Good evening to you too."

"You've got the can in your left hand."

"It's a can. It's got a handle. It doesn't mind which —"

"You've got the can in your left hand, and you're holding your right elbow into your side as if you'd a parcel under it. Come in and sit down."

Pascual comes in and sits down on the bed, which is where he sits on Saturdays. He puts the can on the floor by his foot. He doesn't take his eyes off Reggie.

"I've come to shout at you," he says.

"I know you have. You can do it with your shirt off. Where's the liniment?"

"*What* liniment?"

"The stuff you put on the Colonel's knees. In the brown bottle, the one that smells like a stable."

"It's *horse* liniment."

"You've put it on me."

"You're —" Pascual stops, and considers what Reggie is, and doesn't find a way out of it. "It's on the shelf in my hut. Behind the formalin."

"Stay there."

He goes and gets it. When he comes back Pascual's got his shirt half off and stuck. It's caught on the right arm, which he's holding out stiff from his side, and he's got a face like thunder.

"Don't you laugh," says Pascual.

"I'm not laughing."

"You are. Your mouth's doing the thing."

"My mouth's at rest." Reggie puts the bottle down on the table by the lamp and gets the shirt off him. It's the left sleeve first, then the right, easing it down over the elbow while Pascual swears at the ceiling in two languages. Pascual's done this for him more times than either of them could count, on the jetty and on the verandah, when Reggie's come out of the water with no feeling in his hands. It's the first time it's gone this way round. Reggie finds he rather likes it.

"Lie down?"

"Sit. I'll sit. If I lie down I'll not get up."

So Reggie sits on the bed behind him, with one knee up. He pours the liniment into his palm, and it's cold and reeks of camphor and wintergreen, and he puts his hands on Pascual's shoulder. The muscle across the top of it is as hard as a plank. He finds the edge of it with his thumbs and leans in.

"Right," says Pascual, through his teeth. "The pen."

"Go on."

"You got *in*."

"I did."

"With your *heart*."

"It came with me. I couldn't very well leave it on the wall."

"Don't you *joke*, Reggie Tar, don't you — *ay* — there — no, *there* —" Pascual's head goes forward. "Marguerite said *not one*."

"Marguerite said not one on the reef. Not one in the boat. She said nothing whatever about the pen, and it was waist-deep, and I've checked the wording."

"You have not *checked the wording*."

"I've had the wording by heart since 1970. I could give it you in court."

"And you took a *tablet*."

"I did."

"In front of *everybody*."

"In front of Jean and Baptiste and a calf four minutes old." His thumbs find the real knot, under the shoulder blade, and Pascual makes a noise like a man being murdered, and then a different one. "Were you proud of me?"

"I was *furious*."

"You were proud."

"I was both," says Pascual, into his own chest. "*Madre de Dios*, what've you got in your hands. Don't stop."

He doesn't stop.

The shutters are open. There's a moon over the channel, a day short of full, and a square of it on the floor. The lamp's on the table unlit, with oil in it that Pascual put there last Saturday. Reggie works the liniment into the shoulder and down over the top of the arm, into the long muscle Pascual's used for twenty years to lift animals and buckets and, now and then, Reggie. The smell fills the hut. Out in the north pen there's a small high blow, and in a while a big slow one.

"Well?" says Pascual.

Reggie laughs. He can't help it.

"You were *there*."

"It's Saturday. Tell me the week."

So he tells him the week. He tells him Monday and the Plymouth letter, and Tuesday and Hester Bevan finding a ha'penny out in the February accounts and making Jean go through them twice. He tells him Wednesday, and the Colonel explaining the moon to Baptiste for forty minutes while Baptiste wrote *C. still bored* in the Remarks column, in pencil, where the Colonel couldn't see. Pascual laughs at that, and it hurts him, and he says *don't*, and Reggie tells him Thursday.

He's got to the back of Pascual's neck by then, where the knot in the shoulder runs up under the hairline and ties itself off at the base of the skull. It's the last one, and the worst. He works at it with his thumb while he tells Thursday, small hard circles, and it doesn't want to go. Pascual says *ay* under his breath at intervals, and then stops saying it and just breathes. Reggie keeps on, patiently, the way you'd work a knot out of wet line with a fingernail. Somewhere round the part where he's handing Jean the lamps he feels it give. It goes all at once under his thumb, like something unlocking, and Pascual's head drops forward an inch and he lets out a breath that goes on for a very long time.

"*Ahí*," says Pascual. "That's it. That was the one."

"I know. I felt it go."

"Twenty years I've been doing that to other people." He rolls the shoulder, carefully, and then lifts the arm out to the side. It goes up past his ear this time. It doesn't go the whole way, and he stops it there with a grunt that's more surprise than pain. "Would you look at that."

"Don't push it."

"I'm not pushing it. I'm *admiring* it."

That's done, then. The knots are out. There's nothing left under Reggie's hands that needs doing.

He doesn't stop.

He doesn't think about not stopping. His hand simply goes on, flat now, no thumbs, from the nape of Pascual's neck out along the top of the shoulder and down the arm to the elbow, and back. It goes slowly, the way you'd go on stroking an animal after the examination's over, because it's warm and it's leaning into you. The skin's hot from the liniment and slick with it. The hair at the nape is short and grey and soft, and he goes up into it against the grain, and down, and out along the shoulder again. It feels good. That's all he thinks, as far as he's thinking anything. It feels good under his hand, and he'd like to go on doing it.

Pascual doesn't tell him to stop.

He's sitting with his head a little down. Reggie can see the side of his face in the moonlight, and it's gone quiet, which Pascual's face doesn't often do. The shoulder under Reggie's palm has gone loose all the way through. And each time Reggie's hand comes back up to the nape of his neck, Pascual's head tips back into it, just a little, and stays there till the hand moves on.

Reggie tells him the rest of Thursday like that.

"You had your eyes shut," he says. "In the pen. The whole time. I'd no idea you did that."

"Course I shut them." Pascual's voice has gone slow and low. "There's nothing to see. It's all in the hands."

"You looked —" He doesn't know how Pascual looked. He's got his hand on the back of the man's neck, where it's been for five minutes for no reason at all, and the smell of the Colonel's knees all over the pair of them, and there's a calf a day and a half old breathing in the pen. "You looked like the best thing I've ever seen done."

"You're telling it *wrong*," says Pascual, not very steadily, and turns his head to tell him how.

And Reggie kisses him.

There isn't a moment before it. There's his hand on Pascual's neck and Pascual's face turned to him, close, cross, about to correct him, and then there's his mouth on Pascual's. He doesn't decide it. There's no time to decide anything. It's done the way he'd put a hand out to steady a man in a boat.

Pascual goes still.

Then Pascual kisses him back. His mouth opens under Reggie's with a sound in his throat, low and startled. His left hand comes up and takes hold of Reggie's shirt at the collar. His right comes too, slowly. It comes up past the place where it stopped this afternoon, and Reggie feels him flinch as it gets there. And then it's there anyway, a broad rough palm along Reggie's jaw, and it stays.

They don't stop.

Pascual pulls on the collar and Reggie comes, twisting round on the narrow bed until he's got a knee up against Pascual's back and his whole front along Pascual's side. His hand's still at the back of Pascual's neck, and it tightens there without asking him, fingers in the short grey hair, holding Pascual's head where it is. Pascual makes a sound into his mouth at that, rough, from somewhere down in his chest, and kisses him harder. He tastes of tea. His stubble drags at Reggie's chin. His thumb moves on Reggie's cheekbone and knocks his glasses crooked, and neither of them does anything about it. Reggie's other hand has found its way to Pascual's chest, flat, slick with liniment, and under it Pascual's heart is going like a pump.

Down in the pen, the calf blows. The small high one.

They stop.

Neither of them lets go at once. They stop with Pascual's hand still full of Reggie's collar and the other on his face, and Reggie's hand still in the hair at the back of Pascual's neck, an inch apart, in the square of moon, breathing.

Then they do let go.

---

Reggie's on his feet. He doesn't remember standing. He's got his back against the table and his hand on the edge of it, beside the liniment, and he's cold all through, the way you go cold in the water before you know you're in it.

And he feels it come.

He knows it the way you'd know a step that isn't there. It's the lift in the chest and the face settling and the warmth coming up into the voice, like a lamp being turned up. It's not come for two years. He'd thought it was gone. He hasn't reached for it. It's come on its own, the way it came at thirteen in a school corridor, and he hears it coming out of his mouth before he's had any say in the matter.

"My dear chap," says the voice. "I'm so sorry. That was — it's been an extraordinary two days, hasn't it, and no sleep to speak of, and you were quite marvellous in the pen, and I'm afraid I rather —"

Pascual's looking at him.

"— got carried away with the whole —"

"Don't," says Pascual.

It's very quiet. It stops the voice the way a hand stops a door.

"Pascual —"

"Don't you do that at me." Pascual's on his feet too. He's got his shirt off and his right arm held in close to his side from habit, and he smells like a stable, and his face in the moonlight is doing all of it at once. "Don't you *dare*. That's the *voice*."

"I —"

"I *heard* it. I've been listening for that voice for twenty years. I know it like I know a cough in a pen at night."

"Pascual, I didn't —"

"I heard it come *on*!" And his own voice goes up. It goes up all at once, the way it does, the way it did on the verandah in 1954 in front of the whole station. "It comes on when there's something that'd *cost*! You told me that! On the *step*! And you've just done it at *me*!"

Reggie stands with his back to the table.

He heard it too. He can hear it now it's been named, the way you only hear an engine once it's stopped.

"Yes," he says. "All right. It's the voice. I didn't — it came."

"I know it came! That's what I'm *saying*!"

"Then you know why!" And there's his own voice going up to meet Pascual's, loud and plain, with nothing in it at all. "Of *course* it'd cost! You were by the *window*!"

"I know I was by the —"

"Halloran looked at you for ten seconds, and I put the worst sentence of my life in a file to make him stop! It's still there! And I've just — with the *shutters open* —" He can't find the end of it. He finds the thing under it instead, and it comes out of him flat and hard, each word by itself. "I did not ask you."

Pascual stares at him.

"That is the one thing. After Frank. I swore it on a step. And I did not ask. I put my hand on your neck and took it, because I wanted it, the way I took the four days in the lift, and it was somebody else who —"

"*Nobody's paid!*"

"*Yet!*"

"So you'll decide!" Pascual's left hand comes out, flat, as though he could push the words back across the hut. "You'll decide, will you, *now*, on your own, in thirty seconds, what it costs and who's paying!"

"That isn't —"

"You've done the whole sum! I watched you do it, it's all over your face! The file and the window and Jean and God knows what else, and you've got to the bottom, and the answer's *my dear chap, I got carried away*. And you didn't ask me *that* either!"

Reggie opens his mouth.

Nothing comes out.

"You did this in 'fifty-seven," says Pascual. He isn't shouting now, and it's worse. "You sat in that office and decided what I could carry, and you didn't tell me for fourteen years."

"Pascual."

"And on the step, with the proof, I said you'd no right. And you *agreed* with me. You said *I know*. And you've done it again tonight before I've got my shirt back on."

"Because it's your *neck*."

"It's *my* neck!" He's shouting again, and in a queer way Reggie's glad of it, because the quiet was worse. "*Mine!* I say what I do with it! I'm not a figure in your column, Reggie, I'm *in* the sum, you can't do the thing without —"

Down in the north pen the calf's mother blows, and it isn't the slow one. It's short and hard, twice.

And along the row, somebody's shutter bangs.

They both stop dead.

They stand in the middle of the hut in the moonlight, Pascual with his shirt off and Reggie against the table, and look at the open shutters. Then Reggie's crossing to the one on the pen side, and Pascual's at the one by the door, badly, swearing at the catch. The bolt goes home on the door with a crack like a shot. The square of moon on the floor goes to slats.

They meet in the middle of the floor.

"*Keep your voice down*," says Reggie.

"*You* keep your — you were shouting *Halloran*!"

"You were shouting *neck*!"

"You shouted *the worst sentence of my life*, in *English*, at the top of your —"

"You started it! You said *voice*!"

"Because you *did the voice*!"

They stand a foot apart in the dark, whispering at each other like two boys at the back of chapel, furious. Reggie feels something come up in his chest that isn't the voice and isn't the tightness either. He shoves it back down. This is not the moment.

Pascual sees it anyway.

"Don't you laugh," he whispers. "Don't you *dare* laugh."

"I'm not."

"Your mouth's doing the thing."

"It's dark. You can't possibly see my mouth."

"I don't need to *see* it." And then Pascual's chin comes up, and he isn't whispering any more. "I don't care. Let them hear. Let the whole row hear. I'm sixty in November, Reggie, I'm not going to stand in the dark hissing like a — I don't care who —"

"Jean's name is on the licence."

Pascual stops.

"And the Studentship's in the Board minutes in her hand, with *his* name on it." Reggie keeps his voice down, and keeps it plain. He'd like very badly to shout this too, and doesn't. "And there's a calf in that pen a day and a half old."

"Reggie —"

"The week the licence goes, the pair of them are on a boat to somebody with a tank. You've seen the tanks in Florida. You can say what you like about your own neck. It's not only your neck. It's not only mine."

Pascual looks at him in the dark for a long moment.

Then his shoulders come down, the sore one a beat behind the other.

"No," he says. "All right. No. You're right."

He sits down on the bed, hard, and puts his left hand over his face.

Reggie stands in the middle of the floor. After a while he sits down too, on the station chair by the table, because his knees have an opinion about it. The liniment's by his elbow. The lamp's by the other one, cold.

Neither of them says anything. In the north pen the calf's mother blows again, the slow one, the ordinary one, and after it the small high one. Somewhere along the row a shutter opens again, quietly, and doesn't shut.

"Did you mean it?" says Pascual, from behind his hand.

"I don't know where it came from."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know it isn't."

Pascual takes his hand away. "It came *on*, Reggie. The voice. First time in two years. There was something you didn't want seen, and it came on to hide it." He says it quite flatly, the way he'd say *it's the teeth*. "I don't need you to know where the other thing came from. The voice knows."

Reggie sits with that.

Then he does what he does. He can't not; he's done it since he was thirteen. He turns round in the chair, in the dark, and points himself at himself, and reads.

It doesn't take long. It's all there. That's what winds him. It isn't hidden. It isn't even in a drawer. It's been standing in the middle of the room for twenty years, and he's walked round it every night to get to the bed.

He starts with his hand on the back of Pascual's neck, ten minutes ago, because it's nearest. And he knows the place. It's the exact place Pascual put his own hand in February last year, in the long room, over the cold tea, when Reggie had his glasses off and his hand over his eyes. He'd felt it then as the kindest thing anybody had done him in a year, and thought: *my friend*. Tonight he put his hand in the same place without knowing he was giving it back.

The Saturdays. Two years of them, with no lamp to fill.

The proof on the step, and Pascual's wet furious face, and his own arm round him. *My friend*.

He goes further back, because he can't stop now. There's a plate of eggs put down in front of him in the spring of 1971, and a man standing over it with his arms folded until it was empty. There's a pen taken out of his hand at midnight in 1957: *no, hombre, not tonight, go to bed*. There's a bucket, in 1954, coming at his head across the north pen with a torrent of Spanish behind it, and under the fright, before he'd even ducked, something that felt remarkably like relief. And there's a man off the Kingston boat in November 1952 who looked at the animal in the north pen for ten minutes without a word, and said *it's the teeth*, and was right. Reggie laughed on the verandah that night for the first time since he'd landed.

All of it. The whole time. He'd walked past it twice a day because somebody was standing in front of it. And the somebody was in Norway, and he'd kept a lamp lit to see him coming.

The lamp's by his elbow. Cold, and full. Pascual filled it last Saturday.

He looks up.

Pascual's watching him from the edge of the bed. There's not much light through the slats, but there's always enough with Pascual. His face is doing all of it: frightened, and furious, and sore, and waiting.

"Oh," says Reggie.

"*Oh*," says Pascual. "Twenty years, and it's *oh*."

"It's a great deal of oh."

"It'd *better* be."

Reggie takes his glasses off, and folds them, and puts them on the table by the lamp. He doesn't need them for this.

"I didn't ask you," he says.

"No."

"May I ask you now? It's late for it."

"It's late for a lot of things," says Pascual. His voice isn't steady. "Ask."

So Reggie asks.

"It'll be in a file if anybody sees. Halloran's sentence is in Kingston with my name beside it, and you're in the margin in the shape of a window. Jean has to be asked, and if she says yes she'll be carrying it too. Marguerite doesn't like my heart, and she's right not to. And I've got Alec. He's in the drawer and he's staying there. That's what it weighs. I'm asking whether you'll have it."

Pascual doesn't answer. His face goes through something.

"I got over you," he says. "In 1956."

Reggie stares at him.

"From 'fifty-three," says Pascual, low and fast, as though it's been waiting behind his teeth and has only just been let out. "The spring. You'd been shouting at me about calcium for a week and you were right, and I stood at that rail and thought *oh, no*. Three years. I threw a *bucket* at you."

"You said that was about the calcium."

"It *was* about the calcium! It was also —" He waves his left hand. "It doesn't matter. I got over it. I did it *properly*. I checked."

"You *checked*?"

"*Twice*! And then I was your friend, and it was the best thing I had, and I filled that lamp every Saturday for fifteen years so you'd see a man coming up the path from Norway. And I'd have gone on. I'd have gone on till I *died*, Reggie, and been glad of it."

"Pascual —"

"So you tell me." He leans forward, and his voice drops very low. "Is it *instead*? Now there's nobody coming up the path? Am I what's left over when the lamp's out? Because if I am, I'd rather you went back to your *my dear chap*. I'd rather —"

"You filled that lamp," says Reggie.

"I know I filled it."

"Who for?"

Pascual stops.

"It wasn't his," he says slowly. "He never saw it. It was never his lamp." He looks at it, on the table. "It was yours. I filled it for you."

"Then it isn't instead," says Reggie. "It can't be. You were filling it the whole time he was — you were here before it was lit." He hears his voice go, and lets it. "He's in the drawer, Pascual. He's staying in the drawer. And you wouldn't fit."

Pascual makes a sound. It's half a laugh and half the other thing, and he puts his hand over his mouth.

"That's a *terrible* thing to say to a man," he says through his fingers.

"Is it a yes?"

"You asked. I've said."

"You haven't, actually."

"*Yes*," says Pascual, much too loudly, and they both look at the shutter. He says it again, quieter, furious, into the dark. "Yes. *Sí*. Yes, you idiot. Don't make me say it again or I'll shout it."

Reggie starts to laugh. He can't stop. He sits on the station chair with his glasses on the table and laughs into his hands, without a sound, because of the row. After a moment the bed creaks and Pascual's laughing too, with his hand pressed to his sore shoulder, saying *ay, ay, don't*, because it still hurts to laugh.

When it's done Pascual says, "Jean. In the morning."

"I was going to say."

"We'll ask her together. I'm not having you go in there on your own and do the voice at her."

"I wasn't going to —"

"You were."

Reggie considers it.

"I might have," he says.

---

He gets up. He crosses the hut, which is four steps, and stands in front of the bed, and finds that he doesn't know how to do the next thing. He's fifty-four. He's done the next thing before. Doing it having asked first turns out to be an entirely different operation.

"May I —" he says, and stops.

Pascual looks up at him.

"Are you going to ask about everything now?"

"Yes."

"*Everything*?"

"I've a good deal to make up."

"Ask, then," says Pascual. His face in the slatted dark has gone soft round the eyes, and his voice has gone rough.

"May I kiss you?"

"You've *done* that."

"I'm asking properly this time."

"Then yes. *Properly*."

He puts his hand on Pascual's jaw. He does it slowly, so he can feel himself do it: the stubble under his palm, warm, rough, grey, and the jaw moving under it as Pascual swallows. He tips Pascual's face up, and bends, and kisses him.

It's nothing like the first. The first fell on them. This one he's holding the whole time, and he can feel every part of it. Pascual's mouth is warm and tastes of the tea he had at seven. He breathes out through his nose, hard and uneven, against Reggie's cheek. His left hand finds Reggie's hip and takes hold of it and pulls, and Reggie comes, and ends up standing between Pascual's knees with Pascual's face tipped up to his, kissing him with both hands on either side of his head, and Pascual making a low steady sound into it, as though he'd been waiting to make it for a very long time and wasn't going to hurry now he'd got the chance.

"*Reggie*," says Pascual, against his mouth.

"Yes."

"Say something. You've gone quiet. You go quiet when you're reading."

"I'm not reading. I'm —" He doesn't know. He tries. "I like this. I like your mouth. I'd like very much to go on doing this for some time. Is that something?"

Pascual goes still under his hands.

"There's no voice," he says.

"No."

"You said that in your own voice."

"It came to keep you from seeing something," says Reggie. He finds it's true as he says it. "You've seen it. There's nothing left for it to hide."

"*Hombre*," says Pascual, quite helplessly, and pulls him down.

---

The bed was built for one man in 1950 by a carpenter who didn't hold with comfort, and they get about a minute and a half on it before Pascual puts his weight on the wrong arm and says something in Aragonese that makes the lamp rattle.

"Right," says Reggie. "The mattress."

"What about it?"

"Floor."

Pascual lies on his back on the narrow bed with his hand on his shoulder and looks up at him.

"We're scientists," says Reggie. "It's a practical problem."

"You're not to *lift* it."

"I'm not lifting it. I'm dragging it."

"Then I'm dragging the other end."

"With one arm?"

"With one and a half. I've had a dead porpoise up the slip with less." Pascual's already getting off the bed. "It's a mattress, Reggie. It weighs less than the calf."

"*Everything* weighs less than the calf."

They drag it between them, Pascual with a fistful of the near corner in his left hand and Reggie with the far end, off the frame and down onto the boards in the middle of the floor, with the sheet and the pillow and the blanket after it. Pascual says *left a bit, no, your left* all the way across, in exactly the voice Reggie used on the students in the pen. Reggie tells him if he says it once more he'll sleep on the frame. Pascual says it once more.

Then Pascual comes over and starts on Reggie's shirt, the left hand doing most of it and the right coming in stiffly on the buttonholes.

It takes a long time. Reggie stands and lets him. Pascual gets the top button and the second, and at the third he stops and puts two fingers into the breast pocket and takes out the little brown bottle. He crouches, with a grunt, and sets it on the boards at the head of the mattress, where a hand could find it in the dark. Then he straightens up and goes on with the buttons.

Reggie looks down at the bottle.

"Is that meant to be romantic?"

"That's medicine," says Pascual. "The romantic part's that I've put it where you can reach it."

"I'll bear that in mind."

"You'll *take* one if I say so."

"I'll take one if I need one."

"If I *say* so, Reggie."

"If I need one," says Reggie, "and you'll say so, and we'll have a row about it, and then I'll take it. How's that?"

Pascual thinks about it with his hand on the fourth button.

"No," he says. "You'll take it. *Then* we'll have the row."

"That's the wrong way round."

"It's the right way round. You can't row with me if you're dead, you'll lose on a technicality."

Reggie opens his mouth, and finds he's got nothing to put against that.

"Fine," he says. "Tablet first. Row after."

"Good," says Pascual, and undoes the button.

He gets the shirt open. He puts his hand flat on Reggie's chest, and then he moves it, slowly, down and round to his side, and spreads it. His fingers reach nearly from Reggie's breastbone to his spine. Reggie feels him feel the ribs, one at a time, the way he'd feel a flank for condition.

"Marguerite's right," says Pascual. "You want feeding."

"Not *now*."

"No." His thumb moves on the ribs. "Not now."

Reggie gets his own hands on Pascual properly then, with the liniment still on them. He knows Pascual's back, in the way you know a coastline from a boat: the width of it across the shoulders, the grey on the chest, the thickening at the waist that wasn't there in 1952. He's known it at arm's length for twenty years. He puts his hands on it now, flat, low, and slides them up either side of Pascual's spine, over warm skin and the ridge of the old scar where the Colonel's mare had him in 1958. Pascual shudders all down his length and breathes out *oh* through his teeth.

"Your hands are cold."

"They're *liniment*. You'll smell like the Colonel."

"*Don't*. Don't say the Colonel."

"His *knees*, Pascual."

"*Reggie*."

He's laughing into Pascual's shoulder, the good one, and Pascual's laughing too, and then Pascual turns his head and gets his mouth on Reggie's neck, just under the ear, and Reggie stops laughing all at once.

"Oh," says Reggie. "That's — do that again."

"Is that asking?"

"That's *asking*."

Pascual does it again. He does it slowly, with his mouth open and the edge of his teeth, down the side of Reggie's throat to the collarbone. Reggie stands in the middle of his own hut with his shirt hanging off him and his hands spread on Pascual's back and his head tipped over to give him room, and hears himself make a sound he doesn't recognise.

They get the rest off between them, badly, with three and a half working arms between them and a good deal of swearing. And then they're down on the mattress on the floor in the slatted moon, and Pascual's lying on his left side, which is the side that doesn't complain, with the sore arm across Reggie's chest. And Reggie's on his back with the whole warm heavy length of Pascual along him, shoulder to ankle, and he can't think of a single thing.

"You're on your back," says Pascual.

"Am I."

"Stay there."

"Is that the vet?"

"It's the vet. You're the worst animal on the station, and you'll stay on your back and you'll *let* me."

"I want to see you."

"You can see me. I'm right here." Pascual's face is six inches over his. "Look, then."

Reggie looks.

He'd thought he knew the face. He's had it across a table and a rail and a pen for twenty years, and it's shown him everything the whole time; that's the thing about Pascual's face. It doesn't hide things. But it's not been this close before with nothing in the way, showing him *this*, all of it, pointed at him. And he realises, looking, that it's been showing him a good part of this for a long while, and he hasn't been reading it, because he'd been pointed somewhere else.

"You've gone quiet," says Pascual.

"You're very —" He puts his hand up to Pascual's face. "I'm looking. You said look."

"I didn't say *read*."

"I'm not reading." He isn't. That's what astonishes him. There's nothing to sort, nothing to measure, nothing to keep off anybody. There's just the man. "I'm looking at you. It's different. I've only just found out it's different."

Pascual bends his head and kisses him. He does it hard, as though Reggie had said something that needed stopping, and his left hand goes down Reggie's side, over the ribs, and settles on his hip, broad and certain, and holds on.

It's slow. It has to be. There's the arm, and there's the heart, and they're neither of them young. And every so often one of them stops, stunned, and just breathes, and the other waits. Pascual moves against him, a long steady roll of the hips, and then again, and Reggie's hands find the small of his back and pull him closer, and there's nothing between them at all now but the heat and the damp and the wintergreen. Reggie turns his face into Pascual's neck and breathes there, and tastes salt, and the stable smell, and under it something that's only Pascual, that he's smelt for twenty years on jerseys and in doorways and not once put a name to.

"*Así*," says Pascual, very low, somewhere over his head. "*Así, así*."

"What's that?"

"*Like that*."

"I'd gathered."

"Then why'd you *ask*?"

"I've taken it up," says Reggie. "Asking. You told me to."

Pascual laughs, helplessly, into his hair, and doesn't stop moving, and Reggie laughs too, and then neither of them is laughing.

At some point Pascual's hand comes up and lies flat over the middle of Reggie's chest, over his heart, and stays there. Reggie can feel him counting. He can feel it in the fingers, the way you'd feel a man tapping out a rhythm on a table.

"Stop counting."

"I can't."

"That's *my* line."

"You can have it back when you've a heart I trust," says Pascual, and his voice cracks straight down the middle. "Let me count. *Please*. Let me."

So Reggie lets him. He lies on his back on the mattress on the floor with Pascual's palm on his chest, counting him, held on to thoroughly, and lets that happen too. And he finds he's saying Pascual's name, over and over, low, into the dark, the way you'd say a figure you'd been carrying about all day to keep it from going. Pascual says his back. He doesn't say *hombre*. He doesn't bellow it across the north pen in two languages. He says *Reggie* into his mouth, quietly, as though it were a word he'd been keeping somewhere safe for twenty years and had only just been allowed to take out and use.

It goes through Reggie like the tide turning. It lifts him off the coral all at once, and he holds on to Pascual's back with both hands and lets it. A moment after, Pascual's breath catches and goes, and he shakes all along Reggie's length, and says something in Spanish that Reggie doesn't ask him to translate.

---

Afterwards they lie on the mattress in the middle of the floor. The sheet's gone somewhere, and neither of them looks for it. The hut smells overwhelmingly of horse.

Pascual's hand has gone straight to Reggie's wrist. Reggie lets him.

"Well?" says Reggie.

Pascual counts. He takes his time about it.

"Ninety-six," he says at last.

"Is that bad?"

"It's *magnificent*." He sounds indecently pleased. "It's showing off. It'll be down to eighty in ten minutes. I'll check."

"You'll check."

"I'll check." He lets go of the wrist and doesn't take his hand away. "I'm checking all night."

"You'll be asleep in five minutes."

"I will not."

The brown bottle's by Reggie's head where Pascual put it. He looks at it, and doesn't need it, and is glad it's there.

"I've got to tell you something," says Pascual, into his shoulder.

"Go on."

"I didn't fill the lamp."

"It doesn't need it."

"I know it doesn't. I was going to fill it anyway. I'd got the can." He sounds genuinely troubled. "It's the first Saturday since 'fifty-eight I've not filled it."

"Fill it tomorrow."

"Tomorrow's Sunday."

"Then fill it on Sunday," says Reggie. "It's been a very peculiar week."

Pascual laughs, a long way down in his chest, where Reggie can feel it. Then he doesn't say anything. After a while Reggie realises he's asleep, with his mouth a little open and his sore arm across Reggie's chest and his other hand still loosely round his wrist.

Reggie lies in the dark. There's a shutter along the row that opened and didn't shut. He thinks about it for a minute, and decides that it's a thing for the morning, and that in the morning they'll go and see Jean, together, and he won't do the voice. Out in the north pen the calf's calf blows, high and thin.

He doesn't count it. He goes to sleep.

---

Baptiste's on the verandah of the long room at a quarter to eight on the Sunday, with the green book open on the rail.

The Colonel's beside her in a cane chair, in a very good humour. It's the full moon, and he's explaining to her that if the calf had only waited two days, as any sensible animal would have, he'd be half a crown to the good. Baptiste is listening with every appearance of attention and writing the seven forty-five line at the same time.

Pascual comes up the steps behind Reggie. Reggie takes them one at a time. Pascual's arm's in a sling that Reggie made him at six this morning out of a pillowcase. Pascual has said three times since that the arm doesn't need it, which is nearly true, and hasn't taken it off.

"Good morning, Dr Tar. Good morning, Dr Pobil." Baptiste looks up. She takes in the sling, and the pair of them, and the three feet between them, and whatever their faces are doing. Pascual has no idea what his is doing. He's given up trying to know. "Resp. twelve and forty. Both feeding. Dr Pobil, you'll want to initial the night lines, you're the vet."

She turns the book round on the rail and holds out the pencil.

Pascual takes it in his left hand and bends over the page. He reads English slowly, and he reads it now with his lips moving on the long words, down the column, a line every half hour through Saturday evening. *19.30. 12/40. Both at N. end.* *20.00. 12/38. Feeding.*

*20.40. Mother restless, surfacing short. Raised voices from end of row (Drs T. & P., re water chemistry). 20.52. Settled.*

He stops.

He can feel his whole face go, all at once, ears first. He can't do anything about it. He gave up trying in about 1940.

Reggie's standing at his shoulder. Reggie reads upside down, and has done since 1946, and has certainly read it. Pascual doesn't dare look round at him.

"Water chemistry," says Pascual, to the book.

"Yes, Dr Pobil," says Baptiste. "You were very loud. It seemed the most likely thing."

The Colonel says, from his chair, "Were you? I didn't hear a thing. You two and your water. You've been at it since 1953."

"Since 'fifty-two," says Reggie. His voice is not entirely steady. "Thank you, Baptiste."

"It's a log, Dr Tar." She takes the book back, and turns it round the right way, and looks down at it. "I put down what seemed most likely."

Pascual initials the line. His hand isn't steady either, and it's the wrong hand, and the *P* comes out like a fish-hook. He gives her back the pencil.

Then he and Reggie go along the verandah to the office, where Jean's door is shut and Jean is on the other side of it with the Sunday transects. They go at Reggie's pace. Pascual matches it without saying anything, not three feet apart.

Behind them, the Colonel says, "Were they really shouting about water?"

And Baptiste says, "It's in the book, Colonel," and writes the eight o'clock line.
