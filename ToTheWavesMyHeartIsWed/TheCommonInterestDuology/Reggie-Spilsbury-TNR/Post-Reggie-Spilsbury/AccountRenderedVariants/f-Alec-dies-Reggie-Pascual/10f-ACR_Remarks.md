# Remarks

> *William Martin Institute*
> *19th November 1972*
>
> *Dear Tom,*
>
> *Thank you for the Lowestoft postcard, which is the ugliest thing I have ever received and is now pinned over my bench. The lab sounds grim and cold and you sound happy, so I'm glad.*
>
> *News. The thesis goes on. Chapter four is a swamp. Dr Leslie says it's a very interesting swamp, which is worse than if she'd said it was bad.*
>
> *The Spilsbury Student has come. She's called Baptiste. She's from St Augustine and she's terrifying and I love her. She's been here three weeks. On the first night she sat opposite Tar and Pobil at dinner, and watched them argue about the calf's teeth for forty minutes, and didn't say a word. Afterwards on the verandah she said, perfectly calmly, "How long have they been like that?"*
>
> *I said, like what?*
>
> *She looked at me. You know the look. The one Tar does over a bad set of figures. And she said, "Yvonne. Don't."*
>
> *So I told her. Everything. The Saturdays. The oil can. The lamp, the one that's never lit any more, and him filling it anyway every Saturday. Pobil on the floor of Dr Leslie's office in 1971, the day the photograph came. You know, the day we weren't supposed to have seen through the window, and did. The step. All of it.*
>
> *She listened to the whole thing with her notebook shut. And at the end she said, "And they don't know."*
>
> *And I said no, Tom. They don't know. Neither of them. Not the faintest idea. We've been watching since the reef and they've got no idea at all, and I'm starting to think they never will.*
>
> *She said, "We'll see."*
>
> *I've seen that look on her before. I think we've got a project.*
>
> *Yours, with swamp,*
>
> *Y.*

---

Baptiste has been on the cay for a month when she opens a new notebook for it.

She doesn't mean to. She's here for the calf. She's here for the Spilsbury Studentship, which is the first of its kind, and she's got it in writing that she's to spend three years on the growth and development of the Institute's captive animals. And she's going to, properly, because she's never done anything any other way. She has four notebooks open already. One is for the calf's weights and one for her reading. One is for Dr Leslie's transect methods, which are beautiful. And one is her own, for things she doesn't know what to do with yet.

The fifth one is small, and black, and lives in the pocket of her oilskin. She rules it on the first of December with the edge of a saucer, in columns, because that's how she thinks.

*Date. Time. Location. Observation. Remarks.*

The first entry is from memory.

*29.x.72. Arrival. On the jetty, Dr Tar carried my suitcase. Told me about Dr Spilsbury, a fuse box, 1946. Forty-five minutes. Dr P. brought tea. Dr T. said thank you without looking up. Dr P. stood with the teapot for a while. Remarks: ?*

She looks at the question mark. She doesn't cross it out. She's been told since she was eleven that a question mark is a perfectly respectable remark.

---

It's Dacosta who briefs her, properly, on the second Sunday. They sit on the end of the jetty with their feet over the water after dinner, where you can't be heard from the long room.

Dacosta's in her fourth year, writing up. She stayed on after Whitlock went home because chapter four needs her, and because, she says, somebody has to keep an eye. She's small and sharp, from Bridgetown, with two white coral scars on each forearm that she says she got on the reef the night of the stranding. She says it the way you'd say you got a medal.

"It was the lamp," Dacosta says. "That's how we knew. Tom and me. That night."

"Which lamp?"

"His. The one at the end of the row. It burnt all night every night for fourteen years, you could see it from the Bay. Everybody said it was because he didn't sleep. Then his friend died, Dr Spilsbury, and it was a bad year, the worst." Dacosta looks at the water. "And then in the autumn it went out. And it stayed out. And that night, with the reef, we looked along the row and it was dark, and we didn't knock. Tom said we had to. I said no. He's sleeping. We'd never once seen him sleep."

"And you were right."

"We got her off," says Dacosta, not quite steadily. "By the drill. He'd drilled us all autumn with our eyes shut." She stops. "Anyway. The morning after, he came in and found us asleep at the long table, and read the log. Then Pobil came over on the first boat with his hair on end, and they talked, and we were supposed to be asleep. And afterwards Pobil sat down next to him, and put his hand on the back of his neck, for about a second. And Tar let him."

Baptiste waits.

"That's all," says Dacosta. "That's all it was. A second. But Tom saw it through his eyelashes, and I saw it through mine, and when we got upstairs Tom said, *Yvonne*. And I said, *I know*. And we've known ever since. Ten months. And *they* don't."

"How can they not?"

"I don't know!" Dacosta throws up her hands. "I don't know. He reads *everybody*. You've seen it. He reads you in four minutes flat and tells you what's wrong with your chapter before you know. And he's never once looked at Pobil. Not properly. It's like Pobil's the wall."

"And Dr Pobil?"

"Dr Pobil," says Dacosta, "has a face that shows every single thing he's ever felt, and he's never once noticed what it's showing. He goes into that hut every Saturday with the oil can and fills a lamp that hasn't been lit since November 'seventy-one. He sits on the bed and says *well?* and Tar tells him the week. Every Saturday. And he comes out, and I swear to God, Claudette, he looks like a man who's been to church."

Baptiste looks at the dark row of huts. There's a light in Dr Pobil's, and none in the last one at the end.

"He talks about Dr Spilsbury," she says slowly, "the way my grandmother talks about my grandfather. Since he died. As if he'd just gone out and was coming back for his tea."

Dacosta goes very still beside her.

"You got that too," she says. "Did you."

"In the first forty-five minutes."

"Tom and I took about a year to get that." Dacosta looks at her with something like awe. "*Christ*. You're good."

"Does Dr Pobil know? About —"

"About Spilsbury? He knows everything. He was there. He was on the floor of the office." Dacosta pulls her knees up. "That's the thing, isn't it. He *knows*. He knows all of it and he's still there every Saturday. He's never going to say anything. He'd think it was — I don't know. Taking a place that wasn't his."

Baptiste thinks about that for a long time, with her feet over the dark water.

"Then somebody should help," she says.

---

In January Baptiste tries one more thing. It's the last, she tells Dacosta afterwards, and it's her own fault, because it's the only one she does without writing it down first.

It's a Saturday. She waits at the top of the path at ten, when Dr Pobil comes out of his hut with the oil can, the way he does. She falls into step beside him, as though she's going the same way, which she isn't.

"Dr Pobil," she says. "Can I ask you something about the lamp?"

He stops dead on the path.

His face does everything. She's been on the island three months and she's never seen it do quite so much at once. It goes red, and then very still, and then something she can't name at all.

"What about it?"

"Only why you fill it. When it's not lit." She makes her voice as ordinary as she can. "I wondered if it was for a reason. A method. In case it's needed."

Dr Pobil looks at the oil can in his hand.

"Habit," he says. "Sixteen years of habit. I've done it since 'fifty-seven. You don't stop a thing like that just because it's —" He stops. "Just because."

"No," says Baptiste. "I suppose you don't."

"Someone has to," says Dr Pobil, to the oil can. "In case. He might want it one night. You never know." And then, as though he'd heard himself, he looks up at her quite sharply. "Why?"

"No reason," says Baptiste. "Just curious. Sorry, Dr Pobil."

He looks at her a moment longer. Then he nods, and goes on down the path to the hut at the end of the row. She watches him go in, and hears his voice through the shutter, and Dr Tar's, and the bed creak.

*Well?*

That night she writes it in the book, in the *Observation* column, word for word. In the *Remarks* column she writes, after a long time, only: *In case he might want it one night.*

She doesn't show that page to Dacosta. She doesn't show it to anybody. She puts the book away in her oilskin pocket, and lies awake a long time.

> *We've given up.*
>
> *It was Baptiste who said it, which is how I know it's serious. She closed the black book at dinner on Thursday, in front of me and Farrant, and put it in her pocket. She said, "I think we have to leave them alone now. I think we've been rude." And then, after a bit, "It's theirs. Whatever it is. It isn't ours to push."*
>
> *And she's right, Tom. I know she is. I feel quite ashamed. We've been treating two grown men like the calf's mother's breeding programme. And they're happy. They are. You'd see it if you were here. Tar's so much better than he was that year. He sleeps. He laughs. He talks about Dr Spilsbury in the sun like somebody who's allowed to. And Pobil's there, every day, every Saturday, the way he's always been. Maybe that's all it is. Maybe that's all it needs to be. Maybe we made the rest up because we're young and we want everything to end like a book.*
>

---

The calf calves on the sixteenth of March at twelve minutes past six in the morning. Baptiste will remember the time all her life. She wrote it down.

She's on the wall at the far end of the north pen all night with the green book on her knee and a torch in her teeth, writing a line every five minutes. Dacosta sleeps on the wall beside her for an hour around three and wakes with a crick in her neck. Dr Leslie brings tea at two. Baptiste watches it all.

She watches Dr Pobil in the water to his waist with his hand flat on the calf's side. She watches Dr Tar on the wall above him with a lamp in each hand, not saying anything, for hours. She's never known Dr Tar go two minutes without saying anything. She watches the lamps, which are held out exactly where Dr Pobil needs them every minute of the night. Nobody asks for them to be moved. Nobody says *left a bit*. They're just there.

At four, when the calf goes still, she watches Dr Pobil look up. She sees Dr Tar look back down at him over the lamps with his knuckles gone white, and say *wait*, very quietly. She watches Dr Pobil wait.

She writes it down, the time and the resp. and *posture: still*. In the *Remarks* column her pencil stops. She doesn't write anything. There isn't a remark for that.

At a quarter to six the calf comes round wrong, and Dr Pobil says so, and his voice isn't a vet's voice at all. And Dr Tar's comes back down to him off the wall, very steady, the way it came to Dacosta on the reef in her head: *go with her. Don't fight her.* And then there's a calf in the water, blowing, and the sky over the second head goes gold. Baptiste says *six-twelve* out loud, in a voice she doesn't recognise, because it's the time and somebody has to.

Then Dr Tar's sending them up the path, her and Dr Leslie. The Colonel, the camera, the scales, the small ones. And they go at a run.

She gets to the top of the path and stops.

She doesn't know why. It's the scales. She's not sure, suddenly, whether he meant the small spring balance or the small platform one. She turns round to call back and ask.

And she sees them.

The north pen's all gold and grey, and the calf's blowing, and the two of them are standing in the water to their waists in the middle of it. Dr Tar has his hands on either side of Dr Pobil's face. And he's kissing him.

Baptiste stands at the top of the path with her mouth open.

She sees Dr Pobil's arms come round him. She sees it go on, a second, two, three. And then she sees them stop, both at once, as if a line had been cut, and stand a foot apart in the water, staring at each other. Even from the top of the path she can see Dr Pobil's face. It's doing everything it's ever done, all at the same time.

She hears Dr Tar say, quite clearly, across the whole of the pen, "*What*."

And she turns round and goes after Dr Leslie very fast, and doesn't call back about the scales.

---

She gets the platform balance. It's the wrong one. Nobody notices.

When they come back down the path, the Colonel in his pyjamas on Dr Leslie's arm and on hers, Dr Tar is at the south wall of the pen and Dr Pobil's at the north. They're both in the water to the waist, and both looking at the calf with an attention so ferocious it could have boiled the sea. There are fifteen feet between them. Neither of them looks round.

The Colonel bursts into tears.

Baptiste helps him sit down on the wall. Then she opens the green book at the far end, where she's left it, and finds the last line. *06.12. Calf born. Resp. strong. Mother attending.*

She looks at her watch. It's twenty-one minutes past. She looks at the two men at opposite ends of the pen.

She writes the next line, in the *Time* column, the time she'd looked at her watch at the top of the path without meaning to. It's the sort of thing she does without meaning to. *06.14.*

And in *Remarks*, before she can stop herself, in her smallest hand:

*Oh!*

She looks at it.

Then she takes her pencil and draws one neat line through it, straight, so that you couldn't possibly read what was under it. Unless you were the sort of person who reads everything, upside down. And she closes the book.

---

Breakfast is the worst hour of her life, and the best.

Dr Tar sits at the head of the table between the Colonel and Dr Leslie and eats his toast. He looks at nobody. He's white. He's got the look he gets over a bad set of figures when he's about to find the mistake, and she knows what that means, because she's seen him do it to her chapter plan. He's reading. He's reading something, very fast, and it isn't on the table.

And Dr Pobil is at the students' end.

Dr Pobil has never once in the history of the Institute sat at the students' end. He sits at Dr Tar's elbow and steals his toast. That's where he sits. It's where he's always sat. And this morning he comes in with his hair still wet and sits down in the empty chair between Farrant and Dacosta, directly opposite Baptiste. He eats a bowl of porridge with his head down, as though somebody had told him there was a prize for it.

Nobody at the students' end says anything.

Dacosta, beside Dr Pobil, has gone completely rigid. Farrant, on his other side, is looking at the ceiling as though it had spoken to him. And under the table Baptiste feels a foot find hers and kick it, hard, twice. It's Dacosta. Dacosta's looking at her across Dr Pobil's bent head with her eyes the size of saucers, and mouthing a single word.

*What?*

Baptiste looks back at her. She lifts her eyebrows about an eighth of an inch. It's the only answer she can give with Dr Pobil eighteen inches away.

Dacosta's whole face goes.

It goes the way Dr Pobil's goes. Baptiste watches it happen. It goes red, and then white, and then something she's never seen a face do at a breakfast table. Dacosta puts her napkin up to her mouth very fast and holds it there.

At the head of the table Dr Tar puts his toast down.

He gets up. He comes down the long table past the students, not looking at any of them, and stops behind Dr Pobil's chair. Dr Pobil goes rigid, with his spoon halfway to his mouth.

"The step," says Dr Tar. "Tonight. Please."

Dr Pobil doesn't turn round.

"Yes," he says to his porridge. "All right."

Dr Tar goes out.

The door swings shut. Dr Pobil sits with his spoon halfway. Dacosta has her napkin over her mouth. Farrant's still addressing the ceiling. The whole of the students' end of the long table has turned into a waxworks.

Somebody has to do something. So Baptiste picks up the big brown pot, which is nearest, and holds it out over the table.

"Would anybody like more tea?" she says.

---

She doesn't sleep much. She lies in her hut with the shutters open, and at about nine she hears the step.

It's not her step. It's the one at the end of the row. She hears the door of the hut beside it, Dr Pobil's, open and close, and feet on the path, ten yards. And then nothing. Voices, low. She can't hear what they're saying, and she doesn't try.

She lies on her back and looks at the ceiling. She thinks about a man who's filled a lamp every Saturday for sixteen years, and for a year and a half after it went out, *in case he might want it one night*. About a man who sat on a wall all night with a lamp in each hand, holding them exactly where they were needed, without being asked.

She thinks: *They're on the step. They're on the step, and they're talking.*

Some time later she hears a door. Just one.

She doesn't write it down. She finds she can't. Some things don't go in a column.

---

In the morning at eight she's on the verandah with the green book open on the rail, doing the eight o'clock line for the calf. The office door opens behind her. She hears Dr Leslie's voice, very dry, saying *go away, I've got transects*. Then feet, two sets, coming out onto the verandah.

She doesn't turn round straight away. She finishes the line. Then she looks up.

They're coming out of the office side by side. They're three feet apart, very carefully, like men who've been told to be careful and are taking it seriously. Dr Pobil's ears are red. Dr Tar's face is doing something at the corner of the mouth that it can't quite stop. Both of them look, in a way she can't put into any column in any book, the way the calf's mother looked yesterday morning in the gold light, turning her whole vast body round in the water to find what she'd been waiting for.

"Resp. twelve," says Baptiste. "Both of them. The little one's feeding."

"Thank you, Baptiste," says Dr Tar.

"Good morning, Dr Tar. Good morning, Dr Pobil." She looks back down at the book and writes the line. "It's a very good morning," she says, to the book.

She doesn't look up again. She doesn't need to. She can feel Dr Tar stop beside the rail. She can feel him look at the book, open under her hand, at yesterday's page. And she knows exactly what he's looking at, and that he can read it perfectly well, upside down, through a single neat pencil line.

She keeps writing. She writes *posture: mother and calf together, N end*, very neatly, and doesn't look up.

Out of the corner of her eye she sees his hand come up over his mouth.

He stands there for quite a long time looking at the sea. Then he goes down the steps to the north pen with Dr Pobil beside him. They're not three feet apart any more. Nobody's told them they can stop being careful. They've just forgotten.
