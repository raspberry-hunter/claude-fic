# Windfall

The calf is going to calve.

She's sixteen and enormous and has been the calf since 1957, and will be the calf until she dies, because nobody on the cay has ever been able to call her anything else. She's been carrying for the best part of a year. For the last three weeks she's been lying in the shallows at the north end of the pen, low in the water, blowing slow, with a look in her eye that Pascual says is *contemplative* and Reggie says is *fed up*.

The long room has a book open on it. It's not the stranding log; it's a new one, a green one, and Baptiste keeps it. It's got columns. Reggie didn't ask her to rule it. He came in on the first of March and it was there on the bench, ruled, *Date, Time, Resp., Posture, Remarks*. Every fifteen minutes since, in Baptiste's upright hand, there's a line.

He has the Spilsbury Student timing the calf.

He finds that he minds this a great deal less than he'd have expected. He finds he likes it. He likes coming up the path at six with the thermometer and finding her at the rail in her oilskin with a torch and a watch and the book. She looks up and says *twenty-two, Dr Tar, and she rolled at twenty past*, as though it were the most natural thing in the world to be doing at six in the morning, which it is.

He can say the name now. That's the thing that still surprises him, two years on, most mornings. The Spilsbury Student. The Spilsbury deposit. *Spilsbury's decline curve, Baptiste, go and read it again.* It comes out of his mouth in his ordinary voice and goes where he sends it, like any other word, and nothing comes on behind it. He stood on the jetty last October and told the girl about a fuse box in 1946 in the sun. He's told her a good deal since. She listens with her notebook shut on her knee, and doesn't write any of it down, and he's grateful for that without quite knowing why.

The lamp's been out since November 1971. He sleeps in the dark. He sleeps, most nights, like a stone.

And Pascual's still next door. Of course he is. Pascual's been next door since 1952. There was never a woman at the Bay who made him move, though there were a couple who might have, and Reggie's never once wondered why. Pascual's there on the step at night, and at the rail in the morning, and at the long table shouting about water chemistry, and in the doorway of the hut with the oil can on Saturdays. He goes on coming on Saturdays though the lamp doesn't need him, because it's what he does on Saturdays. He sits on the bed and says *well?* and Reggie tells him the week.

It was Pascual who sat on the floor of Jean's office with his forehead against Reggie's arm in May 1971, when the photograph came. It was Pascual on the step in August, with the proof square between their feet, who heard the whole of the window and called him a manipulative bastard and cried. And it was Pascual who said *it was worth it* back to him, and meant it, and has never once since treated him as though he were anything but the man at the end of the row.

Reggie's never thought about any of that much. It's just how things are. It's the water in the pen. You don't think about the water.

---

It starts on the fifteenth of March at a quarter past nine at night. Pascual knows the moment it starts because Baptiste comes to the door of the long room with the green book in her hand and says, "Dr Pobil. Her resp's gone to eighteen. And she's turned over twice in ten minutes."

He's up and out and down the path before she's finished.

Reggie's already there. Of course he is. He's standing at the rail of the north pen in his shirtsleeves with his glasses pushed up, not doing anything, which is how Pascual knows he's frightened. Reggie Tar at a rail with his hands still is a man holding himself down.

"It's started," says Reggie.

"I can see it's started."

"She's early."

"She's not early. She's sixteen and it's her first and she's exactly on time, and you've been saying *early* since February so as not to lose." Pascual swings his leg over the wall. "Get the lamps. Baptiste, the book. Every five minutes now, not fifteen. And somebody wake Jean."

"Jean's awake," says Jean, from the top of the path in her dressing gown, with a torch. "I've been awake since she turned over. The whole station's been awake since she turned over. Get *on* with it."

---

It takes all night.

It's a long night and a slow one. Most of it is waiting, which Pascual is good at and Reggie is not. The calf lies in the shallows at the north end with her great grey side heaving, and breathes, and rests, and heaves again. Pascual stands in the water beside her to his waist with his hand flat on her flank, feeling it go. Reggie stands on the wall above with a lamp in each hand, holding them out over the water where Pascual needs them, and doesn't say anything. For hours he doesn't say anything. It's the longest Pascual has ever known him keep quiet.

Baptiste sits on the wall at the other end with the green book on her knee, and a torch in her teeth, and writes a line every five minutes, and doesn't miss one.

At about two Jean goes up and makes tea, and brings it down in a can, and they drink it standing in the water and on the wall, and Pascual can't taste it. At about four the calf stops heaving altogether for forty minutes. Pascual feels her go still under his hand and his heart goes into his mouth, and he looks up at Reggie on the wall. Reggie looks back down at him over the lamps. His face is doing nothing at all, and his knuckles are white on the handles.

"Wait," says Reggie. Quite quietly. "She's resting. Wait."

"I *know* she's resting. I'm not blind."

"Then wait."

He waits. After forty minutes she starts again.

At a quarter to six the sky's going grey behind the second head. She makes a sound Pascual's never heard a sea animal make, deep and long, and he feels the whole of her go tight under his hands. And he knows, the way you know, that something is wrong.

"She's turned it," he says. "The calf. It's come round wrong. It's the flipper, Reggie, I can feel the —"

"Can you get it back?"

"I don't know."

"Yes, you do," says Reggie from the wall. His voice has gone very steady, the way it goes at a stranding, the way it went on the reef in 1952. "You've done it with a horse. You did it with the Colonel's mare in 1958, I held the lamp. Same thing. Push it back and turn it and wait for her. Don't fight her. Go with her."

"She's not a *horse*, Reggie, she's —"

"No. She's better than a horse. She's cleverer. Do it."

He does it.

He'll remember it all his life. He'll remember very little of it clearly. There's the cold of the water coming up grey, and his arm to the shoulder, and her great strength closing on him and letting go. Reggie's voice is over his head, calm, saying *now, wait, now*. And there's a moment when it turns under his hand, and goes right, and she heaves.

Then there's a calf in the water.

It's small and dark and perfect, and it's coming up. It's coming up through the grey water under his arms, blowing, a little high thin sound, its first. Its mother turns her whole vast body round in the shallows to find it and puts herself under it and lifts it. The sky over the second head goes from grey to gold.

Baptiste, on the wall at the far end, says in a voice that cracks straight down the middle, "*Six-twelve*."

Jean, beside her, has both hands over her mouth.

Reggie puts both the lamps down on the wall. He does it quite carefully. Then he gets over the wall and down into the pen, in his shirtsleeves and trousers, into the water to his waist. He wades across to where Pascual's standing with his arms still out, shaking all over, soaked to the neck, and looking at the calf.

"Jean," says Reggie, not taking his eyes off it. "Would you get the Colonel. He'll never forgive us. And the camera, it's in my hut. Baptiste, go with her, bring the scales, the small ones —"

They go. They go up the path at a run, the pair of them, Jean in her dressing gown and Baptiste with the book, and the pen's empty. There's the calf and her calf, and the gold coming up over the water, and the two of them standing in it to their waists.

"You did it," says Reggie.

"*We* did it. We'd have lost her without the lamps."

"You did it. I held the lamps." Reggie's laughing. He's laughing the real laugh, the short one, and it's coming out of him in pieces, and there are tears running down his face under his glasses and he doesn't seem to know. "Pascual. Look at it. *Look* at it. It's *breathing*."

"I'm looking."

"Sixteen years. She was *born* in this pen. You were — you had your hand on her when *she* came up, in 'fifty-seven, do you remember, and now she's —"

"I'll not forget it."

"You did it," says Reggie again, and puts both his hands on either side of Pascual's face.

He does it the way he'd take hold of anything he was glad of, a bucket, a result, the side of an animal that's come right. He does it without thinking at all. He's standing in the north pen in the sunrise with the water to his waist and his face wet, holding Pascual's face between his hands.

And then he kisses him.

Pascual doesn't see it coming. He'd swear to that later, and he'd be telling the truth. He doesn't see it coming because it isn't coming. It isn't *anywhere*. It doesn't exist, and then it does. It's Reggie's mouth on his, salt and cold and laughing, hard, in the middle of the north pen at six fourteen in the morning.

And Pascual kisses him back.

He doesn't decide to. He doesn't decide anything. His arms, which are still out from the calf and shaking, come round Reggie by themselves. His hands are full of wet shirt, and he's pulling, and Reggie makes a sound against his mouth, and the calf blows behind them, high and thin.

Then it stops.

It stops because they both stop, at exactly the same moment, as though a line had been cut. Reggie takes his hands off Pascual's face and Pascual takes his off Reggie's shirt. They stand in the north pen with the water to their waists, about a foot apart, and stare at each other.

"What," says Reggie.

"I don't —"

"What was — Pascual, what was *that*?"

"*You* did it!"

"I did *not* — I —" Reggie stops. His face goes through about four things in a row. "I did, didn't I."

"*Yes*, you did! You'd your hands on my face!"

"You *kissed me back*!"

"I —" Pascual stops too. He feels his own face do everything at once, and can't do a thing to stop it, and never could. "*Yes*," he says, in a kind of horror. "Yes, I did. *Madre de Dios*. I did."

There are voices at the top of the path. Jean's, and the Colonel's, very loud and delighted, *where is it, where's the little beggar*, and Baptiste saying *mind the step, Colonel*.

Reggie and Pascual look at each other for one more second.

Then they're at opposite ends of the pen. Pascual doesn't know how. Reggie's at the south wall and Pascual's at the north, both of them in the water to their waists, and both of them looking at the calf, hard, with enormous concentration. The Colonel comes down the path in his pyjamas with Jean on one arm and Baptiste on the other, and stops at the wall, and bursts into tears.

Baptiste looks at the calf. Then she looks at Reggie at the south wall and Pascual at the north. She looks at the fifteen feet of water between them, and at their faces.

She opens the green book on the wall. Pascual watches her write something in the *Remarks* column, very small, and then look at it, and then draw a single neat line through it.

---

Reggie gets through breakfast. He'll never know how.

He sits at the long table in dry clothes with his hair still wet, between the Colonel and Jean. The Colonel is on his third cup of tea and his fourth description of the moment the calf came up, which he saw none of. He eats what's in front of him and doesn't taste it, and doesn't look down the table.

Pascual's at the far end. Pascual's never sat at the far end in his life. Pascual sits at Reggie's elbow and steals his toast. He's at the far end now, with the students, eating porridge with his head down as though somebody had told him there was a prize for it.

Reggie looks at his toast.

*What was that.*

He knows what it was. He's not a fool. He's fifty-four and he's kissed a great many people, a few of them men, one of them for eleven years. He knows what a kiss is. What he doesn't know is where it *came* from. He's standing in a pen in the sunrise, glad, and then his hands are on the man's face and his mouth is on the man's mouth. He'd no more idea it was going to happen than the calf had.

He does what he does. He can't help it. He turns round, at the long table, over his toast, and points himself at himself, and reads.

It takes about four minutes.

It's there. That's the first thing. It's been there, and he walked past it every day like the Colonel's plaque. He goes back through it, now he's started, because he can't stop.

The step in August 1971 with the proof. Pascual's face wet and furious, *you manipulative bastard*, and Reggie's arm round him. He'd thought nothing of it. He'd thought: *my friend*.

The floor of Jean's office in May, when the photograph came. Pascual on his knees with his forehead against Reggie's arm. He'd thought: *my friend*.

The Saturdays. The oil can. *Well?* And Reggie telling him the week, every week, for two years, when the lamp didn't need filling and there was no reason on earth for Pascual to come. He'd thought: *that's what he does on Saturdays*.

He goes back further. He can't stop. It's like pulling a thread. The spring of 1971, when he couldn't eat, and a plate put down in front of him and a man standing there. 1957, and a pen taken out of his hand at midnight, *no*. 1954, and a bucket, a glancing one, thrown at his head across the north pen with a torrent of Spanish, and Reggie thinking *thank God, somebody who'll tell me*. November 1952, and a man coming off the Kingston boat who looked at the animal in the north pen for ten minutes and said *it's the teeth*.

Twenty years. The whole of it there, the whole time, and he'd never once looked. There'd been somebody else in the way. There'd been somebody else in the way since 1946.

And now there isn't.

He's grieved. He knows he has. He's put the letter in the drawer and the lamp out, and he says the name in the sun, and he sleeps in the dark. He's got better. And in all the getting better it never once occurred to him that there'd be room behind it. That when the man in Norway stopped standing in the doorway there'd be anything on the other side but light.

He puts his toast down.

And then he goes cold. It isn't the kiss. It's the thing under it. It's the thing he swore on a step in 1957, and has kept ever since, harder than he's kept anything.

*Not without asking him.*

He didn't ask. He stood in a pen and put his hands on a man's face and his mouth on his, in full view of the whole row, on a licensed station, with the file in Kingston and his sentence in it in Halloran's hand. That's the man by the window. And he didn't ask. He didn't *think*. He just took it, because he was glad. The way he took the lift in 1949, and it was Frank who paid.

"Reggie," says Jean beside him. "You've gone white. Are you all right?"

"Yes," says Reggie. "No. I have to — excuse me."

He gets up. He goes down the long table past the students and stops behind Pascual's chair. Pascual goes rigid, with his spoon halfway to his mouth.

"The step," says Reggie. "Tonight. Please."

Pascual doesn't turn round.

"Yes," he says to his porridge. "All right."

Reggie goes out.

Behind him, as the door swings shut, he hears Baptiste say, perfectly calmly, to nobody in particular, "Would anybody like more tea?"

---

He's on the step at nine. Pascual comes at five past, from his own hut ten yards away. He sits down at the far end of the step, which isn't very far, since it's a step.

Neither of them says anything for a while.

"I didn't ask you," says Reggie.

Pascual turns his head.

"This morning. In the pen. I didn't ask you. I just — I took hold of you, and did it, in front of the whole row, with the file, with the minute, with you by the window. I didn't think for one second."

"Reggie —"

"I didn't *ask*. And that's the one thing, Pascual. It's the one thing I swore. After Frank." His voice goes, and he lets it. "I'm so sorry. Whatever else, I'm sorry for that."

Pascual looks at him for a long time.

"You asked me in 'seventy-one," he says.

"I —"

"On this step. With the proof. In August." Pascual's voice is quite steady. "You told me the whole of it. The window, the minute, all of it, what it'd been for. You'd not left anything out."

"That was about —"

"You said *first, it was worth it*, so I'd have that before the rest. You told me exactly what it weighed, Reggie. You told me better than anybody's ever told me anything."

"I wasn't *asking* you, then. I was telling you about *him*."

"I know you were. And I heard it anyway." Pascual looks down at his hands. "I've known what I'd be carrying for a year and a half."

"You knew. This morning."

"I knew it with my hands full of your shirt." His ears have gone red. "You asked me. You just didn't know it was a question. Neither did I. But I knew what I was saying yes to."

Reggie sits very still.

"You said yes."

"I kissed you back, didn't I? In front of the Colonel. Nearly. It's not as if I'd no say."

"The Colonel was halfway down the path."

"Baptiste wasn't." Pascual puts his face in his hands. "Baptiste *saw*, Reggie. Baptiste wrote something in the book and crossed it out."

"I know. Jean told me. She's crossed it out very neatly."

"*Ay*, the book."

And Reggie laughs. He can't help it. It comes up out of him, and after a moment Pascual's laughing too, into his hands, helplessly. It's the laughing you do at the end of a long night when there's nothing left in you to stop it.

When it's done, Pascual takes his hands down from his face.

"Did you know?" he says. "Before? Had you any idea?"

"No. Not for one second. I worked it out at breakfast. Over my toast."

"Your *toast*." Pascual looks at him. "You worked it out over toast."

"About four minutes."

"*Four minutes*." Pascual shakes his head slowly. "I didn't even have that. I didn't have *anything*."

"Well, you were busy."

"I was in that pen with my *arm* in her all night, and you on the wall with the lamps not saying anything. And then there was a calf, and then there was — and I thought, *oh*. That's all. *Oh*. And then *what*."

"I know. I thought *what* too."

"I'd got over you," says Pascual, "in 1956. I was very thorough. I'd checked, twice."

Reggie stares at him.

"You were — in 1956 —"

"From 1953. On and off. Mostly off. Mostly I wanted to throw things at you."

"You *did* throw things at me."

"It doesn't matter." Pascual waves a hand. "It stopped. It *stopped*, Reggie, I made sure. And then I was your friend for sixteen years and it was the best thing I had. I didn't check for this. It never occurred to me to check. Why would it? You had —" He stops.

"Alec," says Reggie.

"Yes."

They sit on the step with the name between them. And it's all right. That's the strange thing. It's just the name, in the dark, said plainly.

"I've still got him," says Reggie after a while. "I want to say that. I'm not going to pretend. He is in the drawer, and in the *Proceedings*, and in every word I say to that girl about fuse boxes. He is not going anywhere, and I do not want him to."

"I know he isn't. I'm not asking him to."

"But he's not in the *way*," says Reggie slowly. He's working it out as he says it. "He was always in the way. Not his fault. He was in the doorway, and I couldn't see past him. And he is not in the doorway any more. He's in the room. He is in the room with me, and I can see past him. And there you are." His voice goes. "There you are. Right there. You've been there the whole *time*."

Pascual doesn't say anything.

Reggie looks at him. He's doing it on purpose now, pointed, the whole of him. Pascual's sixty in November. Grey at the temples and grey at the chest where his collar's open. There's the long scar on the back of his left hand from a gaff in 1965. His face shows every single thing he's ever felt and is showing all of it now, in the dark on the step, red to the ears, furious and frightened and very nearly laughing. And Reggie reads, because he can't stop, how much room there is. He finds it's the whole of the rest of him. It's been waiting there for twenty years with the door open, and he walked past it every day.

"Is it *instead*?" says Pascual, very low. "Tell me it's not instead. Because I'd rather be shot, *hombre*. I'd rather go back to Zaragoza."

"It's not instead."

"You're sure. You're not just — you're sure."

"I read it," says Reggie. "Over my toast. It's not instead. It was there before. It was there in 1952, with the teeth. I just couldn't see it for the doorway." He puts his hand out on the step between them, palm up. "It's not instead, Pascual. It's *as well*."

Pascual looks at the hand for a long moment.

Then he takes it.

---

They don't get further than Reggie's hut, which is the nearest, and which Pascual has been in every Saturday for twenty years and knows in the dark.

They don't light the lamp. Neither of them even looks at it.

"This is ridiculous," says Reggie, in the middle of the floor, with Pascual's hands on his shoulders. "We're far too old for this."

"You're fifty-four."

"You're *sixty*."

"In *November*." Pascual's got his forehead against Reggie's. His breath is warm and uneven. "And I delivered a calf this morning. I'm not too old for anything."

"You had your arm in her up to the *shoulder*, Pascual, you smell of —"

"I've *washed*!"

"Twice?"

"Three times. And *you* kissed me before I'd washed at all, so you've no —"

Reggie kisses him. This time he knows he's doing it. This time he's pointed at the man, the whole of him, and he can feel himself reading as he does it. The rough warm jaw, the startled breath, the way Pascual's hands close on his shoulders and don't let go. It isn't anything like the pen. The pen was a thing falling out of the sky. This is slow, and a little clumsy, and completely deliberate, and Pascual makes a sound against his mouth that Reggie will think about for a long time.

"*Oh*," says Pascual. "Oh, that's — *Reggie*."

"I know."

"You *don't* know. You don't know anything. You worked it out over *toast*."

"Then tell me."

Pascual pulls back an inch and looks at him. It's dark in the hut, but the shutters are open, and there's a moon over the channel. Reggie can see his face, just, and it's doing everything.

"Twenty years," says Pascual. "I sat on that step twenty years. Every Saturday. *Well?* I was *there*, Reggie. I was there when you couldn't eat. I was there on the floor when the photograph came."

"I know you were."

"And I thought it was — I thought I was just your *friend*. I thought that was *all*. I'd have gone on thinking it till I died. And then you put your hands on my face in the pen and I thought *oh*, and it was like finding out the sea's been there the whole time behind the huts. You just never turned round."

"I've turned round."

"*Yes*," says Pascual, and his voice breaks. "Yes. I noticed."

They don't hurry. Neither of them could have, if they'd wanted to. Reggie's hands are shaking on the buttons of Pascual's shirt, and Pascual's are shaking worse on his. At one point they both laugh at the same time, helplessly, foreheads together, because it's taking so long.

"I've undone your shirt a thousand times," Pascual says. "When you'd been in the water and couldn't feel your hands. I've *never* had this much trouble."

"You weren't trying to do anything *with* it, then."

"No," says Pascual. He stops with his hands flat on Reggie's chest, over his heart, with the shirt open. "No. I wasn't."

He stays like that. Reggie can feel his own heart going under the man's palms, fast and hard.

"That's a good heart," says Pascual, quite quietly. "Strong. Too fast."

"You're the vet."

"I'm the vet," Pascual agrees, and bends his head, and puts his mouth where his hands were.

Reggie makes a sound he hasn't made in sixteen years.

After that it's easier, and harder, and both at once. He learns Pascual's back, broad and warm, with the old ridge of scar low on the left where the Colonel's mare kicked him in 1958. He's known about that scar for fifteen years and never once touched it. He touches it now, following it with his fingertips, and Pascual shivers all over and says *that was your fault, you held the lamp wrong*. Reggie says *everything's my fault*, and Pascual says *yes*, into his neck.

The bed's much too narrow. It was built for one man in 1950 by a carpenter who didn't hold with comfort, and Reggie's never before minded. He minds now. He cracks his elbow on the wall, and swears. Pascual laughs so hard he has to stop and lie there with his face in Reggie's shoulder, shaking. Then his knee does something it shouldn't, and he says a word in Aragonese that Reggie's never heard, and Reggie asks what it means, and Pascual tells him, and Reggie has to stop.

"We'll need a bigger bed," says Reggie, when he can.

"We'll need a bigger *hut*."

"There's the Colonel's spare room."

"*Reggie*."

"I'm joking. I'm joking. I'm —" And then Pascual's hand is on his hip, broad and certain, holding on. His mouth is at the hollow of Reggie's throat, where the pulse is, and Reggie isn't joking any more, and can't think of a single thing to say.

It's slow. It's slow because they're neither of them young, and because the bed's too narrow, and because every few minutes one of them stops, stunned, and just looks. Reggie keeps stopping to look. He can't help it. He's pointed at the man at last, and there's so much of him, and he's been there for twenty years. It's the whole of him, all at once, in the moonlight, with nothing in the way.

"Stop reading me," says Pascual, not meaning it.

"I'm not reading you."

"You *are*. I can feel you. You've gone all quiet and your eyes've gone funny."

"I've got twenty years to catch up on."

"Then do it *slowly*," says Pascual, and pulls him down.

And he says Reggie's name. Not *hombre*, not *Reggie* bellowed across the north pen in two languages. He says it low, into his mouth, as though it were a word in a language he'd only just found out he could speak. And Reggie finds that he's saying Pascual's, over and over, and can't stop that either. He doesn't want to.

---

Afterwards they lie in the middle of the much too narrow bed, in the dark, with the shutters open and the moon on the channel. Pascual's arm is under Reggie's neck, going slowly numb, and neither of them says so.

The lamp's on the table, cold. Reggie looks at it, the way he looks at it most nights, from habit. There's nothing in it to read. It's just a lamp.

"Jean," he says.

"Mm?"

"I've got to ask Jean. In the morning. First thing. It's her name on the licence. It's her the file comes back to."

"I know." Pascual's half asleep. "I'll come."

"You don't have to —"

"I'll *come*, Reggie. It's my neck too. I'm the man by the window." He yawns into Reggie's hair. "She'll say yes."

"You don't know that."

"She'll say yes," says Pascual, "and then she'll say *go away, I've got transects*. She's been waiting for this since about 1960, I should think. Everybody on this island's been waiting for this except us."

"*Everybody*?"

"Baptiste crossed it out, Reggie," says Pascual, with the patience of a man explaining something to a calf. "Baptiste doesn't cross things out."

Reggie lies in the dark and thinks about that.

"What did she write?" he says. "Before she crossed it out. Did you see?"

"No. Did you?"

"No." He considers. "I could look."

"You will *not* look."

"I'm the Consultant. I've got every right to look at a station log —"

"If you look at that book," says Pascual, very slowly and clearly into his hair, "I'll go back to Zaragoza. I will. I'll be on the Tuesday boat. And you can deliver the next one yourself with a lamp in each hand."

Reggie laughs, very quietly, into Pascual's shoulder. It shakes him all over, and Pascual's arm tightens, numb or not.

"All right," he says. "I won't look."

"Good."

"I'll ask her."

"*Reggie*."

But Pascual's laughing too, and then he isn't. He's asleep, all at once, with his mouth a little open and his arm still under Reggie's neck. Reggie lies awake a while longer in the dark, with nothing lit and nothing to wait for. There's a man breathing beside him, and out in the north pen a calf that wasn't there yesterday, blowing high and thin in the moonlight, every forty seconds.

He counts it. He can't help that either. At about the thirtieth, he stops.

---

Jean says yes.

She says it at eight the next morning, in the office, with the door shut, after Reggie has told her the whole of it in order, plainly, with Pascual at the window. It takes him ten minutes. It takes her about four seconds.

"Yes," says Jean. "Obviously. Be careful. Not frightened, careful. Don't upset the Colonel, he cried twice yesterday and he's eighty-three." She picks up her pen. "Go away. I've got transects."

They go.

Baptiste is on the verandah as they come out, with the green book open on the rail, doing the eight o'clock line for the calf. She looks up. She looks at the two of them coming out of the office, three feet apart, carefully, with their faces doing what they're doing.

"Resp. twelve," she says. "Both of them. The little one's feeding."

"Thank you, Baptiste," says Reggie.

"Good morning, Dr Tar. Good morning, Dr Pobil." She looks back down at the book and writes the line. "It's a very good morning," she says, to the book, and doesn't look up again.

Reggie looks at the book on the rail. He looks at yesterday's page, open under her hand. There's the *Remarks* column, and the six-twelve, and the line below it. It's ruled through once, very neatly, in pencil, so that you couldn't possibly read what was under it.

He can read what's under it. He reads everything, and it's upside down, and he's been reading upside down since 1946.

*06.14. Oh!*

He doesn't say anything. He looks at the sea instead, for quite a long time, with his hand over his mouth. Then he goes down the steps to the north pen, with Pascual beside him, not three feet apart.
