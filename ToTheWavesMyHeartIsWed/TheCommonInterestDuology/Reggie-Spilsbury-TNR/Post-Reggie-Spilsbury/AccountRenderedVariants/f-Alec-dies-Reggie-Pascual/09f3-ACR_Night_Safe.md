# Night Safe

The calf's calf comes at twelve minutes past six on the Friday morning. Nobody had Friday, so it goes to the book.

Pascual's shoulder seizes at four that afternoon. He's reaching for the reference book on the shelf over the weighing bench, and his right arm gets as far as his ear and stops there, as though somebody had put a bolt through it. He says a word in Aragonese and takes the book down with his left. It's nine hours in the water with his arm in her and her whole weight closing on it every few minutes. He knows exactly what it is, and exactly how long it'll take if he's sensible, which is a week. He tells nobody.

He's going to have it out with Reggie about the pen. About the wall, and going over it, and a tablet under the tongue at dawn in front of Jean and Baptiste and the calf and God. He's had it ready since six this morning. He's going to do it on Saturday, at the end of the row, where it's Saturday and he can.

---

Reggie opens the door at eight and knows at once.

"Your shoulder."

"Good evening."

"You've got the can in your left hand and your right elbow clamped to your side as if you'd stolen something and put it under there. Come in."

Pascual comes in and puts the can down by the door. "I've come to shout at you."

"You can shout at me with your shirt off. Where's the liniment?"

There's a moment in which Pascual visibly considers refusing and visibly loses. "Behind the formalin. It's *horse* liniment."

"You've put it on me."

Reggie fetches it. When he gets back Pascual's in the middle of the floor with his shirt half off and stuck on the right arm, and a face like a squall coming up the channel.

"Don't you laugh."

"I'm not."

"Your mouth's doing the thing."

"My mouth's at rest," says Reggie, and gets the shirt off him, the left sleeve and then the right, easing it down over the elbow while Pascual addresses the ceiling in two languages. It's been done to Reggie more times than he could count, on the jetty, with his hands gone white and stupid from the water. It's the first time it's gone the other way. He finds he doesn't mind that at all.

Pascual goes to sit on the bed.

"No," says Reggie. "Lie down."

"If I lie down I'll not get up."

"If you sit you'll hunch, and I'll be fighting the whole of your back to get at one shoulder. On your front. It's a clinical recommendation."

"It's *my* clinical recommendation. You've had it off me."

"I've had everything useful I know off you. Lie *down*, Pascual."

Pascual looks at the bed. He's sat on the end of it every Saturday since 1952, and he's never lain on it; it's a foot too short for him. He lies on it now, face down, carefully, with his feet over the end and his right arm hanging off the edge and his face turned to the wall, and says into Reggie's pillow, "If this goes wrong I'm sleeping here."

"If this goes wrong you'll deserve to."

Reggie sits on the edge by his hip. There isn't much edge. He pours the liniment into his palm, cold and reeking of camphor and wintergreen, and puts both hands on the shoulder. The muscle across the top of it's as hard as a plank. He finds the edge of it with his thumbs and leans.

"Right," says Pascual, muffled. "The pen."

"Go on."

"You got *in*."

"I did."

"Over the *wall*. With your — *ay* — with the bottle in your pocket, and me with my arm in a calf and not able to so much as —" He breaks off. "What've you got in your hands?"

"Liniment."

"You've got a *marlinspike*. Go on. No — go on, I'm shouting at you. Marguerite said —"

"Marguerite said not on the reef and not in the boat."

"She meant *water*, Reggie!" It comes out quite loud, even into a pillow. At the same moment Reggie's thumbs find the real knot, under the shoulder blade, and the rest of it goes into a noise like a man falling down a companionway.

There's a pause.

"That's the one," says Reggie.

"*That's the one*," Pascual agrees, faintly. "Don't stop."

"You were shouting."

"I'll shout presently. I've not forgotten." A long breath goes out of him. "I've not *forgotten*. You were grey."

"I know."

"You were grey, and I was sat in the water, and I couldn't get *up*."

That isn't shouting. Reggie keeps his thumbs where they are and doesn't answer it straight away, because there's nothing he could say that's better than letting it be said.

"I took the tablet," he says at last.

"I saw you take it." Into the pillow. "Without being asked."

"Without being asked."

"In front of everybody."

"In front of Jean and Baptiste and a calf four minutes old."

"*Hm*," says Pascual. It's the noise he makes over a wound that's healed better than he'd expected. Then, grudgingly, "Well?"

Reggie laughs. "You were *there*."

"It's Saturday. Tell me the week."

So he tells him the week. He tells him Monday, and the letter from Woods Hole wanting the stranding tables in a form nobody's used since 1961. He tells him Tuesday, and the good thermometer going into the south pen off the end of a student's pencil, and Baptiste going in after it in her clothes without a word to anybody and coming up with it between her teeth. Pascual laughs at that, and it hurts him, and he says *don't*. He tells him Wednesday, and the Colonel explaining the moon to Baptiste for three-quarters of an hour, and Baptiste writing *C. unmoved* in the Remarks column in pencil, where the Colonel couldn't see.

By Thursday he's got to the back of Pascual's neck.

The knot runs up out of the shoulder and ties itself off under the hairline at the base of the skull, and it's the worst of them, and it doesn't want to go. He works at it with the pad of his thumb, small hard circles, telling Thursday: the lamps, the wall, Jean's tea in a can. Pascual stops saying *ay* after a while, and just breathes. Reggie keeps on. He keeps on the way you'd work a knot out of wet line, patiently, without any particular hope, because it'll go when it goes. Somewhere round four in the morning of the story he feels it give, all at once, like something unlocking. Pascual lets out a breath that goes on for a very long time.

"*Ahí*," says Pascual. It's barely a word.

"I felt it go."

"Mm."

That's the knots out, then. There's nothing left under his hands that wants doing.

His hand goes on anyway.

He doesn't notice deciding it. It just goes on, flat now, no thumbs, from the nape of Pascual's neck out along the top of the shoulder and down the arm to the elbow, and back. It's the way you go on stroking an animal after the examination's over, because it's warm and it's leaning into you. The skin's hot with the liniment. The hair at the nape is short and grey and soft, and he goes up into it against the grain and down again, and out along the shoulder. It feels good under his hand. As far as he's thinking anything, that's what he thinks.

"You're telling it wrong," says Pascual after a while. His voice has gone slow.

"Which bit?"

"All of it. You didn't —" There's a long gap. "You didn't say what it was like. In the water."

"I don't know what it was like in the water. I was on the wall."

"I'll tell you," says Pascual. "In a minute."

Reggie waits for the minute. His hand goes up into the short hair, and down. Each time it comes back to the nape, Pascual's head tips into it a little, and stays there till the hand moves on.

"Pascual?"

Nothing. Then, from the pillow, very softly, a snore.

Reggie sits there with his hand on the back of the man's neck.

He should stop. He should cork the liniment and take the chair, and in half an hour wake him and send him ten yards up the row to his own bed. He doesn't do any of it. Pascual's had perhaps four hours' sleep since Thursday. He was nine hours in the water, and then he came out and spent the afternoon guessing at the weight of a calf nobody's fool enough to lift, and then he came down here with his arm clamped to his side to shout at Reggie because he'd been frightened. He's asleep now with his mouth open on Reggie's pillow. His face, turned to the wall, has gone quiet in a way that Pascual's face mostly doesn't.

He'll wake if the hand stops. Reggie knows that as well as he knows anything. Pascual wakes to a change the way animals do; Reggie's seen him come upright out of a dead sleep at a cough in the north pen fifty yards off.

So he'll keep the hand where it is till Pascual's properly under. That's all. Then he'll take the chair.

The chair's a station chair, and hard, and he's fifty-four, and he's had perhaps six hours himself since Thursday. The edge of the bed's right there.

He eases his glasses off with his free hand and puts them on the table by the lamp. Then he lets himself down onto his side on the outermost six inches of his own bed, on top of the blanket, in his shirt and trousers and shoes, with his hand still on the back of Pascual's neck. Just until.

The shutters are open. There's a moon over the channel, a day short of full. Out in the north pen there's a small high blow, and after a while a big slow one. He counts them; he counts things. He gets to four.

---

Pascual wakes because somebody's breathing on the back of his neck.

He doesn't move. He isn't sure yet where he is, and he's learned in thirty-odd years of waking in barns and on decks, and once in a horsebox outside Calatayud, not to move until he's sure.

It's light. It's early, the grey before the gold. There's camphor, and under it the smell of Reggie's hut, which is paper and lamp oil and the green soap Reggie gets from Kingston. He's on his left side, facing the wall; he must have turned in the night. His right shoulder, when he tries it an eighth of an inch, is loose. It's nearly right. It's sore the way a thing's sore when it's been put back where it goes.

And Reggie's along the whole of his back.

Not near him. *Along* him. There's a knee jammed into the crook of Pascual's knee, and a shoe, a *shoe*, hooked over his ankle. There's an arm over his waist, and the hand at the end of it is spread flat on Pascual's chest, about over his heart, as if it had gone looking for it in the night and found it and settled. And Reggie's face is in the back of his neck, exactly where the knot was, breathing there, slow and warm and even, fast asleep.

*Ay*, thinks Pascual. *No*.

He got over this man in 1956. He did it properly. It took him from the spring of 'fifty-three, on and off, and a bucket, and afterwards he checked, twice, and it was gone. He was glad it was gone. He's been Reggie's friend for seventeen years since, and it's the best thing he's got.

He lies in the grey light with Reggie's breath going on his neck and finds out that he did it about as well as you'd set a bone on a boat without a picture. It knitted. It took weight. It was crooked the whole time and he hadn't known, because he'd not put the whole weight on it. And here's the whole weight, all at once, with its shoes on.

His heart's going. It's going under Reggie's palm. There's nothing he can do about that, and Reggie's the one man on the cay who'd count it in his sleep.

Right. He's a grown man. He'll get up. He'll slide out from under the arm and off the end of the bed and find his shirt, and when Reggie wakes he'll be at the table with the tea. None of this'll have happened. Reggie, who didn't know he was doing it, won't ever need to.

He lifts the arm off his waist by the wrist, very gently.

Reggie makes a small cross noise into his neck and takes the arm back. It comes round him again, tighter than before. The hand goes back to where it was on his chest and presses, and Reggie's face pushes into his nape and says something against it that isn't a word in any language, and settles.

Pascual stops trying.

He lies there for what's probably twenty minutes. They're the best twenty minutes he's had in years. They're also very nearly the worst.

Then Reggie wakes.

Pascual feels it happen. The breathing on his neck catches, and stops, and doesn't start again. The body along his back goes stiff, joint by joint, like a man finding out in the dark that what he's leaning on is the edge of a cliff. The hand on his chest spreads its fingers once, and then lifts off, as though the chest were hot.

Pascual shuts his eyes and breathes like a man asleep. He's good at it. He's done it on night watches for thirty years so that the students would think he wasn't listening.

It takes Reggie some time to get off him. He does it the way you'd take a dressing off a burn: the hand, then the arm, then the knee. Then there's a short, silent struggle with the shoe that's hooked over Pascual's ankle. Then the whole length of him's gone, and there's cold air all down Pascual's back, and the bed creaks as Reggie sits up on the edge of it.

Pascual counts ten. Then he makes the noise of a man waking, and rolls over.

Reggie's sitting on the edge of the bed with his back to him, in yesterday's shirt, with his hair up on one side. He doesn't look round.

"Ah," says Reggie.

"Mm," says Pascual.

"You fell asleep."

"I was *treated*." It comes out rough. He clears his throat. "It's your fault. You and your marlinspike."

"I was going to take the chair."

Pascual looks at the chair. After a moment Reggie turns his head and looks at it too.

"You didn't take the chair," says Pascual.

"No." Reggie looks down at his feet. "Evidently not."

There's a pause. Pascual watches Reggie's shoulders come up an inch, and he hears it coming the way you'd hear a squall before it's on the water: the chest lifting, the face settling, the warmth coming up into the voice. "My dear chap, I do apologise, I'd no idea that I was going to —"

"Don't do the voice," says Pascual. "It's six in the morning."

Reggie stops.

"Sorry," he says, in his own voice, after a moment. Then, because he's Reggie and can't not give you the facts: "I sleep like that. I'm told. Like a limpet."

Pascual knows who told him. So does Reggie. Neither of them says the name. It comes into the room and sits there, quite quietly, and it's all right.

"You've got your shoes on," says Pascual.

"So have you."

"I've no *shirt*."

"No," says Reggie. And then they both look, at the same moment, at the shutters.

They're wide open. The sky's going gold over the channel. Anybody going down the row to the pen at first light could have looked straight in. Nobody did; Pascual's fairly sure nobody did. He'd have heard boots.

"Tea," says Reggie.

"Tea," says Pascual.

And that's all either of them says about it.

He goes back up the row to his own hut at twenty past six, with his shirt over his good arm and the oil can in his hand. He walks it like a man carrying a bucket filled right up to the brim.

Halfway, he lifts his right arm out to the side to see. It goes up past his ear. It goes nearly all the way.

He stands in the row and looks at it up there in the sunrise, and swears at it quietly in Aragonese for being well.

---

Sunday's the full moon, and the Colonel's in a very good humour about it. He tells the whole breakfast table that any sensible animal would've waited the two days, and that he's been robbed of half a crown by a calf with no sense of occasion.

Reggie passes Pascual his toast.

He does it without looking, the moment Pascual sits down, before Pascual's even reached for it. Pascual's been stealing Reggie's toast at this table since 1953. He's not once been handed it. He sits there holding it and doesn't know what to do with it. Across the table Baptiste looks at the toast, and at Reggie, and at Pascual, and writes the eight o'clock line.

At eight that evening there's a knock at Pascual's door.

It's Reggie, with the liniment.

"It'll want doing again," says Reggie. "You'd tell anyone else a week."

Pascual has lifted his right arm over his head twice today to reach the top shelf in the store, and once to wave the Colonel off the jetty. Reggie wasn't there for any of them. Probably.

"A week," Pascual agrees, and stands back from the door.

His own bed's longer than Reggie's. He saw to that in 1952 with a saw and two planks off the old jetty. He lies down on it on his front. Reggie sits on the edge and does the shoulder, where there are no knots left to speak of, and they both know it, and Reggie works at them anyway. He tells Sunday. Pascual was present for Sunday from beginning to end.

At half past nine Reggie says, "I'll go," and doesn't take his hand off Pascual's neck.

"Go, then."

"I will."

"Tell me the bit with the Colonel and the half-crown again first."

"You were *there*."

"I was eating. Tell it."

Reggie tells it. At some point in the telling he stops sitting on the edge and is lying on it, on his side. Pascual doesn't see how it happens. He doesn't look.

---

He'd controlled for it. That's what gets Reggie, afterwards.

He'd lain down on Sunday night with a hand's breadth of blanket between them, back to back, on top of the covers, with his shoes off this time and his arms folded on his chest like a knight on a tomb, because he's a scientist and the thing plainly wanted controlling for. He'd told the half-crown story to the wall like that and heard Pascual's breathing go slow behind him and thought: there. Nothing to it.

He wakes at dawn in Pascual's hut, which smells of formalin and wet rope and Pascual. His face is against the broad warm middle of Pascual's back, and his arm's round him, and his knee's where it was yesterday. And Pascual's hand has come down in the night and got hold of Reggie's wrist, where it lies across his stomach. It's holding it, with two fingers flat on the inside, over the pulse.

Pascual's asleep. Reggie can hear that he's asleep. And every so often the two fingers on his wrist press, and let go, and press, lightly and steadily, like a man tapping his foot to a band he can only just hear.

He's counting. He's counting in his *sleep*.

Reggie lies very still and doesn't laugh, and it's a near thing.

Then he stops wanting to laugh. He lies looking at the back of Pascual's neck, six inches off, grey in the half-light, and thinks about the one thing. The thing he swore on a step in 1957, and has kept harder than he's kept anything since.

*Not without asking him*.

He hasn't asked. Two nights now, and he hasn't asked for any of it. The first he was asleep for, and the second he's less excuse for, and either way that's his arm and his knee and his face in the man's neck, and he didn't ask for them.

But Pascual's got hold of his wrist.

He thinks about that for some time. He doesn't know what the rule is, for sleep. He isn't sure there is one. There's a hand holding his wrist and counting him, and he can't tell if that's an answer, or if Pascual would count the Colonel's pulse just the same if the Colonel happened to be lying there.

He decides it's too early to know. He decides that Pascual needs the sleep, and that it'd be unkind to wake him. All of that's true. He notices there's another thing underneath it, and he doesn't turn round to look at it. He files it. He shuts his eyes.

---

At dinner on Monday, Pascual reaches across the long table for the salt with his left hand, and winces.

"Shoulder," says Jean.

"It's nothing."

"Lift it," says Reggie.

Pascual lifts his right arm out to the side. It goes up to the level of his ear and stops there, and he makes a small brave face about it.

Reggie watched that arm go straight up over Pascual's head at eleven this morning, on the jetty, with a full bucket on the end of it, while Pascual shouted at a student about the tide.

"That'll want doing," he says.

"If you've time," says Pascual, to the salt.

"I've time."

Jean looks from one of them to the other, and eats her fish.

That night it's Reggie's hut again. When Reggie comes back from washing, the shutters are shut. Pascual's lying on his front on the bed with his face to the wall, and he doesn't say anything about the shutters, and neither does Reggie.

---

Tuesday's Reggie's shilling day, and there's nothing left to spend it on. Pascual tells him so at breakfast. Reggie says he'll keep it for the next calf. Pascual says the next calf's four years off at the least, and Reggie says he's a patient man, and the Colonel laughs so hard at that he has to be patted on the back.

Tuesday night they don't.

It isn't decided. Nothing gets decided. It's only that at eight Pascual's in his hut and Reggie's in his, and nobody knocks. Pascual lies on his back in his own bed with the shutters open, and knows that ten yards off Reggie's lying on his back being sensible too. He'd give a year's pay to know if it's because Reggie thinks Pascual wants him to be.

He doesn't sleep. He seems to have got out of the way of it.

The squall comes over the channel at a quarter to two. He hears it on the water before it's on the roof, and he's in his oilskin and down the row before the rain's properly arrived. Reggie's at the rail of the north pen already. Of course he is. He's got his oilskin on over his pyjamas and a torch in his hand.

"The dinghy," says Reggie.

The station dinghy's broken its painter at the jetty and come round on the wind. It's banging against the outside of the north pen wall, hollow, *boom*, *boom*, right where the calf and her calf are lying. The mother's up and blowing, short and hard, shoving the little one into the far corner.

"She'll crush it," says Pascual. "She'll crush it against the —" and he's got a leg over the wall.

"*No*," says Reggie. "Your shoulder."

"My shoulder's —" He stops. The torch is in his face, and he can't see Reggie's.

"Your shoulder," says Reggie, "can't lift its own salt."

There's a short pause, in the rain.

"It's better than it was," says Pascual.

"Is it."

"Considerably."

"Then you hold the torch and I'll get in."

"You'll *not* get in, with your —"

"Then neither of us gets in, and we do it from the wall," says Reggie. He shoves the torch at him and goes along the top of the wall on his hands and knees in the rain to where the dinghy's banging. He lies flat along the stones and reaches over the outside for the trailing painter. He gets it on the third try, and the dinghy yanks half of it out of his hands.

"*Pascual*."

Pascual's already there, with the torch in his teeth. He gets both hands on the rope behind Reggie's. Both hands, and the right one's fine; it's perfectly fine. Between them they haul the dinghy's nose round off the wall. They walk it along the outside of the pen to the jetty hand over hand, Reggie on his knees and Pascual stooping over him, the rain coming in sideways. Reggie says *left, your left, no, your other left* the whole way, and Pascual says something round the torch that he'll not translate.

They make it fast to the jetty ring. Reggie puts two half-hitches on it with a round turn. Pascual adds a third half-hitch of his own.

Reggie looks at it in the torchlight. "That's a granny."

"It's a *vet's* knot. It'll hold."

In the pen the banging's stopped. The mother blows, slower. Pascual watches her come out of the corner, and the little one come after her and get itself under her, where it belongs.

Baptiste arrives at the top of the path in her oilskin, with the green book shut in a biscuit tin to keep it dry. She looks at the pen, and the jetty, and the pair of them. "Oh," she says. "It's done." She sounds faintly aggrieved about it. She sits down on the wall under the rain, opens the tin, and writes it in.

They're both wet through. Reggie's pyjama trousers are black to the knee, and he's breathing hard. Pascual has hold of his wrist before he's thought about it, standing by the jetty in the rain, counting.

"Well?" says Reggie.

"Hundred and four."

"Is that bad?"

"It's *wet*," says Pascual, which isn't an answer. He lets go. "Go to bed. Get dry. If it's not under ninety in half an hour — go to *bed*, Reggie."

Reggie goes. Pascual goes up the row to his own hut and gets the oilskin off, and the wet shirt, and he's standing in the middle of the floor with a towel over his head when the door goes.

It's Reggie, in a dry shirt and the wet pyjama trousers, with his pillow under his arm.

"The roof's come in," he says. "Over the bed. The whole corner. There's water on the mattress you could float the dinghy in."

Pascual looks at him.

He's lived ten yards from that roof for twenty years. He knows every tile on it. He's known since the autumn that the corner over the bed was going, and he meant to see to it at Christmas, and didn't. There's the settee in the long room. There's the Colonel's spare room. There's the floor of the office.

"Well, come *in*," says Pascual. "You're letting the rain in."

Reggie comes in, and shuts the shutters. Pascual lets him.

Nobody pretends about the edge tonight. Pascual lies down facing the wall, and Reggie lies down behind him, and his arm comes round Pascual before either of them's anywhere near asleep. His hand's cold. It finds the place on Pascual's chest where it goes.

"You're cold," says Pascual.

"It's raining."

"Mm."

That's all. Pascual lies in the dark with the rain on his own roof, which doesn't leak, and Reggie's breathing on the back of his neck. After a while he puts his own hand over Reggie's, on his chest, and holds on.

---

On Wednesday Reggie wakes the other way round.

His face is against Pascual's chest, in the grey hair there, and Pascual's chin is on the top of his head. Pascual's arm is across his back. And Pascual's other hand is in Reggie's hair, moving in it, slowly.

He's awake. Reggie can tell by the hand. It's going through his hair the way Reggie's own hand went on Saturday, after the knots were out: for no reason, because it's warm, and it's there, and it's leaning into you.

He could move. He doesn't. Pascual could stop. He doesn't either. They lie there in the grey light for a very long time, both perfectly awake, and neither says a word.

"Tea," says Pascual eventually, into his hair.

"Tea," says Reggie.

At breakfast Pascual says, "I'll see to your roof at the weekend," and steals Reggie's toast, and doesn't look at him.

"Thank you," says Reggie.

He goes down to the north pen at ten with the thermometer. Pascual's in the water up to his chest with the new calf, trying to get a tape round her middle. She's five days old and knows exactly what she's about. She keeps swimming off with the end of the tape. Pascual keeps wading after her, laughing, calling her names in Spanish that Reggie's fairly sure aren't endearments and are spoken like endearments anyway. Her mother watches the whole thing from the shallows with an expression Reggie can only describe as long-suffering.

The sun's on the back of Pascual's neck. His hair's wet and short and grey there, and the skin's brown, and Reggie's hand knows exactly what it feels like. It knows the heat of it, and the grain of the hair, and the place at the base of the skull where the knot tied itself off.

He sits on the wall with the thermometer and forgets what it's for.

And then, for no reason at all, he thinks: *the weekend*.

It's Wednesday. When the long-room roof went in the storm of 'sixty-three, Pascual was up on it with a tar bucket before the rain had properly stopped, shouting down for nails. He did the Colonel's gutter at midnight in 1968, with Reggie holding the lamp. He'll do a stranger's roof the same afternoon; he did the harbourmaster's at the Bay, once, because he happened to be passing. He's lived ten yards from Reggie's for twenty years. He's leaving it till the weekend.

Reggie looks at Pascual in the water.

He does what he does. He can't help it; he's done it since he was thirteen. He turns round on the wall and points himself at Pascual, and reads.

It doesn't take long. The roof. The arm at dinner on Monday, stopping at his ear with a brave face on it. Reggie saw that, and he filed it with the other thing, and he didn't look. The fingers on his wrist, keeping time. The hand in his hair this morning, awake. *Tell me the bit with the Colonel again*.

*Oh*, thinks Reggie.

And then, because he's started, and can't stop, he turns round and points himself at himself.

That takes longer. It isn't hidden. There's just a great deal of it, and he has to go past all of it to get to where it starts. He goes past the pillow under his arm last night at Pascual's door. He'd been *pleased*. He'd been standing in the rain with a mattress behind him you could float a boat on, and been pleased. He goes past Sunday night, and the liniment, and *it'll want doing again*. He'd said that at Pascual's door with the bottle in his hand, and known it was nonsense, and come anyway, and told himself it was kindness. It was kindness. It wasn't only that. He goes past Saturday, and the hand going on after the knots were out. *It felt good under his hand. That's all he thought.*

It wasn't all.

He looks at the back of Pascual's neck in the sun, and it isn't the neck of a man he's fond of. He wants to put his mouth there. He wants to put his mouth there, and feel Pascual's head tip back into it the way it tipped back into his hand.

The thought arrives entire. His face goes hot all at once, ears first, the way it hasn't done since he was nineteen. He has to put the thermometer down on the wall.

"Reggie?" Pascual's looking up at him from the water, with the tape in one hand and the calf in the other. "You all right? You've gone red."

"Sun," says Reggie.

"You've your hat on."

"Then it's the thermometer," says Reggie, which means nothing at all. He gets up and goes along the wall with it as if it had somewhere to be.

He gets as far as the jetty. He sits down on the bollard where the dinghy's made fast with its vet's knot, and he looks at the channel, and the cold comes in behind the heat.

He hasn't asked.

Four nights. There was the first, which he was asleep for. There were the ones since, which he wasn't. There were the shutters, shut every night since Monday, by one or the other of them, without a word. They both knew what the shutters meant. They've both known since Monday, and neither of them's said anything, and it's all been done anyway, in the dark, without anybody being asked. That's how it went in the lift in 1949. That's how Frank came to pay for it.

He could ask tonight. He could go to Pascual's door with the pillow under his arm.

And there'd be the roof in the way. Pascual could say yes to a man with a wet mattress, and not to Reggie, and never have to say which it was. Or he couldn't say no without putting a man out in the rain to do it. That isn't a question. That's a thing that looks like a question with a roof on top.

So the roof comes out of it first.

He'd go up this afternoon, but the tiles are in the store, and Hester Bevan's got the key, and she's at the Bay till the evening boat. It wants daylight. It'll have to be tomorrow, first thing.

He sits on the bollard, and makes up his mind, and feels a great deal better.

---

It doesn't rain on Wednesday night. There's a moon three days past full, and not a cloud between the cay and Kingston.

At nine o'clock Reggie's at Pascual's door with his pillow under his arm.

Pascual looks at the pillow. Then he looks past Reggie, at the sky.

"It's not raining," says Pascual.

"It might," says Reggie.

Pascual looks at the sky a moment longer. His face is doing a great deal, and Reggie can read every bit of it now. It goes through him like a current.

"It might," says Pascual, and stands back from the door.

Reggie doesn't sleep.

He lies on his back with his hands folded on his chest, the pillow he brought under his head. He doesn't put his arm round anybody. He knows now, and he hasn't asked, and he isn't going to take it awake. So he lies there beside Pascual, not touching him, in the dark, with the shutters shut, and it's about the hardest thing he's done since the tables.

At some time after three, Pascual turns over in his sleep.

He does it all at once, the way a big animal turns in the shallows. He rolls over and puts his arm across Reggie's chest, and his knee over Reggie's knee, and his face into Reggie's shoulder. And he says, into Reggie's shirt, quite clearly, "*Reggie*," and goes on sleeping.

Reggie lies under the arm with his eyes open until the sky goes grey behind the slats.

Then he gets out from under it, very carefully, and goes to find a ladder.

---

Pascual wakes at six to hammering.

He's alone. The other side of the bed's cold. The hammering's coming from ten yards down the row, and he knows what it is before he's got the shutter open. It's Reggie on his roof, in his shirtsleeves, with a bucket of tar and a stack of tiles from the store, doing the corner over the bed. He's doing it neatly and fast. He's good with his hands; he always has been. He's not looking this way.

Pascual shuts the shutter.

Right, he thinks. Right. So that's that.

He isn't a fool. He's known since Sunday what it was on his own side. He's known since Monday, since the salt, that Reggie saw the shoulder and said nothing, and he'd let himself think that meant something. It meant Reggie being kind. Reggie's kind the way he does everything else, thoroughly and without talking about it. He saw what was on Pascual's face, and Pascual's face shows everything and can't be stopped. And he let it run a few days, so as not to hurt him. And now he's up a ladder at six in the morning mending the reason, so that neither of them'll ever have to say it out loud.

It's decent. It's the decent way to do it. Pascual stands in the middle of his hut and hates it with his whole heart.

He got over him in 'fifty-six. He'll do it again. He knows how. It's a matter of not going to the end of the row, except on Saturdays.

Reggie isn't at breakfast.

His chair's empty at the long table. His toast's on his plate where Jean put it, going cold, and there's nobody to steal it off. Pascual can hear the hammer from where he sits, through the window, steady as a clock. He eats his porridge. He doesn't touch the toast. In the end the Colonel takes it, and says the lad's up his roof, and a very good thing too, those tiles have been a disgrace since the autumn.

"Yes," says Pascual. "They have."

He takes the reference collection off the shelves in the long room and puts it back in a new order that nobody asked for. He's short with a student about a bucket. He apologises to the student at lunch, which frightens the boy a good deal worse.

Reggie comes in to lunch late. His hands are scrubbed pink and there's a smear of tar he's missed along one wrist. He sits down across the table from Pascual, and looks up, and smiles at him.

It's a real smile. It isn't the smirk, which lives on Reggie's face in public and means nothing at all. This one comes up from somewhere and gets all the way into his eyes, and it's pointed straight across the table at Pascual, and it's *happy*.

Pascual knows every face Reggie's got. He knows this one. It's the face of a man who's had a hard thing to do and has done it, and feels a great deal better. He's seen it on Reggie after a stranding that went right, and on the jetty when a paper's gone off on the Kingston boat and there's nothing more to be done about it.

So that's how it is. The roof's done, and Reggie's glad of it. He's smiling across the fish because it's over and they can go back to how it was, and he thinks Pascual'll be glad of that too.

Pascual looks at his plate.

"How's the shoulder?" says Jean.

"Fine."

"Lift it, then," says Jean, who has heard Reggie say it.

Pascual can't be bothered with any of it any more. He puts his right arm straight up over his head, all the way, and holds it there, and says, "*Fine*."

Across the table, the smile goes out of Reggie's face.

It goes all at once, like a lamp with the wick turned down. Pascual sees it go and doesn't know what to make of it, and decides he doesn't want to know. Reggie opens his mouth. Pascual gets up and takes his plate to the hatch.

He doesn't let himself be caught after that. It isn't hard. Reggie will want to say something decent about it, and Pascual'll have to stand there and be decent back, and he can't. Not today. So when Reggie comes into the store at three, Pascual goes out of the other door with a box of specimen jars he doesn't need. When Reggie comes down to the pen at four, Pascual's already in the dinghy with two students and a tide table, pulling out to have a look at the north reef. The north reef doesn't want looking at in March, and he knows it. He stays out till the light goes.

He comes in at half past eight with the salt dried white on his arms, and goes straight to his hut without any supper. It's Thursday, not Saturday. He doesn't go to the end of the row. He sits in his own hut with the lamp unlit and the shutters open, because there's no reason now to shut them.

At nine there's a knock.

It's Reggie. There's nothing in his hands. No bottle, no pillow, no torch. He has them in his pockets, and then he takes them out, as though he'd sooner they were where Pascual could see them.

"I've no liniment," says Reggie.

"I'd noticed."

"Your shoulder's better."

"Since Sunday."

"I know. I watched it go up over your head on Monday with a bucket on the end."

Pascual's ears go. He can feel them go.

"You said nothing."

"No."

"And you've mended the roof."

"At six. You'll have heard me. I'm sorry; I'd have done it quieter, but it's tar and tiles, there's no quiet way to do it."

"I heard." Pascual's got hold of the door. "I know what it was."

"I don't think you do," says Reggie. "I think you've had it since six this morning that I went up a ladder to let you down gently."

Pascual doesn't answer.

"I saw it on you at lunch. I came to the store at three to tell you it wasn't that. You went out of the other door with a box of jars."

"I wanted the jars."

"You've got four hundred jars. And then you took the dinghy out to the north reef."

"The north reef wants looking at."

"Not in March, it doesn't. And then you came in at half past eight and shut yourself up without any supper. I gave you till nine to get the salt off. I'd sooner not be told to go to the devil through a door."

"I'd not have —" Pascual stops. He might have.

"I'm sorry," says Reggie. "I ought to have come at six with the hammer in my hand and said. I wanted to do it properly."

"Do *what* properly?"

"Ask," says Reggie.

Pascual doesn't say anything.

"There's been a reason every night," says Reggie. "The shoulder, and the rain, and the roof. I wanted there not to be one. So that if you said yes it'd be to me, and not to a man with a wet mattress. And if you said no, you'd not have to put me out in the rain to do it. I haven't got a reason. I'd like to come in. May I?"

Pascual stares at him.

"It's not raining," he says, stupidly.

"No," says Reggie. "Not a cloud."

Pascual stands back from the door.

Reggie comes in. He goes to the shutter on the pen side, and gets his hand on it, and stops. He turns round.

"Shall I?"

"We've shut them every night since Monday."

"I know we have. Neither of us said so."

"No."

"I'm saying so now," says Reggie. "It's Jean's licence. I'd like us to ask her. Tomorrow. Together. If there's anything to ask her about."

"Yes," says Pascual. His voice isn't doing what he wants. "*Yes*. Shut it."

Reggie shuts it. He shuts the one by the door. The moonlight goes to slats on the floor between them.

"You let me fake a shoulder at you," says Pascual. "For *three days*."

"You faked it very badly."

"I made a brave *face*!"

"You made a brave face about the *salt*, Pascual."

"And *you* —" His voice goes up, and he doesn't care. "*It might rain!* There wasn't a cloud from here to *Kingston*!"

"There's always a chance."

"There is *not* always a —" He stops. He puts his hand over his face. "*Madre de Dios*. Look at us. Look at the pair of us. A shoulder and a roof. We're a hundred and thirteen years old between us."

"A hundred and thirteen and a half. You're sixty in November."

"Don't you *dare*." He takes his hand down. "Why the roof? Why not just — you could've *said*, Reggie, on Sunday, at six in the morning, with your shoe on my —"

"Because I didn't ask." Reggie says it quite plainly. "I woke up round you every morning like weed round a buoy, and I didn't ask for any of it. The first night I was asleep. By Monday I wasn't, and by last night I knew what it was. So I lay awake all night so as not to do it without asking."

"You lay *awake*?"

"All night. On my back. Like a bishop."

"And?"

"And you turned over at three," says Reggie, "and did it to me. And said my name."

"I did *not*."

"You did. Into my shirt."

Pascual feels his whole face go, all at once. He can't stop it. He gave up trying about 1940.

"*Ay*, *no*."

"So I thought I'd better get the roof out of the way before I asked," says Reggie, "in case you thought I was asking because of it."

Pascual sits down on the bed. He sits down hard, because his knees have decided about it.

"I got over you," he says, "in 'fifty-six."

Reggie goes still.

"From 'fifty-three. I checked, after. Twice. It'd knitted." He looks at his hands. "Sunday morning you'd your shoe on my ankle, and it turned out I'd set it crooked."

"Pascual —"

"So you tell me. Is it the sleeping?" He can hear his own voice, and doesn't like it, and goes on anyway. "You've slept like a stone all week, and I know why. It's warm, and there's somebody there. I'm not a *hot-water bottle*, Reggie. If that's what —"

"A hot-water bottle doesn't take hold of my wrist in its sleep and count," says Reggie.

Pascual looks up at him. "I did not."

"Monday. Two fingers. You kept time."

"I did *not*."

"You're doing it now," says Reggie. "Look at your hand."

Pascual looks. His right hand's lying on his own knee. The first two fingers of it are tapping, very lightly, steadily, on the cloth.

He stops them.

Reggie crosses the hut, which is four steps, and sits down on the bed beside him. Not on the edge. Beside him.

"It isn't the sleeping," says Reggie. "I'll tell you when it was. Yesterday, at the pen. You were in the water with the calf and she kept swimming off with the tape, and you were laughing. And the sun was on the back of your neck. And I wanted to put my mouth there. That's all. That was the whole of the reading. I went red as a boy and I had to go and walk along the wall with a thermometer as if it had an appointment."

Pascual looks at him in the slatted dark.

There's no voice in it. There's nothing in it at all but Reggie, telling him a thing plainly. And his face, which Pascual's had across a table and a rail and a pen for twenty years, is turned to him, and there's nothing in front of it.

"*Well*?" says Pascual. His voice is almost gone.

Reggie laughs, very quietly.

"It's Thursday."

"Do it anyway."

So Reggie gets up on his knees on the bed behind him, and puts one hand on Pascual's shoulder, the right one, the one that was hurt. He puts the other flat on the back of Pascual's neck, in the place it's been every night for a week. He pushes it gently, and Pascual's head goes forward. Then Reggie bends and puts his mouth there, at the nape, just under the hairline, where the knot was.

Pascual makes a sound he's never heard out of himself.

Reggie's mouth stays there. It's warm, and open a little, and he can feel Reggie breathing against the skin, uneven. Then the mouth moves, a half-inch, and presses again. And Pascual's head tips back into it, the way it tipped back into the hand. He can't stop it doing that any more than he can stop his face.

"*Reggie*."

"Mm."

"Is that asking?"

"That's asking."

Pascual turns round. It's clumsy, on the bed, with Reggie on his knees behind him. He gets himself round, and gets both hands on Reggie's face, and kisses him.

It isn't careful. It's a week of lying still in the grey before six, and it isn't careful at all. Reggie makes a noise into his mouth, startled and then not startled, and his hands come up and take hold of Pascual's shirt at the collar and hang on. His mouth's warm and it opens. His glasses go crooked against Pascual's cheekbone and neither of them does anything about it. Pascual can feel Reggie's heart going through two shirts, and he doesn't count it. He doesn't count anything.

When they stop, it's only to breathe. Their foreheads stay together.

"Oh," says Reggie.

"*Oh*," says Pascual. "Twenty-one years and a roof, and it's *oh*."

"It's a very large oh."

"It'd *better* be."

Reggie takes his glasses off. He folds them, and reaches over Pascual and puts them on the table by the lamp, and Pascual watches him do it with his heart going in his throat.

"Shoes," says Reggie.

"What?"

"I'm taking my shoes off. I've learned."

---

They know the bed. That's the strange thing.

They've learned it this week without either of them saying a word: where the plank dips under the middle, which side's the wall, how two men lie on it if they're not to go off the edge. Now it's easy. It's the easiest thing there is. It's only the clothes that are new.

They get the clothes off between them, badly, laughing. Reggie's hands are shaking on Pascual's buttons, and Pascual's are worse on Reggie's. It takes longer than the calf. At one point Reggie's got his shirt stuck over his head, and Pascual says *don't you laugh*, and Reggie says, muffled, *my mouth's at rest*, and Pascual has to stop and put his forehead on Reggie's bare shoulder and shake.

Then there's nothing left to take off, and Reggie lies down on his back in the dip in the middle. Pascual lies down along him on his side, the way Reggie's lain along Pascual every morning since Sunday. And there's the whole of Reggie under his hand for the first time, with nothing between.

He's smaller than he looks in a shirt. He's all long muscle and bone, fine-made, lean from the water. There's grey in the hair on his chest that Pascual's seen a thousand times on the jetty and never once had his palm on. He puts it there now, flat. Under it Reggie's heart's going fast and hard.

"You're staring," says Reggie.

"I've had your shoe on my ankle for a week," says Pascual. "I'm allowed to stare."

"Then I'll stare back."

"Stare, then."

Reggie does. He lies there and looks up at Pascual in the slatted moonlight, and Pascual lets him. He can feel his face doing all of it: every bit. He doesn't try to stop it. There's nothing he wants to keep off this man.

Then Reggie's hand comes up and goes to the back of Pascual's neck.

"*There*," says Pascual. It comes out unsteady. "Every night. Every night that hand's been there."

"I know."

"You *didn't* know. You were asleep."

"I know now," says Reggie. His fingers move in the short hair, up against the grain, and down. "I'm doing it on purpose now."

Pascual bends his head and puts his mouth on Reggie's throat.

He goes slowly. He's been wanting to go slowly since about half past five on Sunday morning. He goes down the side of Reggie's throat, where the pulse is, and he can feel it under his mouth and doesn't count it. He goes along the collarbone and down, over the chest, with his mouth open. Reggie's breath goes short, and his hand tightens at the back of Pascual's neck. His hips come up off the plank an inch, and down.

"Pascual."

"Mm."

"You've gone quiet."

"I'm busy."

"Tell me something."

Pascual lifts his head. "You want me to *talk*?"

"I've been lying next to you for a week not saying anything," says Reggie. "I'd like to hear you."

So Pascual tells him. He tells him with his mouth on Reggie's ribs, where there's not enough on them. He tells him about Sunday, and the shoe on his ankle, and lifting the arm off and having it taken back. He tells him about lying still for twenty minutes with his heart going under Reggie's palm like a pump, thinking *he'll count it, the devil'll count it in his sleep*. Reggie laughs at that, breathlessly, and then stops laughing, because Pascual's hand has gone down over his belly and stopped low, flat, and stayed there.

"May I?" says Pascual.

Reggie goes quite still under his hand.

"You're asking."

"I've caught it off you. Like a cough." He can hear his own voice, low and rough. "May I?"

"*Yes*."

Pascual's hand goes down, between his thighs.

Reggie's already hot there, and slick, and his breath goes out of him all at once, and his knees come apart. Pascual hears himself make a sound. He goes slowly. He goes very slowly, with his fingers, and watches Reggie's face in the slats of moon, and Reggie's face does a thing Pascual's not seen it do in twenty years of watching it. It stops holding anything. The mouth comes open. The eyes go shut. His head tips back into the pillow, and his hips lift into Pascual's hand.

"Like that?"

"Like —" Reggie's voice has gone somewhere. "Yes. Slower. No — don't stop."

"I'm not stopping."

"You were going to ask again."

"I was *not*."

"You were. I could feel you getting ready to. Don't stop to ask me *every* —" And it breaks off in the middle, because Pascual's found the place, and Reggie's hand clenches in the hair at the back of his neck hard enough to hurt.

"*There*?"

"*Pascual*."

"That's a yes."

"That's a — *yes*. God. Yes."

Pascual keeps on. He keeps on the way Reggie kept on at the knot, patiently, steadily, with no hurry in it at all, and Reggie moves under his hand. Reggie's hand at the back of his neck pulls his head down, and he kisses Reggie's open mouth. Reggie kisses him back badly and doesn't care, and says things into his mouth that aren't in any order.

"I lay there last night," says Reggie, against his lips. "With you on top of me asleep. And I thought about this. And I thought I'd have to get up and walk up and down the row."

"You got up and mended a roof."

"It was that or walk up and down." His breath catches. "Pascual. Come here. I want you. All of you. *Here*."

Pascual comes.

He moves over him, and Reggie's knees come up on either side of his hips, and Reggie's hands go to the small of his back and pull. Pascual's hard against the inside of his thigh, and he stops there, a breath from it, with his weight on his arms. Both arms. The right one takes it and doesn't say a word.

"Look," says Pascual. "Both arms."

"Show-off."

"I'm a *vet*. I'm admiring my work."

"*Pascual*."

"All right. All right." He puts his forehead down on Reggie's. "*Así*. Slowly."

He presses into him, slowly, and Reggie's whole body opens and takes him in. Then they both stop, stunned, and just breathe, forehead to forehead, with the moon in slats across them.

"Oh," says Reggie. He sounds astonished.

"All right?"

"Don't you *dare* ask me that."

"I'm asking."

"*Yes*. Yes. Move. Pascual, *move*."

He moves.

It's slow. It has to be slow, and neither of them wants it any other way. It's a long steady roll, and back, and again. Reggie's heels come up and press into the backs of his thighs and hold him there. Reggie's hand goes back to the nape of his neck and stays. Pascual can feel Reggie's heart hammering under his own chest, and he doesn't count it, and he tells Reggie so.

"I'm not counting."

Reggie laughs, breathlessly, into his shoulder. "You are."

"I'm *not*. I can feel it going and I'm not counting it. I don't care what it is. I'll care after." He can hardly get the words out. "*Reggie*."

"Yes."

"*Reggie*."

"I know. I've got you. I know."

It goes on a long time. At one point they both stop, and lie there locked, not moving, because it's too much, and Reggie says *wait*, and Pascual waits. Then Reggie says *now*, in the voice from the pen, the level one, the one with nothing in it but what's true, and Pascual laughs out loud into his neck and moves again.

Reggie comes apart first. It goes through him all at once, and he arches up off the plank. His heels dig in. His fingers clench in the hair at the back of Pascual's neck, and he says Pascual's name, and it isn't quiet. Pascual holds him through it, the whole shaking length of him, and says *sí, sí, así, ya está* into his hair. And then it takes him too, and he goes, with his face in Reggie's shoulder, and says something in Aragonese that nobody in his family would ever have let him say in the house.

---

Afterwards they lie in the dip in the middle of the bed, in the dark, with the shutters shut. The blanket's on the floor with everything else. Reggie's hand is on the back of Pascual's neck. It hasn't moved off it once.

Pascual's fingers have found Reggie's wrist.

"Now I'll count," he says.

"Go on, then."

He counts. He takes his time about it.

"Well?" says Reggie.

"Ninety-four."

"Is that bad?"

"It's showing off." He doesn't let go of the wrist. "It'll be eighty in ten minutes. I'll check."

"You'll be asleep in five."

"I will *not*."

The brown bottle's on the floor by the head of the bed. Pascual put it there while Reggie was shutting the shutters, without a word, where a hand could find it in the dark. Reggie saw him do it. He didn't say anything either. He looks at it now and doesn't need it, and is glad it's there.

"Jean," says Reggie. "In the morning."

"Together."

"Together." A pause. "And you'll tell her about the shoulder."

"What about the shoulder?"

"You lifted it over your head at lunch and said *fine* at her as if she'd insulted your mother. She'll want to know."

"She'll want to know *nothing*," says Pascual, into Reggie's hair, "because we're not telling her any of it. We're telling her the licence part. The roof's *private*."

"The roof's the best part."

"The roof's a *disgrace*." He yawns. "A man of fifty-four, up a ladder at six in the morning, so's he can come round and *ask*. Whoever heard of such a thing."

"You'd have liked it better with a wet mattress?"

"I'd have liked it *Sunday*," says Pascual. And then he doesn't say anything else, because he's asleep.

---

Reggie wakes at first light, round Pascual like weed round a buoy.

He's along the whole of Pascual's back. His arm's over Pascual's waist and his hand's flat on Pascual's chest, about over his heart. His knee is in the crook of Pascual's knee. There are no shoes this time. His face is in the back of Pascual's neck.

He doesn't move.

He lies there and breathes, and knows exactly where he is, and doesn't move. After a while he feels Pascual wake. The breathing changes under his hand. Pascual lies still for a moment the way he does, making sure. Then Reggie feels him find out where he is and who's round him, and feels the whole length of him go loose.

"You're doing it again," says Pascual, into the pillow.

"I know."

"You're *awake*."

"I know I'm awake."

There's a pause.

"You're doing it on *purpose*," says Pascual.

"I'm doing it on purpose."

Pascual's hand comes down and finds Reggie's wrist, on his chest, and holds it. Two fingers settle flat on the inside of it.

"Well," says Pascual. "About time."

There's a sound on the roof. It's soft at first, and then steadier, a light grey shower coming in off the channel at dawn, the kind that's gone again in ten minutes. Reggie listens to it. Ten yards down the row, his own roof, mended, tarred, done properly, isn't letting in a drop.

"It's raining," says Pascual.

Reggie puts his mouth on the back of Pascual's neck, where the knot was.

"Good," he says, and doesn't move.
