# Countersigned

He wakes with his hand round Reggie's wrist, and before he's anything else he's counting.

He doesn't know he's doing it till he's at forty. Then he knows, and goes on, because you don't stop a count halfway, and at the end of the minute it's seventy-two. Seventy-two, and regular, and a bit soft under the fingers the way it always is in a thin man. He lies there on the floor in the dark with the figure in his head, and something goes out of him that he hadn't known was in him, a long way down, as if a hand had been holding on to something in there all night and had only now been told it could let go.

Seventy-two. He'd have taken eighty. He'd have taken ninety and been glad.

It's not light yet. The slats in the shutters have gone from black to a sort of dark grey, and the grey's lying across the floor in stripes, and across the blanket, and across Reggie, who's asleep on his back with his mouth shut and his face turned towards Pascual. His glasses are on the table. Without them his face looks younger and more tired at once, and Pascual can see where the lines are going to be in ten years' time, and wants to see them, and that frightens him more than anything else has so far.

The hut smells like a stable. It smells like the Colonel's knees and the Colonel's mare and the whole of the tack room at the Bay in 1958. It's on the sheet, wherever the sheet's got to, and it's on the blanket, and it's on Reggie's neck, which is where Pascual's face is, more or less. He breathes in. Under the camphor there's Reggie, who smells of salt and soap and the paper of old books, and has done for twenty years. Pascual knows the smell from standing next to it at a rail. He hadn't known that he knew it until last night, with his mouth on the man's throat, when it turned out he'd have known it anywhere.

He shuts his eyes.

---

His body's a wreck. That's the next thing, and he takes stock of it the way he'd take stock of an animal that had come in off the reef.

The left hip's on a floorboard, through a mattress that was never much of a mattress even on a frame. It's gone past aching into a kind of dull report. The left arm's under Reggie's neck and has no feeling in it at all from the elbow down. His knees are sixty, and have views on floors, and are expressing them. There's a place on his collarbone that stings, where Reggie's teeth were, and a place under his jaw that's tender and a bit raw, which is his own stubble dragged the wrong way against Reggie's. He's made a mess of the man's chin. He'll see it when it's light. He'll have to look at it across the long table at breakfast.

And the right shoulder.

He lifts it, carefully, without moving anything else. It goes. It goes up, and out, stiff as a new hinge, and it doesn't go the whole way. But it goes past where it stopped yesterday afternoon at the shelf, and there's no bolt through it. There's only the ache you get in a muscle that's been hauled on and then been let alone. Somebody's worked every knot out of it and then gone on, afterwards, working nothing.

He puts it down again, very gently, on Reggie's chest, where it was.

He'd known.

That's the thing he lies there and thinks about, in the grey, with a numb arm and a sore hip. He'd known the moment the last knot went. He'd felt it let go at the back of his neck under Reggie's thumb, like a line coming off a cleat, and his whole head had gone forward with the relief of it. And he'd said *that was the one*, and admired his own arm like a fool. And then Reggie's hand had gone on.

It hadn't been working any more. You can tell. He's done it for a living for forty years, on horses and dogs and a hippo at Lisbon, and on the Colonel, and on students who'd pulled something in the boats. He knows the difference in his own hands between doing a job and doing the other thing. The hand on his neck had stopped doing a job, and gone on anyway. It was flat and slow, and it went up into the hair at the back of his head and down, and out along the shoulder, and back. He'd sat on the edge of Reggie's bed with his shirt off and understood, quite clearly, that there was no reason at all for it. The man had simply liked it, and hadn't noticed that liking it was a thing he was doing.

And Pascual hadn't said anything.

He'd opened his mouth to. He's sure of that. He's nearly sure. He'd had it ready, something light, something about the fee. And then the hand had come back up to the nape of his neck again, and he'd found that his head had gone back into it. He hadn't told it to. He'd sat there, with his eyes shut, while Reggie told him about the Colonel and the moon, and let his head go back into the man's hand every time it came up. And he'd thought, as clearly as he's thought anything: *don't move. Don't say anything. Don't move and it won't stop.*

He'd been like that at the rail in 1953. He hadn't thought about that rail in fifteen years. And he'd sat on Reggie's bed and thought *don't move* in exactly the same voice, as if nothing had gone past in between at all.

So when Reggie kissed him, it wasn't a surprise. Not to him. It was a surprise to his head, which had been asleep. It wasn't a surprise to the rest of him, which had been sitting there for five minutes with its eyes shut waiting for it.

---

Beside him, Reggie breathes in, and out, and doesn't wake.

Pascual looks at the ceiling.

The voice. He makes himself think about the voice, because he's going to have to, and it's better done now, in the dark, with the man asleep.

It had been like being put outside a door. He hadn't known it would be like that. He'd heard the voice a dozen times in twenty years, aimed at Directors and Commanders and the man from Plymouth and once, in 1957, at an Ulsterman with a file. He'd watched it go out of Reggie across tables and verandahs. He'd thought it the oddest and saddest thing about him, and had never had it aimed at himself. Not once in twenty years. Not when he'd thrown the bucket, not when he'd shouted him down on the verandah in 'fifty-four, not on the step with the proof. Reggie had been furious with him, and ashamed in front of him, and wrong in front of him, and wrecked in front of him on the floor of Jean's office. But he'd been all of that in his own voice. The other one was for people who weren't Pascual.

And last night, with his mouth still wet, Pascual had got *my dear chap*.

He'd felt it go into him like cold water. Before he was angry, before anything, there'd been a second of plain animal fright. The ground had gone out from under him and he was on the other side of a door he'd been on the right side of for twenty years, with the man from Plymouth.

Then he was angry. God, he was angry. He can feel the ghost of it now in his chest, lying on the floor, the way you can feel where a fever was. He'd been so angry his hands shook, and he'd have thrown something if there'd been anything to hand but the oil can, and he'd have thrown that. He can't remember the half of what he said. He remembers *it's my neck*, because he'd meant it with the whole of himself. And he remembers the look on Reggie's face when he'd said *you did this in 'fifty-seven*. That he won't forget.

And under the anger, all the way through it, like the bottom under the water, he'd known what the voice meant.

It only comes when there's something that'd cost. Reggie told him that himself, on the step, with the proof. So Pascual had stood there in the moonlight in his bare feet with his arm held in against his side, and shouted at the man. And some part of him, the part that does the post-mortems, had been standing quite still behind the shouting, taking the finding. *It came on. So there's something. So it's real.*

He'd been so frightened it wasn't.

He lies on the floor and lets himself know that, now it's over. He'd been more frightened of it being nothing than of the file, or Halloran, or the licence. More than Jean's face, or the Colonel's, or Baptiste's. That it would turn out to have been the calf, and no sleep, and the liniment and the moon, and Reggie glad of something and putting his hands on it. And in the morning Reggie would be kind about it, and careful about it. And Pascual would go on coming on Saturdays with the can, and it'd sit between them like a dead thing on the step for the rest of their lives.

The voice had told him it wasn't that. He'd never have believed Reggie telling him. He believed the voice.

---

He'd not meant to tell him about 'fifty-three.

That's the other thing he turns over, in the grey. He'd have gone to his grave with it, and been glad to. It wasn't a secret, exactly. It was a thing he'd dealt with, properly, over three years. He'd put it where you put a thing you've dealt with, and it wasn't anybody's business, least of all Reggie's. And then he'd opened his mouth last night to answer a perfectly plain question, and out it had come, the rail and the calcium and the bucket, like a drawer falling open.

*You checked?*

*Twice!*

He laughs, without a sound, into Reggie's neck. It shakes him, and his shoulder twinges, and he lets it.

He had checked, too. In 1956, at the end of it, he'd gone and stood at the same rail on purpose, next to Reggie, for an hour, feeding the calf's mother, and waited for it, and it hadn't come. And he'd done it again a month later to be sure. And he'd been so pleased with himself. He'd felt like a man who'd got a bad tooth out and could chew on that side again. And for sixteen years after he'd had the best friend he ever had, and it had been enough, and more than enough, and he'd have said so to anybody.

He doesn't know, now, whether he'd got over it or folded it very small. He's not sure it matters. He's a vet, not a philosopher. It was folded and it's open, and here he is on the floor.

What he'd felt, saying it out loud, wasn't what he'd have expected. He'd have expected shame. There was a bit. But mostly it had been like putting down something heavy he'd forgotten he was carrying. His arms had come up light, the way they do. He hadn't known how much it weighed until he'd set it down in front of the one man it was about, and the man had looked at him as if he'd been handed the calf.

And then he'd asked *is it instead*, and that had been the worst moment of the night. Worse than the voice. He hadn't meant to ask that either. It had been sitting under everything else, and it came out in a voice he didn't recognise, low and frightened.

Because he'd filled that lamp. For fifteen years, every Saturday, for a man in Norway. And he'd liked Alec. God help him, he'd liked him the first day, with his face like the ash and his wretched exact figures. And there was a drawer in this room with the man's letter in it, and his photograph face down. And Pascual had lain awake more than once, since November 1971, wondering whether a lamp that's gone out gets filled with something else, and whether the something else knows.

*You filled that lamp. Who for?*

He hadn't known the answer until he said it. That's the truth. He'd opened his mouth to say *for him*, because that was the answer he'd been giving himself for fifteen years, and what came out was *for you*. And the second it was out he knew it was the true one, and had been all along. He'd been filling the lamp so Reggie could sit up by it. He'd never cared who was coming up the path. He'd cared who was sitting up.

*And you wouldn't fit.*

He laughs again, silently, into the man's neck. It's a terrible thing to say to anyone. It's the best thing anybody's said to him in years.

---

The grey's getting paler. He can see the drawer from here, if he turns his head. He turns his head.

It's a plain deal drawer in a plain deal table, with a brass knob gone green. There's a letter in it, soft as cloth at the folds, and a copy of the *Proceedings* open at a page, and a photograph face down with a date and a figure on the back in pencil. He knows all that. He was there when every one of them went in.

He finds he isn't sorry. He'd thought he might be. He'd thought he might wake and look at that drawer and feel like a man who'd come into somebody else's house while they were out. He doesn't. He feels a sort of sadness, plain and quiet, the kind you feel for a good animal that died before you could do anything. And under it, oddly, something close to fellow-feeling. The man had his thumb on the posts of the north pen in 1970 and read off a stone and a half, and wrote it down on the only thing he had. Pascual had his hand on the same ribs last night, spread flat, reading the same thing.

*He wants feeding*, Pascual thinks, at the drawer. *You were right. You'd have wanted the four pounds.*

He thinks the man would have wanted Reggie fed more than he'd have wanted anything else in the room. He can't prove that. But he'd put money on it.

---

He turns his head back.

He's been trying not to think about the rest of it, because he's sixty in November and he's lying on a floor next to the man, and it seems indecent. It does no good. It comes anyway.

He'd been frightened the whole time, a bit. Not of Reggie. Of Reggie's heart. He'd had his weight on the man, and the man's under ten stone and has a heart that Marguerite won't let into a boat. Every time Reggie's breath went short, Pascual had felt his own go with it and thought *is that the heart or is that me*. That's why he'd put his hand there. He'd said *let me count* and meant *let me know*. And Reggie had let him, which he hadn't expected, and lain there with Pascual's palm flat over his breastbone, counting him, and said his name.

It had gone right through him, that. More than anything else. Reggie saying *Pascual*, low, over and over, in his own voice, while Pascual was counting his heart. He hadn't known a name could feel like that in the ear. As though Reggie was holding on to it to keep it from going somewhere.

He'd been thinking about it, some years. He can admit that too, now, lying here. Not often; he's a practical man. There'd been a widow in Kingston in 'sixty-six, a good kind woman who laughed at his English, and that had been over by 'sixty-eight. Since then he'd supposed, the way you do at fifty-five, that that part was done with. He hadn't minded much. He'd got the work, and the pens, and Saturdays.

And then last night a small thin man with liniment on his hands had pulled him down onto a mattress on the floor, and looked at him. Just looked, in the slatted moon, with his whole face open and nothing arranged on it at all. And he'd said *I'm looking at you. It's different. I've only just found out it's different.*

Pascual had kissed him then to make him stop. He couldn't have borne another word. He'd have wept, and he'd not been going to weep, not then.

He weeps a bit now, into Reggie's neck, in the grey. It isn't sad. It's the other thing. It comes and goes, like the end of a fever, and he lets it.

---

Reggie wakes at about six.

Pascual feels him do it: the breathing changes, and then the whole of him goes still in a different way, and then there's a hand on Pascual's arm, the right one, where it lies across his chest.

"You've been awake," says Reggie. His voice is wrecked with sleep.

"A bit."

"You were counting me."

"Seventy-two."

"Is that good?"

"It's very good. It's dull. It's the dullest heart on the island."

"You said *magnificent* last night."

"That was last night." Pascual takes his arm back and sits up, slowly, in stages, and every stage has something to say about the floor. "Last night it was showing off. This morning it's a heart. I'd rather have a heart."

Reggie watches him sit up. His eyes go to the shoulder, and Pascual lifts it for him without being asked, out and up, past his ear, stiff, and holds it there.

"Better," says Reggie.

"Better." He lowers it. "Not right. Better."

"You'll want a sling."

"I will *not* want a sling."

"For today. So you don't forget and lift something."

"I'm not a *child*, Reggie, I'll not forget —"

"You'll forget at the first bucket," says Reggie, and he's right, and Pascual knows it and doesn't say so.

He gets up instead. It takes a while. He finds his trousers, and puts them on. He goes to the table, past the drawer, and picks up the oil can from where he left it by the bed last night, and unscrews the cap of the lamp.

It's full. Of course it's full. He filled it a week ago and it's not been lit since.

He tips the can anyway. A few drops go in. It takes almost nothing. He screws the cap back on and puts the can down on the floor, and stands there with his hand on the lamp.

"It's Sunday," says Reggie, from the floor, behind him.

"I know it's Sunday."

"You've filled it on a Sunday."

"You told me to. It's been a very peculiar week." He doesn't turn round. His ears are hot. "It didn't need it."

"No."

"I did it anyway."

"I know," says Reggie. "I saw."

Pascual stands in the grey with his hand on the cold lamp. He's sixty in November, and he's got a stable on his skin and a floor in his hip and the best part of a working shoulder. There's a man on a mattress behind him who's going to walk into Jean's office in two hours and not do the voice. The man's going to take the steps one at a time on the way, and Pascual's going to go at his pace.

Behind him the pillowcase comes off the pillow with a soft tearing sound, and he knows exactly what it's for.

"Come here," says Reggie. "Arm."

He goes and gives him his arm.
