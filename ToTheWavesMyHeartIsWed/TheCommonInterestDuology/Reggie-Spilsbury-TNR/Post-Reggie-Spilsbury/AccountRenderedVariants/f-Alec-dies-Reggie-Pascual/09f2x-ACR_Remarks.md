# Remarks

The lines are half-hourly now. Dr Pobil changed them at noon on the Friday, when the little one had fed three times and the mother's resp. had come down to twelve and stayed there. *Every half hour, Baptiste, and sleep in between, that's an order, I'll not have two patients.* So she does the line on the hour and the half hour. In between she sits at the table in her hut with the shutter open onto the pen and writes up.

That's why the shutter's open. The pen's forty yards below the row, and there's nothing between but rock and a few sea grapes. With the shutter open she can hear both of them breathing from her table: the big slow blow and, a while after, the small high one. She counts them without meaning to. If either one went wrong she'd hear it before she'd see it. She'd rather hear it.

She does the half-past-seven line at the wall. *19.30. 12/40. Both at N. end.* The moon's up, nearly full, so she doesn't need the torch. At a quarter to eight she's back at her table with the calf-weight guesses and Dr Pobil's reference book. At eight she's at the wall again. *20.00. 12/38. Feeding.* Coming back up the path she sees Dr Pobil go along the row to the last hut, with the can in the wrong hand.

She notices that because she notices things. She's noticed him go along with the can every Saturday since October, because it's the kind of thing she notices. It's a thing people on this island do and don't discuss, like the Colonel's plaque and the Tuesday boat. She'd asked Dacosta about it once, before Dacosta went home to Bridgetown in December. Dacosta had looked at her for a long moment and said, *It's Saturday.* That was all. Baptiste had understood that it was all she was going to get, and that it was, in its way, a complete answer.

She sits down at her table. The shutter at the end of the row's open too. She can hear them the way you hear anybody on the row on a still night: not words, only the shape of two voices, Dr Pobil's going up and down and Dr Tar's under it, and once Dr Pobil's big laugh and a *don't*. She doesn't listen. She's working out whether the little one's taking enough, from the mother's posture and the time between feeds, and she's got two columns of figures that don't agree.

At about twenty-five to nine it goes quiet at the end of the row.

She doesn't notice the quiet, exactly. She notices it afterwards, the way you notice a clock has stopped: it had been going, and then it wasn't. She's got her pencil on the second column. The mother blows, slow. The little one blows. Nobody along the row says anything at all.

And then Dr Pobil says, quite clearly, "*Don't you* dare. *That's the* voice."

She puts her pencil down.

---

She's heard Dr Pobil shout. Everybody on the island's heard Dr Pobil shout. He shouts at the Tuesday boat when it's late, and at the students when they leave a bucket where an animal could reach it, and at the pump. He shouts at Dr Tar across the long table about water chemistry, and Dr Tar shouts back, and Dr Leslie says *children* without looking up, and after ten minutes they're both laughing. It's the island's weather. You don't put it in a book.

This isn't that.

"*It comes on when there's something that'd* cost*! You told me that! On the* step*!*"

And then Dr Tar.

She has never — no. She has heard Dr Tar say *that's wrong* in a clear carrying voice across a room perhaps a hundred times since October. She's heard him say *again*, and *no, try again*, and once, to a boy from Leeds who'd left the pen gate on the latch, a thing so quiet and so exactly put that the boy went white. She hasn't heard him raise his voice to anybody. She'd assumed, if she'd thought about it, that he didn't have one to raise.

He has one.

"*Of* course *it'd cost! You were by the* window*!*"

It carries over the water like a bell. Everything carries over the water on a still night. She sits at her table with her hands flat on the two columns of figures and hears the rest of it come across the rocks and the sea grapes and in at her shutter.

"*Halloran looked at you for ten seconds, and I put the worst sentence of my life in a file —*"

She doesn't know who Halloran is. She knows what a file is. Her father's a clerk in the Red House in Port of Spain, and was a clerk there before 1962, when the files had a different crest on them. He used to say that a file was a place where a sentence went to live longer than the man who said it. She'd thought that was a clerk's joke. She sits very still and understands that it wasn't.

Then there's a gap. And into it, in a voice she wouldn't have known for his, flat and hard and each word laid down by itself like a figure in a column:

"*I did not ask you.*"

She's been on this island five months. She's listened to Dr Tar talk for a good many hours of them, on the verandah, in the sun, about a man called Spilsbury and a fuse box and a corridor and a curve. She has never once heard him say *did not* when he could say *didn't*. She's the kind of person who'd notice.

Then they're both shouting at once, and she can't sort it. There's something about *paid*, and something about *yet*, and *so you'll decide*. And then Dr Pobil, louder than all the rest of it put together, with his voice cracking straight across:

"*It's* my *neck! Mine! I say what I do with it!*"

And down in the pen the mother blows, short and hard, twice.

Baptiste is on her feet before she's decided anything. She knows that sound. It's not trouble, not yet. It's an animal that's heard something it doesn't care for and has come up to see what it is. The little one's pressed in under her, and there's a noise coming off the row that she doesn't understand, and she's got a calf a day and a half old.

And along the row the shouting's still going.

Baptiste takes hold of her own shutter, and shuts it, as hard as she can.

It goes like a pistol shot. It rattles the pane in the door. It's meant to.

The shouting stops as if somebody had cut it with a knife.

She stands with her hand on the catch and her heart going. Along the row, very clearly in the silence, one shutter closes and then another, and then there's a crack that has to be a bolt going home. Then there's nothing but the pen: the mother blowing again, slower, and after a while the small one.

She thinks: *I did that.* She isn't sure whether she means the calf or the rest of it. She thinks she may mean both.

---

It's quiet for a while. Then, through two sets of shut shutters and forty yards of night, she hears Dr Pobil again. He isn't shouting now. He's past shouting and out the other side into something that just carries.

"*I don't care. Let them hear. Let the whole row hear —*"

She stands with her hand on her own shutter, in the dark, and doesn't move.

Whatever Dr Tar says back, she can't hear it. He must have said it quietly. It's only a few words. Then there's nothing at all from the end of the row, nothing for a long time, long enough that she starts to count the mother's breaths just to have something to count.

The mother's settled. She can hear that even through the shutter. The big slow blow, regular, and the small one after it, regular. Whatever it was has gone past, and the mother's decided it wasn't anything to do with her.

Baptiste looks at her watch. It's eight minutes to nine.

She opens the shutter again. She does it slowly and quietly, because she needs to hear the pen and because there's no reason now to make a noise. She leaves it open. She sits down at the table, and picks up her pencil, and looks at the two columns of figures that don't agree, and doesn't see them.

---

The nine o'clock line is due, so she goes down to the wall with the book and does it. *21.00. 12/40. Both at N. end. Settled.*

Then she doesn't go back up.

She sits on the wall of the north pen in the moonlight with the green book on her knee. From down here she can see the whole row laid out along the rock above her: the long room, Mrs Joseph's verandah lamp, her own open shutter with the light behind it, and the last hut. The last hut's shut up tight, shutters and door, with a line of lamplight showing at none of them. Nobody's lit the lamp.

She can't hear anything from it down here. The sea's too close. She finds she's glad.

She doesn't know, exactly. She's twenty-three and she's from Port of Spain, and she isn't a fool. She's got a window, a file, a man who didn't ask, a neck that belonged to somebody who wanted it understood that it was his. She's got five months of Saturdays with an oil can, and a lamp that nobody's filled for its own sake since before she came. And she's got Dr Tar on the verandah in October with his tea going cold, telling her about a man in Norway, in a voice she'd understood even then was the voice of somebody telling her the thing and not the other thing. She can add. She's been adding since she got off the boat.

She doesn't want to add. It isn't hers.

At some point, from the last hut, very faintly, over the water and the sea grapes and the tide, somebody says *yes*, much too loudly, and stops.

She looks at the sea for a while after that.

---

At half past nine she does the line, and then she opens the book at the eight o'clock page and looks at the gap in the Remarks column.

There's got to be a line. The mother surfaced short at twenty to nine, twice, and it's in the Resp. column already in her own hand, because she wrote the figure down when she got back to her table, before she knew why. A figure without a remark is a figure somebody'll ask about. Dr Pobil will initial these lines in the morning, and in a year some student will read them and want to know why the resp. went short at twenty to nine on the second night. The book has to say.

She thinks about what Dacosta told her in November, sitting on the jetty, about the stranding log from the February before. About Whitlock, with his hand shaking, asking what to put in the *Staff called* column, and Dacosta telling him *put what happened. It's a log.*

So what happened?

She writes it in her head first, to see what it looks like. *Raised voices from end of row.* That happened. It's true, and it's the animal's business, because it's why the animal came up. It's in the book for the animal, not for anybody else.

Then she sits and thinks about who reads a station log. Dr Pobil in the morning, and Dr Tar, who reads everything, and reads upside down. Dr Leslie, who signs it at the end of the month. A student in a year. The Board, if the Board ever wants to see one. And anybody who comes out from Kingston with a leather case and three days to spend looking at everything and writing very little.

She thinks about her father, and the Red House, and a sentence that lives longer than the man who said it.

She thinks about Dr Tar, on the jetty, in the sun, saying *I'll tell you the rest*, and then telling her, and her keeping her notebook shut on her knee and not writing any of it down. He'd noticed that. She'd seen him notice, and be grateful, and not know why.

She puts the pencil to the paper.

*20.40. Mother restless, surfacing short. Raised voices from end of row*

She stops there, with the pencil on the paper. A remark that says *raised voices* and nothing else is a remark somebody asks about.

She thinks about what they are, on this island. What people are used to hearing. The Colonel at the long table saying *you two and your water*. Dr Leslie saying *children*. The weather.

She adds a bracket.

*(Drs T. & P., re water chemistry).*

She looks at it. It's the most likely thing. Anybody who knows the station would read it and nod and turn the page, and anybody who doesn't know the station would read it and learn something true about it. Two senior men on this island have been shouting at each other about water chemistry since before she was born. It's the most likely thing, and she's written it in the Remarks column in pencil, neatly, where it'll stay.

It isn't what happened.

She knows that. She sits on the wall with the pencil still in her hand and knows it, and decides she'll carry that herself. She's the one who keeps the book. It's hers to carry.

*20.52. Settled.*

She does the ten o'clock line, and the half past, and at eleven she goes up to bed. She leaves the shutter open for the pen, and doesn't look along the row, and sleeps.

In the morning she'll turn the book round on the verandah rail and hold out the pencil, and say *it seemed the most likely thing*. That will also be true.
