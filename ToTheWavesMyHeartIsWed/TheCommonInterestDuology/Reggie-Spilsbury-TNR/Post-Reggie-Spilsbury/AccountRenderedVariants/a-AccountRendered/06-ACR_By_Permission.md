# By Permission

He is angry at breakfast every morning, as advertised.

It is not a performance. He would like that understood, although there is nobody to understand it but the man across the table, who understands it perfectly and eats his egg. It is a real anger, and it arrives each morning at about the same point, somewhere between the coffee and the second slice of Fru Aasen's bread. That is when Tar gets up, puts on the old jacket with the leather at the cuffs, and says he'll be down at the tank room by nine. And Spilsbury, who has a key to the tank room and a salary and a bench and a name on the door, looks at the jacket and feels the whole thing go through him again as if for the first time.

"You are going to the tank room."

"I'm going to the tank room."

"As what?"

"As Rasmussen's guest. He's asked me to look at the seals' diet sheets. He says they're wrong." Tar puts his cup down. "They are wrong. They're getting herring and nothing else."

"And you are going to be charming to him about it."

"I'm going to be *correct* to him about it. Charming's extra, and he gets it for nothing." Tar stands up. "Are you coming down, or are you going to sit here being furious at the bread?"

"I am going to sit here," says Spilsbury, "being furious at the bread, for a further four minutes, and then I am coming down."

"Four minutes," Tar says. "I'll tell Rasmussen." And he goes out. Spilsbury hears him on the stair, and hears him stop at the bottom to say something to Fru Aasen, who does not speak English. It is evidently the right thing, whatever it was, because she laughs. He has lived above that woman since last May and has not made her laugh yet.

He sits for the four minutes. The anger does not go anywhere. It is a fixed quantity, like the temperature of the sound, and it will be there tomorrow at the same point between the coffee and the bread. He has stopped expecting it to do anything else and has begun, rather to his own surprise, to find it companionable. It is the one thing in the flat that is entirely his, and Tar has left him to have it.

---

Rasmussen is a big slow man of sixty with a beard like a bird's nest and three words of English for every thirty of German. He has taken to Tar the way the seals take to the slip. He took one look at him in the doorway of the tank room on the first of April and decided that here was a man who knew what he was looking at. By the end of the first week he had given Tar the seals, the diet sheets, the key to the fish store and the use of his own chair. The last has not been offered to Spilsbury in a year.

The seals are a pair of young harbour seals, a male and a female, taken out of a salmon net off Giske in May of last year with the mesh still round their necks. They came into the station on a trawler hatch with their eyes shut. Nobody knows how old they are. Nobody knows whether they were a pair before the net or became one after it. What everybody knows, because it is the only thing about them anybody has ever written down, is that they will not be parted.

Tar knows what that means better than any man alive. He does not say so. He stands at the end of the tank in the mornings with the diet sheet and watches them lie on the slip against one another, the female's head along the male's back, the male's flipper over her. And he writes very little. What he writes is about fish.

"They're getting herring and nothing else," he says to Rasmussen, in English, slowly, with his hands. "Herring. Only herring. Every day. It's like feeding a child on bread and jam."

Rasmussen considers this.

"Bread and jam," he says.

"Bread and jam." Tar makes a gesture of a child eating a very large quantity of something. "They want cod. Some sprat. A bit of squid if you can get it. And they want the fish whole."

"*Hel*," says Rasmussen. "Whole."

"Whole. With the insides in. That's where the vitamins are, you see —"

And Spilsbury, standing at the bench by the window with a tray of gill sections he has not looked at in twenty minutes, watches the man do it. It is exactly the voice. It is the warm one, the one that likes you, the one that got four days out of Frank Nicholson in a lift. It is pointed at a Norwegian of sixty about the gut contents of a herring. And Rasmussen is nodding along to it like a man hearing a good sermon, and by Thursday the seals are getting cod.

It is the coat. He has watched it for twelve years and he knows every seam of it. And it is the same coat, and it is doing what it has always done, which is to make people give Tar things; and the thing they are giving him is cod, for two seals he does not own and cannot study and has no standing to touch.

He finds he has to look at the gill sections for rather a long time.

---

The letter from Antwerp comes in the second week of May, forwarded from Great Ormond Street in Fru Aasen's slow capitals. Tar reads it at the desk in the study with his elbows on either side of it and his glasses pushed up on his forehead, and at the end of it he laughs out loud.

"What."

"Maes. The keeper, the one who did the diagram. The Director's gone and put the pair in the new tank on the *Director's* say-so and had himself photographed with them for the newspaper, and Maes has sent me the cutting and underneath it he's written in pencil *I am the man holding the bucket.*" Tar holds it up. There is a grainy photograph of a stout man in a homburg beside a tank, and at the very edge of the frame an arm, and a bucket. "Look at that. Look at that *arm*."

"It is a very correct arm."

"It's the best arm in Belgium." Tar puts the cutting down, and pulls the draft towards him across the desk, and the laugh goes out of his face and something else comes into it. It is the look Spilsbury has seen in doorways for twelve years: a man who has had an idea and can already see it built.

"Right," Tar says. "This is it. This is what I needed for the Federation piece."

"Which Federation piece."

"The one on Marseille." The draft has been on the desk since the middle of April: twelve pages, much crossed out, in Tar's quick sloping hand. It is about a zoological park near Marseille. For six years it has held a single animal of the species in a concrete pool twenty feet long, and it has answered three of Tar's letters with a printed card. "The Major wants it for the summer bulletin, and it's been missing something. It's all argument. There's nothing in it a Director can look at and see himself in. And *this* —" He taps the letter. "This is it. The keeper at Antwerp wrote to me on his own account, in English, with a diagram, because his Director wouldn't. And six weeks later there's a new tank, and a pair in it, and the Director's in the paper."

"And you propose to say so."

"I propose to *print* it. His letter. The diagram. The cutting, if the Major can get a block made." Tar is already writing, a new line in the margin, fast. "*The following is reproduced from a letter received from Mr J. Maes, keeper, of the Antwerp Zoological Society —*"

"No," says Spilsbury.

---

He does not know he is going to say it until it is out.

He would like that recorded, and there is no book to record it in, so he records it the other way: sitting at the table by the stove with his cup halfway to his mouth, attending to it. He has done no sum. That is the extraordinary thing about it. There is no column, no two sides of the page, no tally. There is not even the half-second in which he customarily looks at a result before he decides whether to decline it. The word arrives on its own, fully formed, from somewhere he has not previously had occasion to visit. It is in the room before he has checked it.

Tar looks up.

"No?"

"No." He puts the cup down. "You will not print his letter."

"Alec, it's the whole *piece* —"

"I am aware that it is the whole piece. I have read the piece. It is very good and it needs exactly this, and you will not print his letter." He hears his own voice and does not recognise it; it is quite level, and not cold, and there is nothing behind it at all. "He wrote to you on his own account. You told me so. He is twenty-two and he is frightened of his Director, and he went round him, in writing, in a language he does not write well, to a man he had met once, because he thought it would help the animals. You put his name and his letter in the Federation's summer bulletin, and it goes to every zoological society in Europe, and one of them is Antwerp. And the Director reads it."

Tar has stopped writing.

"And the Director," says Spilsbury, "who has just had himself photographed in the newspaper with a tank that he has told the newspaper was his idea, discovers that a keeper of twenty-two went behind his back to an Englishman. And that the Englishman has published the fact, with a diagram, as an example to Marseille."

The room is quite silent. Somewhere below, Fru Aasen is doing something with a pan.

"And Maes," Tar says slowly, "is the man holding the bucket."

"He is the man holding the bucket."

Tar looks down at the draft for a long time.

Spilsbury watches it arrive. He watches it the way he has watched everything in that face for twelve years. It comes in at the brow, where it always comes in, and goes down through the whole of him. And somewhere in the middle of it he sees Tar see something that is not Maes at all. It is not in the room. It is a verandah at the William Martin Institute in 1954, and a postgraduate standing at the end of it holding his own elbows, and it is quite a long time ago now and it has not gone anywhere.

"God," Tar says, very quietly. "I was going to do it. I'd have done it by *Friday*."

"Yes."

"I'd have been *pleased*."

"You would have been extremely pleased. It would have been a very good piece." Spilsbury picks the cup up again, because his hand wants something to hold. "You may still write it. You may use every word of the argument and the photograph of the Director, since he has chosen to be in the newspaper. And you may write to Maes and ask him whether he would like his name in it. He may say yes. He may be delighted. It is a very correct arm. But you will ask him first."

And then, because he can hear the shape of what he is saying and cannot for the life of him stop it:

"Not without asking him."

---

Tar stares at him.

He stares at him for about three seconds. Then his face does a thing Spilsbury has not seen it do in twelve years, which is to go entirely blank with astonishment, like a boy's. Then he puts both hands over it and laughs into them until the chair creaks.

"You —" He can hardly get it out. "You *heard* that. You heard yourself say that."

"I did."

"You said my sentence at me. Across a *breakfast table*. About a *keeper*."

"It seemed the correct sentence."

"It's the — Alec, it's the *only* sentence, that's the —" Tar takes his hands away. His eyes are wet and he is still laughing and it has gone somewhere else in the middle, the way the man's laughter does. "When did you start *doing* that?"

"About forty seconds ago," says Spilsbury. "I should like to know as much as you would."

He means it. He sits at the table in Kirkegata with his cold coffee and looks, as honestly as he knows how, at what has just happened. It is this. For twelve years there has been one man on earth whose costs he counts. He has counted them to three figures, and declined his own arithmetic three times on the strength of them, and burned his notebooks, and very nearly spent Frank Nicholson. And a moment ago, without any arithmetic at all, he has refused that man a thing that man wanted, on behalf of a Belgian keeper of twenty-two whom he has never met and does not especially like the sound of.

He does not know where it has come from. He has a strong suspicion that it came from a front room in Southport he has never seen, by way of a sentence said to him at a table in a hut, and that it has been sitting in him since February of last year, waiting for a Belgian.

It frightens him considerably. It is also, he notes, not at all unpleasant.

"You've never stopped me," Tar says. He has put his elbows back on the desk and is looking at him across the room with his chin on his fists. "Not once. Not the partitions. Not Nineteen. You held the *watch*."

"I did."

"And you've just stopped me."

"It appears so."

"*Why?*"

Spilsbury considers the question.

"Because you would have been sorry," he says, "and he would have been sacked. And I find — I have found, apparently, at some point in the last fifteen months, without being consulted — that I am no longer able to count only the first of those." He looks at the cup. "I should like it noted that I do not approve of this development and did not authorise it."

"Noted," says Tar. His voice is very odd. He picks up his pen, and draws a line through *The following is reproduced from a letter received from Mr J. Maes*, and then another, and then pulls a fresh sheet towards him and writes *Dear Mr Maes,* at the top of it.

"I'm going to ask him," he says, not looking up.

"Yes."

"And if he says yes I'm going to put the *arm* in."

"I should hope so," says Spilsbury, "it is the best arm in Belgium," and Tar laughs again, into the letter, and doesn't stop writing.

---

Maes says yes by return, in English, with a second diagram. The piece goes into the Federation's summer bulletin under the title *A Note on Pairs*, with the photograph of the Director cropped so that the bucket is in the middle of the frame. Underneath is a line in italic: *Photograph and diagram by permission of Mr J. Maes, keeper, to whom the author is indebted.* In the third week of July, Marseille writes, not with a printed card.

But that is later.

---

In June it does not get dark.

Spilsbury knew that it would not; he has been in Ålesund a year and he has the tables. The sun goes down behind the islands at a quarter to midnight and comes up again at a quarter past three. In between, the sky over the Brosundet does not go black at all. It goes a deep clear blue, like the inside of a mussel shell, and stays that colour, and the gulls do not stop.

He knew. What he had not allowed for is what it is like with Tar in the flat. Tar has never been this far north in summer. He stands at the study window at midnight in his shirtsleeves with his glasses on, looking out at a sky he can read by, as if somebody had given him a whole new animal to look at.

"It's *blue*," he says. "It's the middle of the night and it's *blue*."

"It will be blue until August."

"You didn't *say*."

"I sent you the tables in October."

"You sent me *numbers*, Alec. Nobody reads *numbers* about the sky." Tar turns round. The blue is behind him and the lamp is not lit, and his face is in shadow and the edges of him are lit. "Come and look."

Spilsbury comes and looks. They stand side by side at the window with their shoulders touching, looking out over the roofs of the town to the islands and the sea. It is very quiet except for the gulls. Somewhere out on the sound a boat is going out with its engine going, a small steady knock.

And he finds that there is a thing he wants.

It is not a large thing. It arrives in him quite plainly, the way the word arrived at the breakfast table in May: without arithmetic, fully formed. He knows what it is. He has asked for things three times in twelve years: twice in November, both times having worked out beforehand exactly what the words would cost, and once in a hut on a cay in February of last year. That was *leave the lamp*, and he was three feet behind his own eyes the whole time, and Tar felt it and told him so.

He has not asked since. There has been the year. And then there was the thirty-first of March, which was not a night for asking; there was a great deal too much else to get through. And there have been the weeks since, which have been good, and ordinary, and angry at breakfast, and he has not asked, because he has not been sure that he had the standing to ask for anything at all.

He finds, at a window at midnight in June, that he is sure.

"Reggie."

"Mm."

"Leave the curtain."

Tar turns his head and looks at him.

"It's open," he says slowly.

"I know it is open."

"It's been open all *evening* —"

"Leave it open," says Spilsbury. "That is what I am asking you for."

---

Tar doesn't say anything for a moment. He looks at him in the blue light, from about a foot away. Spilsbury watches him recognise it: the lamp, the hut, the words, the whole of February 1957 coming up behind his eyes at once.

"Are you here?" Tar says.

It is a real question. It is the one that matters, and they both know why he has asked it.

"Yes," says Spilsbury. "Entirely."

And Tar puts a hand up, very slowly, and lays it flat on his chest, over the ribs, where it goes. He keeps it there for a long moment, reading, the way he'd read a pulse, the way he read everything in the hut and found the man three feet behind his own eyes.

"You are," he says. There is a great deal in his voice. "You are, aren't you. The whole of you."

"I said so."

"You've said so before."

"I had not been, before," says Spilsbury. "You were right. I was taking you away with me. I am not taking you anywhere now. There is nowhere to take you."

Tar kisses him.

It isn't like the thirty-first. There's nothing to get through this time, no letters, no bench, nothing to shout about in a whisper. There's just the man, in a window, in a light that has no business being there, and all the time in the world. It is slow the way things are slow when nobody is counting. Tar's mouth is soft and unhurried and tastes of the coffee they had at ten. His hand stays on Spilsbury's ribs the whole time, and his other hand comes up and takes the glasses off Spilsbury's face and folds them and puts them on the sill, carefully, where they will not be knocked off.

"Say what you're doing," Tar murmurs against his jaw.

It is his own old request, from the hut. It is asked back, quite deliberately, the first thing of its kind Tar has asked him for in sixteen months.

"I am standing at a window," says Spilsbury, "at twelve minutes past midnight, in full daylight, in the middle of a Norwegian town, being kissed by a man who has no job."

Tar laughs against his neck.

"Go on."

"I am about to take him to bed, and I am not going to be quick about it, and I should like him to know that I have been thinking about it since approximately a quarter past eleven, when he took his jacket off."

"*Quarter past eleven.*"

"It is an estimate," says Spilsbury, "and it is conservative," and Tar makes a sound against his throat that isn't a laugh at all.

---

The light in the bedroom is the same blue and there is no lamp, and there is no need of one. He can see everything. That is the thing he has asked for, and he gets it, all of it, for as long as it takes.

He sees Tar pull his shirt off over his head, impatient, without doing the buttons, the way he did on the thirty-first, and emerge with his hair on end. He has put on flesh since March on Fru Aasen's bread, and is no longer London pale but a sort of Norwegian beige, with a line of real brown at the back of the neck from the tank room door. He sees his own hands go to the man's shoulders and down the arms, and he sees Tar watch them go. Until tonight he has not been able to watch a thing he was doing and be in it at the same time. It was always one or the other: the man doing it, or the man three feet back taking notes.

He puts his mouth to the notch of the collarbone, and to the hollow of the throat, and to the place under the jaw where the pulse is. He does it slowly, and says what he is doing as he does it, in the voice he uses at a bench, with the measurements in, because he has been asked to. Tar tips his head back against the pillow and gives it to him and says his name, the short one, and then, when Spilsbury has got further down, the long one.

"Alexander —"

"Yes."

"*There*. Don't — slower, do that slower —"

"You said that in 1946."

"I *know* I said it in 1946," says Tar, breathless and furious and laughing, "I've been saying it for twelve *years*, you've *heard* me —"

"I have heard you every time."

"Then *do* it."

He does it. He takes a very long time about it and permits no hurry at all. He watches Tar's face in the blue light the whole way through, and does not look away once, and is not anywhere but here. And at the end Tar has both hands in his hair and his back arched off the bed and his eyes wide open, looking straight at him, as he did in the hut, because the curtain was staying open and he has kept his side of it. And what Tar says at the very last is not a name at all. It is *yes*, just that, several times, with nothing left in his voice.

---

Afterwards they lie with the quilt thrown off, because it is warm, for Ålesund, and the sky outside the window is beginning very faintly to go pale again at the far edge.

Tar is lying half across him with his hand on his ribs.

"A hundred and eighteen."

"You cannot possibly."

"I can. It's coming down." A pause. "You weren't counting."

"No."

"The whole time. You weren't anywhere. You were just *there*."

"I was there," Spilsbury says. "I asked to be, and I was."

Tar is quiet for a while, and then he says, into his shoulder, in the voice of a man nine-tenths asleep:

"You asked me for something."

"I did."

"First time since the hut."

"Yes."

"I noticed," says Tar. "I've been waiting. I didn't know if you would. I thought you might not, ever, after the — after all of it. I thought you might think you'd used your turn up."

Spilsbury looks at the ceiling, where the light has begun to come back.

"I did think so," he says. "For some time. I believed I had asked for a great deal more than I was owed, and had been given it, and had then very nearly spent a man to keep it. I did not think I had the standing to ask for a curtain."

"And now?"

"Now I find I do not care whether I have the standing." He puts his hand in Tar's hair and keeps it there. "It is one of several things I seem to have stopped caring about. I did not authorise any of them. I suspect you."

"Not me," says Tar, almost asleep. "Frank."

And Spilsbury lies in a bed in Kirkegata in a light that will not go away, with the gulls going on outside and Tar's hand on his ribs and Tar's breath slowing against his collarbone. He thinks about a man he has not seen in eight years: a big fair man with a greengage tree, who painted the canal behind the Labs frozen and got the ice wrong, and who wrote *Supper* on the back of a postcard of a pier.

He thinks, quite clearly, *I should like to go.*

It is not a sum. He notes that. It is just a thing he would like. He has not had many of those that were not this man.

He goes to sleep before he can decide what to do about it, which is also new.
