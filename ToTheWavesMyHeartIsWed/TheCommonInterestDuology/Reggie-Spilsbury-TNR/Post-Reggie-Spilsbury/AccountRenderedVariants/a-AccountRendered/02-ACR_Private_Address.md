# Private Address

*Not without asking him.*

Spilsbury has the answer to it before the man has finished the sentence. It has been ready since Thursday. It is short and correct and he could give it in the voice he uses for a referee's report: that asking is itself a line on paper, or worse, a line in a railway timetable; that a visit to Southport by the Director of Research of a colonial institute, in the month in which an enquiry agent may be anywhere in the north of England, is the single most dangerous act available to either of them; that Nicholson cannot be protected by being consulted, only by being left alone; that the arithmetic comes out the same whether or not he is asked, and a man should not be troubled with a question whose answer will not change the result.

He opens his mouth to give it.

What comes out instead is, "You asked me what I should have written on Thursday."

Tar takes his hands down from his face.

"Yes."

"I will tell you now. It will not take long. It is in the same form as the others." He does not look away, because the man did not look away from him in the lift, in the story he has just been told, and it seems to him the least that can be managed. "*Result of arithmetic: F. N. Accepted.*"

The lamp is burning a little high. He notes it. He does not turn it down.

"There are three before it. You know two of them. The passage in 1946, and the Southport money. The third was the report in 1954."

"I know about the report."

"Each of the three says *declined*. Each time the arithmetic came out one way and I did the other thing, and each time the reason was you." He finds he has put his hand flat on the table, beside the letter, and leaves it there. "And each time the cost of declining was mine. That is the thing I should have written underneath on Thursday, if I had been honest with the page, which I am generally. *Fourth time. First time the cost is not mine.*"

He hears it.

That is the difficulty with saying a thing aloud, and the reason he has spent thirty years writing things down instead: a written sentence stays on the page where it was put, and a spoken one goes out into the room and comes back to you as though somebody else had said it. He hears a man at a table in lamplight say *the cost is not mine* about a fifteen-pound payment and a house in Southport and a stranger on a doorstep, in a level voice, and he knows exactly what kind of man says that. He has sat across tables from several. He has written letters about them to firms.

Tar is watching him. Tar reads him the way he reads a column of intake figures: the whole of it at once, the wrong entry first.

"Alec."

"Yes."

"You heard that."

"I heard it."

And there is the other half of the sum, which does not appear in any of the four entries because in thirty years there has been no occasion to put it down. It is the half that says what this is worth to him. Not the post, which is gone, and not the notebooks, which are ash. It is the man across the table, who asked him in November 1946 to do something again, slower, and who has been told the truth by him every day since, and who will stay, because he has stayed through two animals and a boy and a verandah and the thirtieth of May, for exactly as long as the one thing holds that has held them since 1946.

Spilsbury can run the other result forward as well as he can run anything. Tar at the inspector's table in March, saying the canteen's sentence, or not saying it; either way, alive and on the licence and in post, and gone from him by Easter. Not in a temper. Tar does not leave people in a temper. He goes quietly, the way he left the Labs, having seen that if he stayed he would keep doing it.

"Very well," Spilsbury says. "Ask him."

---

Tar does not say anything for a moment. Then he puts both hands flat on the table, one each side of the letter, and leans on them, and lets out a breath that goes on for rather longer than a breath should.

"You mean that."

"I do not say things I do not mean."

"You don't agree with it."

"I do not agree with it at all," says Spilsbury. "I think it is a grave risk to you and very likely no use to him. I have been told no, and I have decided that I would rather lose the argument than lose the other thing, and I should like you to understand that that is the whole of my reasoning and I am not proud of the proportions."

"What other thing?"

"You know perfectly well what other thing."

And the man laughs. It is not much of a laugh. It comes out through his nose, wet at the edges, and he wipes his face with the heel of his hand like a boy, and says, "*Christ*, Alec," and comes round the table.

He does not do anything clever. He stands behind the chair and puts both arms round Spilsbury from behind, across the chest, and his face down into the side of his neck, and holds on. His glasses are still on the table. The top of the case at the foot of the bed catches the lamp.

"You burned them," Tar says into his collar.

"Yes."

"I'm still furious about it."

"Yes."

"I'll be furious about it for years."

"I should think considerably longer," says Spilsbury, and puts his hand up over the forearm across his chest and keeps it there.

---

They do the rest of it at one in the morning, because there is no other hour available and neither of them is going to sleep.

"You cannot go to Southport."

"Then who?"

"A letter —"

"Is paper," says Tar, "and it's a letter from a man in the West Indies to a man with a conviction, asking him a question about his private life. You told me that yourself in 1950. And you don't ask a man a thing like this by post. You don't ask him *anything* by post. You go and sit in his front room."

"There is an agent in the north of England."

"There's an agent somewhere in England. He's looking for a door on the second floor of the Labs. He hasn't got Southport yet, because the only line to Southport is fifteen pounds a quarter in your mother's maiden name, and he hasn't got that either." Tar is sitting on the bed now, in his shirtsleeves, with his elbows on his knees. "So I go before he does. And I go with a reason."

"Which is."

"Hester's wanted somebody in the Colonial Office before the inspection since November. She's said so three times. That's London. And the Marine Station at Port Erin has had a standing invitation out to this Institute since 'fifty-four to come and see their tank room, which nobody's ever taken up. Port Erin's the Isle of Man. You get to the Isle of Man from Liverpool."

"And Southport is eighteen miles from Liverpool."

"Twenty minutes on the electric."

Spilsbury looks at him.

It is very good. It is the kind of arrangement Tar makes without apparently trying, out of whatever happens to be on the bench, and it is clever, exactly as Pobil said it would be, and Spilsbury would take it apart in three moves if the thing at stake were anybody else.

"You will not stay in Southport."

"No."

"You will not sign a register there."

"I'll stay at the Adelphi in Liverpool as the Director of Research of the William Martin Institute, going to the Isle of Man, and I'll go out on the electric after dark and come back on the last one."

"You will not write to him first."

"I wasn't going to."

"And you will not," says Spilsbury, and stops.

Tar waits.

"You will not do the voice," Spilsbury says. "At the door. You will want to, because it is the thing you do at doors, and because you will be frightened, and it will get you inside. Do not."

Tar does not answer that for some while.

"No," he says eventually. "I know."

---

Pobil is on the jetty on the Tuesday with his hands in his pockets, standing a little apart from everybody else, as though he had come down to look at the weather.

"Hester says you're going to charm the Colonial Office."

"Hester's said worse."

"And then the Isle of Man."

"Port Erin. They've got a tank room."

"I know they've got a tank room. I sent them the drawings of ours." Pascual looks at the boat, and at Alleyne's boy, and at the sea, and at Reggie, in that order. "Is he all right?"

"No. He's up the row pretending to read."

"And you?"

"Ask me when I'm back."

"I'm asking you now, *hombre*."

"Then no," says Reggie. "But I've got a thing to do, and it's the right thing, and that's more than I'd have said on Saturday."

Pascual takes a hand out of his pocket and puts it on the back of Reggie's neck, briefly and hard, the way you'd steady a man on a deck, and takes it away again.

"Eat on the aeroplane," he says. "I don't care what it is. And come back."

---

It takes two aeroplanes and a night in New York to get to London, and London in the middle of February is grey on grey with the lamps lit at four, and so cold after five years of the other thing that Reggie stands on the steps at Victoria in the coat he has not worn since 1952 and cannot for a moment work out what is wrong with his hands.

He does the Colonial Office on the Monday. He does it well. He does it, in fact, in the voice, all of it, forty minutes with an assistant secretary in the West India Department, in a room with a coal fire, and comes out with a clearer idea of what the inspector will look for than Hester has managed to get out of four months of letters. On the pavement in Great Smith Street afterwards he stands and does up his coat and thinks, quite coldly, that the coat is what it is and does what it does, and that there is one door this week where he is not going to put it on.

On the Tuesday he takes the train to Liverpool, and signs the register at the Adelphi in his own name and the Institute's, and goes up to a room with a view of a wet station roof and lies on the bed with his shoes on until it is dark.

At a quarter to seven he walks to Exchange and gets the electric.

It runs out along the coast in the dark with the carriage lights on and hardly anybody in it: a woman with a string bag, two boys in school caps, a man asleep with his mouth open. Beyond the glass there is nothing at all for a long time, and then the sandhills at Freshfield, grey on black, and then nothing again. He sits by the window with his hands between his knees and thinks about the lift, and the rack of electrodes, and a big fair man with ink on his cuffs saying *come to supper, bring Spilsbury, since you're joined at the hip*, laughing, in a hot library, in 1947.

He has not seen Frank Nicholson since the corridor outside the committee room in October 1950. He had gone in after Alec. Frank had been in the corridor, although he had no business there any more, collecting a box of his things. Reggie had walked past him, because there were two Ministry men on the landing, and had not said good afternoon.

He has not thought about that for six years. He thinks about it now for twenty minutes, all the way to Southport.

---

The house is in a terrace off Lord Street, the far end, where the verandahs give out, and it is exactly the sort of house Reggie would have guessed if anybody had asked him: two storeys, bay window, a gate that sticks, the step whitened. There is a light on in the front room behind a drawn curtain, and another upstairs.

He stands at the gate for rather a long time.

He knows what he'll do at the door. He can feel it arriving, the way you can feel a sneeze. The slight lift in the chest, the face arranging itself. *My dear Frank — you'll forgive this, turning up out of the blue —* and the man will say yes, come in, of course, because he is decent, and decent men don't like to be difficult once, let alone twice.

He takes his hat off. He goes up the path and knocks.

It is not Frank who opens the door. It is a small grey man of about sixty, in a cardigan with leather at the elbows and a pair of reading glasses pushed up on his forehead, holding a tea towel. He looks at Reggie, and at the hat in his hand, and at his face, and whatever he sees there makes the whole of his own face go quite still.

"Yes?"

"I'm sorry to call so late. My name's Tar. I used to work with Frank Nicholson. Is he —"

"Frank," the man calls over his shoulder, not taking his eyes off Reggie. His voice is perfectly level. "There's somebody for you."

There are steps in the passage, slow, heavy, a big man's steps. And then Frank is in the doorway behind the small man's shoulder, in his shirtsleeves, with a pencil behind his ear and a smear of something blue on his wrist.

He is fifty. He is grey all through now, and he has got heavier, and his face has settled into itself in the way faces do when they have had to stop being surprised by anything. He looks at Reggie for about three seconds.

"Tar," he says.

"Hello, Frank."

"Well." Frank lets out a breath. "I'll be damned. I thought it'd be him."

---

The front room has a coal fire and two armchairs drawn up to it, and between them a small table with two cups on it and a plate with the crust of something on it and a library book face down. The walls are covered in watercolours. Reggie takes that in first, before anything else, because there are so many of them: the sands, the pier, the Marine Lake, the sands again, a hundred shades of grey-brown water under a hundred shades of grey sky, one after another, in narrow black frames.

Over the mantelpiece, in a better frame than the others, is a canal in winter, frozen, with a factory wall behind it and a sky the colour of dishwater.

"That's the one," says Frank, seeing him look. "The one I made you look at. You said it was very good."

"You said it was terrible."

"It *is* terrible. The ice is all wrong. I kept it because it's the last one I did down there." He stands with his hand on the back of the nearer armchair. "Sit down. George, this is Reggie Tar, from the Labs. Tar, this is George Tolley, whose house it is."

"How d'you do," says George, and does not offer to shake hands, and does not leave either. He stands in the doorway to the passage with the tea towel.

Reggie sits down.

Frank sits in the other chair, the one that is plainly his, with the cushion gone into the shape of him. George, after a moment, goes out, and Reggie hears him in the kitchen along the passage, not doing anything very much, with the door open.

"You look well," says Frank. "Brown. Is it still the West Indies?"

"Still the West Indies."

"And him?"

"He's there too. Since May."

Frank nods slowly, as if this confirms something he had worked out a long time ago and filed.

"So," he says. "You've come a long way in February. You'd better tell me what for."

---

Reggie tells him.

He has rehearsed it, on the aeroplane and in the hotel and on the electric, and none of the rehearsals survive the first sentence, because the first sentence is supposed to be about the letter and what comes out instead is, "I walked past you in the corridor. The Thursday. You were collecting your things and I walked past you, because there were two men from the Ministry on the landing, and I didn't say good afternoon."

Frank looks at him.

"I'd forgotten that," he says.

"I hadn't."

"No, I can see you hadn't." Frank rubs his thumb across the blue on his wrist. "Go on. That's not what you came about."

So Reggie goes on, and he does it plainly. He does it the way Alec gives an account, in order, with nothing in it to make anybody look better: Dewar & Lecky, and the animal, and the pound, and Bedford Row; Mrs Greaves and the sheets; paragraph five, and a man paid by the week who is somewhere in England now looking for a door. And then the part he has been dreading since Saturday, which is Alec's plan. He tells it as it was told to him. The money moved into Alec's own name, the letter to Bedford Row, the trail from the Labs to Nicholson and from Nicholson to Southport and from Southport back to Alec, and the file closing on *Spilsbury and Nicholson, 1950*, which is not true, and which nobody on either side would ever have to say a false word to establish.

Frank does not interrupt. He sits with his big hands on his knees and his head a little on one side, the way he used to sit at a seminar, listening to a man he suspected was wrong and wanted to be sure of before he said so.

When Reggie stops, Frank is quiet for a while.

"And he'd have done that without asking," he says.

"Yes."

"And you stopped him."

"I told him not without asking you. He said very well." Reggie finds he has his hat in his hands and is doing something to the brim that will ruin it. "He didn't want to. He thinks it's no use to you and a danger to me, and he's probably right about both."

"He usually is. It's the worst thing about him." Frank almost smiles. "I used to watch him at your door, you know. On the second floor. Not the door, the doorway, with the pair of you shouting about a figure. He'd stand in it with his arms folded and look at you like a man looking at the only good instrument in a bad laboratory."

Reggie does not say anything.

"I knew in 'forty-seven," Frank says, quite gently. "I'm sorry if that's a shock. It wasn't anything either of you did. It's only that you know the look, if you've had to learn not to have it yourself." He pauses. "Margaret used to say you were the nicest young man at the Labs. And I used to think, yes, he is, when he's looking at Spilsbury, and at the rest of us he's very nice indeed."

---

"There's another thing," Reggie says.

"Go on."

"The four days. On Nineteen. In the lift."

Frank's face changes very slightly. It doesn't close. It goes still and attends, the way a man's face does when the thing he's been waiting for has come round the corner at last.

"I talked you into it," Reggie says. "I knew you didn't want to and I knew why, and I did the voice at you, and you said yes before we got to the bottom."

"And then I said you were very good at it."

"And I carried that about for a day and then I went on." His voice is doing something he hasn't told it to. He lets it. "I did it to the Director the same week, about the spring animals. I did it to the Ministry men in 1950. And what it got me was permission to keep going. That's what it's for."

Frank is silent for a moment.

"I know what it's for," he says. "I was in the lift."

"Then you know —"

"I know you did it. I watched you do it. I've a good eye." He leans back in the chair and the chair creaks. "And I'll tell you what I've told myself about that lift, most weeks for seven years, because I'm tired of carrying the whole of it on my own."

"Go on."

"You did the voice at me. And I said yes because you were very good at it, and because I wanted to be the sort of man who said yes to you, and because I wanted the lift to get to the bottom." He stops. "And mine watched the door. I did the nerve work, Tar. Four days. It was my hands. You can have the voice. You can't have the hands."

Reggie looks down at his own.

"All right," he says, after a while.

"All right."

---

"About the money," Frank says.

"You know."

"Of course I know. I'm a scientist." He hears himself, and his mouth moves. "Was. I get a letter out of a clear sky from a man called Ashby, offering me a bench, in the same fortnight as a Post Office order comes from a Mrs Mercer in Hampstead. I'd never met either of them."

"No."

"And the bench is in optics. Which was the one thing I'd have gone into if I hadn't gone into nerves, and only one man at the Labs ever heard me say so, over a slide rule, in 1948." He shakes his head. "I nearly wrote to him. A dozen times. There was nowhere to send it that wasn't in his own name."

"He didn't tell me," says Reggie. "Not till Saturday."

"No. I don't suppose he would." Frank looks at the fire. "I lived on it for a year and a half. I'd have been in the gutter otherwise, I don't mind saying. Ashby started me at five pounds a week and the fines and the solicitor had had everything else."

"And then?"

"And then in the autumn of 'fifty-two I got made up to senior draughtsman, and George —" He stops for just a moment. "George said I could have the back room, at a rent I could manage. And after that I didn't need it."

"Did you send it back?"

"Where to? Mrs Mercer in Hampstead?" Frank gets up, heavily, and goes to the bureau against the wall, and opens the flap, and takes a Post Office savings book out of a pigeonhole and holds it up. "It went in here. Every quarter since the autumn of 'fifty-two. I've not touched a penny of it. It's two hundred and seventy pounds and the interest, and I've not known what to do with it for four years. It's one thing to be helped up. It's another thing to be kept."

He puts the book down on the table between the two cups.

---

"So," Frank says, sitting down again. "You've come to ask me."

"Yes."

"Then ask it properly. Don't do it round the side."

Reggie takes a breath.

"He wants to let them think it was you. In 1950. You and him. He'll put it in writing that the money was a gift, so it can't be made into blackmail, and the file'll close on the two of you, and nobody need ever look any further. And it'll mean your name in a solicitor's file in Bedford Row, and very likely a Colonial Office minute. And maybe a man on the step. And I don't know what else, Frank. I don't know what else, and nor does he. He said so."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you say no."

"And what happens?"

"Then I resign," says Reggie. "I come off the licence. The Institute gets a Director of Research who's never been anywhere near a second floor, and there's nobody left for Bedford Row to pull on. The paper goes out under Pobil and Leslie. The Martin's all right."

"And you?"

"I'm not your concern."

"Don't be an ass," says Frank. "Of course you're my concern. You're sitting in my front room with your hat in bits." He looks at Reggie for a long moment. "You'd do that."

"Yes."

"You'd give up the work."

"Yes."

"You're telling me the truth."

"I haven't told you anything else since I sat down."

Frank looks at him a moment longer. Then he nods, once, like a man accepting a figure he has checked.

"Then it's a real question," he says. "Right. Give me a minute with it."

---

George comes in while Frank is having his minute.

He comes in with the teapot, which is plainly a pretext, and fills Frank's cup and then, after a pause, finds a third cup in the sideboard and fills that and puts it by Reggie's elbow without a word. He does not go out again. He stands by the window with the curtain at his shoulder.

"He'll say yes to you," George says. He says it to Reggie, quietly and without any heat at all. "He says yes to people. That's how he came to be in Camberwell magistrates' court with his name in the paper and no job. It's how he came to be here."

"George."

"I'm only saying it, Frank. Somebody ought to."

Reggie looks at the small grey man by the window, and at the two armchairs, and the two cups, and the library book face down, and the second pair of reading glasses on the mantelpiece beside the frozen canal. He reads the room the way he'd read a pen, all at once, and it's not hard. It was never going to be hard, once he looked.

"He's right," Reggie says. "Frank, he's right. That's why I've told you what happens if you say no. So it's not me you're saying yes to."

"I know that," says Frank. "I heard you." He picks up his cup, and doesn't drink from it, and puts it down. "George, sit down. You're making the curtain nervous."

George sits on the arm of the other chair.

---

"Yes," says Frank.

Reggie doesn't move.

"Yes, with conditions. You'll want them in order, because he will."

"Go on."

"One. He puts it in writing, over his own name, that the money was a gift I never asked for. That's the only thing in this whole business that could put me back in front of a magistrate, and I've been in front of one."

"He'll do it."

He holds up a big finger. "Two. I write to Bedford Row myself. Three lines. I've had money from Dr Spilsbury since 1950. I didn't ask for it. I've nothing further to say. It's true, and it doesn't deny anything, and it means nobody from Bedford Row has any reason on God's earth to come out on the electric and knock on this door."

He doesn't look at George when he says the last part. He doesn't need to.

"Three," he says. "The money stops. This quarter. I'm not having it go on in his own name for the whole world to read. That's done. After this we're square."

"Frank —"

"Four." And now he does look at George. "Nothing about this house. Nothing about who lives in it. Not in his letter, not in mine, not in anybody's file. There's a man called George Tolley who takes photographs of other people's weddings from a studio in Chapel Street, and you've never heard of him, and nor has Bedford Row, and nor has anybody in Whitehall. That one isn't a condition. That's the price."

"Yes," says Reggie. "Yes. All of it."

"And I'll not say a false word. I'll not say I was his. I'll not say I wasn't. If anybody asks me, I'll say I've nothing further to say, and that'll be true." Frank sits back. "That's his trick, isn't it. I saw him do it to the Ministry men. I watched him come out of that room in 1950. Fourteen minutes, and not a word in it you could hang him with."

George makes a small sound.

"You're sure," Reggie says. He has to. "You've heard what happens if you don't."

"I've heard it. It's a good offer and you mean it, and I'm turning it down." Frank leans forward with his forearms on his knees. "Listen. They can't fine me twice for the same Saturday. They've had the job and the name and Margaret."

"I know."

"There's nothing left in this for them to take that they haven't taken already, except that." He tips his head, very slightly, towards the man on the arm of the chair. "And I've kept that out of it, just now, in front of witnesses. So what it costs me is my name in a drawer in Bedford Row that I'll never have to look at."

He stops, and rubs his thumb over the blue on his wrist again.

"And I'll tell you something, Tar, since you've come all this way and ruined your hat. It's the first time since 1950 anybody's asked me anything. The magistrate told me. The Director told me. Margaret's mother told me in a letter. You're the first one who's sat in that chair and asked."

Reggie can't say anything at all.

"And there's the animal," Frank says, more quietly. "Nineteen. And the one they've got now. If your paper says what you say it says, that you can't keep them apart and not kill them, then I'd like to have my name somewhere in that, even if it's a place nobody can read. Mine watched the door." He sits back. "That's all. That's my reasons. I don't make speeches."

"You just did," says George.

"I've had six years to get it ready," says Frank. "Stop it."

---

It is twenty past ten, and the last electric is at eleven.

At the door, George gets Reggie's coat off the peg and holds it for him, and Reggie puts his arms into it, and George smooths the collar down at the back without being asked, the way you would for anybody going out in February.

"Thank you," Reggie says. He means it for the coat and a good deal else, and George seems to take it for both.

"He'll be all right," George says. "He's better at being all right than people think."

Frank is behind him in the passage, filling the whole width of it.

"One more thing," he says. "Not a condition. A request."

"Anything."

"Don't say anything. You'll regret it." Frank puts a hand in his pocket and brings out the Post Office book, and looks at it, and puts it back. "When this is over, whenever that is, and wherever the pair of you end up, you'll come to supper. Both of you. George does a fish pie that's the best thing in Lancashire, and he'll deny it. Bring Spilsbury, since you're joined at the hip. And I'll give him his book back myself, and I'll shake his hand, and he'll have to stand there and let me, which will be the worst thing that's happened to him in years."

Reggie laughs. It comes out of him with no warning at all, on a doorstep in Southport in the dark, and it isn't very steady, and Frank looks enormously pleased with himself.

"I'll tell him."

"Tell him I said it was terrible. The canal. Tell him I said the ice was all wrong."

---

The electric back to Liverpool is nearly empty. Reggie sits by the window with the sandhills going past grey on black and his ruined hat on his knee, and for a long time he doesn't think about anything.

Then he thinks about the canal.

It's terrible. Frank's right: the ice is all wrong, it's flat, it's the colour of a plate. And Frank kept it over the mantelpiece for seven years, in the better frame, in another man's front room, because it was the last one he did down there. Reggie sits in the swaying carriage and understands, for the first time, and too late to be any use to anybody, what Alec has been paying for since November 1950 at fifteen pounds a quarter. Not the bench, and not the rent. The man had the bench and the rent inside two years. It was that.

At Exchange he goes to the telegraph counter, which is still open, and writes on the form in capitals, and the clerk counts the words twice, which is correct procedure.

> SPILSBURY MARTIN INSTITUTE
> PORT ERIN ARRANGEMENTS SATISFACTORY STOP TERMS AGREED STOP ICE ALL WRONG STOP
> TAR

It costs him three and fourpence, and it's the only thing he's bought all week that he doesn't begrudge.
