# Notice

Reggie comes back on the twenty-sixth with a cold he picked up in New York and the Colonial Office in his satchel, and he is up the row and into the long room before Pascual has finished taking the dressing off the calf's flipper.

"Well?"

"Well, it's done. Both letters went on the twenty-first." Reggie drops the satchel on the bench and sneezes. "His three lines and Alec's page and a half. I read Alec's. It's the most frightening thing I've ever seen him write, and there isn't an adjective in it."

"And the man? In Southport?"

Reggie sits down on the stool, which he does not do in the long room, and puts his elbows on his knees.

"He's got a bench and a back room and a friend who does a fish pie," he says. "And a picture of the canal behind the Labs, frozen, over the mantelpiece. He said yes. He gave me four conditions and a speech and he said he doesn't make speeches."

Pascual finishes the dressing. It takes him a minute, because the calf wants to know what he's doing and he has to keep explaining it to her.

"Good," he says. "That's good, *hombre*."

"It isn't good. It's a man putting his name in a drawer in Bedford Row so we don't have to."

"It's good that he got to say so." Pascual wipes his hands. "What's the trouble, then?"

"There's still the sheets." Reggie rubs his face. "Frank's letter gives them a better story. It doesn't take the old one away. There's still a report in a drawer that says the doctors share a hut, and a licence office that'll read it in a fortnight, and all we've done is make it the second most interesting thing in the drawer."

Pascual stands and looks at him.

He has been thinking about this since the ninth, on and off, in the way he thinks about things, which is not in order. And a part of it has been sitting at the back of his head since Kingston like a stone in a boot, because in Kingston he took Lecky apart on the dates in front of a man from Agriculture who said *yes* out loud. And that man from Agriculture is now sitting in the same office as the inspector, reading Dewar & Lecky's application for a licence of their own.

"Who d'you think they are to the licence office?" he says. "Dewar and Lecky?"

"A firm."

"A firm that wants a licence. From the same desk. This spring."

"Go on."

Pascual leans on the bench. "A firm whose adviser walked off with one of their animals and sat on a wall for fifty-one days writing down how it died, and whose record of it was read out against them in November. That's who they are. And now they've sent a man with a card to ask our washerwoman about our beds."

Reggie takes his hands away from his face.

"Oh," he says.

"Right. So that isn't a report, is it. That's a commercial party hiring a private agent to go round the staff of the one institution that gave evidence against it."

"And?"

"And the only thing you do with that is make sure the licence office hears about the agent from us first." He straightens up. "Not what he asked. Just that he asked. Just that there was a man, and a card, and questions put to a woman in our employ about her work, without so much as a by-your-leave to the Institute."

"We can't tell them. If we write to the Colonial Office they'll want to know what he asked her."

"*We* can't," Pascual agrees. "Hester can."

---

He goes to Hester that evening, alone, which he has thought about, and tells her exactly what he has decided to tell her and nothing else. A man with a card from an insurance agency in Nassau came to Alleyne's clerk in September and again in January asking about the Institute's boats, its cold store and its staff. He got some of his answers from Iris Greaves, on a Monday, about her own work, without any notice to the Institute. And the card, as far as Pascual can find out, belongs to an agency that does enquiry work on commission for London solicitors, one firm of whom acts for Dewar & Lecky.

Hester listens with her pen in her hand and her pad on her knee, the way she listens to everything. She does not write anything down. When he stops, she is quiet for a long moment.

"They questioned a member of my staff," she says.

"Yes."

"About her work in this Institute. Without application to me."

"Yes."

"Iris Greaves has done the linen on this island since 1938," says Hester Bevan. "She did it through the war, when there was no soap. She has never been asked a question about it by anybody but me." She caps her pen. "I shall write to the West India Department in the morning. I shall say that a commercial party with a licence application before them has seen fit to employ an agent to interview the domestic staff of a licensed institution that gave evidence against it at a public hearing. I shall say that I regard it as an impertinence. And I shall ask that it be noted on the file before the inspection."

"That's — yes. That's it exactly."

"I shan't ask you what he asked her, Dr Pobil," Hester says, getting up, "because I expect I know, and because it is none of my business. And it is certainly none of theirs." She looks at him from the door. "Goodnight."

Pascual stands in the Bevans' sitting room for a while after she's gone, with his hands in his pockets, and has no idea at all what she knows, or has known, or since when. And he finds, rather to his surprise, that he likes her better than he did at supper.

---

The letter from Bedford Row comes on the fourteenth of March, three days after its own deadline and two days after the inspector's boat. It is addressed to Dr Spilsbury and he brings it up to the long room at four, as he brings everything now, and reads it aloud to the two of them in exactly the voice he'd use for a referee who had got something wrong.

It is short. Their client notes the contents of Dr Spilsbury's letter of the twenty-first ultimo, and of a letter received from a third party. Their client regards its enquiries in the matter as concluded. Their client reserves its position as to the material.

"*Reserves its position*," says Reggie.

"It means they will not sue," says Spilsbury, "and would like it to be thought that they might."

"And *concluded*."

"It means they have found what they were sent to find, and have been made to look like men who sent a man to find it." He folds the letter. "Something has happened to them at the West India Department. I do not know what it is. It has the shape of Mrs Bevan."

Pascual doesn't say anything. He is doing the calf's weight on the sheet, and it is up four pounds, and he writes it down very carefully.

---

The inspector's name is Halloran, and he is a tall stooping Ulsterman who has been in the Colonial Service since 1931. He spends three days on the island, looking at everything and writing very little. He weighs the fish himself. He measures the north pen with a tape he has brought from Kingston in a leather case, and asks Pascual forty questions about the calf, and on the second afternoon stands at the rail for twenty minutes without speaking, watching her sleep against her mother's flank in the shallows. Then he says, to nobody in particular, "Well, that's what it's for, isn't it," and goes back up to the office.

He asks his one question on the third morning, in the office, with the licence schedule on the table and Hester at the side with her pad, and Pascual there because he holds the veterinary part of the schedule and cannot decently be sent out.

"Dr Tar. I'm obliged to put a matter to you, and I'll put it plainly, and you'll forgive me. Representations have reached the office concerning Dr Spilsbury. Their provenance has also reached the office." He does not look at Hester. "You brought him onto this island."

"I did."

"Why?"

Pascual watches Reggie, and watches for the voice. He has heard it perhaps a dozen times in four years, the warm one, the one that likes you, the one that got a Director and a Commander and a man in a lift. He can feel Reggie feel for it. You can see it in him the way you can see a man reach for a rail in a swell: the lift in the chest, the face beginning to arrange itself.

It doesn't come.

"Because he's the best anatomist I've ever worked with," says Reggie. He says it flatly, the way he'd read a weight off a sheet. "And because he's the only man alive who kept a record of that decline, and he'd been sacked for keeping it, and he'd nowhere to put it. So he brought it here, and I let him in."

Halloran looks at him for rather a long time.

"And the representations?"

"I've nothing to say about his private life, Mr Halloran. I'm not the person to ask."

"Who is?"

"Nobody who'd tell you anything, I should think," says Reggie. "That's rather the point of a private life."

It is not the canteen's answer, and it is not a lie, and it is not the voice, and Pascual, sitting by the window with the veterinary schedule on his knee, finds that he has been holding his breath for the whole of it and lets it go through his nose.

Halloran writes something short on his sheet. Then he screws the cap on his pen.

"That'll do," he says. "Mrs Bevan, I'll want the pairing figures again, if you have them to hand. I'm going to recommend the condition."

---

Spilsbury has worked out what is coming on the eighteenth of March, three days before it arrives. He has worked it out at a feed, as it happens, watching Tar stand at the north pen rail with a bucket and not throw anything into the water for nearly a minute, which is a thing Tar does not do.

He does nothing with the knowledge. He notes it, as he notes things now: without a book, in the place behind the eyes where he has found, since the seventh of February, that he can keep a surprising amount if he is careful.

On the twenty-first Tar says, after dinner, "Walk with me," and they walk up past the top of the row and out along the path to the windward end of the cay, where the rocks go down to the reef in shelves and the trade wind comes in straight off four thousand miles of water and takes every word out of your mouth and away over your shoulder before anybody fifty yards off could hear it.

It has been chosen for that. Spilsbury notes that too.

Tar stands on the second shelf with his hands in his pockets and his back to the wind, and says it in one piece, without any preparation that shows.

"I'm resigning. The end of August, when the paper's out and Hester's got the pairing condition written into the schedule. I told Pascual on Tuesday. I'm telling Hester tomorrow."

---

There is a thing that happens in Spilsbury that he has observed perhaps four times in his adult life and has not once managed to record while it was happening. It happened on the landing at the Labs in October 1950, with the *Mercury* in somebody's hand. It happened on a wall above a stone pound on the morning of the sixth of May. It is a kind of whiteness. It starts at the back of the skull and comes forward, and while it lasts his hands are perfectly steady and his voice is perfectly level, and everything he says in it is true, and he would not say any of it in any other condition.

"No," he says.

"It isn't a question, Alec."

"I am aware that it is not a question. You have made very certain that it is not a question. You have told Pobil, and you are telling Mrs Bevan in the morning, and you have brought me to the one place on this island where I cannot raise my voice, so that I shall have to hear it at conversational volume." The wind takes the end of it. He lets it. "That is very well done. I should like to know who taught you to do it."

"You did."

"Then I should like to know why you have used it on me."

"Because you'd have argued."

"*Yes*, I should have argued." He hears his own voice, perfectly level, perfectly cold, going out into the dark over the reef. "I should have argued because it is the single most stupid thing you have proposed in thirteen years, and I have been present for the partitions. You are forty years old. You are the best field man of your generation. There is a paper going out in June with your name on it that will be cited for fifty years. And you propose to take the one bench on earth where you are allowed to stand and walk away from it, for what?"

"You know for what."

"I know what you will say. Say it anyway."

"Because as long as I'm on that licence, Frank's letter is holding up this Institute." Tar doesn't raise his voice either. He doesn't need to; the wind is on his side. "Every inspector who opens that file for the next twenty years reads *Spilsbury and Nicholson, 1950*."

"Which is on paper and nowhere else."

"And the only thing it's doing, the *only* thing, is keeping it from reading *Spilsbury and Tar*. As long as I'm here, he's paying for it. He said *after this we're square*. I can't make us square from here."

"He refused it."

"He refused it as the price of saying no."

"He refused it *outright*. You offered him your resignation in his own front room and he turned it down. He gave you four conditions and a speech and not one of them was *and Tar is to give up the work*." Spilsbury finds that he has taken a step down onto the second shelf, which puts them a foot apart, and that his hands have come out of his pockets. "And you are going to do it anyway. You are going to take the one thing he gave you and hand it back to him, unasked, and call it square."

"Yes."

"*Why?*"

"Because I asked him and he said yes," says Tar, "and I can't live on a yes. Not his. Alec, getting people to say yes is the thing the *coat's for*. I did it in a lift and it got me four days. I did it at the Labs for seven years. And the one time I went to a door and didn't do it, the man inside gave me the whole of what he had left, and I'm not going to stand on this island and spend it."

The wind comes up over the reef, hard, and there's spray in it, and neither of them moves.

---

"And there's the other thing," Tar says. His voice has changed. "You know the other thing."

"Say it."

"The Labs." He looks out at the water. "I didn't leave because I'd seen what we were doing to them. I'd seen that in the spring, with the window in the paint, and I stayed six more months."

"I know."

"I left because I liked it. I'd have gone on liking it until they carried me out, and all the while something else would've been paying. Nineteen. Four and Five. Frank's four days. I'd have looked at the bill and signed it and gone back to work."

"That was the Labs."

"And this is Southport." He takes his hands out of his pockets and holds them out, palms up, in the dark, as though showing Spilsbury something small he'd found on the rocks. "I went to a man's door and asked him to pay so I could keep my bench. It was the right thing to ask. I'd ask it again tomorrow. And he paid."

"And therefore —"

"And therefore if I keep the bench, it's the Labs again." His voice doesn't rise. "It's a better class of bill, that's all. It's a kinder man signing it."

Spilsbury does see.

That is the whole difficulty. He sees it the way he sees everything, instantly and completely, with the measurements in, and there is no argument available to him that he has not already had and lost. He was in the room in Alma Terrace in November 1950 when the man worked it out the first time, standing up, at half past ten at night. He had offered the tables then, and no animals within a mile, and Tar had said *I'd be in that hall by Friday*, and it had been true, and it had taken nine minutes.

He can feel himself about to make the same offer. He makes it anyway.

"You could keep it," he says. His voice is no longer level. He hears it go and does not try to stop it. "You could keep it and not ask anybody for anything, ever again."

"Somebody's already paying, Alec. I can't un-ask him." Tar puts his hands back in his pockets. "I can only stop taking it."

---

He is incandescent for a long time after that, and most of it is not at Tar.

He knows that while it is happening, which is a new and unpleasant experience. He stands on a rock in the wind and says a great many true things at a level voice to a man a foot away from him: about waste, and about fifty years of citations, and about a man of forty with no post and no licence and no standing to set foot in a tank room anywhere in Europe except as somebody's visitor; about what it is to be good at a thing and have nowhere to do it, which he knows now in considerable detail. And under every one of them there is a quieter sentence, which he does not say, because he has given up saying true things only in the order that suits him and has not yet learned to say them in any other.

*It was my doing.*

Nicholson is in the file because he put him there. He asked first, this time, or was made to; that is the difference between this and the other thing, and it is a real difference and he will not pretend it is not. But the drawer in Bedford Row is his, and the letter holding up the Institute is Frank's, and the man in front of him on the rock is giving up the work to take the weight off Frank. And Frank is carrying it because in November 1946, in a passage at four degrees, Alec Spilsbury did a sum and declined the result.

Every road on this branch runs back to that passage. He can see all of them from here.

"Alec."

"I am aware that I am shouting."

"You're not shouting. That's what's frightening." Tar puts a hand out and takes hold of his wrist, not hard, round the bone, the way he'd take hold of an animal to count a pulse. "Stop a minute."

He stops.

"It's not your fault," Tar says. "I know that's what you're doing."

"You do not know that."

"I do. I've been watching you do it for about nine minutes. You get a line down the middle of your forehead when you're doing a sum you don't like." The hand stays on his wrist. "It's not your fault, and it's not Frank's. I'm the one who knocked on his door. Frank said I could have the voice but I couldn't have the hands. Well, the knock's mine. I'm keeping it."

Spilsbury stands with his wrist in the man's hand and the wind in his face and says nothing for a long time.

Then he turns his own hand over, and takes hold of Tar's wrist back, the same way, round the bone.

"I do not accept it," he says.

"No."

"I shall not accept it for a very considerable time."

"I know." Tar's thumb moves, very slightly, on the inside of his wrist. "Then you'll have to be angry with me somewhere we can both hear it. Not on a rock. Not in a letter."

"I intend to be," says Spilsbury, "at length."

---

They walk back along the path in the dark, a yard apart, because there are lamps in the row. Before they reach them Tar says, not looking at him, "Where will you go?"

"Bergen has answered. There is a place at the state station at Ålesund from September. It is not a post, exactly. It is a bench and a salary and a man called Rasmussen who does not read the English journals and would not care if he did."

"Norway."

"It is a long way from anybody's drawer."

Tar walks for a while.

"And me?"

"You will leave here at the end of August. You will go to London and do whatever it is you are going to do, and do it visibly, for a winter, somewhere a great many people can see you doing it."

"Write to people about their tanks, probably. Somebody ought to."

"They will hate you."

"They'll do it anyway."

"You will not," says Spilsbury, "come to Norway in the same year."

"How long?"

It is the question. He has known since the rock that it was coming, and he has the answer ready, because he has been in an open interval before and knows exactly what it does to him.

"The thirty-first of March 1958. A year to the day from Sunday week. Ålesund."

Tar stops on the path.

"That's fixed."

"It is fixed. I will not have it otherwise. I have done six years of letters and I am not going to do a seventh with no end on it."

"Nor am I," says Tar. And then, in a different voice, "*Christ*. A year."

"A year," says Spilsbury, "and then I shall be extremely difficult to live with, and you will have given up the work, and I shall be furious about it every morning at breakfast, and you will be unbearable about the tanks at Bergen. And we shall have a very great deal to say to each other that cannot be said at conversational volume on a rock."

Tar laughs. It isn't steady and it isn't much, and it is the first time in six weeks.

---

He deposits the material on the twenty-eighth, in front of Mr Purdie, who reads every word of it aloud: *the depositor retains no interest and reserves no condition, save that the material be available on application to any qualified person.* Harcourt Tate & Co. are not informed. It occurs to Spilsbury on the boat back that he has at last done a thing in writing that Bedford Row would very much have liked him not to do, and that nobody at all is going to stop him, and he turns his face into the wind for the whole of the crossing.

On the thirty-first the boat goes at half past nine.

He has two cases, as he came with. There is nothing to write in, and he has noticed that his hand goes to the inside pocket every so often for a thing that is not there, the way a man's tongue goes to a gap in his teeth. Hester gives him an envelope with the deposit receipt in it and tells him that he will write to the Institute twice a year, because she intends to know where the depositor of that material is at all times. Pascual shakes his hand, then says "*Ay*, come here," and takes hold of him by both shoulders, briefly and very hard, and lets go.

Tar stands at the end of the jetty with his hat in his hand.

They have about ninety seconds, with five people standing at the top of the jetty being tactful.

"You haven't written it down," Tar says.

"I have nowhere to write it."

"You'll forget."

"I shall not forget the date," says Alec Spilsbury. "I find there are a number of things I no longer need a book for." He looks at the man in front of him for as long as the ninety seconds will stand. "The thirty-first of March. Ålesund. Unless I am dead."

"Don't be dead."

"I shall make every effort."

He gets into the boat. Alleyne's boy casts off. And Spilsbury sits in the stern with his two cases and his hat on his knee and does not look back until they are round the head. Then he does, once, and the man is still standing at the end of the jetty, small and upright, with his hat in his hand, exactly where he was left.
