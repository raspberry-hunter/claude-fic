# Under Separate Cover

> *Brown's Hotel, Albemarle Street*
> *12th April 1957*
>
> Dear Tar,
>
> I arrived on Wednesday. London is cold and the trains run late and nobody at the Linnean has heard of me, which I find restful.
>
> The material I have held at the bank in the Strand since 1939 was removed on the 9th and disposed of. I attended to it personally. There was rather more of it than I had allowed for.
>
> Bergen has confirmed. I sail on the 2nd of May and shall be at Ålesund by the 5th, four months before the salary begins, which Rasmussen regards as eccentric and I regard as the only sensible use of four months.
>
> I have written to Dr Leslie separately asking that my name be taken off the paper, since I know perfectly well that you will not do it, and she will. Please do not be difficult with her about it. She is right, and she is the corresponding author, and it is her decision.
>
> I have bought a notebook, at a stationer's in Holborn. It is not in use.
>
> Yours,
> A. S.

---

Reggie reads it in the long room with the shutter half up, and then reads the second paragraph again, and then puts the letter down on the bench and goes out and stands at the north pen rail for twenty minutes without a bucket.

*Rather more of it than I had allowed for.*

He knows exactly how much of it there was. He wrote it. Every third Tuesday from August 1952 to February 1956, on the Institute's paper, about fixatives, signed with two initials: something over ninety letters, and not one sentence in any of them that couldn't have been read out at the long table, and every one of them a thing he'd meant.

And a man stood at a grate in London on the ninth of April, or a furnace in a bank basement, or wherever it is you take ninety letters and nine notebooks, and fed them in one at a time, and waited for each to go before he put in the next. Reggie knows that's how he did it. He knows the man did not read them.

When he goes back in, Pascual is at the bench with the calf's sheet and doesn't look up.

"From him?"

"Yes."

"Bad?"

"Correct," says Reggie, and puts the letter in his pocket.

> *William Martin Institute for Marine Studies*
> *30th April 1957*
>
> Dear Spilsbury,
>
> Thank you for letting me know the date.
>
> You'll be pleased to hear that I lost. Jean took your letter to the long table and read out the relevant paragraph, and I argued for an hour and ten minutes, and Pascual voted with her, and so did Hester, who hasn't got a vote. The acknowledgement now reads: *We are indebted to Dr A. Spilsbury for the daily record of accession 197, and for its deposit with this Institute on terms which make it available to any qualified person.* Jean wrote that last clause and wouldn't let anybody touch it. It goes to the printer on the 9th.
>
> I've told Hester the end of August and she has begun being tiresome about my successor, which is a comfort. The calf is up to fifty-one pounds. Her mother lets her take fish off the bucket now, provided nobody looks.
>
> I hope Rasmussen is not too eccentric.
>
> Yours,
> R. T.

---

> *William Martin Institute*
> *17th June*
>
> Dear Spilsbury,
>
> You'll have seen the paper by now, or you won't, since I don't suppose they get the June number in Ålesund before August. It's out. It looks well. The figures came up better than the proofs, and John did the plate for fig. 2, and there's a letter already from Plymouth and one from Naples. I'll send you both when he's done with them.
>
> I'm not writing about the paper. He'd kill me. I'm writing because you ought to know, and he won't tell you, so it's got to be me.
>
> He's all right. That's the first thing. He eats, because I see to it, and he sleeps, because he's worn out, and he does his afternoons. But he's written a forty-one-page protocol for the north pen for whoever comes after him. Hester says it's the most thorough thing she's ever read. I say it's a man who's given notice and can't stop working, and I'm going to wedge the sick bay door open with it. And last Tuesday I found him at the rail at four with the bucket in his hand, and the calf came up to the side, and he just stood there. Didn't feed her. Didn't do anything. Just stood. So I took the bucket off him and did it myself, and he said "thank you" like I'd passed him the salt.
>
> I'm not telling you to do anything. There's nothing you can do from Norway and you'd only do something correct. I'm telling you because it's not fair you should be the only one of the three of us who doesn't know.
>
> The calf's fifty-six pounds. Tell Rasmussen from me that a man who starts four months before his salary isn't eccentric, he's English.
>
> Yours,
> Pobil
>
> P.S. Iris Greaves asked after "the tall doctor" on Monday. I said you'd gone somewhere cold. She said that'd suit you. I didn't argue.

---

> *Statens Havforskningsstasjon, Ålesund*
> *9th August 1957*
>
> Dear Tar,
>
> The June number arrived yesterday, with a covering note from Dr Pobil which I shall answer separately. The paper is very good. The acknowledgement is correct. Tell Dr Leslie that I have read her clause four times and have no amendments.
>
> The station is a harbour building on the Brosundet with a tank room below the laboratory, fed from the sound, at ten degrees in August. Rasmussen tells me it is four in February, and I have informed him that I have worked at four before. We have herring, cod, a quantity of plaice, and in a separate tank at the end, two harbour seals which were brought in by a fisherman in May with nets round their necks and have not been released because they will not go. They lie against one another on the slip at the end of the tank, and when one is taken out to be weighed the other comes to the edge of the water and waits.
>
> I have not suggested that they be separated. I record it only because I thought you would want to know that I had not.
>
> I have taken two rooms in Kirkegata, above a chandler's, on the grounds that a single man's salary is poor value on one room and I prefer a study to a bedsitting room. The landlady is a widow called Fru Aasen who does not speak English and has asked me, through Rasmussen, whether I intend to take a lodger. I have said possibly.
>
> Yours,
> A. S.

---

> *14 Great Ormond Street, W.C.1*
> *4th October 1957*
>
> Dear Spilsbury,
>
> I've been in London a month. I have a room on the second floor, a gas ring, and a desk at the Federation in Lamb's Conduit Passage, where there is a Major who runs the Handbook and who has given me the chapter on aquatic mammals held in captivity, for which I am paid nothing. I've also got a list of every establishment in Europe that holds one, which is shorter than it should be and longer than it ought to be.
>
> I've written to nineteen of them so far. I tell them I've read their published figures and have some observations on husbandry arising from recent work, and would they be good enough to let me see their tanks. Twelve have answered. Four have said yes. Antwerp has said yes *and* asked me to come in November and speak to their keepers, which I'm going to do.
>
> It turns out I'm rather good at this. You'll have guessed. It's the same thing I've always been good at, only I'm not asking them for anything for me.
>
> I left on the 31st of August. Pascual carried my cases down and Hester gave me an envelope with a reference in it that I haven't been able to read all the way through yet, and John photographed the whole station for me and put the prints in a box, and Jean said if I got maudlin on the jetty she'd push me in. I did and she didn't. The calf is sixty-four pounds.
>
> Yours sincerely,
> R. Tar

---

> *William Martin Institute for Marine Studies*
> *1st November 1957*
>
> Dear Dr Spilsbury,
>
> Thank you for your letter of the 30th September, which conforms in every respect with the terms I set out on the 31st March, and which I have placed on the file.
>
> You will wish to know that the licence granted to Dewar & Lecky Limited by the West India Department in September contains a condition that animals of the species be held not fewer than two together. I understand this to be the first such condition to appear in a licence of that office. I was obliged to write to four people about it, and to one of them three times.
>
> Dr Pobil has consented to act as Director of Research until a permanent appointment is made. He has informed me that he intends to go on acting until one is not. The Colonel concurs.
>
> I have had a letter from Dr Tar, who is writing to half the aquaria in Europe and being extremely tiresome to all of them. I approve of this without reservation.
>
> Dr Pobil tells me that Norway is cold. I have told him that I am aware of it.
>
> Yours sincerely,
> Hester Bevan

---

The card comes to Great Ormond Street on the twenty-third of December, in an envelope with a Southport postmark and no letter in it.

It's a watercolour, not a printed card: a pier going out over grey sand towards a grey line that might be the sea, under a sky the colour of a dishcloth, in a mount cut by hand from stiff white paper. On the back, in a big square draughtsman's print:

*Fish pie stands. Ice still wrong. Merry Christmas to you both.*
*F. N.*

Reggie sits on the edge of the bed in his overcoat, because the gas ring doesn't heat anything, and looks at it for a long time. *To you both.* In an envelope addressed to one man in London, from a man with a conviction and his name in a drawer in Bedford Row, and written in plain capitals on the back of a picture where anybody could read it.

He props it on the mantelpiece beside the box of John's photographs, and doesn't mention it in his next letter, and thinks about it every day until March.

> *14 Great Ormond Street*
> *18th January 1958*
>
> Dear Spilsbury,
>
> Antwerp has doubled the tank. Not the pair of them — the tank. They had the builder in the week after I went, and the keeper I spoke to, who's called Maes and is about twenty-two and terrified of the Director, wrote to me himself to say so, in English, with a diagram. Rotterdam has asked for the chapter in draft. Hamburg has said no, twice, and I'm going in March regardless, because a man at Hamburg who says no twice is a man who hasn't been in a room with me yet.
>
> The Handbook chapter is at 14,000 words and the Major says it's the most useful thing he's had for the new edition. He also says it's too long. I say the animals are too long. We'll see.
>
> It's snowed twice. I've been to the zoo on a Sunday to look at the sea lions and been asked by a keeper to stop writing things down.
>
> Yours sincerely,
> R. Tar

---

> *Kirkegata 9, Ålesund*
> *3rd February 1958*
>
> Dear Tar,
>
> Your letter of the 18th arrived on the 29th. The tank at Antwerp is excellent news. Tell Maes from me that a diagram is the only correct form of gratitude.
>
> It is dark here now by three in the afternoon and light again at about half past nine, and the harbour has frozen at the edges, and the seals lie on the slip against one another and do not appear to mind anything. Rasmussen has asked me to write up the pair for the station bulletin. I have said that I shall, and that the method section will consist of the sentence *nobody separated them*, and he has laughed, which I did not intend.
>
> Fru Aasen has asked again about the lodger. I have told her the end of March.
>
> ~~I find that I am counting~~
>
> Yours sincerely,
> A. Spilsbury

---

The line has been gone over three times. Reggie holds it up to the window in Great Ormond Street, and then to the lamp, and then, because he is who he is, to the lamp with a sheet of white paper behind it, and gets four words out of the first half and nothing at all out of the rest. Under the fourth word the pen has dug in hard enough to raise the paper on the back.

He sits on the bed with the letter on his knee.

Alec does not strike out. He didn't strike out *4.xi.56* on a page he was burning, he turned it face down. He has kept a notebook since he was nineteen, and in all of it, every line of it, Reggie has never once seen him put a line through a thing that he'd written, because it had been written, and was therefore a fact.

And here's a line he's struck, in ink, three times, on a letter that was going to be read, and then sent anyway, with the line still showing. Because the only other choice was to copy the whole letter out fresh, and that would have meant pretending the line had never been written.

*I find that I am counting.*

Reggie puts the letter face down on the blanket and his hand flat on top of it, and sits like that until the gas goes out.

> *14 Great Ormond Street*
> *4th March 1958*
>
> Dear Dr Spilsbury,
>
> The chapter has gone to the Major. Hamburg let me in on the second morning, which is one more morning than I'd allowed. I've written up the pairs at Antwerp and Rotterdam for the Federation's bulletin.
>
> I've given notice on the room from the 28th. I shall be at Newcastle on the 29th.
>
> Yours faithfully,
> R. Tar

---

He knows exactly what he has done the moment the envelope goes into the box on the corner of Great Ormond Street, and it is too late, and he walks the whole length of Lamb's Conduit Street with his face burning, and doesn't care.

*Yours faithfully.* To a man he has addressed by name. Any schoolboy knows it's wrong. It's what you put to a firm, to a *Dear Sir*, to a man whose name you don't know. And Alec Spilsbury, who has corrected the proofs of three hundred papers and the grammar of every man he ever taught, will open it at a desk in a harbour building on the Brosundet in the dark at three in the afternoon, and see it, and know it for a mistake, and know that Reggie Tar doesn't make that mistake. And then he'll read it the other way. The only way left.

---

The last letter arrives on the twenty-second of March, a Saturday.

Reggie takes it off the mat in the hall and opens it on the stairs, because he can't wait to get to the second floor, and sits down on the fourth step from the top with his back against the banister.

There's no address at the head of it. There's no date. There's no *Dear* anything, and there's nothing at the foot, not initials, not a name, not anything. Just a half-sheet of the station's paper with five lines on it in the small upright hand that has written him something over two hundred letters since 1950, every one of them signed.

> *Leda. Newcastle, Tyne Commission Quay. Saturday 29th, 1600.*
> *Bergen, Sunday 30th, about noon.*
> *Coastal steamer from Bergen, Festningskaien, Sunday 30th, 2230.*
> *Ålesund, Monday 31st, 1005.*
> *I shall be on the quay.*

Reggie reads it twice.

Then he sits on the stairs of a lodging house in Great Ormond Street with a half-sheet of paper in his hand. The woman from the first floor comes out with her shopping basket, looks at him, and goes back in again. He laughs, once, out loud. It isn't steady, and it isn't much, and it's the first time since the jetty.

It's the most unmanaged thing Alec has ever written him, and there isn't an adjective in it.
