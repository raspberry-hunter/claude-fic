# Received with Thanks

It is George Tolley who solves the arrangements, by post, in June, in a letter to Tar of such brevity and good sense that Spilsbury reads it twice for the pleasure of it.

*Come separately. Come on a Saturday at the end of August, when there are forty thousand visitors in this town and every house on the terrace has somebody's cousin in it. Nobody on Lord Street looks at a man who arrives for his supper. They look at a man who arrives for his supper with another man, so don't. Frank says to tell Dr Spilsbury that the pie is at seven and he is not to be early, because he will be.*

He is early.

He takes the electric out of Exchange at twenty past five, having told himself that he would take the one at six. Tar went out on the four o'clock, and Spilsbury has spent the intervening hour and twenty minutes at the Adelphi, sitting on the end of a bed that is not his, looking at his hands.

The train is full of trippers going home to Southport with sand in their shoes and children asleep across their laps. He sits by the window with his hat on his knee and the Post Office savings book in his inside pocket, where the notebook goes. Frank sent it on in July, registered, with a note that said *so you can't pretend it got lost*. He has not opened it. He knows what is in it to the penny, because he paid it in.

He has composed nothing. That is a decision, and he has held to it since Newcastle. He composed his way into the committee room in 1950, and the hut on the thirtieth of May, and a reply to Bedford Row, and he has decided that he will not compose his way into this one. He will walk up the path and knock and let whatever happens happen at the speed it happens, and it is the most frightening thing he has undertaken since the incinerator.

The sandhills go past at Freshfield, yellow this time, in late sun, with marram grass on them.

He finds that his hand has gone to the inside pocket, to the book, and is resting on it, the way it used to rest on the notebook. It is not the same shape. He leaves it there anyway.

---

George opens the door.

He is exactly as Tar described him, a small grey man of sixty in a cardigan with leather at the elbows. Tar did not mention his eyes, which are very pale and very steady and do not miss anything. George looks at him for about two seconds, and Spilsbury feels himself being read with a thoroughness he has not experienced from anybody but Tar.

"Dr Spilsbury."

"Mr Tolley."

"You're early."

"I am twenty minutes early," says Spilsbury, "and I was told I should be, and I was determined not to be, and here I am. I apologise."

Something happens at the corner of George's mouth.

"He's in the front," George says, standing aside. "Tar's in the kitchen with me, peeling. You'll want to go in on your own."

"Yes," says Spilsbury, who had not known until this moment that he did, and knows now that it is exactly right.

---

Frank is standing by the fireplace.

He has plainly been standing there since the knock. He is enormous, in the small front room with its hundred watercolours, bigger than Spilsbury remembered, grey all through, his shirtsleeves rolled and a smear of Prussian blue on the inside of his wrist. His face has settled into itself the way Tar said it had, into the face of a man who has stopped being surprised. And it is not settled now. It is doing a great deal.

They look at each other across the room.

The last time Spilsbury saw this man, it was the Thursday in October 1950, in the corridor outside the committee room on the first floor. Frank was carrying a cardboard box of his own things, and Spilsbury walked past him into a room where he said that Frank's judgement had been deteriorating for a year. It was not true. He said it because it was what a man in his position would have noticed if it had been true. He came out fourteen minutes later and was thanked.

"Frank."

"Spilsbury."

Frank comes across the room. It takes him three steps. He holds out his hand.

And Spilsbury, who was warned about this in February of last year, on a doorstep, by proxy, and has had eighteen months to prepare for it and has prepared nothing, takes it.

It is a big hand, warm and dry and hard across the palm, a draughtsman's hand now and not an electrophysiologist's. Frank does not shake it and let go. He holds it. He stands there in his own front room, with a smear of blue on his wrist, and holds Alec Spilsbury's hand in his for a good long while, and looks at him. Spilsbury stands there and lets him.

It is, exactly as promised, the worst thing that has happened to him in years.

"There," says Frank, at length. His voice is not very steady. "That's done. I've been wanting to do that since the first quarter."

"I know."

"Do you? I doubt it." Frank lets go, finally, and stands back. "Have you got it?"

Spilsbury takes the savings book out of his inside pocket and holds it up.

"Good. Then it's yours, and we're square, and I'll hear no more about it." Frank drops into the armchair with the cushion gone into his shape and gestures at the other. "Sit down. You look like a man at a hanging."

Spilsbury sits.

---

He had meant to wait. He had decided nothing, but he had assumed without deciding that there would be supper first, and conversation, and that the thing he has to say would find its own place somewhere after the pie, when the others were in the kitchen. He sits in George Tolley's armchair with the savings book on his knee and finds that he cannot wait at all. It is in his mouth. It has been in his mouth since the corridor in 1950.

"In the committee room," he says. "In October. The Ministry men."

Frank's face changes very slightly.

"They asked me about your work," Spilsbury says. "I told them that your judgement had been deteriorating for a year. It was not true. I had no evidence for it whatever. I said it because it was what a careful man in my position would have been expected to have noticed, and because saying it made me a careful man in their eyes. I was out in fourteen minutes."

Frank doesn't say anything.

"You were never told that. It was not in any report you would have seen. But it was in theirs, with my name on it, and it went into the file that finished you, and it was a lie, and I told it to save myself and Tar." He looks at the savings book, because he cannot look at the man. "I told Tar once that an apology you know to be worthless is only a noise. I have made very few in my life, because I have generally known that I would do the thing again. I would not do this again. So I am able to say it and mean it. I am sorry. I have been sorry since the corridor. I walked past you with your box."

The fire ticks.

After a long time Frank says, "Look at me."

Spilsbury looks at him.

"I knew," says Frank.

"You cannot have known."

"I didn't know what you *said*. I knew you'd have said something." Frank leans forward with his forearms on his knees, the way Tar described him doing it, listening. "I watched you go in, Spilsbury. I'd seen you in rooms for four years. You're the most careful man I ever met. And I knew you had Tar in the next room, and I knew what they were looking for, and I knew you'd walk in there and give them exactly what a careful man gives them. And that it'd have my name in it, because that's what they'd come for." He stops. "I didn't hold it against you. I'd have done the same. I *did* the same, as it happens, in 1948, about a man called Pearce in the physics wing, and I've not told a living soul that until this minute."

Spilsbury stares at him.

"So you're sorry, and I believe you, because you said you wouldn't do it again, and you're not a man who says that for show." Frank sits back. "And I'm sorry about Pearce, who's in Canada and never knew. And that's two apologies in one front room on a Saturday, which is two more than this house has had in a year, and I think that'll do. George'll want to know why we look like that."

"Like what?"

"Like a pair of men who've been crying," says Frank, "which we haven't, obviously," and wipes his face with the heel of his hand, and gets up to see about the sherry.

---

*— Frank —*

The pie is the best thing in Lancashire and George denies it, as promised, at length, and they eat it at the gate-legged table in the back room with the leaf up. It is the first time the leaf has been up since George's sister came at Easter.

Frank sits at the head, because George puts him there, and watches.

He watches Tar mostly, to begin with, because Tar talks. Tar talks the way he talked in the library in 1947 with the *Lancet* all over the tables, except that it isn't the same voice at all. He has heard that voice, the one Tar did at him in the lift. It isn't this one. This is quicker and plainer and funnier, and it doesn't care whether you like it. Tar tells them about the seals at Ålesund, and a man called Rasmussen who speaks tone, and a keeper at Antwerp who is the best arm in Belgium. He does George's voice asking *and is the tank heated* without being asked to, perfectly. George laughs so much he has to put his fork down.

And every so often Tar looks down the table at Spilsbury, and Spilsbury looks back.

Frank has seen that look before. He saw it in doorways on the second floor in 1947, going in the other direction, and he said *joined at the hip* about it to a man at a sherry party and meant it as a joke. It isn't the same look now either. Then it was one man looking at another who didn't know he was being looked at. Now they both know. They know it at a gate-legged table in a back room in Southport, in front of two other men, and neither of them is doing a single thing about hiding it, because there's nobody at this table to hide it from.

He hadn't known, until he saw it, how much he'd wanted to see that. It's for George and for him too, in a way. It's a thing that happens in this house every night and has never once had anybody to see it.

"You're very quiet," says George, down the table.

"I'm eating."

"You're *watching*."

"I've a good eye," says Frank. "I told Tar. It was the only thing I had at the Labs besides my hands." He takes another helping of pie, because he can. "Spilsbury. Tar says you've got two seals nobody's separated."

"That is so."

"How are they?"

"Well," says Spilsbury. "Both. The female has put on nine pounds since April. They lie on the slip at the end of the tank in the afternoons, and when one is lifted out to be weighed the other comes to the edge of the water and waits." He puts his fork down. "I have written them up for the station bulletin. The method section is one sentence."

"What's the sentence?"

"*Nobody separated them.*"

Frank looks at him for a long moment across the table. Then at Tar, who is looking at his plate. Then back.

"Mine watched the door," he says. "Did Tar tell you that? In the lift."

"He told me."

"Nineteen. I've thought about him every week. I didn't know what it was, then, the watching. I just knew I didn't like doing the nerve work on an animal that watched the door." He turns his glass round on the tablecloth. "It's in your paper now, isn't it. What it was. Tar sent me the offprint. I read it on the front with George, on a bench, twice."

"It is in the paper."

"Good," says Frank. "That's good. That's where it wants to be." He looks at the glass. "Nobody separated them. I like that. I'd like that on my stone."

George says "*Frank*," sharply, and Frank says, "I'm fifty-one, George, I'm allowed to think about my stone," and the moment goes, the way moments do at a supper, into an argument about whether it ought to be granite.

---

*— Spilsbury —*

The washing-up is done by Tar and George, who will not hear of anything else, and he can hear them through the wall of the back kitchen doing it. George is telling Tar something in a low voice. Tar says "*no*," and then laughs, and then goes quiet in a way Spilsbury knows. Whatever George has said, it has been true, and about him.

He is left in the front room with Frank and the watercolours.

Frank has fetched his box of paints out of the bureau, not to use, just to have them on his knee, the way some men have a pipe. Spilsbury is standing at the mantelpiece looking at the canal.

It is very bad. Tar was right, and Frank was right. The ice is the colour of a dinner plate and has no depth in it at all. The factory wall is a flat brown shape. The sky is the colour of dishwater. And it is the canal behind the Labs in February 1947, the worst winter in living memory, the best year of Tar's life, the year of the paraffin stove and the partitions. Spilsbury has not seen that canal in eight years and he can smell it.

"Take it," says Frank, behind him.

He turns round.

"It's no good to me. I've kept it seven years to remember by, and I've found I don't need to remember any more. I know where I was." Frank shrugs his big shoulders. "And you'll want something on the wall in Norway that isn't a chart."

"I cannot take your —"

"It's not *mine*. It's the canal's. And you walked past it every morning for seven years, same as me, and you never once looked at it, I'll bet." Frank points at it with a paintbrush. "Go on. It's terrible. You'll hate it. Put it over the stove and look at it at breakfast, and when he's angry at you, or you're angry at him, look at the ice and think, *well, it's not as wrong as that.*"

Spilsbury looks at him.

"He told you about breakfast."

"He told George. George tells me everything. It's his only vice." Frank grins. "Take the picture, Spilsbury. It's the last thing I did down there. I'd like it to go somewhere it can see a sea."

---

They leave at a quarter to ten, separately, for the last electric but one and the last. Tar goes first. At the door, George holds his coat for him and smooths the collar down at the back, and Tar lets him. Frank takes Tar's face in both his hands for a moment, without a word, and lets it go.

Spilsbury goes twenty minutes after, with the canal wrapped in brown paper under his arm, and the savings book in his inside pocket.

Frank walks him to the gate. It sticks, as Tar said it would; Frank puts his hip to it without thinking and it opens.

"You'll come again," Frank says. It is not a question.

"If I am asked."

"You're being asked. I'm asking." He stands with his hand on the gate. "Next summer. Bring him. Same arrangement. George'll write." A pause. "And Spilsbury."

"Yes."

"The book. Don't you dare spend it on me. And don't you dare put it back in a drawer and look at it for four years like I did, because I'll know, and I'll come to Norway."

"Then what am I to do with it?"

"How should I know? You're the careful one." Frank's face does something in the lamplight. "Spend it on something nobody's paying for. That'll be a change for both of us."

---

The last electric is nearly empty. He sits by the window with the parcel on his knees and the sandhills going past black on black, and thinks about the book. Two hundred and seventy pounds and the interest. Six years of fifteen pounds a quarter, less what a man needed to keep out of the gutter for a year and a half, put away since the autumn of 1952 by a man who did not want to be kept.

Something nobody is paying for.

He thinks about it all the way to Exchange, and by Exchange he knows. He does not write it down, because there is nothing to write it in, and because he does not need to.

---

*— September —*

The canal goes up over the stove in Kirkegata on the fourteenth. Fru Aasen comes up with the coffee on the fifteenth, looks at it for a long time, and says something in Norwegian to Rasmussen on the stair. Rasmussen translates it, laboriously, the next morning in the tank room, as *she says the ice is wrong.*

"*Everybody* says the ice is wrong," says Tar.

The savings book goes to the Federation on the twentieth, in an envelope addressed to the Major, with a covering letter of four lines. It asks that the sum be applied to a fund to meet the travelling expenses of keepers, below the rank of curator, who wish to visit other establishments to see how animals are kept there. The fund is to be administered at the Major's discretion and named for nobody. The Major writes back by return, bewildered and delighted, to ask what he is to call it. Spilsbury writes back to say that he supposes it will have to be called something, and that if it must, it may be called the Southport Fund. He does not say why, and the Major does not ask.

The first application comes in November. It is from a keeper at Antwerp who would like to see the tank room at Rotterdam, and who encloses a diagram.

---

He is angry at breakfast on the sixteenth of September, and on the seventeenth, and every morning after. It is a fixed quantity, like the temperature of the sound.

On the morning of the eighteenth, somewhere between the coffee and the second slice of bread, at about the point where Tar gets up and reaches for the old jacket with the leather at the cuffs, Spilsbury looks up from his cup at the canal over the stove.

It is a very bad painting. The ice is the colour of a plate.

"What," says Tar, at the door, with one arm in the jacket.

"Nothing." Spilsbury puts the cup down. "I was looking at the ice."

"And?"

"And it is not as wrong as that," he says. "Go on. I shall be down in four minutes. Tell Rasmussen."

Tar looks at him, and then at the painting, and then back at him. His face comes open, from the brow down, the whole of it at once, the way it does when nobody is watching. He doesn't say anything at all. He goes down the stair two at a time, and Spilsbury hears him stop at the bottom and say something to Fru Aasen, and hears her laugh.

He sits for the four minutes. Then he gets up and puts on his coat and goes down after him.
