# Conversational Volume

The coastal steamer comes round the end of the breakwater at one minute past ten, four minutes early, which Spilsbury would like it recorded he had not allowed for.

He has been on the quay since half past eight. There is no reason for this. The timetable was published in January and the steamer has been on it, give or take a quarter of an hour, every morning since he arrived; he knows to the minute when it passes the light on Molja and how long it takes to warp in. He came at half past eight anyway, in his overcoat and the fur hat Rasmussen made him buy in November, and he has walked the length of the quay nine times and read the notices on the shipping office door in two languages, the second of which he does not speak.

It is sleeting, lightly and without conviction. The Brosundet is the colour of pewter. A man is shovelling slush off the planks with a wooden shovel and looking at him.

The steamer is white and black and very dirty along the waterline, and there are people at the rail: a woman with two children, a priest, three men in fishermen's jerseys, a commercial traveller holding his hat on. He looks for a small man in glasses and does not find one, and in the second and a half in which he does not find one something happens inside his chest that he has no instrument for.

Then the commercial traveller moves, and there he is.

He is at the rail with his hat in his hand and no gloves on. He is paler than he was on the jetty, a London pale, with the brown gone out of his face and something gone out of the flesh of it, a winter on a gas ring. He has not seen Spilsbury yet. He is looking at the town: the painted house-fronts going up the hill in tiers, the towers, the whole absurd confection of it that the Kaiser's money put up after the fire. And he has on his face the expression he used to have at the tank hall door in 1947, looking at something new that he intended to understand by teatime.

Then he looks down at the quay, and finds him.

Neither of them does anything. They cannot. There are thirty people on the steamer and a man with a shovel, and a customs officer in a box, and it is ten o'clock in the morning in a small town where Spilsbury has been a foreign doctor for nearly a year and will go on being one. So he stands with his hands in his overcoat pockets and Tar stands at the rail with his hat in his hand, and the gangway goes down, and they look at each other across twelve feet of water at conversational distance for the whole of the time it takes.

Tar comes down with one case and a satchel.

"Dr Spilsbury," he says, at the foot of the gangway.

"Dr Tar."

"You've got a hat on."

"Rasmussen insisted."

"You look like a *Russian*." Tar's voice goes somewhere on the last word and comes back. "It's a very good hat. I'd like it noted that I've been awake since Newcastle."

"It is noted," says Spilsbury, and takes the case off him, which is what one colleague does for another off a boat, and his hand closes round the handle where Tar's hand has been, and it is still warm.

---

It is four hundred yards from the quay to Kirkegata, and Reggie couldn't have said what any of them looked like.

He knows they walk up a street with a slope to it. He knows there's a chandler's with coils of rope in the window and a stair beside it. He knows a large woman in a black cardigan comes out of a door at the bottom of the stair and says something in Norwegian, and Alec says something back in about four words of Norwegian that are plainly all he's got, and she looks Reggie up and down and says, "*Velkommen*," and goes back in.

The stair is narrow and steep and it smells of tar and coffee. Alec goes up first with the case. At the top there's a door, and Alec unlocks it, and holds it, and Reggie goes past him into a room with a stove and a desk and a great many books and a window full of grey sky and the tops of masts, and hears the door shut behind him, and the bolt.

He turns round.

"You burned my letters," he says.

He hadn't meant to say that first. He'd had a first thing ready since the Tyne. He's had a first thing ready for a year, and it wasn't that. It comes out anyway, in a voice he doesn't recognise, before he's got his coat off or his hat down or anything, and Alec, standing with his back to the door and the case still in his hand, puts the case down very slowly.

"Yes."

"*Ninety* letters. Every third Tuesday for four years. You didn't ask me. You told me the date."

"I told you the date because it was the only thing about it I could tell you that was not a sentence I could be hanged by."

"You could've told me before. You could've *asked* me."

"Yes."

"Don't *yes* me, Alec, it isn't a reading —"

And Alec crosses the room in two steps and takes hold of his face in both hands, cold, the fingers still cold from the quay, and kisses him.

---

It isn't gentle. Neither of them has any gentle in them. It's been a year, and they're both still in their overcoats, and Reggie gets both hands in the front of Alec's coat and holds on as if the floor might go. The fur hat goes somewhere and Reggie's hat goes somewhere and his glasses are knocked crooked, and he doesn't care. He's shaking. He can feel Alec shaking too, all down the length of him, the way a man shakes when he's been cold for a long time and has only just come in.

"That isn't an answer," Reggie says against his mouth.

"No."

"You don't get to do that instead of an answer."

"I am not doing it instead." Alec's voice has nothing level left in it at all. He has his forehead against Reggie's, and his glasses are pressed crooked into Reggie's cheekbone, and his breath is going in and out much too fast. "I am doing it *as well*. I have had a year of doing things instead. I have had a year of writing *Yours sincerely*."

"You *struck out a line*."

"I did."

"You don't strike out. You've never —"

"I did it three times," says Alec, "and then I sent it, because the alternative was to copy out the whole letter and pretend I had not written it, and I have *done* pretending, Reggie, I did it for a year, I am not doing it on a Monday in my own rooms —"

Reggie kisses him to stop him, which doesn't work, and then because he wants to, which does.

---

They get the coats off, eventually. It takes a long time. Alec's buttons are the Norwegian kind, big horn toggles on loops, and Reggie's fingers are too cold to manage them and he swears, and Alec laughs, a short cracked sound like something breaking under a boot. He does them himself one-handed, the other hand spread flat on the small of Reggie's back as if he's afraid of what will happen if he takes it away.

"You're thin," Reggie says.

"I have been told."

"By who?"

"By Fru Aasen, through Rasmussen, every week since January." Alec gets Reggie's jacket off his shoulders and down his arms and lets it drop, and puts his hands on the shoulders underneath, through the shirt, and holds them. "You are not in a position to comment. You have been living on a *gas ring*."

"I ate."

"You ate *bread*. I can feel your shoulder blades through a shirt and a vest."

"The Major took me to lunch on Thursdays."

"The Major," says Alec, with enormous contempt, and puts his mouth to the side of Reggie's neck, just under the ear, and holds it there, and doesn't do anything else, just breathes. Reggie shuts his eyes and feels every one of the breaths go in and out against his skin, and thinks, very distinctly, *I'm going to be angry with him for about the next ten years and I'm going to do all of it in this room.*

"The money," he says, with his eyes still shut.

Alec goes still against his neck.

"Six years. You let me think you'd said no to me."

"I did say no to you."

"And then you did it. And you let me think the man I — you let me think you were the kind of man who'd say *we do nothing that can be traced* and mean it." He opens his eyes. He's looking at the ceiling, which has a crack in the plaster the shape of a river. "I spent six years thinking that was the one thing about you I'd have to forgive. And it wasn't even *true*."

"No."

"That's worse, Alec. Do you understand that? That's *worse*."

"Yes," says Alec, into his neck. "I understood it in the hut. I have understood it every day for thirteen months." His hands tighten on Reggie's shoulders. "I am not going to apologise for it, because I would do it again, and you would know that I would, and an apology you know to be worthless is only a noise."

"Then what *are* you going to do?"

Alec lifts his head.

He looks terrible. He looks like a man who's been sleeping four hours a night since Christmas, with the skin under his eyes gone the colour of the sleet. And he looks at Reggie from about five inches away with the whole of his face doing what it does when nobody's watching, which is everything at once, and says:

"Let you be angry. Here. For as long as you like."

---

The bedroom is through a door off the study and it's barely wider than the bed, which is Norwegian, narrow and high, with a feather quilt on it like a snowdrift. There's a second window full of the same grey sky. Reggie sits down on the edge of the bed to get his shoes off and finds he can't bend far enough, because Alec is kneeling on the floor in front of him and has got there first, with the laces.

"You don't have to —"

"I am aware that I do not have to."

He gets one shoe off, and the sock, and holds Reggie's bare foot in both his hands for a moment, just holds it, the way he'd hold an animal's paw to look at the pads. Reggie has to look away.

"You resigned," Alec says, to the foot.

"Here it comes."

"You resigned the best bench in the Western Atlantic and went to *Great Ormond Street* —"

"It's a perfectly good street —"

"— and wrote fourteen thousand words for a *Major*, for *nothing*, and I have a harbour building and a tank room and two seals and Rasmussen, and a salary, and a *bench*." He gets the other shoe off with considerable violence. "Do you have any idea what it is to go down to a tank room every morning of your life and know that the man who ought to be in it is on a gas ring in Holborn because of you?"

"It isn't because of you."

"I was *there*, Reggie."

"It's because of Frank and it's because of me, and I *told* you on the rock —"

"You told me on the rock and I did not accept it and I said I should not accept it for a very considerable time, and it has been a very considerable time and I *still do not accept it*." He is on his knees on a rag rug in his shirtsleeves and he is shouting in a whisper, which Reggie has never heard him do and would not have believed possible. "And I will tell you something else, since we are doing this, since you have come three days across the North Sea to have it out with me. You were *right*. On the rock. About the Labs, and the bill, and the kinder man signing it. You were entirely right and I knew it at the time, and I have had to be right with you about it for a *year*, alone, in the dark, at three in the afternoon, and I *hate* it."

Reggie stares at him.

And then, completely without warning, he starts to laugh.

He can't help it. It comes up out of him from somewhere under the ribs and he puts his hand over his mouth and it goes on coming, and Alec, kneeling on the rug with a shoe in his hand, looks up at him with an expression of such outrage that it makes it worse.

"*What.*"

"You're *whispering*." He can hardly get it out. "You're furious and you're *whispering*, because of Fru Aasen —"

"She is directly underneath."

"She doesn't speak *English* —"

"She speaks *tone*," says Alec, with dignity. "Everybody speaks tone."

And Reggie slides off the edge of the high bed onto the rug, onto his knees, into him, and gets both arms round his neck and holds on and laughs into his shoulder until it turns into something else, and he feels Alec's arms come up round him hard, and a hand at the back of his head, holding it.

---

They don't talk about the rest of it in any order.

It comes up the way things come up, in pieces. Alec gets Reggie's shirt off over his head without undoing all the buttons and says, into his hair, "You wrote *faithfully*," and Reggie says, "I know I did," and Alec says, "To a named correspondent," and Reggie says, "*I know*," and Alec kisses his collarbone and says, "I read it nine times, I had to go and stand on the breakwater," and Reggie doesn't say anything at all.

Alec's hands are cold and then they're not. They go down Reggie's back one vertebra at a time, the way he'd count them on a specimen, and stop at the small of it and spread there and pull him in. And Reggie gets the buttons of Alec's waistcoat and his shirt and gets his own hands in underneath, on his skin, on the ribs, and they're *there*. They're further out than they were, sharper, the whole cage of him thinner under his hands. He finds himself counting them without meaning to, the way he did in the hut, the way that sent him to sleep there, and he gets to the bottom and has to stop and put his face against Alec's chest with his eyes shut.

"What."

"You're thinner," Reggie says, into his shirt. "I can feel it. Every one."

"You said that."

"I'm saying it *again*." He can hear the heart, under his ear. It's going far too fast. "What were you *doing*?"

"Counting," says Alec. "I told you. In a line I struck out."

They get onto the bed eventually. It's much too narrow and the quilt tries to eat them both. Somewhere in the getting there, Reggie's trousers go and Alec's go, and Alec takes his glasses off and puts them on the sill with enormous care and then knocks them off with his elbow and doesn't pick them up. And then there is the whole length of him, all at once, cold at the edges and warm through the middle. And Reggie has not had this for a year, not once, not anything like it: the weight of him, his hip against Reggie's hip, the long thigh, the rough of the jaw that hasn't been shaved since six. He makes a sound he didn't approve first. He doesn't care about that either.

"You were going to spend him," he says, with his mouth against Alec's shoulder. He doesn't know why it's now. It's now. "Frank. If I hadn't said. You'd done the sum."

"Yes."

"*The cost is not mine.* You said that. You said it out loud, at the table."

"I said it and I heard it," Alec says. His hand is moving on Reggie's back, slow, up and down the spine, and it doesn't stop. "I have heard it every night since. In that voice. I should like it on record that it was the worst thing I have ever heard a man say, and that the man was me."

"Did you write to him?"

The hand stops.

"To Frank. After. Did you write?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"From Brown's. In April." A pause. "It was four lines."

"*Four lines*, Alec —"

"It took a fortnight." Alec's voice is very low. "I wrote it thirty-one times. Every version had a sentence in it that asked him for something, and I could not find a way to thank a man that did not ask him for something, because I have had no practice at it. So in the end I wrote that I was in his debt, and that I had no means of discharging it that he would accept, and that I knew this. And I signed it with my name."

Reggie lifts his head.

"What did he say?"

"He sent a postcard," says Alec. "In May. Of the pier. On the back, in capitals, it said *Supper. F. N.* That was the whole of it."

Reggie puts his face down in Alec's neck and doesn't come up for a while.

---

He is angry the whole way through, and so is Alec, and it doesn't stop anything. That's the thing he'd got wrong. He'd thought it would have to be one or the other. That they'd have to have the row first, all of it, every item, and then when it was done there'd be room for this. But there isn't going to be a *done*. He's going to be angry about the letters for years, and Alec's going to be angry about the bench for longer. And here they are anyway, in a bed too narrow for either of them, in the middle of the morning, with the sleet on the window, and it all goes on at once.

Alec says his name, not the short one, the whole of it, against his mouth, as if he's checking that it's still there. Reggie says *yes* and doesn't know what he's saying yes to.

Alec's mouth is on his throat and his shoulder and the hollow inside his hip, where it used to go, and it's the same and it isn't. It's slower than it was in the hut and less careful. There's nothing measured in it, nothing Alec is taking away to keep. And Reggie gets his hands into Alec's hair and holds on and says, "Don't you *dare* leave me again for a year," which is unfair, since it was his own resignation that did it, and Alec says, "I shall not," and then, "you left *me*, I was on the *boat*," and Reggie laughs so hard he can't breathe and then he really can't, for quite a different reason.

At the end, Alec has his face pressed into the side of Reggie's neck and his whole weight on him, and he's saying something over and over, very quietly, that Reggie can't catch at first. And then he can, and it's *here*, just that, *here, here*. As though he's been writing it down in a book he hasn't got for a year and has only just found somewhere to put it.

---

They lie in the narrow bed afterwards with the quilt over them both and the stove ticking in the other room and the light, which in Ålesund at the end of March doesn't go until eight, turning very slowly from pewter to something almost gold on the plaster.

Reggie has his hand on Alec's ribs, in the place where it goes.

"A hundred and twenty," he says, after a while.

"You cannot possibly."

"I can. It's coming down." His thumb moves. "I'm still angry about the letters."

"I know."

"I'm going to be angry about the letters for years."

"I should think considerably longer," says Alec. "I am still angry about the bench."

"I know."

"I intend to be angry about it every morning at breakfast."

"Good," says Reggie. "I'll be down in the tank room at nine being charming to Rasmussen about his seals, and you can come and be angry at me in front of him."

"His English does not extend much beyond herring."

"He speaks *tone*," says Reggie, and feels the man laugh, under his hand, all through the ribs, silently, so that Fru Aasen won't hear.

---

At about half past eleven, with Tar asleep, Spilsbury gets up.

He puts on his dressing gown and goes through to the study, where the stove has gone down to a red eye, and puts a log on it. He takes the notebook out of the desk drawer where it has been since the fourteenth of April 1957. Green cloth, from a stationer's in Holborn, the corners still sharp.

He sits at the desk and opens it and uncaps his pen.

He looks at the first page for some time. It is very white. He has kept a notebook since he was nineteen, and every one of them since 1945 was a record of this man: the pulses, the ribs, the nights, the passage at four degrees, the Southport money, the date on the jetty. Every one of them could have hanged him, and he burned them all.

He writes the date.

*31.iii.58.*

Then, under it, after a moment:

*Wind SW, sleet clearing. Harbour seals both on the slip at 1600 (Rasmussen, by telephone). Weights not taken.*

He reads it over. It is correct. There is nothing else in it. It is the first page he has written in thirty years that anyone on earth could read, and it is complete.

He does not write anything under it. He does not write *fifth time*, or *accepted*, or *declined*, or any sum at all, because there has been no sum. There is nothing on this page that he has had to work out alone at a desk at two in the morning and then carry. It is all in the next room, asleep, with its glasses on the floor.

He caps the pen.

When he turns round Tar is standing in the bedroom doorway in the quilt, like something that has come in out of a snowdrift, with his glasses on crooked and his hair on end. He has plainly been standing there for some time.

"You've got a book," Tar says.

"I have had it since April."

"What've you written?"

Spilsbury holds it out.

Tar comes across the cold boards in his bare feet with the quilt dragging and takes it, and reads it, the whole of it, which takes about four seconds. And then he reads it again, which takes rather longer.

"There's nothing in it," he says. His voice is very odd.

"There is the weather. And the seals."

"There's nothing about —"

"No."

Tar looks down at the page for a long moment in the red light from the stove. Spilsbury watches him understand it. It goes through the whole of him, the way things do, the brow first and then everywhere at once.

"You don't need to write it down," Tar says.

"I find," says Spilsbury, "that I do not."

Tar shuts the book very carefully and puts it on the desk, square to the edge, the way Alec would have done it. Then he opens the quilt, and Spilsbury gets up out of the chair and goes into it, and it closes round them both.
