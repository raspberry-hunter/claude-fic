# Without Prejudice

The Thursday boat is late, as it is every February, and Tar goes back across on it at a quarter past five with Hester's licence papers for Mr Purdie in a satchel under his arm, standing in the stern with his hat raised, and will not be home until Saturday.

The post is on the rack by the office door by half past.

There are four for Spilsbury. Three are what he has been getting since November: a journal regretting that it will not renew a subscription, a circular, and a note from the bank at the Bay about the Institute's box. The fourth is air mail from London, a long envelope of good paper with a firm's name printed small on the flap. He knows its weight in his hand before he has turned it over, because he has spent twenty years reading letters from men who would rather not have had to write them.

*Harcourt Tate & Co., Bedford Row.*

He reads it standing at the rack.

> *WITHOUT PREJUDICE*
>
> *Dear Sir,*
>
> *Re: Dewar & Lecky Limited — the animal designated 197*
>
> *1. We act for Dewar & Lecky Limited, with whose previous correspondence you will be familiar.*
>
> *2. Our client is advised that the carcass, tissues and records of the animal designated 197 in its accession register remain its property, having been removed from its premises without its consent on or about the 11th February last. We are instructed to require delivery up of the whole of that material, together with your written undertaking that no part of it, nor of any record derived from it, will be published, exhibited, or deposited with any person.*
>
> *3. In the course of enquiries made on our client's behalf in connection with the recovery of its property, certain information has come to our client's attention concerning the domestic arrangements of yourself and of a senior member of the scientific staff of the Institute at which you are at present residing. The information derives from a person employed there in a domestic capacity.*
>
> *4. Our client has no wish to concern itself with matters of that kind. It is conscious, however, that the Institute's licence falls to be considered in March, and it would regard itself as under a duty, should the occasion arise, to place before the officer charged with that inspection, and before such other authorities as may be appropriate, any information in its possession bearing upon the fitness of persons proposing to hold animals under licence.*
>
> *5. Our client's enquiries in England are continuing.*
>
> *6. We should be glad to hear from you on or before the 11th March, after which our client will consider itself free to act as it thinks fit.*
>
> *Yours faithfully,*

He reads it a second time, which is his practice with anything that has been drafted by a professional, because the first reading is for what it says and the second is for how it has been built.

It is very well built. He would like that noted somewhere. Paragraph two asks for a thing; paragraph four describes a duty; and nothing whatever joins them except that one comes after the other on the same sheet. There is no demand in it that could be read out in a court. There is only an arrangement of paragraphs, set down by a man in Bedford Row who knows exactly what the space between two and four is for, and has left it empty so that the reader will furnish it himself.

He furnishes it. He stands at the rack in the last of the light with the circular in his other hand and does it thoroughly, in order, the way he does a bench.

*A person employed in a domestic capacity.* That is Mrs Greaves, who comes over from the Bay on Mondays and does the linen for the row. She has done it since before Pobil came, and she takes the sheets off two beds in the house and three in the huts. Since the thirtieth of May the hut the Institute offered him has had its bed made up and not slept in, because Pobil carried his cases past it without slowing and set them down at the foot of the bed in the last hut in the row, and nobody has moved them. That is two hundred and fifty-odd nights. A man with a card from Nassau asked a washerwoman which beds were slept in, and she told him, because it was true and she had no reason in the world to think it mattered.

That is paragraph three, and it is not much. It is sheets. By itself it would not hang a cat.

Paragraph five is the one that matters.

*Our client's enquiries in England are continuing.* He runs it forward. A competent man, paid by the week, with six weeks in hand. He goes to the Labs first, because that is where a man goes who is asking about Dr Spilsbury, and at the Labs he finds a senior technician called Hollis, pensioned now and living, Spilsbury believes, in Woking, who shut a connecting door on the second floor in the winter of 1950 because of the draught and who has a memory for doors. He finds the list the Ministry's men carried in October of that year, of every man who had worked with Frank Nicholson in the previous two years, with two names on it from the second floor side by side. He finds a landlady in the Fulham Road. And at the end of each of those, if he is any good, he finds the same second man.

What he does not find, because he cannot, is paper. The letters are in a deposit box in a bank in the Strand, and the bank will open it for one man only. The nine notebooks are in the same box.

The other two are in his case, fifty yards up the row.

He puts the letter in his inside pocket. He puts the circular in the wastepaper basket, because that is where it goes. He notes, going up the path in the dusk with the frogs starting in the cistern, that his hands are perfectly steady and that his mind is quite calm, and that the calm is a thing he is watching, as he would watch any reading that came out too clean.

---

The incinerator is behind the sick bay, a squat brick thing with an iron door and a flue, and it exists for what is left after Pobil has done a post-mortem. It is not used often. It is not supposed to be used at two in the morning.

He takes the notebooks out of the case at ten, by the lamp, and does not open them.

One runs from February to December of last year: the boarding house, the pound, the wall, the fifty-one days, the crossing, the thirtieth of May, and every night since in this hut. The daily record of the animal is not in it. That was kept on separate sheets, which are in the Institute's box at the bank at the Bay, and have been photographed twice, and are the thing Bedford Row wants. What this book holds is everything else. The other runs from the first of January to this afternoon.

Two books, green cloth, the corners gone soft. Between them they hold the initials *R. T.* several hundred times. He has never counted. He could, and he does not.

He carries them down at two with the hurricane lamp turned very low. He opens the iron door on the dark, lays paper and a little kindling in the grate, and gets it going with three matches, because the air is damp. When it is drawing properly he tears the covers off the first book, because boards burn badly, and feeds the quires in one at a time and waits for each to go before he puts in the next.

He does not read them. He has decided that at the table in the hut, and it holds. A man who reads what he is burning is not burning it. He is making one last copy in the only place nobody can subpoena, and that is a sentimental economy and he declines it.

The pages catch at the edges first. The ink goes brown before the paper does, so that for a second or two each sheet shows him its own handwriting in a colour it has never been, and then it curls and goes. Once a quire falls open against the bars before it has caught, and the lamp is on it, and he sees a date in his own hand in the top corner, *4.xi.56*, and nothing else, because he takes the rake and turns it face down into the fire.

It takes fifty minutes. At the end he rakes the grate through until there is nothing left bigger than a thumbnail, and shuts the iron door, and stands with his back to the warm brick and the lamp at his feet, looking at the sea, which is doing nothing in particular under a quarter moon.

He has kept a notebook since he was nineteen.

It occurs to him, with the precision of a man noticing that a bench has been left without a clamp, that there is an entry he would make tonight if there were a book to make it in. It is the fourth of its kind. He knows the form of it exactly.

He goes up the row, gets into a bed that smells of Tar's soap and his own tobacco, and does not sleep, which he had budgeted for.

---

Pobil comes up to the long room at about eight on the Saturday evening with his sleeves down.

Spilsbury has been expecting him since Friday morning, when the man went past the sick bay door at seven, stopped dead, went round the back of the building, and came out again with a face on him like weather.

"A man went to Alleyne's clerk in September," Pobil says. "And again last month. Card from an insurance agency in Nassau. Asked nine or ten questions."

"About the boats, the cold store, and who signs for the fish," says Spilsbury. "And whether the doctors live in."

Pobil stops with his hand on the back of a chair.

"How d'you know that?"

"Because he got an answer to the last one." Spilsbury takes the envelope out of his inside pocket and lays it on the bench, face down, with his hand flat on it. "This came on Thursday."

"From?"

"From Dewar & Lecky's solicitors, by way of the answer."

Pobil does not ask to see it. He looks at the hand on the envelope for a long moment and then at Spilsbury's face, and whatever he reads there, he reads quickly.

"Who'd he get it from?"

"I do not know," says Spilsbury, "and I do not propose to find out."

"I'll tell you who," says Pobil. "Iris Greaves. Who's done the row since before I came here, and has six grandchildren, and got asked a question about her own work by a man in a good hat who'd have given her a shilling for it."

The heat comes up in him all at once, the way it does, into the voice and the face together.

"And if anybody on this station goes near her about it — if anybody so much as looks at her sideways on a Monday — I'll —"

"Nobody is going near her."

"I'm telling you."

"And I am agreeing with you." Spilsbury takes his hand off the envelope. "She told the truth to a man who asked her for it. There is nothing to be done about that and nothing that should be."

Pobil breathes out through his nose, and pulls himself back from wherever he had gone, and sits down on the chair the wrong way round, with his arms along the top of it.

"Does he know?"

"No."

"You've had it since Thursday."

"He has been at the Bay since Thursday."

"You've had it since Thursday," Pobil says again, not raising his voice at all, "and somebody fired the incinerator on Thursday night, and I've had nothing in there since the first of the month. The ash was warm at seven."

"Yes."

"What was it?"

Spilsbury looks at him.

It is the first question the man has asked him in four years. He has not asked about the letters on the fixative paper, or about the thirtieth of May, or about Christmas, and he is asking this. Spilsbury is aware of the distinction, and that he is being done the courtesy of having it drawn.

"Two notebooks," he says.

Pobil's face does everything at once. It is a very expressive face and it hides nothing it has been given, and for about three seconds Spilsbury watches it take in what the notebooks were, and what was in them, and what kind of man keeps a book for thirty years and then burns two of them without telling the other man whose name is on every page.

"*Ay*, hombre," Pobil says, very quietly.

"It had to be done before anyone came to ask for them."

"I'm sure it did." Pobil gets up and puts the chair back where it was, square to the bench, which is not how he usually leaves a chair. "His boat's in at nine. You tell him tonight. Not in the morning, not after breakfast when you've got it all in order."

Spilsbury says nothing.

"Tonight, or I go down to that jetty and tell him myself. And I'll make a far worse job of it, and you'll have to listen to him about that as well."

"Tonight."

"Say it again so I know you heard it."

"Tonight," says Spilsbury. "I had not intended otherwise."

"You'd intended to have the whole thing worked out first."

That is true, and he does not answer it, and Pobil nods as though he had.

"He'll want the answer as well as the question," Spilsbury says. "He will want to do something. If I hand him the letter without the rest of it he will spend the night being clever."

"Then let him be clever," says Pobil. "He's good at it. It's what you like about him."

He goes to the door, and stops there with his hand on the frame.

"And *eat* something before he gets in. You look like the ash."

---

Reggie comes up the row at twenty to ten with the satchel over his shoulder and Purdie's receipt in it, and the first thing he notices is that Pascual's lamp is out.

Pascual doesn't put his lamp out before midnight. He reads in bed, detective stories, badly printed, with the pages falling out, and swears at them in two languages through the wall. The hut is dark and silent, and it's so plainly a man being tactful that Reggie stands on the path in the dark for a second with his hand on the strap and feels the back of his neck go cold.

The second thing he notices is that his own lamp is lit. It shouldn't be. Alec doesn't sit up; he's asleep by ten, flat on his back like a man laid out for inspection, and Reggie sleeps too these days, properly, the moment his head's down, and he's grown to rely on it.

Alec is at the table. He's in his shirt with his collar off and his glasses on, and there's an envelope squared to the edge of the table in front of him and a plate beside it with a piece of bread and cheese on it that somebody has plainly made him take and he hasn't touched.

"Purdie's signed," Reggie says from the door, because it's what he came up the path to say. "Both copies. He read the whole of it aloud to me, including the heading, and then asked me to read it to him."

"Good."

"What's happened?"

"Shut the door," says Alec, "and put the bolt across, and sit down."

---

He reads it at the table, with Alec across from him watching his face.

He's quick on the page, and he takes the whole thing in on one pass, and it lands in the wrong order, the way things do when they arrive fast. It's paragraph two that reaches him first. He is on his feet before he's got to the bottom of it.

"They want 197."

"Yes."

"They want the *record*. The blocks and the jars and fifty-one days of —" He has the sheet flat on the table under his hand and he can hear his own voice going up. "They can't have it. It isn't a carcass, Alec, it's the only quantitative account of that decline that exists anywhere, and it's in the draft, it's in figure one, it's —"

"Read the rest of it."

"I don't need to read the rest of it to know they can't —"

"Reggie."

He stops.

"Read," says Alec, "the rest of it."

He sits down and reads paragraphs three to six.

When he gets to the end he reads them again, because he has the kind of mind that rereads a thing it wishes it had misunderstood, and then he puts the sheet down on the table very carefully, face up, and takes his glasses off, and doesn't do anything with them.

"Mrs Greaves," he says.

"Yes."

"Sheets."

"Sheets. And my cases at the foot of your bed, where Pobil put them."

"That's nothing. That's a washerwoman and a bed. You can't —" He stops. "It's nothing on its own."

"It is nothing on its own," Alec agrees. "It is paragraph five that is something."

Reggie reads paragraph five for a third time and understands it this time, all of it, the way he understands a curve once someone has drawn the axes in: Hollis and the door, and the list the Ministry men had in the October, and the small committee room on the first floor, where he went in on the Thursday after Alec and answered questions about his associations for twenty minutes in a voice that liked everybody.

He has the whole of it in about ten seconds, and it's worse than anything he'd been thinking about on the path.

"They'll go to the Labs."

"They will have gone already. The letter was posted on the twenty-ninth."

"And find Hollis."

"And the list. And a landlady in the Fulham Road who will remember, if she is asked the right way, that the young gentleman from the Admiralty came to dinner a very great deal in 1947." Alec's voice doesn't change. "None of which is evidence. All of it is a direction, and a man paid by the week will follow a direction until it gives out."

"And the letters."

"Are in a box in the Strand, which will not open for anybody but me."

"And your notebooks. The nine."

"In the same box."

"Then there's nothing." He hears himself say it and hears how thin it is. "There's nothing on paper anywhere they can reach."

Alec looks at him across the table for a moment, and in that moment, before the man opens his mouth, Reggie knows there's something else. He knows it the way he'd know an animal had gone off its food by the set of it in the water, and he knows he's going to be told, because that is the one rule in this hut that has never been broken by either of them.

"There were two more," Alec says. "Last year and this. They were in my case."

"Were."

"I burned them on Thursday night. In the incinerator behind the sick bay, at about two in the morning."

---

For some while nobody says anything at all.

Reggie finds that he's looking at the case, at the foot of the bed, where it has been since May, with its lid down and its two brass catches done up. He has never opened it. It hadn't occurred to him that there was anything in it he'd want to see.

"You burned them."

"Yes."

"On Thursday. The night I left."

"Yes."

"Without saying anything."

"You were at the Bay."

"You could have written. You could have sent a note over with Alleyne's boy, you could have —" He hears it and stops, because of course the man couldn't, and they both know he couldn't, and that isn't what he means. "You've been doing a sum since Thursday, and you've done it all by yourself, and you were going to hand me the answer at the table like a — like a *result*."

"Pobil told me to tell you tonight, and I agreed with him, and I am telling you tonight."

"*Pascual* told you."

"Pobil asked me a question," says Alec, "for the first time in four years, and I answered it, and then he told me what to do. He was right on both counts."

"And if he hadn't?"

"I should have told you tomorrow, with the rest of it."

"With the rest of it worked out."

"Yes."

Reggie puts the glasses down on the table beside the letter.

"There's a sentence for that," he says. "You gave it me yourself. October 1950, in Alma Terrace. You said *we do nothing that can be traced*, and I said —"

"That it was the same sentence with more words in it," says Alec. "I remember."

"You burned the books and you said nothing that was untrue."

"Yes."

"*That's* the same sentence with more words in it."

Alec takes his own glasses off, and cleans them on his handkerchief, slowly, the whole of both lenses. It takes about fifteen seconds and Reggie has seen him do it perhaps twenty times in ten years, and never once because the glasses wanted cleaning.

"Yes," he says, when he's put them back on. "It is. I should like it recorded that I know that."

"Recorded *where*, Alec?"

And there it is. He watches it reach the man. It doesn't show on the face, which has one expression and uses it for everything, but it goes through the rest of him: the shoulders, the hand on the table, the very slight movement of the jaw. Reggie reads it the way he reads nobody else alive without being asked, and he wishes to God he hadn't said it, and he isn't sorry.

"There isn't anywhere now," he says, more quietly. "Is there. Thirty years, and you haven't got anywhere to put it."

"No."

"What were you going to write in it? On Thursday. You'd have written something."

Alec looks at him.

"I shall tell you," he says, "when I have told you the rest. It will not make sense in the other order."

---

"Go on, then."

"There is one line in England," says Alec, "that points at me and not at you."

"Frank."

"Frank."

Reggie sits back. The chair takes it badly; it's a station chair and it has been a station chair since the Colonel came out. "Frank points at both of us. We were both on the list. I went in after you, the Thursday. And I did the four days on Nineteen, and the whole of the second floor knew I'd talked him into it."

"The list is a list. A great many men were on it, and every one of them went home. What points at me and not at you is paper, and it has my name on it, and it has been going out every quarter since November 1950."

Reggie doesn't understand. He understands the words and not the sentence, which doesn't happen to him often, and he sits there with it for a moment, and then he does.

"You said *no*."

"I did say no."

"I asked you. In Alma Terrace, the week after, I said somebody should send him something and you said *no*, and you gave me the whole — the Division, and paper, and a line drawn to a convicted man in that month by the only instrument they had —"

"All of which was correct."

"And then you *sent it*."

"On the Thursday," says Alec. "I wrote to a man called Ashby, whom I taught in 1937, who has a firm in Southport that makes optical parts, and told him that a first-class draughtsman with an unfortunate history was likely to be at liberty. And I arranged a standing payment through my bank to a Southport address, from an account in my mother's maiden name. Fifteen pounds a quarter. Twenty-five payments. The twenty-sixth goes on the first of March."

Reggie can't say anything.

He sits in the lamplight with his hands on the table on either side of the letter and discovers that he can't say anything at all, because everything he could say has two halves and they point in opposite directions. The first thing, which arrives before he can stop it, is a sort of enormous rushing relief that goes through the whole of his chest and out of the top of his head: that it was done, that Frank had something, that in the worst month of that man's life somebody got him a bench and fifteen pounds a quarter and it was *Alec*.

The second thing is six years.

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because it was a line on paper," says Alec, "and the fewer people who knew where it ran, the better, and you were the one it most had to be kept away from."

"That isn't an answer. That's the reason you'd give a committee."

"It is the reason. There was another one."

"Then give me the other one."

"Because you would have been glad," says Alec. "And then you would have thought it made us square. And it did not, and I did not want you to think so."

Reggie looks at him.

It's a terrible answer. It's also the truest thing anybody has said to him in some while, and it goes in under the ribs and stays there, and he has absolutely no idea what to do with it, so he puts it aside for later. He learned that from the man across the table, and this is the first time he's been grateful for it.

"Right," he says. "Go on."

---

Alec goes on.

He does it in order, because he is who he is, and he does it without once raising his voice, and most of it, Reggie realises about halfway through, is not a plan. It's a description. It is what a competent man paid by the week is going to find anyway, with one thing altered.

The account in the false name is closed. The twenty-sixth payment, and every payment after it, goes from an account in Alec's own name, in his own hand, to the same Southport address. Monday he moves up the row to the spare hut with the wrong roof. And he answers Bedford Row himself, in writing, before the eleventh of March: he declines paragraph two, and says in terms that his private affairs are his own and touch no member of this Institute's staff.

"And then you wait," says Reggie.

"And then they go to the Labs, and find Nicholson, because they cannot help finding him. And from Nicholson they go to Southport, because a man who was ruined in 1950 is always worth a visit. And in Southport they find six years of money with my name at the bottom of it." He turns his hand over on the table, palm up. "They will stop looking for a second name. They will have one."

"Frank's."

"Frank's."

Reggie has his hands flat on the table now. He can see all of it, the way you can see a curve once someone's drawn the axes, and every line of it is correct, and it runs straight through a man's front room in Southport and out the other side.

"And the inspector," he says. "In March. If he asks me."

"He will ask you. You are the Director of Research and you brought me onto this island." Alec says it without any inflection at all. "You will not lie to him. You are poor at it with anybody who has read a file, and he will have read one. You will say the true thing."

"Which is?"

"That there had always been something about me."

It takes a second to arrive. Then it does, and Reggie is back in the canteen at the Labs, on the Monday, with the tea urn going, listening to two men who'd known Frank Nicholson for nine years agree that there had always been something.

"No," he says.

"It is true."

"I *know* it's true. That's why no."

---

"Frank," Reggie says.

"Frank has been convicted. He has been fined, and dismissed, and named in a newspaper, and his wife has gone to her mother's. There is nothing further they can do to him that has not already been done."

"They can call it blackmail." He's quick now, all of him, the way he is at a bench when the thing is moving, and he hates it and can't stop. "Six years of anonymous money from a false name to a man with a conviction. Do you know what that looks like to a policeman?"

"Yes. And I shall put it in writing, over my own name, that it was a gift."

"And then they'll go and see whether it was. They'll go to Ashby's. They'll go to his lodgings. There'll be a man in a good hat on the step asking the landlady what time he gets in at night."

"Very likely."

"He's got a *life* there, Alec. Whatever it is. He's got a bench and a room and a — for all we know he's got his paints out on the front at Southport on Sundays, and you want to send them round to him again."

"He will still have the bench and the room at the end of it."

"You don't know that."

"No," says Alec. "I do not know that."

He doesn't look away when he says it. That's the thing Reggie will remember afterwards. The man sits in lamplight at a station table with his collar off and his glasses on, and says the one thing in the whole account that is not a certainty, and doesn't look away from it once.

And Reggie is in the lift.

He's thirty, in October 1949, and the gates are going down past the first floor with a noise like a coal chute, and Frank is beside him with a rack of electrodes under his arm and a cardigan under his white coat, saying *mine watches the door, you know*. Reggie puts on the voice, the warm one, the one that likes you, and before the lift reaches the ground floor it has got him four days. And at the bottom Frank says *you're very good at that, Tar*, kindly, and goes off to do what he's been talked into doing.

He did that. It was the coat, and he did it, and a decent man said yes to him because decent men don't like being difficult twice. The next year the same man was on the list, and in the magistrates' court, and in the *Mercury* on page five.

Now there's a sum on the table, it's correct, and at the bottom of it is Frank Nicholson, paying.

Reggie puts both hands over his face for a moment, and takes them away.

"Not without asking him," he says.
