# Interest Accrued

The schoolmistress from the Bay brings nine children to see the animals on the second Thursday in October, and Pascual gets them, because he's the vet and it's a Thursday.

He likes children. He likes them the way he likes the calf, from a slight distance, when they're doing something interesting. He puts all nine of them along the wall of the north pen in a row with a bucket each, and tells them the rules, which are *don't lean* and *don't throw the bucket*, and one of them leans and one of them throws the bucket. The calf, who is seven and enormous and has seen everything, takes the fish and the bucket both and gives the bucket back. The children scream with joy.

The schoolmistress doesn't scream. She stands at the end of the row in a grey cotton dress with her hat in her hand, watching the calf give back the bucket. She says, in a low clear voice that carries without trying, "She's done that before."

"Every time," says Pascual.

"Do you teach her?"

"No. She teaches us. We're slow."

The schoolmistress laughs. It's a good laugh, surprised out of her, and she puts her hand to her mouth after it as if she's been caught. She's about forty. Her name's Mrs Bourne. She's a widow, he finds out later, of a ship's engineer who went down off Hispaniola in 1951, and she's run the school at the Bay since, and she corrects the Colonel's grammar in public. He finds all of that out over the next three weeks, because he finds that he's taking the Tuesday boat to the Bay rather more often than the Institute strictly needs him to.

---

He's fifty-one. He's been on this island since 1952.

He was in love with Reggie Tar for about four years, from the spring of 1953. It was never a comfortable thing. He spent most of it furious with the man, correcting him in the long room and shouting at him across the north pen. The charm had put his back up from the first afternoon, and he was never sure how much of the wanting was wanting to win. It stopped in the winter of 1956, while Spilsbury was still on the island. He had expected that to be the worst of it, and instead it was the end of it. He watched the two of them for nine months and saw what Reggie looked like when he was being given what he wanted. He saw it wasn't anything Pascual would have known how to give. Some time around Christmas he noticed that he'd stopped minding. It was like noticing a noise has stopped.

What was left after that he's never had a name for, and never needed one. He fills Reggie's lamp on Saturdays. He puts a plate in front of him at breakfast when he's come up from the pens without eating. He takes the pen out of his hand at midnight and says *no*, and Reggie goes to bed. He's walked round the business of 1957 for seven years without once being let into it. Reggie said *I can't tell you, not won't, can't*, and Pascual believed him, and doesn't ask.

There have been a couple of things at the Bay in those years, with women. They were pleasant and brief. There was a nurse at the General in 1959 who went home to Barbados. There was a widow with a guest house who wanted a husband with a shop, and was entirely right to. He's a vet on an island at the edge of the map. He likes his own company and his animals and his friend at the end of the row, and a good argument at dinner. He'd have said, if asked, that he'd done with that kind of thing, and was glad to have had it.

And then a schoolmistress laughs at a calf, with her hand over her mouth, and he takes the Tuesday boat.

---

Reggie meets her in November, on the verandah of the long room. Pascual brings her across on a Saturday with a basket of her own guavas as an excuse.

He's been dreading it. He tells himself that's silly. He dreads it anyway, because Reggie can be very clever at people. Reggie can be clever at you until you feel like a specimen with your insides pinned out on the board, and he can do it in four minutes without meaning anything by it. And Esme Bourne is proud and quick, and doesn't like being laughed at, and Pascual very much wants the afternoon to go well.

It goes well. It goes better than well.

Reggie's lovely to her. He's lovely in the ordinary way, the real way, not the other thing. Pascual can tell the difference across a room with his eyes shut. Reggie asks about the school and actually listens to the answer. He asks about the nine children and remembers which one threw the bucket, because Pascual told him. When Esme corrects his Latin, *it's the ablative, Dr Tar, not the dative*, he stares at her, and then at the Colonel's plaque on the verandah wall, which he's walked past every day since 1950. Then he says, "Good *God*, so it is," with such delight that she laughs out loud, and Pascual loses the thread of the conversation entirely for about a minute because he's looking at her laughing.

When he walks her down to the jetty at six, Reggie is at the rail of the verandah, looking pleased.

"She's far too good for you," Reggie says, when Pascual comes back up.

"I know."

"She corrected my Latin. On my own verandah. In front of the Colonel's plaque."

"I know. I was there."

"Nobody's corrected my Latin since school." Reggie's got his elbows on the rail and his face is lit up like a boy's. "I think I'm in love with her. You'll have to fight me for her."

"Get in line, *hombre*," says Pascual, and Reggie laughs, and it's all perfectly ordinary. It's the most ordinary afternoon Pascual's had in years. He goes to bed that night pleased with everything, including himself.

---

It's the ordinary thing all winter.

Reggie teases him about Esme, which he'd expected. It's the same teasing he's had since 1952, about his drawings and his Spanish and the calf. It's affectionate and relentless and exactly as funny as Reggie thinks it is, which is quite funny. When Pascual takes the Tuesday boat, Reggie says *give my regards to the ablative*. When Pascual comes back on the Thursday with his shirt ironed by somebody else, Reggie looks at the shirt and says nothing, very loudly. In February, when Pascual goes to the Bay for a whole Sunday and misses a feed, Reggie does the feed, and leaves a note on Pascual's bench in his small neat hand: *Calf ate 22 lb. Missed you. Bring back a guava.*

Pascual keeps the note. He isn't sure why. He puts it in the drawer with his pen drawings.

He's fond of Esme. He's more than fond, some weeks. She's the cleverest woman he's met since he came out, and she doesn't take any nonsense from him. She says things like *you're a very good man, Pascual, and you talk too much at dinner*. She walks with him on the church path at the Bay on Sunday evenings with her hand on his arm, and he likes the weight of it, and he doesn't know where it's going, and doesn't mind not knowing.

In May she asks him to Sunday dinner with her mother.

That's a different thing. He knows that, and she knows he knows. Mrs Bourne's mother is ninety and was born in the Bay and remembers the old sugar wharf being built, and she doesn't have people to Sunday dinner. It's an examination. Esme says it lightly, on the church path, looking at the sea and not at him. He says *yes, I'd like that*, and means it, and goes back to the cay on the evening boat with his heart going like a boy's.

He tells Reggie at dinner.

He hadn't planned to. It's the long table on a Tuesday, the students at one end and the Colonel at the other, and he's pleased with himself. Reggie asks what he's grinning at, and he says, "I'm going to Sunday dinner at the Bournes'. With her mother."

And he watches the lamp come on.

---

He's seen it perhaps fifty times since 1957. He'd know it anywhere. He'd know it from the end of a long table in a bad light, and he'd know it in his sleep, and he isn't asleep.

Reggie's shoulders come down. They come down perhaps half an inch, the way they come down for one name only, and have come down for that one name only since the spring of 1957. His face goes open. His voice goes warm and easy and delightful, and there's a lamp behind it, turned right up.

"The *Bournes'*!" says Reggie. "With old Mrs Bourne? Good God, Pascual, you're going to be examined. Did you know she once had the Governor thrown out of the church at the Bay for talking during the Nunc Dimittis? Hester told me. In 1931. The *Governor*. Hester, it was 1931, wasn't it? The Governor and the Nunc Dimittis?"

Hester, at the end of the table, puts her fork down.

"It was 1929," says Hester.

"1929! Even better. Now I want you to promise me you'll say nothing at all during grace, Pascual, not one word, because —"

And off it goes. It's a lovely story. It's a very funny story, about the Governor and his hat and the Nunc Dimittis, and it goes on to the Colonel's father and a goat, and then a bishop. The students laugh. The Colonel laughs until he has to wipe his eyes. Hester doesn't laugh. Hester sits at the end of the table looking at Reggie over her glasses with no expression at all.

Pascual doesn't laugh either. He sits with his fork in his hand.

He looks at the clock on the long room wall, over the door. It's a quarter past eight when the lamp comes on. He watches the minute hand. He watches Reggie tell the story of the Governor and the Colonel's father and the bishop and the goat. He watches his hands, which have gone flat on the tablecloth, and his breathing, which is a little high in the chest. And at twenty-five past, near enough, he watches it go. It lets go of him all at once, the way it always does. Reggie looks down at his plate as though he's surprised to find it there.

Ten minutes.

It's always ten minutes. For seven years it's been ten minutes, for one name. Pascual has watched it at this table with visitors, and on the verandah with the Colonel, and once with a man from the Admiralty. It's the only thing on earth that makes Reggie Tar do that. It's the thing he does to keep something from being seen that mustn't be seen. It's the only thing he does that Pascual has ever been afraid of.

And he's just done it for Esme Bourne's mother.

No. He's just done it for Pascual going to Sunday dinner.

Reggie looks up from his plate. He looks down the table, and finds Pascual. And he smiles at him. It's the plain smile, the real one, the one that means nothing at all except that Pascual is there. And he says, quite ordinarily, "You'll be fine. She'll love you. Everybody does," and goes back to his fish.

Pascual sits with his fork in his hand and looks at him.

*Ay, no*, he thinks, very clearly. *No. No, no, no.*

---

He doesn't sleep. He lies in his hut with his hands behind his head and the shutters open, and ten yards away through the wall the lamp at the end of the row is burning, the way it's burnt every night since the spring of 1957.

He tries every other explanation. He's a scientist. He does it properly.

It could be the Governor story. Reggie tells stories. Reggie tells stories all the time, it's half of why anybody comes to dinner. Except that Pascual has heard Reggie tell a thousand stories. He knows what the ordinary kind looks like, and it isn't that. The ordinary kind hasn't got a lamp behind it.

It could be that Reggie doesn't like Esme. Except that he does. Pascual watched him like her on the verandah in November, the real way, and has watched him tease about her all winter. He'd stake his life that Reggie likes her. Reggie likes clever women. Reggie's closest friend on earth, other than Pascual, is Jean Leslie.

It could be that Reggie's afraid of being left. The best friend going off and getting married, the evenings on the step gone. That's a thing people feel. It's a sad, ordinary, decent thing, and it'd make sense of the ten minutes. Pascual lies there and tries very hard to believe that it's that.

He can't. He knows what it's for. He's known what it's for since the spring of 1957. It isn't for being lonely. Reggie's been lonely for eight years with the lamp lit, and it's never once come on for that. It comes on for one thing. It comes on when there's something in Reggie Tar that mustn't be seen, by anybody, and least of all by Reggie.

And Reggie didn't know he'd done it. That's the part Pascual can't get past. With the other name he always knows. He knows it's coming and can't stop it, and afterwards he looks as though it's cost him a pint of blood. Tonight he looked up from his plate and smiled, pleased and easy, and said *everybody does*. He'd no more idea what had just gone across his face than the calf has.

Pascual turns over and looks at the wall.

He has not asked for this. He'd like that on the record. He got over this man at Christmas 1956, properly, all the way down. He knows it was properly, because he's checked, twice, most carefully, the way you'd check a set of figures you'd had to redo. He has been fond of him since, in the plain nameless way, for eight years, and it's been the best thing in his life. And he has a schoolmistress at the Bay with a hand on his arm on the church path and a mother who had the Governor thrown out in 1929. He's been going to Sunday dinner. He's been happy.

And now Reggie Tar, who reads every human being on the island in four minutes flat and has never once been pointed at the man in the next hut, has gone and done *this*. Without even the courtesy of noticing.

"*Hombre*," Pascual says to the wall, quite loudly, "you absolute —"

The wall doesn't say anything. Ten yards away the lamp burns on.

---

He goes to Sunday dinner. Old Mrs Bourne examines him for two hours, on his Spanish, his church, his intentions, and his views on the Nunc Dimittis, and appears to pass him. Esme walks him down to the jetty after with her hand on his arm, and it's lovely, and he's miserable the whole way.

Then he watches. He can't help it. He's a scientist, and it's there, and once you've seen a thing you can't unsee it. He doesn't go looking. He just stops not looking.

He finds that it's everywhere.

It's in the way Reggie's on the step when Pascual comes back on the late boat from the Bay. He's been there, Pascual realises, most Sunday nights since about January. Reggie's sitting on the step of the hut at the end of the row in his shirtsleeves with a book he isn't reading, and he says *oh, you're back*, as though he were surprised, and goes to bed.

It's the way Reggie knows what he's eaten. Pascual comes in to breakfast, and Reggie, without looking up from the weight sheets, pushes the marmalade across the table, because Pascual didn't have any yesterday. Reggie Tar has never once in thirteen years known what he himself ate yesterday.

It's the hand. It's the hand on the back of Pascual's neck at the north pen rail, for a moment, when something's gone right. It's been there since 1953. Pascual has always taken it for what it is from anybody else, which is *well done*. He watches it now, and it's not *well done*. It stays a moment too long, and goes away as though it didn't know it had been there.

And it's Esme. It's that Reggie has stopped making jokes about Esme. He stopped in May. Pascual goes back through it in his head and finds the day: the Tuesday of the Governor. After the Governor he never made another one. *Give my regards to the ablative* was the last. Reggie asks about everything. Reggie asks what's in the post and who's on the boat and what the Colonel thinks about the war in Aden. And since May he hasn't asked a single thing about Esme Bourne. When Pascual mentions her, Reggie is very kind and says *oh, good*, and changes the subject to the calf. Not the lamp, not that again. Just a door, shut, very politely.

Reggie can read anybody. Pascual's watched him do it for thirteen years, at dinners, with inspectors, with frightened students and with the Colonel. He reads them in four minutes if he's pointed at them. He's never once been pointed at Pascual. Why would he be? Pascual's the man in the next hut. He's the furniture, the wall, the plate on the breakfast table. You don't read the wall.

---

"Hester," says Pascual in the office, in July, with the veterinary schedule in his hand, "I've got something to ask you, and you don't have to answer it if you don't want to."

Hester Bevan looks at him over her glasses.

She's been the Institute's secretary since before he came. She types every word that leaves the island, and she addressed every one of Reggie's letters to Norway, in the form, twice a year since 1957. Pascual is fairly sure she's the most frightening person he knows. He'd trust her with his life.

"Sit down, Dr Pobil," says Hester.

He sits down.

"You've noticed," says Hester.

He stares at her.

"Noticed what? What've I noticed?"

"Whatever it is you've come in here about with the veterinary schedule, which you have not needed my help with since 1954." She puts her pencil down and folds her hands on the pad. "Dr Tar. At dinner. With the Governor and the Nunc Dimittis."

"*Hester*."

"It was 1929," says Hester. "I didn't say anything, because it wasn't my business. I'm saying something now, because you've come into my office with a face like a wet week and it's upsetting the typewriter."

Pascual puts the schedule down on the desk very carefully.

"How long have you —"

"Since about Easter." Hester considers. "Possibly Christmas. He did the Sunday feed for you in February and was insufferable about it for a week. I have never known him do a feed for anybody without saying so at least four times."

"He left a note," says Pascual weakly.

"Yes, I saw it. It was on your bench for a day and a half." Hester looks at him. "Does he know?"

"No." It comes out of him with enormous feeling. "*No*. He's got no idea. None. He did the — the thing, Hester, the thing he does, for the other one, in front of the whole table, and then he looked up and smiled at me as if he'd been talking about the weather."

"Yes," says Hester. "I saw that too."

"He reads *everybody*."

"Dr Tar," says Hester, "is extremely slow about anything that is not a fish. I have always thought so. I have never once said so, because he is the Director of Research and it would have been impertinent." She picks up her pencil again. "What are you going to do?"

"I don't know."

"You're walking out with Mrs Bourne."

"I know I am."

"She's a very sensible woman. She'd make you a very good wife." Hester looks at him over her glasses. "I say that without prejudice to anything else."

He sits in her office with his hands between his knees. He can hear the typewriter ticking as it cools, and the calf blowing in the north pen through the open window, and Reggie's voice in the long room, telling a student his labels are a disgrace.

"He's still in love with the other one," Pascual says. It's the first time he's ever said it aloud to anybody. "The one in Norway. I've watched it for eight years. It hasn't stopped."

"No," says Hester. "I don't suppose it has."

"So what would I even be? If I —" He stops. "I'm not going to be the thing he has instead. I'd rather be shot. I'd rather go to Kingston."

Hester is quiet for a moment.

"I've addressed sixteen envelopes to Ålesund," she says. "In the form. And I've watched him read what comes back, sixteen times, standing at that window, with his face doing nothing. I have never once in eight years seen him look at one of them the way he looked at you over his fish on that Tuesday." She uncaps her pen. "I don't know what that makes you, Dr Pobil. I'm a secretary. But I don't think it's *instead*."

---

Esme tells him about Kingston in August, on the church path.

It's a headmistress's post at a girls' school in St Andrew, with a house, and she's been offered it and wants it. She tells him the whole of it plainly, walking, with her hand on his arm, looking at the sea. And then she stops on the path, under the tamarind by the churchyard wall, and turns round and looks at him.

"I'd like you to come," she says. "I'd like that very much. I'm not asking you to decide tonight. But I'd like to know if you'll think about it, properly, and not just be kind to me."

He opens his mouth.

"You've been thinking about something since May," says Esme. "Don't tell me you haven't, you've been like a man with a stone in his shoe. I don't know what it is and I don't need to. I just want to know whether it's something that stops you coming to Kingston."

And Pascual stands on the church path at the Bay with the sea going silver below, and a good woman's hand on his arm, and thinks about Kingston.

It's a big town. There's a university hospital. There's a veterinary department that would have him tomorrow. It's a house with a verandah, and Sunday dinner, and a woman who corrects the Governor's Latin and would correct his, and nobody on a step at night pretending to read. It's a good life, and entirely possible, and he could walk into it in September.

And he thinks about the cay. He thinks about the calf, and the north pen, and Reggie at midnight with a pen in his hand that somebody has to take out of it. He thinks about the lamp that somebody has to fill on Saturdays. And the man who'd be on the step in his shirtsleeves on the first Sunday night in September, waiting for the late boat. It'd come in, and there'd be nobody on it. He'd sit there for a while. Then he'd go to bed with the lamp lit and not know why he'd been sitting there.

It isn't the calf. He'd like it to be the calf. He's an honest man, and it isn't.

"It's something that stops me," he says.

Esme looks at him for a long moment.

"Yes," she says. "I thought it might be." She doesn't take her hand off his arm. "Is it the island?"

"Some of it's the island."

"And the rest of it?"

He doesn't answer. He can't. He finds that his face is doing something, the way it always does, the way it's always done, and he can't stop it. And Esme Bourne, who has run a school for fourteen years and can read nine children at once from the end of a wall, looks at it for a moment longer, and then puts her other hand up and pats his cheek, briskly, twice, the way you'd pat a boy who's done his best.

"Well," she says. "I hope they've got the sense to know it."

"They haven't."

"No," says Esme. "They never do." She takes her hand off his arm, and puts it through again, more firmly, and turns them both back down the path towards the jetty. "Come on. You'll miss the boat. I shall write to you from Kingston, and you'll write back, and you'll tell me how the calf is. And one day you'll tell me how the rest of it went."

---

She goes on the first of September. He sees her off on the Kingston boat, with her mother, who shakes his hand like a man and tells him he was a great deal better than she'd expected. He stands on the quay at the Bay till the boat's round the point.

He takes the late boat back. It's gone half past ten when Alleyne's boy puts him off at the cay jetty. He comes up the path in the dark with his hands in his pockets, and there's the row, dark, all of it, the students' and Hester's and the Colonel's. There's his own. And at the far end, by itself, the lamp.

And Reggie on the step, in his shirtsleeves, with a book.

"Oh," says Reggie. "You're back."

"I'm back."

Pascual sits down on the step beside him. He doesn't mean to. He's very tired, and the step's there. Reggie shifts over a few inches to make room without looking up from the book, which is upside down.

"She's gone, then," says Reggie. "Mrs Bourne. Hester said. To Kingston."

"She's gone."

"I'm sorry, Pascual." And he is. Pascual can hear that he is. His voice is quite plain, with no lamp in it, full of real sorrow for his friend. "I'm truly sorry. I liked her very much. I thought you'd — I thought perhaps you'd go with her."

"She asked me. She'd have had me, too."

Reggie goes very still on the step.

"Did she?"

"Yes."

"And you —" Reggie shuts the book. He looks at it, and turns it the right way up, and puts it on his knee. "You didn't want to go?"

"No," says Pascual. "I didn't want to go. I'd've been no good in Kingston."

He watches Reggie take that in. He watches it go all the way through him, and he knows exactly what it does, because he's been watching that face since 1952. The shoulders come down, not half an inch, not the lamp. That's a different thing, a thing Pascual has seen perhaps twice in thirteen years. It's the thing that happens when something that's been held up a long time is let down. The man breathes out, a long way, without knowing he's been holding it. For a second, in the dark, on the step, Reggie Tar looks more relieved than Pascual has ever seen anybody look about anything.

And then it's gone, and he's the Director of Research again, and he says, "Well. The calf'll be pleased."

"The calf," says Pascual.

"She'd have missed you. She'd have gone off her food." Reggie's looking out at the channel. "I'd have had to get another vet."

"You'd have got another vet."

"I'd have got another vet," Reggie agrees, "and he'd have been no good, and I'd have had to — well." He stops. He looks down at his hands, flat on the book on his knee. He seems for a moment to be listening to something a long way off. "I'd have minded," he says. "A great deal. You know that."

"Yes," says Pascual. "I know that."

"Good," says Reggie, quite simply. He gets up, and puts his hand on the back of Pascual's neck on the way past, for a moment. It's too long, and it goes away as though it didn't know it had been there. "Go to bed. You look terrible. I'll do the six o'clock."

He goes into the hut at the end of the row, and shuts the door. And through the shutters Pascual watches him lie down on the bed in his shirt with the book, meaning to read, and be asleep inside a minute. His arm is flung up over his head. The lamp's burning on the table beside him.

He's got no idea.

He's got no idea at all. He's just sat on a step and breathed out like a man let off a hanging, and said *I'd have minded a great deal*, and put his hand on the back of Pascual's neck. Now he's gone to bed pleased, because the calf won't go off her food, and he'll wake at six and do the feed and never once wonder why.

Pascual goes into his own hut. He doesn't light his own lamp. He sits down on the bed in the dark.

It isn't only Reggie. He knows that. He's known it since the church path. He's an honest man, and it isn't the calf, and it isn't even pity. He's been sitting on that step with the man for eight years. He's watched him at the table and the rail and the tank drawings at midnight, and he knows the whole of him now, the worst of him and the most ridiculous. That's the difference. In 1953 it was a fight. He wanted the man and wanted to throw a bucket at him in about equal parts, and a good half of it was wanting to win, and it burnt hot and cross and wore itself out. This doesn't want to win anything. It hasn't got any shouting in it. It's quieter than that, and it goes further down. It's for the one who reads a book upside down on a step in the dark and doesn't know why he's waiting.

It's worse. It's a great deal worse. It's the worst thing that's happened to him since 1953, and he knew it on the church path, and he let a good woman go to Kingston for it. And the man ten yards away through the wall, asleep with his arm over his head, is going to go on having no idea about it for years. He's going to go on having no idea unless somebody tells him. And there is nobody else to do it.

He knows exactly what that's going to mean. There's the other one in Norway, and whatever happened in the office in 1957, and *can't*, and a file in Kingston. There's a man at the end of the row with a lamp he doesn't know he leaves lit. None of it is simple. All of it is going to have to be walked through, slowly, a step at a time, by a man who talks too much at dinner and can't keep anything off his face.

He sits on the bed. After a while he puts his face in his hands.

"*Ay, no*," says Pascual Pobil, to the wall, for the second time in his life.
