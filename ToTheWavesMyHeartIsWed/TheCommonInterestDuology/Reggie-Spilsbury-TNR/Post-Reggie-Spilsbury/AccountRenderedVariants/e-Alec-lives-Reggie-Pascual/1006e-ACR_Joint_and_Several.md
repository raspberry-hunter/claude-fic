# Joint and Several

The depositor's March letter comes on the last Tuesday in April, by sea, as they always do.

Reggie reads it at the office window at a quarter past eleven. *In accordance with the terms of deposit, I write to inform the Institute that the depositor of accession 197 remains resident at Kongens gate 18, Ålesund, and may be reached there. Yours faithfully, A. Spilsbury.* Four lines. The typewriter's the same one. There's nothing pressed through and no overstrike. The water in the Brosund was whatever it was on whatever morning in March, and Alec has kept it to himself, as he's kept everything since 1960.

He reads it twice. Then he folds it along its creases and puts it in his breast pocket and doesn't go down to the pens.

He goes to the long room instead. He sits at the bench with a sheet of the Institute's paper and the letter book, and turns back through the letter book to April 1957. He reads every acknowledgement he's written since, one after another, in his own hand, eighteen of them. *Dear Sir. The Institute acknowledges receipt of the depositor's letter of the —. The accession remains in the custody of the Institute under the terms of deposit. Yours faithfully, R. Tar, Director of Research.* Eighteen of them, and not one word different except the dates. He'd thought, when he started, that he'd find something in them. Some place where his hand had pressed harder, or a word he'd hesitated over. He'd have read it off Alec's letters in a moment. There isn't anything. He'd been very good at the form.

Then he turns to the back of the letter book, where Hester keeps the deed. It's the copy Mr Purdie made in March 1957, on the long paper, with the Institute's seal and Hester's terms of custody in the third schedule. He reads the third schedule twice.

*Letters from the depositor shall be addressed to the Director of Research, as custodian of the accession.*

As custodian. He'd read that clause a dozen times in 1957 and never been able to decide whether Hester had done it on purpose. He reads it now for what it doesn't say.

It doesn't say *sole*.

He sits there for some time with the deed open on the bench. Then he writes the acknowledgement, in pencil, on the back of a weight sheet. He crosses a word out, and puts it back, and crosses it out again. When he's got it the right way up he goes to find Pascual.

---

Pascual's in the north pen to his thighs with the calf, looking at a sore place on her flipper. He looks up as Reggie comes down the steps, and his face does what it does, which is everything, and then settles on *wary*.

"You've got a letter."

"I've got a draft."

"Of what?"

"The acknowledgement." Reggie sits down on the wall. "I'd like you to read it. I'd like you to read it before anybody else does, because it's got your name in it, and I'm not sending anything with your name in it that you haven't said yes to."

Pascual looks at him for a long moment. Then he wades over, and wipes his hands on his shirt, and takes the weight sheet, and reads it standing in the water. He reads English slowly. His lips move on the long words.

*Dear Sir,*

*The Institute acknowledges receipt of the depositor's letter of the 14th March, and notes that the depositor remains resident at Kongens gate 18, Ålesund.*

*The depositor is further informed that with effect from the date of this letter Dr P. Pobil, the Institute's veterinary officer, is associated with the undersigned in the custody of accession 197, and will countersign acknowledgements hereafter.*

*Yours faithfully,*

*R. Tar, Director of Research*

He reads it twice. The calf comes up behind him and blows, and puts her head against the back of his knees, the way she does, and he doesn't notice. He reads it a third time. Reggie watches him get to the word *associated* and stop there, and his face go through something.

"*Associated*," says Pascual.

"It's the form."

"It's —" Pascual looks up. His ears have gone red. "That's the line. You said the form had a line."

"That's the line."

"And he'll read it."

"He'll read it," says Reggie. "Anybody could read it. Hester could read it aloud at the long table and the Colonel would say *quite right, sensible*. A man from Kingston could read it and write nothing down. It's administration."

"And he'll read it."

"He'll read your name under mine on a letter about the thing he left in my keeping," says Reggie. "Yes. He'll read that. He'll know exactly what it means. He wrote *there is another man on this row* across a table from me in February 1957. He'll know."

Pascual stands in the water with the weight sheet.

"Is it all right?" says Reggie. "You don't have to. It's your name. It'll be in the letter book for as long as there's an Institute, and it'll go to Ålesund, and he'll have it. If you'd rather not be —"

"Is this you asking me?"

"Yes."

"Before you've done it."

"Before I've done anything. I haven't told Hester. It isn't copied. It's pencil." He holds out his hand for it. "If you say no, I'll rub it out and write the other eighteen again. And I'll find another way. It'll just take longer."

Pascual doesn't give it back.

He looks down at it. And Reggie watches him think, not about the file, or Hester, or the letter book. He's thinking about the man. Pascual liked him. He liked him the first afternoon, with his face like the ash and his wretched exact figures, and he's been decent about him for nine years. He's filled his lamp every Saturday since 1957 without a word.

"He'll be hurt," says Pascual.

"Yes."

"You know he'll be hurt."

"I know he will. I can't *ask* him. I've nothing to ask with. I can only tell him, and not go behind his back."

"No."

Reggie hears his own voice go, and lets it. "I'd rather he were hurt by me in my own hand than found it out some other way in five years. He's owed that."

Pascual nods, slowly.

"And it's true," he says. "The line."

"It's true."

"*Associated*." His mouth goes at the corner, not quite a smile. "It's a terrible word."

"It's the only one the form's got."

"I know." Pascual folds the weight sheet, carefully, along the middle, and gives it back. "Write it in ink. I'll sign it."

---

He writes it in ink that evening, at the table in the hut, with the lamp turned down to its bead and a candle for the work. His hand's quite steady. He signs it *R. Tar, Director of Research*, as he's signed the other eighteen, and blots it, and pushes it across the table.

Pascual takes the pen.

He sits there for a moment with it in his hand, over the space below Reggie's name. Then he writes, slowly, in the big square upright hand he uses for veterinary schedules. He doesn't write *P. Pobil*. He writes it with both his names, all the way out: *Pascual Pobil*. Under it, *Veterinary Officer*.

"It should be initials," says Reggie. "It's the form."

"He'll want to know which one." Pascual puts the pen down. "There's only one of me. He should have the whole name. It's the least I can do. I'm putting it under yours on a letter to him."

Reggie looks at the two names on the page, one above the other.

He's got nothing to say to that. He puts his hand over Pascual's, on the table, by the letter, and leaves it there, and Pascual turns his hand over under it and holds on.

---

Hester reads it for the letter book on the Tuesday morning.

She reads every letter that leaves the island for the letter book. She's done it since 1938. She reads this one standing at her desk with her glasses on the end of her nose. She reads it twice. Reggie stands in the doorway of the office with his hands in his pockets, and Pascual stands about a foot behind him, not three. Nobody says anything.

"*Associated*," says Hester.

"Yes, Hester."

"In the custody of accession 197."

"Yes."

"The deed," says Hester, "names the Director of Research, as custodian."

"It doesn't say *sole*."

Hester looks at him over her glasses.

"No," she says. "It doesn't."

She copies it into the letter book, both signatures, in her own clear hand. Pascual watches her write *Pascual Pobil* out in full and goes red to the ears. She blots it, folds the letter, and puts it in the envelope. She types the envelope, *A. Spilsbury Esq., Kongens gate 18, Ålesund, Norway*, as she's typed every one since 1957. She holds it out.

Then she stops, with it held out, and looks at the corner.

"By sea, Dr Tar?"

Reggie takes a breath.

"By air," he says.

Hester's face does nothing at all. She takes the envelope back, and goes to the drawer, and gets out the blue airmail labels that the Institute uses perhaps four times a year. She wets one on the sponge, and presses it on, very firmly, with her thumb, in the top left corner.

"By air," says Hester. "I should think it'll be read very carefully in Ålesund."

"Yes," says Reggie. "I should think it will."

---

The reply comes on the fourteenth of June, by air, in a blue envelope. It's addressed in Alec's hand, and there's no *Director of Research* on it at all.

*Dr R. Tar*
*William Martin Institute for Marine Studies*

Pascual sees it before Reggie does. He's in the office for the veterinary schedule at eleven when Hester sorts the Tuesday post. She comes to that one and stops, and holds it a moment. Then she puts it on its own on the corner of the desk, face down. That's where she's always put the letters from Ålesund. It's the first one in nine years that hasn't said *The Director of Research*.

"Dr Pobil," says Hester, without looking up, "you'll tell him it's here."

"Me?"

"You." She goes on sorting. "I think that's best, now. Don't you?"

He takes it down to the long room. Reggie's at the bench with the calf's weights. He looks up, and sees the blue envelope, and goes quite white.

Pascual puts it down by his elbow.

"Hester said I was to bring it," he says. "Do you want me to go away?"

He's asking. He's learnt that from Reggie this winter, and he finds he's glad of it. He'd have gone without asking a year ago, out of decency, and left the man alone with it. He's not sure any more that that was decency. He'd like to know what Reggie wants.

Reggie looks at the envelope for a long time.

"No," he says. "Stay. Please. I'd like you to — I'd like you here."

He opens it at the bench, with the students at the far end and Pascual standing beside him. He reads it. And Pascual watches his face while he does, the way you'd watch an animal's breathing, and can't read any of it. For the first time in thirteen years, Reggie Tar's face isn't doing anything at all.

Then he holds it out.

"You read it."

"It's yours."

"It's to me," says Reggie. "It's *about* you. I'd rather you read it than I told you what's in it. I'd get it wrong."

Pascual takes it.

It's one sheet, on the letterhead of the station on the quay, in a small, very exact hand. He reads it slowly, with his lips moving on the long words, standing at the bench in the long room with the tank-lights glowing green along the back wall.

> *Statens Havforskningsstasjon*
> *Ålesund*
> *3rd June 1966*
>
> *Dear Reggie,*
>
> *I have the Institute's acknowledgement of the 10th of May, and the line in it, and the second signature. I have read them a good many times. I think you meant me to. I think you meant me to understand that there was no other way you could have put it, and I do.*
>
> *I should like you to know that I costed him in 1957. I wrote it down: there is another man on this row. I meant it as a risk to you. I have had nine years to see that it was also something else, and I did not see it, which is not like me. I find I am not surprised. I find I ought to have been the least surprised man in Europe.*
>
> *I find, too, that I am glad. I did not expect that, and I have checked it, twice. It is not the whole of what I am this week, and I shall not pretend it is. But it is true, and you should have it, as you have given me the rest.*
>
> *I had gone on expecting, without any grounds, that the form would one day change back. That was not a calculation. It was the other thing. I have put it down now.*
>
> *I am to be in London from the 12th to the 16th of September, at the Society's rooms in Regent's Park. I should like to see you once, before it is only the form again. I do not want anything. I have nothing to ask for, and I shall not pretend to. I should like to see your face when you tell me, because I have not seen it for nine years, and I should like to know with my own eyes that it is all right.*
>
> *If you would rather not, you need do nothing at all. No answer is an answer, and I shall read it as one.*
>
> *Please tell Dr Pobil, with my regards, that he is to see that you eat. He will know that I mean it.*
>
> *Yours,*
>
> *A.*

Pascual reads it twice.

He reads the last line three times. And then he has to put it down on the bench and put his hand flat over his eyes, standing up, in the long room, with the students at the far end. He can't do anything about his face. He never could.

"Pascual."

"Give me a minute."

"Take it."

He takes it. When he takes his hand away Reggie's watching him, and Reggie's face has come back. It's doing everything now, all of it, and Pascual can read it again. It's grief, and something like pride, and under both a sort of stunned tenderness, and it isn't pointed at the letter. It's pointed at him.

"He's *glad*," says Pascual. His voice won't do what he tells it. "He's checked it. Twice."

"I know."

"I checked too. In 'fifty-six. Twice. I've not told you that."

"You've told me now," says Reggie.

"And he wants me to feed you."

"He's always wanted somebody to feed me. He fed me for years." Reggie's mouth goes. "He's handing it over. In writing. With regards."

Pascual sits down on the bench stool. It's that or fall over.

"*Hombre*," he says. "Oh, *hombre*. The poor man."

"Yes."

"He brought it on himself. He did the sum, you said, and the man in England paid, and nobody asked him. And you sent him away for it."

"Yes."

"And he's *still* —" He can't finish it. He looks at the letter on the bench. "I liked him. I liked him the first afternoon. He looked at the calf's mother for ten minutes and told me I'd got the dentition wrong, and I had."

"I know you did," says Reggie. "He liked you. He said so. In 1957. He said you were the only man on the island who'd ever told him he was wrong and been right."

Neither of them says anything for a while. At the far end of the long room a student drops a tray of slides and swears, and somebody laughs.

"He wants to see you," says Pascual.

"Yes."

"In London. In September."

"Yes."

"Are you going to go?"

Reggie looks at him. It's a long, steady look, and Pascual knows what it is now. He's had it pointed at him for seven months. It's reading him. It's reading every line of him, carefully, the way it'd read a set of figures from a new run.

"I don't know yet," says Reggie. "I'd like to talk to you first. Tonight. On the step. Not here."

"Why not here?"

"Because you're going to say *go*," says Reggie, "much too quickly. And then I'm going to have to tell you why that wasn't the truth, and I'd rather not do that in front of the second-years."

---

He does say *go* much too quickly.

He says it on the step at half past nine, with the moon coming up over the channel and a foot of planking between them, because the row's awake. He says it before Reggie's finished asking.

"Go."

"I haven't —"

"Go. You should go. He's asked to see your face, he's owed that, you said yourself he's owed it. It's London, it's a symposium, it's the most respectable thing on earth, nobody'll write down a word. Go."

Reggie looks at him sidelong.

"That was very quick."

"It's the right answer."

"It's *a* right answer," says Reggie. "I'm not sure it's yours."

Pascual opens his mouth. He shuts it again.

"You're doing it," he says. "The reading."

"You asked me to, in the launch."

"I said I *hated* it in the launch —"

"And that it was wonderful. And that you'd like me to stop and never stop." Reggie's voice is very quiet. "So I'm not stopping. You said *go* before I'd got to the question. You do that when you want something over before it's started. You did it on the church path."

"I did not —"

"You're frightened."

Pascual sits on the step with his hands between his knees.

He *is* frightened. He's been frightened since the blue envelope. He's been frightened in a way he can't put his hand on, the way he was in the launch, and he'd hoped that if he said *go* quickly and firmly and nobly enough, the fright would go too.

It hasn't.

"All right," he says. "All right. Yes. I'm frightened."

"Of what?"

"Of London." It comes out rough. "Of you sitting in a hotel across a table from him. You had *years*, Reggie. And he'll look at you with that face like the ash, and you'll remember what you had, and think —" He stops. He makes himself go on. He's a vet. He finds the place and puts his hand on it. "*What am I doing with a vet on a rock?*"

Reggie doesn't say anything.

"There. That's it." Pascual's ears are burning in the dark. "He wrote that letter and I cried in the long room, and I'm still sitting here frightened you'll not come back. I'd like it on the record that I know it's not noble."

"It's on the record," says Reggie.

"Good."

"Can I tell you what I'm frightened of?"

"You're going to anyway."

"I'm frightened of going." Reggie's looking out at the channel. "Not for your reason. The last time I saw his face, I told him I couldn't come up his path. He said I was right, and went."

"In the spare hut."

"In the spare hut. And now I've to sit across a table from the man I sent away over the man in England, and tell him to his face that I've let somebody up mine."

Pascual goes still.

"I can't let him be glad for me in a letter and not say it where he can see me," says Reggie. "That's what I'm frightened of. Not that I'll want him back. I'll want him. That was never why."

"*Hombre*."

"And I'm frightened of not going." Reggie turns his head. "He's asked to see my face."

"Yes."

"He's asked, this time. He didn't ask the man in England. And if no answer's the answer, then he's done it right this time and I haven't. I'd have learned it from you, in the launch, and not done it for him."

Pascual sits with that for a long time.

Down in the north pen the calf blows. The moon's clear of the channel now.

"So," he says at last. "Neither of us is noble."

"No."

"And we're both frightened."

"Both."

"Then —" Pascual lets out his breath. "Then go. I'm not saying it quick this time. I'm saying it slow. I've thought about it and I've been frightened about it and I'm saying it anyway. Go to London. Sit across the table from him. Tell him what you did in the office. Let him have your face. It's the least anybody can give him and it's the most you've got." He looks at Reggie. "And then come back on the boat."

"I'll come back on the boat."

"I know you will. I'll be frightened anyway. I'll be frightened from the day you go to the day the boat comes round the point. I'll not be noble about it."

"I shouldn't want you to be."

"And you'll tell him —" Pascual stops. He finds he has to swallow. "You'll tell him I'll see that you eat. That I've got his regards. That I know he meant it."

"I'll tell him."

"And that he was right about the dentition."

Reggie laughs. It comes out cracked.

"I'll tell him that."

They sit on the step with a foot of planking between them. Then Reggie looks along the row, where the lights are going out one by one, the students' and Hester's and the Colonel's. When the last one's out he moves along the step. He doesn't do it by accident. He does it quite deliberately, on purpose, knowing it, and he doesn't stop until the foot's gone and his shoulder's against Pascual's arm.

"Pascual."

"Mm."

"He knows."

Pascual feels it go through him before he's understood it. Then he understands it.

*I can't go to bed with you while he doesn't know.*

"He knows," Pascual says.

"He's known since the twentieth of May, or thereabouts, depending on the air. He's answered. He's glad. He's checked it twice." Reggie's shoulder is warm against his arm through two shirts. "And I'm not going to his lamp tonight."

Pascual turns his head.

"*His* lamp?"

"That one." Reggie's looking at the hut at the end of the row, where the lamp's burning on the table behind the shutters, turned down to its bead, as it has been every night since November. "I'm not putting it out. I told you I couldn't, and I can't. Not till September. Not till I've seen his face. But I'm not going to do this by it, either. That'd be —" He stops. "He's been decent. I'd like to be."

"Then where —"

"You've got a bed," says Reggie, "that you bought from the Bay in 1960 because you said the station bed was built for a man the size of a ferret." He looks up at him. "Ten yards. I've counted."

Pascual looks at him in the moonlight for a long moment.

Then he laughs. It comes up out of the middle of him, helpless. He puts his hand over his mouth, because of the row, and laughs into it. Reggie sits beside him and watches him with that look on his face, reading him, every line of him, and letting him see it.

"Is that asking?" says Pascual, through his fingers.

"That's asking."

"*Dios*," says Pascual. "Yes. Yes, you *ferret*. Come on."

---

Pascual's hut is ten yards up the row and Reggie has been in it perhaps five hundred times since 1952. He has never once been in it like this, and it turns out to be an entirely different hut.

He notices it the way he's noticed everything since November: on purpose, knowing it. There's the shelf of Spanish books, and the drawings pinned to the wall over the bench, the calf at three and the calf at seven, and the dentition of the calf's mother in pen and ink. There's the smell of him, soap and salt and the linseed he uses on his boots. There's the bed from the Bay, which is a real bed, with a proper frame and a mattress with some opinion in it, wide enough for a man the size of Pascual and, as it turns out, a ferret.

Pascual shuts the door behind them, and bolts it. The crack of the bolt going home goes right down Reggie's back. Then Pascual doesn't do anything. He stands with his back to the door and his hands at his sides.

"Well," says Pascual.

"Well."

"I don't know how you start."

"You start," says Reggie, "by coming here."

He comes. Reggie puts his hand on his face, the way he did in November across the candle, and kisses him. He does it slowly and knows it, and Pascual makes the sound in his throat that he always makes, low, as if something's been let go of. They've done this a hundred times since November. They know how this part goes. It goes on for a while, standing up by the door, with Pascual's hands on his hips and his fingers in the grey hair at the back of Pascual's neck. It's familiar and it's good, and at the end of it Reggie can feel it in the man's hands, the place coming. The edge of the reef.

Pascual stops.

"Reggie."

"Yes."

"I'm there. The place."

"I know."

"I don't know what's on the other side of it." His voice has gone hoarse and very low. "I'd like it on the record. I'm fifty-two and I've no idea. I'm going to be —"

"Clumsy," says Reggie. "And slow. You told me. In the launch."

"I'm going to get it *wrong*."

"Then we'll get it wrong," says Reggie, "and do it again. First run." He puts his hand flat on Pascual's chest, over his heart, and leaves it there. "You asked me in the launch whether I'd be measuring. I'd like you to hear the answer before we start."

Pascual's heart is going under his palm like a pump.

"Go on."

"I thought I would be. I was frightened of it too. I thought there'd be a sort of ghost of him in the room, and you'd see me feel it." He makes himself hold Pascual's eyes. "And I've been in this room five minutes, and he isn't here."

Pascual doesn't say anything.

"It isn't that I've forgotten him. It isn't that. It's that you're so different it's not the same *sea*. You don't measure a thing against something that's in a different ocean. I stood by that door kissing you just now and the whole time I was thinking about *you*. About what your mouth does. About how you breathe when I've got my hand here." He moves it, an inch, up to the side of Pascual's throat, and feels the breath catch under it. "Like that. That's all I was thinking. It isn't an examination. It never was. There's nobody else in here to be examined against."

"*Hombre*," says Pascual, and his voice cracks straight down the middle.

"So you'll be clumsy. And I'll show you. And you'll tell me what you like, and I'll tell you. Out loud. Both of us." He takes his hand off Pascual's throat and puts it to his shirt buttons. "May I?"

"*Yes*. God. Yes."

He undoes the shirt. He's done it before, in the launch, in the hut, on the camp bed. He does it now knowing that there's no stopping at the end of it, and his hands aren't entirely steady. It comes off. Pascual's chest is broad and heavy and going grey, and the skin's hot, and there's the long white scar under the ribs on the left where the Colonel's mare had him in 1958. Reggie puts his mouth to the scar, and Pascual's hand comes down on the back of his head and holds it there, and Pascual says something in Spanish that has *Dios* in it twice.

"Tell me," says Reggie, against his ribs.

"Tell you what?"

"You said you'd tell me. What you like. Out loud."

"I don't *know* what I —" Pascual stops. His hand's still in Reggie's hair. He breathes. "That. What you're doing. The — your mouth, there. I like that."

"Good."

"And your hands. I've liked your hands since 1952. You hold a pipette like it owes you money." His voice goes rough. "Put them on me."

Reggie puts them on him. He puts them flat on the broad back and slides them down, slow, to the small of it, where it's damp. Pascual shudders all down his length and swears, softly, in two languages.

"And you," says Pascual, when he can. "You tell me. You've got to tell me too, or I'll not know."

"I'll tell you."

"*Now*, Reggie. You tell me something now."

Reggie looks up at him.

He's forty-six. He's been in a few beds. With Alec he'd talked the whole time, in his seminar voice, flat and dry and specific, *higher, slower, there*, as though he were asking for a figure. And Alec had talked back the way he talked at a bench, in order, with the measurements in. They'd been young and very clever, and it had been a thing two clever men did in the dark, and it had been the right way for the two of them. It's the only time all night that Alec comes into the room. It's only for a second, and Reggie lets him come, and looks at him, and lets him go. Because this isn't that, or not only that. This is a man who's going to have to be told what Reggie *wants*, as well as where, and who wants to hear it. And Reggie finds, all at once, that he wants to tell him more than he's wanted to do anything in years.

"I'd like you to take my shirt off," he says. "I'd like your mouth under my ear, where it was in November."

Pascual's breath goes.

"And I'd like to lie down on that bed, which is too good for you, and have you on top of me. All of you. I'd like the whole weight."

Pascual stares at him.

"You can say *that*."

"I can say that."

"Out loud. In your own voice."

"It's my own voice," says Reggie. And it's true. There's nothing in this hut that needs hiding, so there's nothing for the other one to do.

Pascual takes his shirt off.

He does it clumsily, as he said he would. He gets a sleeve stuck on Reggie's wrist, and swears at it, and Reggie laughs, and Pascual says *don't you laugh*, and gets it off. Then his mouth's at the place under Reggie's ear, exactly where it was in November, as though he'd drawn a map of it. And he's walking Reggie backwards, three steps, to the bed from the Bay. They go down on it together in a heap that isn't graceful. Pascual's elbow gets Reggie in the ribs on the way down and he says *sorry, sorry, ay*, and Reggie's laughing too hard to answer. And then Pascual's on top of him, the whole weight, as asked. Reggie stops laughing.

It's the weight. He'd asked for it and he hadn't known. Pascual's a big man and heavy, and he comes down along the whole length of Reggie, chest to chest and hip to hip, warm and damp and solid as the ground. For a second Reggie can't breathe, and doesn't want to. He gets his arms round Pascual's back and holds on as hard as he can.

"Too much?" says Pascual into his neck. "Am I — is it too much —"

"No. God. No. *Stay*."

"You said all of it."

"I meant all of it."

Pascual stays. He's still for a long moment on top of Reggie, just breathing, both of them. Reggie can feel the man's heart against his own chest, going far too fast. He can feel the tremor in the big arms braced either side of him. Pascual's frightened, and wants, and is here, all at once, and trying to hold it all still.

"You're shaking," Reggie says.

"So are you."

"I know. Kiss me."

It's slow. It's slow because Pascual said it'd be slow, and because neither of them is young, and because every so often one of them stops, stunned, and just breathes, and the other waits. Pascual kisses him for a long time, as if he's making sure of something. Then he lifts his head.

"Show me," he says. "You said you'd show me."

So Reggie shows him.

He does it with his hands, mostly. He takes Pascual's hand and puts it where he wants it, and says *there*, and *like that*, and *slower*. Pascual does it slower, and says *like this?* in a voice Reggie has never heard out of him, low and entirely unsure. Reggie says *yes*, and *yes*, and then he doesn't say anything for a while, because he can't.

Pascual's clumsy for about ten minutes.

After that he isn't. He's a man who's spent forty years learning bodies through his hands in the dark, horses and dogs and the calf and the Colonel's knees. It's all in the hands, he said once in the pen. He gets there by himself, somewhere in the middle of it, without being shown. Reggie feels the moment he gets there. The unsure hand goes sure, as if it's found the right tooth. And Pascual lifts his head and looks at him with his whole face lit up in the dark, and says, astonished, "*Oh*."

"Yes."

"Oh, that's — Reggie, that's —"

"I *know*," says Reggie, and pulls him down.

He'd been afraid it'd be quiet, the way he'd been afraid of so many things this winter that turned out not to be there. It isn't quiet. Pascual's never been quiet about anything in his life. He talks the whole way through, in two languages, low and rough and helpless. He says Reggie's name, and *Dios*, and *there*, and a great many things in Spanish that Reggie doesn't ask him to translate. And Reggie finds he's talking back. He says *yes*, and *Pascual*, and *don't stop*, and *I've wanted this*, and *I didn't know I wanted this*, and *I want it now*. Out loud, in his own voice, with nothing hidden in it, to the man above him in a bed from the Bay with the shutters bolted and the moon coming through the slats.

At some point Pascual's hand comes up and lies flat over the middle of Reggie's chest, over his heart, and stays there.

"What are you doing?"

"Nothing," says Pascual. "Holding on."

It goes through Reggie like a wave coming in over the reef edge. He holds on to Pascual's back with both hands and lets it lift him off the coral, all at once, and says the man's name into his shoulder. He can't do anything but say it. A moment after, Pascual's breath catches and goes, and he shakes all along Reggie's length, braced on his arms. He says *Reggie* into his hair, not loud, as if it were a word he'd found in a drawer and couldn't believe was his to use.

---

Afterwards they lie in the bed from the Bay with the sheet somewhere on the floor. The moon's coming through the slats in stripes across the pair of them.

Pascual's on his back with his arm out. Reggie's on his side against him, with his head in the hollow of his shoulder, where it goes. Neither of them says anything for a long time.

"Well?" says Pascual at last.

Reggie laughs. It shakes both of them.

"It isn't an examination."

"I'm asking anyway."

"Then —" Reggie thinks about it. He does it properly, knowing it. "First run. Some technical difficulties at the start. An elbow in the ribs. A shirt caught on a wrist. The subject's language was unacceptable in two languages throughout." He feels Pascual's chest begin to shake. "Very promising results. Replication strongly advised."

"*Replication*."

"Tomorrow, I thought. And the night after. I'd like a large sample."

Pascual laughs until he has to put his hand over his own mouth, because of the row. Reggie lies against him and listens to it. It's the best sound he's heard on the island. He knows that, and he's quite sure of it, and he doesn't have to check it twice.

When it's done Pascual turns his head on the pillow.

Through the slats of his shutter, ten yards down the row, there's a line of light. It's very faint, a blue bead behind the shutters of the hut at the end, on the table, burning half what it did.

"It's still lit," says Pascual.

"Yes."

"You said September."

"September," says Reggie. "When I've seen his face. When I've told him to his face, and he's had it, and decided what to do with it." He looks at the line of light for a while. "I'll know then. I'll know what it's for. I'll come back on the boat and I'll know."

Pascual doesn't say anything for a moment. Then his arm tightens round Reggie's shoulders.

"I'll fill it Saturday," he says. "Till then."

"You don't have to."

"I know I don't. I'll do it anyway." His voice is rough. "It's his. He's been decent. I'd like to be."

Reggie shuts his eyes.

He isn't going to sleep. He's quite sure. There's London in September, and a table in a hotel, and a thing he's going to have to say to the face of the man he sent away. There's a lamp ten yards off that he'll have to decide about. There's a big warm man under his cheek, whose heart has come down at last, whose hand is still flat over the middle of Reggie's chest, holding on.

He'll lie here and go through all of it, one thing at a time.

He's asleep inside a minute.
