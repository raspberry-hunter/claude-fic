# Days of Grace

He decides, on the Thursday, to know what he's doing.

It's the only rule he makes. He makes it at the bench at half past seven in the morning, with his right hand in its bandage lying on the weight sheet like a thing somebody else has left there. It seems to him a very modest rule. Most men manage it without writing it down. He's forty-six and has apparently managed without it for six months, leaning on his friend on a step every night and never once asking himself why, and he has the blister to show for it.

So: to know what he's doing. Not to decide anything he doesn't have to. Not to lean on anybody by accident. And, he adds, after some thought, not to lean on anybody on purpose where the row can see, because that isn't knowing what he's doing either. That's only doing it with his eyes open.

He writes none of this down. He considers writing it down, and thinks of a feed sheet in a drawer in Pascual's bench with a column of inches on it, and doesn't.

At a quarter to eight Pascual comes into the long room with two cups of tea and puts one down by his left hand, not his right. He's got it right without being told. He looks at the bandage and then at Reggie's face, and his own face goes through three or four things in quick succession, all of them perfectly legible.

"How's the hand?"

"It's fine."

"Let me see."

"It's *fine*, Pascual, it's a blister, I've had worse off a Bunsen —"

"Let me *see*."

Reggie lets him see. Pascual unwinds the bandage at the bench, in the morning light, with the students coming in at the far end, and turns the hand over in both of his, and looks at the three stripes. He isn't looking at them the way he'd look at a flipper. It's quite different, and Reggie's noticing it this morning because he's decided to notice things. It's the way you'd look at something you'd been told you could have.

"It'll do," says Pascual. His voice isn't steady. He winds the bandage back on, slowly, too slowly, and his thumb goes once across the back of Reggie's wrist before he lets go. "Gentian violet. Tonight. I'll bring it."

"Hester said gentian violet."

"Hester's right. Hester's always —" Pascual stops. His ears go red. "I'll bring it after dinner."

He goes away to his own end of the bench.

Reggie sits with his tea and his bandaged hand and knows, quite distinctly, what's just happened. A man's put his thumb across the back of his wrist in the long room at a quarter to eight in the morning, and his whole arm has gone warm to the shoulder. He knows it. That's new. It's alarming, and extraordinary, and he'd like a great deal more of it, and he'd like it tonight, after dinner, with the gentian violet.

He's forty-six. He puts his face down over the weight sheet so the students can't see it.

---

Pascual brings the gentian violet after dinner.

He paints it on at the table in the hut, by the lamp. The lamp's turned down to a bead, as it was on Tuesday, and there's a candle for the work. He paints the stripes a vivid purple, very carefully, with a camel-hair brush he uses for the drawings, while Reggie watches his face. When he's done he doesn't let go of the hand.

"There," says Pascual.

"Thank you."

"You'll look like you've been eating blackberries."

"There aren't any blackberries within four thousand miles."

"Then you'll look like a man who's been in the ink." He's still holding it. He's holding it by the wrist, in both hands, as though he's forgotten there's anything to do next. "Reggie."

"Yes."

"I don't know what we're doing."

"No," says Reggie. "Nor do I. I've decided to know, but I haven't got there."

Pascual looks up at him.

"You've *decided* to know."

"This morning. At the bench." It sounds absurd, said aloud. He says it anyway. "I've done six months not knowing. I'd like to try the other way. I thought I'd start by knowing what I'm doing and see what that turns out to be."

"And what are you doing?"

Reggie looks at the hand in Pascual's hands, purple across the fingers. He looks at the candle. And he points himself, very carefully, for the first time in thirteen years, at the man sitting across the table from him.

He's never done it to Pascual. He finds that out as he starts. He's done it to every other soul on the island, to the Colonel and Hester and every student since 1950, and a Commander with a moustache. And he's never once pointed himself at Pascual, because Pascual was the wall and the plate and the man in the next hut, and you don't read the wall. Now he does it, and it's like opening a door onto a room full of light.

It's all there. It's always been all there. Pascual's face has never hidden anything in its life. There's the want in it, which is enormous, and hasn't been put away since Tuesday; it's sitting right on top where anybody could see it. There's the fright under that, of him, of this, of the file. And under the fright something so patient and so tired that it makes Reggie's throat hurt. Six months of it, going cold down the left side at half past ten.

"I'm going to kiss you," says Reggie. "I'm telling you first. So I know I'm doing it."

Pascual's hands tighten on his wrist.

"Do you always announce it?"

"I've no idea. I've only done it by accident so far." He stands up. The chair scrapes. "Is it all right?"

"*Hombre*," says Pascual, and it comes out of him cracked right across, "it's been all right since *May*."

So Reggie comes round the table, and puts his left hand on Pascual's face, and bends, because Pascual's sitting down and for once Reggie's the taller. He kisses him slowly, knowing it, the whole time.

It's very different from the step. The step was a fall. This is like walking into the sea on purpose, a step at a time, feeling the water come up. He can feel everything: Pascual's mouth, a little open, still for a moment and then answering; the stubble along his jaw under Reggie's palm; his breath going out through his nose in a long, shaken rush against Reggie's cheek. Pascual's hands have let go of his wrist and come up to his hips and are holding him there, very lightly, as if Reggie might go off like a bird if they held any harder.

He doesn't go off. He stays. He tips Pascual's head back a little with his hand and kisses him more deeply, and Pascual makes a sound in his throat, low. His hands on Reggie's hips stop being light. They pull, and Reggie comes, until he's standing between the man's knees with Pascual's face turned up to his and his fingers in the short grey hair at the back of Pascual's head. He could stay there all night. He knows it. He knows it so thoroughly he could draw a graph of it.

When he stops, Pascual keeps his eyes shut.

"Well?" says Reggie. He doesn't know why he says it. It's what Pascual says on Saturdays.

Pascual opens his eyes. They're wet.

"You knew you were doing that?"

"The whole time."

"*Madre de Dios*," says Pascual, and pulls him down into his lap.

The chair's a station chair and was never meant for it. It creaks. Reggie ends up sitting sideways across Pascual's thighs with his arm round his neck and his purple hand held carefully out of the way, laughing, and Pascual's laughing too, into his throat. Then neither of them is laughing, because Pascual's mouth has found the place under Reggie's ear that it found on Tuesday. He does it on purpose this time, slowly, with his lips and his tongue and the edge of his teeth. Reggie hears himself make a sound he'd have been ashamed of on Tuesday. He isn't ashamed of it now. He knows exactly why he made it. He'd make it again.

"Pascual."

"Mm."

"That's — do that — *yes*, there —"

"I know," says Pascual, against his neck. "I've been looking at that place for six months across the long table. I *know* where it is."

---

That's how it goes, the rest of November.

He decides nothing. He knows everything he does. He knows, on the Friday, that he goes to the north pen at six to do the feed and finds Pascual already there, and that he stands closer on the rail than he needs to, and that he's doing it. He moves off six inches, on purpose, because the row's awake. And Pascual watches him do it, and his mouth goes at one corner.

He knows, on the Saturday, that Pascual comes along the row with the oil can at eight, as he's come every Saturday since 1957. He knows Pascual fills the lamp, and stands there with the can, and says, in an odd voice, "It's taking half what it did."

"Half?"

"Half. It's been turned down four nights." Pascual looks at the lamp, and not at him. "I measure it. I've always measured it. I could tell you what you've burnt every week since 'fifty-seven."

Reggie looks at him.

"You measure the oil."

"I measure everything," says Pascual, rather desperately. "You know I do. It's the same as the inches."

"I didn't know. I didn't know about the oil. I didn't know about the inches till Tuesday." He takes the can out of Pascual's hand and puts it on the floor. "What else have you measured, that I don't know about?"

Pascual goes red to the ears, and doesn't answer, and Reggie kisses him standing up by the table. He knows he's doing it, and does it for a long time.

He knows, too, what he doesn't do. That's the other half of it.

He doesn't go past a certain point. He doesn't know, at first, that there is a point. He finds it on the last Tuesday in November, in the hut, with the lamp at a bead. Pascual's on his back on the narrow bed and Reggie's on top of him, and they've been at it for most of an hour. His shirt's out and Pascual's hands are under it, on his bare back, spread wide. Pascual's own shirt is open to the waist and Reggie's mouth has been all down the middle of his chest in the grey hair. Pascual's hips come up under him, once, hard, with a sound out of his chest like something tearing. And Reggie feels himself stop.

He doesn't decide it. He finds it, the way you find the edge of the reef under the keel. He's lying there on top of Pascual with his mouth on the man's collarbone, breathing, and he isn't going on. He isn't going to go on. He knows it the way he'd know a figure was wrong before he'd found the mistake.

Pascual feels it. Of course he does.

"Reggie?"

"Yes."

"You've stopped."

"Yes."

A pause. Pascual's hands are still flat on his back. They don't move.

"Is it — did I —"

"No. *No*." Reggie lifts his head. Pascual's face in the bead of light is flushed and open and frightened, and he can read all three at once. "It isn't you. It isn't anything you did. I just — I'm not there. I got to a place and I'm not past it."

"All right," says Pascual. He says it at once. His hands move, at last, slow circles on Reggie's back, the way you'd settle an animal. "All right. That's all right."

"Is it?"

"It's all right." He breathes out, a long way, and Reggie feels the whole big body under him go down a notch, and something in the breath that might be relief. He files that away. "You said you'd tell me. On Tuesday. That you'd want to know what you were doing first. You're telling me."

"I'm telling you."

"Then that's all right." Pascual pulls him down, and tucks his head in under his own chin, and holds him there. His heart's going like a pump under Reggie's ear. It takes a long time to come down. "Stay, though."

"Yes."

"Just stay."

Reggie stays. He lies with his ear on the man's chest and listens to the heart come down, beat by beat, and counts it. And he thinks about that breath, the one that went out of Pascual when he said *I'm not past it*. It had sounded like relief. He thinks about it a good deal, over the next month.

---

In December Pascual takes to kissing him in the long room.

Not in the day. After dinner, when the students have gone along the row and the Colonel's gone up to the house. The long room's dark except for the lamp over the bench and the green glow of the tank lights along the back wall. Pascual comes in to finish a drawing, and Reggie comes in to finish a weight sheet, and they both know perfectly well that neither of them is finishing anything.

It's different in the long room. In the hut it's slow. In the long room it's fast and slightly ridiculous and they have to keep stopping to laugh. Once Pascual backs him up against the specimen shelves and the shelves rattle, and three jars of formalin sway, and they both freeze with their mouths still together. Then they reach out at the same moment and steady a jar each, like two men catching the same falling plate, and Reggie laughs so hard he has to sit down on the floor.

"We'll be found," says Pascual, sitting down on the floor beside him with his back to the shelves, "in the morning. Pickled. By a student."

"Two specimens, adult, male. Cause of death —"

"*Don't*."

"Found in the Institute reference collection, accession two hundred and —"

Pascual kisses him to shut him up, sitting on the floor of the long room in the tank-light, with his hand at the back of Reggie's neck. It goes on for some while. It doesn't stop being ridiculous. It doesn't stop being the best thing Reggie's done since he came to the island, either, and he knows it the whole time.

And every time they get to the place, they stop.

He doesn't always stop first. That's what he notices, in December. That's what the month's for, in the end.

The first time it's him. The second time, in the long room, a week before Christmas, it's Pascual. They're on the old camp bed at the back, the one Reggie's slept on in his clothes more nights than he'd like to count. Reggie's hand has gone down to Pascual's belt, and he knows it's gone there, and he means it to. And Pascual catches his wrist, not hard, and holds it, and breathes, and says, "Wait."

Reggie waits.

"Just —" Pascual laughs, not quite steadily. "Just a minute. I'm — give me a minute."

"All right."

"It's nothing."

"All right," says Reggie, and does, and lies with his wrist held in Pascual's hand, and points himself at the man's face in the tank-light.

It isn't nothing. It's the same thing he saw on the Thursday in November, across the table with the candle, under the want, and he'd filed it as *fright, of him, of this, of the file*. It's fright, yes. But it isn't of the file. The file's nowhere in it. It's a narrower, sharper, older thing than that, and it's pointed at Reggie's hand.

He doesn't say anything. He isn't sure yet. He kisses Pascual on the forehead and takes his hand back and lies down beside him on the camp bed, and Pascual's whole body goes loose against his with something that is certainly relief.

---

He thinks about Alec a great deal that winter. He'd expected to.

He'd expected it to be like the day of a letter: a flat square in his breast pocket, not hurting. It isn't like that. It's worse, and more interesting, and it comes at odd times. It comes at the rail at six, with Pascual's shoulder against his. It comes on the camp bed in the long room with his mouth on the man's throat. It doesn't come as guilt, exactly. He keeps waiting for guilt and it keeps not being quite that. It comes as a question with no one to ask it of.

There's the lamp, turned down to a bead every night, burning half what it did. That's the part he turns over at three in the morning with Pascual asleep ten yards away through the wall.

He lights it every night for a man he sent away.

He'd sat on the end of the bed in the spare hut with the wrong roof in March 1957 and said *I can't come up this path again*, and meant it. And Alec had said *it is correct*. Alec had done a sum that winter, and a man in the north of England had paid for it. He'd paid for Reggie once already, at the Labs: Reggie had had the four days in the lift, and Frank had lost his bench for it. Nobody had asked him either time. Reggie wasn't going to be the man who had what he wanted, with his arms round it, on what Frank paid. He'd left the Labs so as not to be that man. He'd left Alec for the same reason. He'd mean it tomorrow. If Alec walked up the path from the jetty tonight, Reggie would go down the step to meet him at the bottom and turn him round, as gently as he knew how. Alec knows that. It's why he's never come.

And still he lights the lamp.

He's never asked himself what for. He asks now, lying awake. It isn't for Alec coming; he's known that for nine years, under everything. It isn't even for Alec knowing. Alec doesn't know it's there. Nobody's told him, and nobody will. It's a lamp on a table on a rock four thousand miles from the only man it could be for, and Reggie is forty-six and a Fellow of the Zoological Society, and he knows exactly how absurd that is.

He can't put it out.

He knows why, lying there. If he put it out he'd be a man who'd sent Alec away over Frank and then got on with things, as though the *no* had cost him nothing. And it has to cost something. If it doesn't cost anything, it wasn't a principle. It was convenient. The lamp is what it costs. It's the only way he's had of paying it where he can see.

And now here he is, on the camp bed, with his mouth on the man Alec made him keep out of the file.

He doesn't know what Alec would make of that. That's the thing he can't get past. He can't ask. He's got nothing to ask with but the form, and the form hasn't got a word in it for this. And he's frightened, in a way that he doesn't know how to hold, that if he found some way to say it Alec would read it straight back to the spare hut. *So it was never the principle. You sent me away over Frank, and nine years later you've let a man up your path anyway.* And Reggie doesn't know what he'd answer. He'd have known a month ago. A month ago he didn't know he'd been leaning on a man on a step since January.

He doesn't tell Pascual about the three in the morning. He isn't sure yet. He's decided to know what he's doing, and this is a thing he doesn't know, and he'd rather not hand it to Pascual till he's got it the right way up.

---

In February they go out in the launch.

Pascual will say afterwards that it was a mistake to go out in the launch, and he'll be wrong.

It's the last Saturday of the month, and flat calm, the first real calm since Christmas. There's a count to be done of the young males off the second head, which is a thing you do in a launch, and it takes two, one at the tiller and one with the glasses and the book. They've done it a hundred times. They do it this time. They get the count by eleven, forty-one, which is good, and write it down. Then Pascual takes them round the second head and out past the reef edge, where you can see the whole island laid out like a map and nobody on the island can see you. And he cuts the engine.

The quiet comes down like a hand.

Reggie puts the book in the locker and looks at him from the thwart.

"We've done the count," says Pascual.

"I know. I wrote it down."

"There's nobody for two miles."

"I know that too."

"Then *come here*," says Pascual, and Reggie laughs, and comes.

It's the best it's been. It's the first time in daylight, the first time with no roof over them and no row within earshot, with the sun hot on the back of Pascual's neck and the boat moving under them on the long slow swell. Reggie's in his shirtsleeves and he's lost his hat. He's kneeling up on the bottom boards between Pascual's knees with his arms round Pascual's neck and kissing him as if they'd got all the time in the world. In the long room they kiss as if they've got ten minutes before a student comes in. There's salt on Reggie's mouth. There's sun in his hair, which has gone mostly grey at the sides since 1952 and which Pascual has, it turns out, wanted to put his hands in for a very long time. He puts his hands in it. Reggie makes a sound and pushes up into them, like the calf into a scrubbing brush.

It goes on. It goes on for a long time. Reggie's shirt comes open and Pascual's comes off, because it's hot, and the sun's on his bare back and Reggie's small cool hands are on his chest. Reggie's mouth is at his throat, and the boat's rocking, and somewhere past the reef edge a young male comes up and blows and goes down again, and neither of them looks.

And Pascual gets to the place.

He feels it come. He always does. It's like the step down off the edge of the reef into deep water, where the colour goes from green to black in a yard. He's there, with his hands on Reggie's back and the man's mouth on his collarbone and his own hips starting to move without his leave. And his whole body goes cold. It goes still and cold from the inside, the way it did on the camp bed at Christmas, and in the hut in January, and twice more since. And he hears his own voice say, "Reggie. Wait."

Reggie waits. He always does. He sits back on his heels on the bottom boards, flushed and breathing, with his shirt open, and waits.

And Pascual, sitting there in the sun with his heart going and his shirt off and no reason at all to stop, loses his temper.

"*Why* do you do that?"

Reggie blinks.

"Do what?"

"*That*. Stop. Wait. Every time. You just — *stop*, as if somebody'd pulled a line. You've done it since November. We get to the place and you stop."

"*You* stopped. Just now. You said wait."

"Because you were going to!"

"I was not going to." Reggie's voice is rising to meet his. There's nobody for two miles, and it goes up easily. "I wasn't going to stop at all. I was very much going on. *You* said wait."

"Because I could *feel* you going to —"

"You could feel nothing of the kind!"

"Don't you tell me what I can feel, Reggie Tar! I've had my hands on you for three months, I know what you —"

"Then you know I wasn't stopping!"

Pascual stares at him.

He's furious. He doesn't know why. The launch rocks on the swell, and the sun beats on his neck, and he's shouting at the man he's wanted since May, in the middle of the sea, with his shirt off. And he knows, as he does it, the way you know a bone's broken before you've looked, that he's shouting because it's easier than the other thing.

"It's three months," he says. "Three months, *hombre*. I've been so patient you could stand a lamp on me."

"I know you have."

"And every time we get there, somebody stops, and I don't know if it's you or me, and if you don't want to —"

"I want to."

"Then *why* —"

"Because you're not ready."

The launch rocks.

"*I'm* not ready."

"No."

"I've been ready since *May*!"

"You haven't," says Reggie. He isn't shouting now. He's gone quiet, and that's worse, the way it always is with him. "You've wanted to since May. That's a different thing. I know it's a different thing, because I've got both, and they're not the same. You're not ready, Pascual, and I'm not either, and I've been waiting since November for one of us to say so."

"Don't you *read* me."

"I've started," says Reggie. "You asked me to. On the step. You said you'd been waiting four months for me to notice. I'm noticing."

"I didn't ask you to *read* me, I asked you to —" Pascual stops. He doesn't know what he asked. He puts both hands over his face. His ears are burning, and not from the sun. "*Dios*, I hate it. Thirteen years I've watched you do it to the whole colony and thought, *at least he doesn't do it to me*."

"And now I do."

"And now you *do*, and it's —"

"Is it horrible?"

"*Yes*."

"I'm sorry."

"It's *wonderful*," says Pascual, into his hands, "and it's horrible, and I don't know what I'm frightened of, Reggie. That's what I hate. I'm a vet. I find the place and I put my hand on it."

Reggie doesn't say anything. After a moment the boat shifts as he moves on the bottom boards. Then there's a hand on Pascual's wrist, not pulling, only there.

"Shall I tell you what I read?"

"No."

"All right."

"*Yes*. God help me. Yes."

"You've not been with a man," says Reggie.

Pascual takes his hands off his face.

It isn't a question. Reggie isn't asking it. He's saying it the way Pascual says *it's the teeth*, and he's right, and Pascual sits in the sun and feels it go into him like a hand onto the place.

"No," he says.

"That's some of it."

"That's some of it," Pascual agrees. His voice has gone hoarse. "I've been with women, and I was good at it. I'd like that on the record. I was *good*."

"I don't doubt it."

"And now I get to the place with you and I don't know where anything goes." He can't finish it. He finishes it anyway, because it's there. "And you've *done* this."

"Yes."

"With him."

"Yes," says Reggie. "With him."

"For years."

"For some years."

"Then *that's* it." It comes out of him all at once, and it's the thing. He knows it's the thing as soon as it's out, the way he'd know the right tooth. "You're going to have *him* to measure against. Eight years and a lamp. I'd be *examined*, Reggie. I'd be old Mrs Bourne's Sunday dinner, and I'd fail, and you'd be kind about it."

He waits.

He waits for Reggie to say *don't be absurd*. He waits for *of course not*, or for the voice. None of it comes.

Instead Reggie laughs.

It's a real laugh, sudden and helpless, surprised out of him. He puts his forehead down on Pascual's bare knee and laughs into it, and the boat rocks.

"*Don't you laugh*," says Pascual.

"I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I'm not — Pascual." Reggie lifts his head. His face is wet at the corners. "You're not Sunday dinner. I'm not old Mrs Bourne. There isn't an examination. There isn't a *mark* —"

"You can't know that."

"I *can*. Listen. Listen to me." He's got both of Pascual's wrists now. "I'll tell you what I'm frightened of, and then you'll see. You think I've stopped every time because of you. Some of the times it was. Most of them, it wasn't. Most of them it was *him*."

Pascual goes still.

"Not the way you think," says Reggie. "Not comparing. God, no." He looks down at their hands. "I stopped because I can't go to bed with you while he doesn't know. That's the whole of it." He stops. "I sent him away, Pascual."

Pascual goes still.

"*You* did."

"Me. In the spare hut, the week before he left. He'd done a sum that winter, and a man in England paid for it who'd paid for me once already, and nobody asked him." Reggie's voice is very quiet. "I wasn't going to be the man who had what he wanted with somebody else paying. So I told Alec I couldn't come up his path. He said I was right."

"And the lamp."

"He doesn't know about it. He's never seen it. It's for me." Reggie looks at the reef edge. "So the *no* costs something. It's absurd, I know it is. But I'll not put it out, and I'll not go past the place with it lit and him not knowing about you."

"You can't tell him. You've said. The form."

"I know I can't. That's what I've been doing at three in the morning since November. Trying to find a way. The form hasn't got a word in it for you."

"*Hombre*."

"And there's the other thing." He makes himself say it. Pascual watches him make himself. "If I told him, he'd read it straight back to the spare hut. He'd think, *so it was never the principle. You sent me away over Frank, and nine years on you've let a man up your path anyway.*"

"Reggie —"

"And I don't know what I'd say. I'd have known a month ago. I thought I knew what I was doing on the step, too."

It's quiet in the launch. The swell lifts them and sets them down. Somewhere past the reef edge a young male blows.

"So," says Reggie. "You see. That's mine. You've been lying there thinking I was comparing you with him. And I've been lying there thinking about a path. And neither of us said."

Pascual looks at him for a long time.

"Who's paying?" he says at last.

Reggie looks up.

"You sent him away because somebody else was paying. A man in England who'd not been asked." Pascual's hand has come flat onto the bottom boards between them. "So who's paying for this? I've been asked. I've said. You've been asked. There's nobody else in it."

"You can't know that."

"I can. I've watched you keep that rule for nine years, at a lamp, every Saturday. I know what you look like keeping it." He takes one of his wrists back, and puts his hand flat on the side of Reggie's face. "That's not the principle broken, Reggie. That's the principle kept. It's the first time since 1957 you've had something and nobody else has paid for it."

Reggie shuts his eyes.

"Tell him," says Pascual. "Find a way. You're the clever one. And not for my sake. I'll wait."

"Then whose?"

"Because you'll not be able to live with yourself if you don't," says Pascual, "and I'll not be able to live with you."

"And the other thing? Yours?"

Pascual breathes out.

It's still there. He can feel it, the cold place at the edge of the reef. But it's smaller now, with a name on it. You can work with a thing once it's got a name.

"I'll be clumsy," he says.

"Probably."

"And slow."

"I'm counting on slow."

"And I'll not know where anything goes."

"Then I'll show you," says Reggie. He opens his eyes, and they're quite clear, and there's nothing in them at all but Pascual. "You'll tell me what you like, and I'll tell you. And the first time won't be very good, and then there'll be a second. There isn't an examination. There's just the next time." His mouth goes at the corner. "You're a scientist. You don't get the result on the first run."

Pascual laughs. It hurts his chest.

"That's a terrible thing to say to a man," he says.

"Is it?"

"The *first run*."

"It's true."

"I know it's true. That's what's terrible about it." He pulls Reggie in by the back of the neck, and kisses him, hard, once, and lets him go. "When you've told him."

"When I've told him."

"And not before."

"Not before," says Reggie. "And meanwhile?"

Pascual looks at him, kneeling in the bottom of the launch in the sun with his shirt open and his hair all over the place and his mouth red. He looks at the island two miles off, where nobody can see them. He looks at the long slow swell, and the reef edge going from green to black.

"Meanwhile," he says, "there's nobody for two miles, and I've had my shirt off for half an hour for nothing."

"Not for *nothing* —"

"Come *here*," says Pascual.

---

They drift onto the reef at about a quarter past one.

Neither of them notices. Neither of them notices anything until the keel touches, with a long soft grinding noise like a man clearing his throat. Then the launch heels, gently, and stops, and there's coral a foot under the starboard side, bright and yellow and quite close.

Reggie lifts his head from Pascual's chest.

"Oh, *hell*," he says.

What happens next takes perhaps four minutes, and afterwards neither of them can remember saying a word. Pascual's got the pole out of the bottom of the boat and over the side before Reggie's got his shirt closed. Reggie's at the tiller with his eye on the swell, because you don't push off coral in a swell, you wait for the lift. *Wait. Wait. She's coming. Now.* And Pascual shoves, and the launch comes off with a lurch and a scrape, into the deep water. Reggie's got the engine going on the second pull, and swings her head out, and they're away from the reef edge. There's water in the bottom boards, but not much, and nobody's hurt, and the paint's gone off a foot of the keel.

Reggie sits at the tiller with the engine going and looks at the scrape, and at Pascual, bare to the waist with the pole in his hands, breathing hard.

"The Colonel," says Reggie, "is going to want to know how we put the Institute launch on the reef on a flat calm day in broad daylight with the chart in the locker."

Pascual looks at the island. He looks at the scrape. He looks at Reggie.

"The count," he says. "We were doing the count."

"We'd done the count. At eleven. It's in the book. Forty-one."

"We were doing it *again*. To check."

Reggie looks at him. Then he starts to laugh, at the tiller, with the engine going, until the launch wanders off her course and Pascual has to lean over and put his hand on his to straighten it. He leaves his hand there, on Reggie's, on the tiller, all the way back round the second head. They take it off at the jetty.

---

On the Sunday night Reggie tells him he's found a way.

He hasn't, quite. Pascual can see that. He's got the look of a man who's found the beginning of one at three in the morning, and sat up in the dark and said *oh* to nobody, and doesn't trust it yet. He won't say what it is. He doesn't hand you a thing before he's got it the right way up.

"It's in the form," is all he says, on the step, with a foot between them because the row's awake. "It's due in May. The acknowledgement."

Pascual looks at him sidelong.

"You said the form hadn't got a word in it for me."

"It hasn't," says Reggie. "It's got a *line*."

He won't say more. Pascual doesn't ask. He sits on the step with a foot of planking between them and doesn't ask, the whole length of the foot.

At half past ten Reggie gets up to go in. He doesn't say *well*. He stops at the door of the hut.

"Pascual."

"Mm."

"The feed sheet. With the inches."

Pascual's head comes round slowly.

"What about it?"

"You've stopped writing in it."

"How do you know I've —"

"Because I went and looked." Reggie holds out a hand. There's a folded sheet in it, gone soft at the creases. "It was in your drawer. Under the guava. I'm sorry. It's data."

Pascual stares at the sheet. His face goes through everything, in the dark, all of it.

"You *went in my drawer*."

"I did."

"You —"

"I added a line," says Reggie. "At the bottom. I thought it was incomplete."

Pascual takes the sheet. He unfolds it, slowly, and holds it to the light from the Colonel's window, and reads down the column in his own pencil. *3.ix: 14. 20.ix: 11. 8.x: 8. 29.x: 0.* And under it, in a small neat hand he'd know anywhere, the hand that wrote *Missed you. Bring back a guava*:

*16.xi: 0 (by accident). 18.xi et seq.: 0 (on purpose).*

Pascual doesn't say anything.

"Good night," says Reggie, and goes in, and shuts the door.

Through the shutters Pascual watches him cross to the table and turn the lamp down to its bead. Then he lies down on the bed in his shirt. His arm goes up over his head, and he's asleep inside a minute, as though there were nothing on his mind at all.

Pascual sits on the step for a long time with the feed sheet on his knee.

Then he folds it along its creases, very carefully, and puts it in his breast pocket, over his heart, and buttons his coat over it, and goes to bed.
