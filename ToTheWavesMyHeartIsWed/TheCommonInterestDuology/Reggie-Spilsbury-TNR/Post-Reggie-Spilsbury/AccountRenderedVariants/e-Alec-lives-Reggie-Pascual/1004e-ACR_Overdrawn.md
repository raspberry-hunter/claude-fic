# Overdrawn

On the third of September the gap on the step is fourteen inches.

Pascual knows it's fourteen because there's a knot in the planking of Reggie's step, a dark one the size of a shilling. On the night Esme's boat went round the point, Reggie shifted over to make room and stopped with his hip on the far side of it. Pascual's own hip was about a hand and a half from the other side. He noticed it without meaning to, because he'd sat down on that step three or four hundred times since 1952 and had never once looked at where anybody's hip was. Then he went to bed and lay in the dark and couldn't stop knowing it.

On the twentieth of September it's eleven.

On the eighth of October it's eight, and Reggie's knee is touching his whenever Reggie crosses his legs, which he does every few minutes. He uncrosses them with an absent little *sorry*, and crosses them again.

By the end of October there isn't a gap. There's Reggie's shoulder against his arm, and Reggie's weight coming on to him a bit more with every page of whatever he's pretending to read. At about half past ten Reggie says *well* and gets up and goes in, and the whole left side of Pascual goes cold at once, as though somebody had opened a door.

He's written it down. He isn't proud of that. It's on the back of a feed sheet in the drawer of his bench, in pencil, under the note that says *Calf ate 22 lb. Missed you. Bring back a guava*. It looks like a record of something going down in a tank. He looks at it sometimes and feels like a fool.

---

It isn't only the step.

It's the long table, where Reggie's chair has been at Reggie's end since 1950. The students have their end and the Colonel has his, and Reggie's always sat across the corner from Pascual, a table's width off, where they can argue properly. In October the chair starts to wander. It isn't all at once. One night it's round the corner a bit, and the next it's a bit further, and by November Reggie's eating his fish at Pascual's elbow. He's got his arm along the back of Pascual's chair so he can lean across and point at Pascual's weight sheets with his fork, which he does, to criticise the handwriting.

It's the north pen rail, where there's four yards of rail and Reggie stands on the one foot of it that Pascual is standing on.

It's the long room. Pascual's at the bench doing a drawing, and there's a breath on the back of his neck, and it's Reggie reading over his shoulder with his chin about an inch from it. He's saying *that's lovely, that fin, do the other one like that*, and he stays there for the whole of the other one, while Pascual's hand shakes.

It's the hands. Reggie takes his wrist to look at a scrape off the reef and holds it up to the light and turns it and says *you want iodine on that*. Then he stands there for some while holding it, talking about something else entirely, with his thumb resting where the pulse is. Pascual stands and lets him, and hears his own pulse go up under the man's thumb. He thinks, *he'll feel it, he'll feel it, he can't not feel it*. Reggie doesn't feel it. He lets go and goes on talking about the Admiralty.

Pascual tries moving.

He's a scientist. You test a thing. He sits at the far end of the step, hard up against the post, on a Tuesday in early November. On the Wednesday Reggie comes out and sits at the far end of the step, hard up against Pascual. He goes to the wrong end of the rail. Reggie drifts down the rail after him like a boat on a mooring, talking about calcium, and fetches up against his shoulder. He moves his chair back to the corner of the long table before Reggie comes in. Reggie comes in and moves his own chair round to the corner of the long table, and sits down at Pascual's elbow. He says *you've moved your chair, why've you moved your chair*, and doesn't wait for the answer.

"He follows me," Pascual says, in the office, to Hester.

Hester doesn't look up from the quarterly return.

"Like the calf," she says.

"*Exactly* like the calf. I move, he moves. He doesn't know he's doing it. He's the cleverest man in the colony and he's following me round the island like a *bucket*, Hester, and if I say anything he'll look at me as though I'd gone mad."

"Have you tried not moving?"

"Then he just *comes*."

"Yes," says Hester. She turns a page. "I should stop moving, Dr Pobil. You'll run out of step."

He stops moving. It's no better. It's a great deal worse.

---

He's been meaning to tell him since September. He knows that's what has to be done, and he knows nobody else is going to do it. He'd thought about it on the step in the dark with his face in his hands, the night Esme went. It'd be done slowly. It'd be done a step at a time. He'd find the right evening and say a plain thing, and let the man go away and think, and come back.

He hasn't found the right evening. Every evening he's got a man leaning on his arm, reading a book upside down. Every evening he opens his mouth and finds that what he'd have to say first is *you're leaning on me*. Then Reggie would look down and see that he was, and sit up at once, *sorry, sorry*. He'd move over fourteen inches and go in at ten, and not come out on the step again for a month. Pascual can see all that so clearly he could draw it.

So he doesn't say anything. He sits, and gets leaned on, and goes cold down the left side at half past ten, and writes it down in pencil on the back of a feed sheet. He is fifty-one years old. He's quite aware how that looks.

And his body's not been asked about any of it. It's been leaned on every night for six weeks by the one person it's decided it wants, and it's decided a good deal more than Pascual would have chosen. It wakes him at three with its heart going. It goes hot to the ears at the long table when a fork comes over his shoulder. It holds its breath at the bench when there's a chin an inch from its neck. He's had enough of it. He's had enough of the whole business. He'd like it on the record.

On the sixteenth of November the Tuesday boat comes in with a letter from Ålesund.

---

It's in the form. They always are.

Reggie reads it at the window of the office at a quarter past eleven, standing, the way Hester says he's read all of them. *Dear Sir. In accordance with the terms of deposit, I write to inform the Institute that the depositor of accession 197 remains resident at Kongens gate 18, Ålesund, and may be reached there. Yours faithfully, A. Spilsbury.* Four lines. It's dated September and it's come by sea. Hester will type the envelope for the acknowledgement, as she's typed every one since 1957.

He reads it twice. He reads it a third time for the typewriter, because he always does. He looks for a pressed-through letter or an overstrike, anything with a hand behind it, and there isn't one. There hasn't been one since 1960. Then he folds it along its creases, which are sharp, and puts it in his breast pocket, and goes down to the pens, and works.

He's fine. He's always fine on the day. He weighs the calf and has a row with the pump and tells a student his labels are a disgrace. At dinner he eats what Pascual puts in front of him. Pascual puts rather a lot in front of him, and then stands over it with his arms folded, which is a thing Pascual does on the Tuesdays when the boat's brought something from Ålesund. Reggie has noticed that. He's never said so. It's one of the kindest things anybody does for him.

After dinner a norther comes down.

They get two or three a winter this far south. The wind goes round in an hour and comes straight down off the sea from somewhere near Florida, cold and dry and hard. The palms along the row lash and rattle. The temperature drops fifteen degrees between the soup and the washing-up. The students run about shutting things. The Colonel says it's nothing to Poona.

Reggie takes the blanket off his bed and goes and sits on the step anyway.

He doesn't think about why. It's where he sits. He sits there most nights now; he's been sitting there since about the spring. It's pleasant, with the channel and the lights at the Bay. After a while Pascual comes along the row from his own hut in a jersey and his old boat coat, and stands at the bottom of the step and looks at him.

"It's a norther."

"I'd noticed."

"You'll freeze."

"I've got a blanket." Reggie holds up one side of it. "There's room."

Pascual looks at the blanket, and at him, for a moment longer than Reggie understands. Then he says something short in Spanish and sits down on the step and gets under it.

He's warm. That's the first thing, and it's very welcome. Pascual's a big man and runs hot, and Reggie's small and doesn't, and the wind's coming across the channel like a knife. Reggie moves in against him without thinking about it. He fits his shoulder into the space below Pascual's shoulder, where it goes, and pulls the blanket round the pair of them, and sighs.

Pascual's gone very stiff.

"You're cold," says Reggie.

"I'm not cold."

"You're like a board. Come here." He gets an arm round the man's back, under the coat, and pulls, and Pascual comes, all of a piece, like something being hauled out of the water. "Better?"

"*Reggie*."

"Mm."

Pascual doesn't say anything else. Reggie can feel his breathing, high and careful, through the jersey. It's odd. He puts it down to the cold.

They sit. The wind comes down the channel. The lights at the Bay go out one by one. The letter's in Reggie's breast pocket, over his heart, and he's aware of it the way he's always aware of them for a day or two, a flat stiff square against his ribs. It doesn't hurt. It hasn't hurt for years. It sits there, being four lines long.

He lets his head go down on to Pascual's shoulder. It's a good shoulder. It's always been a good shoulder, broad and solid, with the coat smelling of salt and tar and whatever Pascual washes his hands with in the long room. Reggie turns his face into it a little, out of the wind, and shuts his eyes, and breathes.

He isn't thinking about anything. That's the extraordinary thing, afterwards. It's the most peaceful he's been in a month. Pascual's arm has come round him under the blanket, stiffly, at last, as if it had been told to and didn't want to. The hand's settled on his hip and it's very warm and very still. Reggie turns his face a little further in, into the gap between the coat collar and the man's jaw, where it's warmest of all. There's stubble there, and the pulse under it, going hard. He can feel it against his mouth.

"*Reggie*," says Pascual again. It's hardly a word. It's a breath with his name in it.

Reggie lifts his head to see what's wrong.

And Pascual turns his, at the same moment, to say something. Their faces are an inch apart in the dark, in the wind, and Reggie's mouth brushes the corner of Pascual's as he turns. It's hardly anything. It's the sort of thing that happens on a narrow step in a high wind.

They both stop.

They stay stopped for one breath. Reggie can feel Pascual's breath on his lips, uneven. He can feel his own, going out to meet it.

Then neither of them is stopped.

---

He doesn't know who moves. He'll never know. There isn't a *who* in it. There's Pascual's mouth, and his own, and then there's nothing else on the step at all.

It isn't careful. It's the opposite of careful. Pascual's hand comes up off his hip and into his hair and takes hold, hard, and pulls his head back, and kisses him as if he'd been starved of it. Reggie makes a sound he doesn't recognise and gets two fists full of the man's jersey and pulls. The blanket goes somewhere. Reggie can taste the coffee from dinner and the salt of the wind on the man's mouth. The stubble drags at his chin and his upper lip, and he doesn't care, he wants more of it. He wants his whole face in it. He's pulling Pascual down to him by the jersey, and Pascual's coming, bending right over him on the step, and still he can't get him near enough.

"*Dios*," says Pascual into his mouth. "*Dios mío*, Reggie —"

"Don't talk."

"Somebody'll —"

"Don't *talk*."

He doesn't. He kisses him again instead, harder, with his teeth on Reggie's lower lip. His other hand's gone inside Reggie's jacket and it's spread flat on his back through his shirt, and it's hot, and it's pressing him in. He's pressing him in as if he's been meaning to do it for a very long while and has only this second been let.

Then he wrenches his mouth away.

"Not *here*." It's rough, almost angry, gasped out against Reggie's cheek. "Not on the step, *hombre*, the whole row —"

He's right. Reggie hears that he's right, somewhere a long way off, the way you'd hear a bell on another boat. He gets up. Pascual gets up with him, and neither of them lets go, because it doesn't seem possible to let go. They go back through the door of the hut in a sort of stagger. Pascual's back hits the frame and he laughs, breathless, into Reggie's mouth. Reggie gets a heel to the door and kicks it shut behind them, and it bangs, and the wind's gone.

It's warm in the hut. The lamp's lit on the table. It always is.

He doesn't look at it. He hasn't got anywhere to look but Pascual. Pascual's leaning back against the door in the lamplight with his coat half off one shoulder and his hair wild and his mouth wet. His face is doing everything. Every single thing a face can do, all of it, pointed straight at Reggie, open as a door. Reggie gets hold of the coat and drags it the rest of the way off him. It drops on the boards. And he goes up on his toes, because Pascual's a head taller, and gets both hands in the man's hair and pulls him down.

Pascual comes. He comes with a sound like something giving way. His arms go round Reggie, both of them, all the way, and lift. Reggie's heels come off the floor. He's been lifted by this man before, out of the water, out of a boat. He's never been lifted like this, with his mouth open under Pascual's and the man's heart going like a pump against his own and his whole body pulled up into him as if he weighed nothing.

"Put me down."

"No."

"You'll do your back —"

"*Now* who's talking," says Pascual, and walks him backwards across the hut, three steps, still kissing him.

The edge of the table catches Reggie across the backs of the thighs and he sits down on it hard. Pascual's between his knees, bent over him. Reggie's got his hands under the man's jersey, on the bare skin of his back, which is broad and hot and slick with sweat from the step and the stagger and all of it. And Pascual's mouth has gone down off his mouth to his jaw, and under his jaw, to his throat. Reggie tips his head back to give him room, and hears himself say *oh*, and *yes*, and *there*, in a voice he's never heard. Pascual's teeth are at his collar. Pascual's hand's at his shirt buttons, and it's fumbling, and it's shaking. Reggie puts his own hand back on the table to brace himself, because he's going over backwards.

And knocks the lamp.

It rocks. He hears the base go on the wood, the heavy knock and scrape of it, and the flame jumps and roars up in the chimney. And his hand does what his hand does. It's done it since the Labs, before he's thought anything at all. It goes out and grabs the chimney to steady it.

The glass is very hot.

He feels it the way you feel cold water, a full second late. By then he's steadied the lamp, and it's standing upright in the middle of the table, burning. The flame settles back down. And he's sitting there with three fingers of his right hand still wrapped round the chimney, looking at it.

It's the lamp.

It's the lamp he's lit every night since the spring of 1957. It's the one he sits up by. It's the lamp Pascual fills on Saturdays. It's the lamp for a man in Ålesund who writes to him twice a year in the form. That man wrote to him this morning. The letter's in Reggie's breast pocket, four lines long, over his heart. And Reggie is sitting on the table with his shirt half open and another man's hands on him. And he doesn't know how he got here.

He lets go of the chimney.

"Reggie?"

He's looking at his hand. There are three white stripes across the insides of the fingers, going red at the edges as he watches.

"*Reggie*. Your hand. *Ay*, your hand, let me —"

"What am I doing," says Reggie.

He says it quite quietly. He isn't asking. He hears it come out of himself in a flat voice, very slow. He looks up from his hand at Pascual, who's standing between his knees with his jersey rucked up and his face gone from one thing to another in the space of a second.

"Pascual. What am I *doing*."

---

"You've burnt yourself. That's what you're doing." Pascual's already moving. He's got Reggie's wrist, and he's turning him, and he's got the enamel jug off the washstand and the water in the basin in the same movement. He puts Reggie's hand in it, hard, to the knuckles, and holds it there. "In the water. Keep it there. Don't *move* it."

The water's cold from the norther. It bites. Reggie sits on the table's edge with his hand held down in a basin, and he's shaking. He hadn't noticed he was shaking. It's coming up from somewhere in the middle of him in long waves, and he can't stop it.

"I was —" he says. "We were —"

"Yes."

"On the *step*."

"Yes."

"I don't —" He can't get his breath. "I do not know what I am doing. I do not know what I *did*. I was sitting on the step. I was sitting on the step with you and I — how did I —"

"Keep your hand in the water."

"*Pascual* —"

"I'm not talking about anything until that hand's been in the water five minutes. It's what I'd do for the calf." Pascual's voice is quite level. His face isn't. "So you keep it there. And you breathe. And then you can have your fit."

Reggie keeps it there. He breathes. It doesn't work very well. Pascual's standing beside him with one big hand round his wrist, holding it under, and the other flat on his back between the shoulder blades. After a minute Reggie realises that the hand on his back is doing exactly what he'd do for a man who'd come out of the water. It's pressing, steadily, slowly, as though the breathing could be put back in from outside.

"How long," says Reggie.

Pascual doesn't pretend not to understand.

"Since about May."

"*May*."

"You sat closer on the step every night from September. I measured it."

"You *measured* —"

"There's a knot in the plank. It was fourteen inches in September." Pascual's ears are going red in the lamplight. "Tonight it was nothing."

"Nothing."

"Since the end of October. You lean on me, Reggie. Every night. Then at half past ten you say *well* and go in, as if you'd been sitting on your own."

Reggie stares at him.

He goes back through it. He can't not. It's what he does, and he does it now in a rush, sitting on the table with his hand in a basin, and it all comes at once. There's the step. There's the chair at the long table, and he'd thought, when Pascual moved his, *why's he moved his chair*. There's the rail. There's the chin at the bench. There's Pascual's wrist in his hand with the pulse going under his thumb, and him standing there talking about the Admiralty for five minutes, because he liked it. He just liked it, and hadn't asked himself why.

He'd thought it was the norther, on the step tonight. He'd thought the man was *cold*.

"Oh, God," says Reggie. "Oh, *God*, Pascual. I've been — for *months* — and you've been —" He can't finish it. "Why didn't you *say*?"

"Say what? *Reggie, you're leaning on me*?" Pascual's voice cracks up the middle. "You'd have sat up. You'd have said *sorry, sorry*, and moved over fourteen inches, and gone in at ten, and not sat on that step again till Easter. I know you. I've known you since 1952."

"And that would've been *better* —"

"*Would* it?"

Reggie opens his mouth, and shuts it.

"You'd still have been doing it," says Pascual. "Only you'd have been doing it from further away. That's all. It wasn't going to stop because you moved over. It's not in the *step*."

"Then where —"

"*May*," says Pascual. "The Governor."

Reggie looks at him blankly.

"The Governor and the Nunc Dimittis. At dinner. When I said I was going to the Bournes' for Sunday dinner with her mother." He's watching Reggie's face. He's watching it very closely. "You did the voice."

The water's cold round Reggie's fingers.

"I did not."

"You did. Ten minutes. By the clock over the door. I watched the hand go round." Pascual says it quite flatly, like a figure off a weight sheet. "Hester watched it too."

"It's for — Pascual, it's only ever for —"

"One name. I know. I've watched you do it for him for eight years. And you did it for me going to dinner with Esme Bourne. Then you looked up from your fish and smiled at me as if you'd been talking about the tide."

Reggie sits on the table.

He doesn't remember it. He remembers the story, the Governor and the hat and the Colonel's father and the goat. He remembers Hester saying *1929*. He remembers being pleased with it, and the students laughing, and looking down at his plate after, and being a bit surprised by the fish. And he remembers looking down the table and seeing Pascual, and being glad of him, simply glad, the way you're glad of a light in a window.

He doesn't remember the lift in the chest, or the face settling. He never remembers those. You don't. You only find out afterwards, from what's been done.

He takes his hand out of the water.

"Put it *back*."

He puts it back.

"It comes for things I don't want seen," he says. "That's what it's for."

"I know what it's for."

"It comes when there's something in me that mustn't be —" He stops. He's looking at the lamp. "It came for *Esme*. For you, going to dinner with Esme. Pascual. You didn't go to Kingston."

Pascual doesn't answer.

"She asked you. You told me on the step. She'd have had you."

"Reggie —"

"And you didn't go." It's coming up out of him faster than he can stop it, and there's a terrible cold thing at the bottom of it that he knows. He's known it since he was thirty, in a lift, for four days. "You didn't go because of *me*. And I was sitting on a step not knowing, and you paid for it. A *wife*, Pascual. And I didn't even have the decency to know what I was —"

"Stop it."

"— taking. I'd no *right* —"

"*Stop it*." And Pascual's got hold of him, both hands on his shoulders, and the basin slops over onto the table. "You don't get to *do* that. You don't get to make it into something you did to me. *I* chose. I stood on the church path with a good woman's hand on my arm and I thought about you on this step waiting for a boat with nobody on it, and I *chose*, and it was the most honest thing I've done in years. You'll not take it off me and turn it into a debt."

Reggie looks up at him.

"She knew," says Pascual, quieter. "Before I did, near enough. She patted my face. She said she hoped they had the sense to know it." His mouth does something. "I said they didn't. She said they never do."

"She was right."

"She usually is. She's a headmistress now. God help St Andrew."

It's so unexpected that Reggie laughs. It comes out of him wet and broken in the middle of everything else, and he puts his wet burnt hand over his face, and Pascual says *not the burnt one, the other one, ay, you're hopeless*, and takes it away again, and puts it back in the basin.

---

When the five minutes are up Pascual dries the hand very gently on the corner of the sheet. He turns it to the lamp and looks at it the way he'd look at a flipper. Three stripes, and one's going to blister. He says *butter's no good, whatever the Colonel tells you*, and wraps it loosely in a clean handkerchief out of his own pocket.

Then he sits down on the bed, facing the table, with his elbows on his knees. There's the width of the hut between them, which is about five feet.

Reggie notices that. He notices it because it's the first time in weeks that he's noticed where Pascual is sitting, and it's much too far.

"I've got a letter in my pocket," he says.

"I know. The boat came. You ate everything I put in front of you."

"You do that every time."

"Every time."

"I'd seen you do it." He looks at his wrapped hand. "I thought it was the kindest thing anybody did for me. I didn't — I didn't wonder why."

"No. You wouldn't."

"I love him," says Reggie.

"I know you do."

"I've kept that lamp lit eight years. For a man I sent away." He stops. He's never once said the rest of it out loud, not to Jean, not to himself, not at three in the morning at the rail. "If he walked up from the jetty tonight I'd go down the step and turn him round. He knows I would. It's why he doesn't come."

Pascual stares at him.

"*You* sent him —"

"Me. In the spare hut, the week before he left. He'd done a sum that winter, and a man in England paid for it who'd been made to pay for me once already. Nobody asked him." Reggie's voice is flat. "I'll not be the man who has what he wants while somebody else pays. I left the Labs so as not to be. I left Alec for the same reason."

"*Hombre*." Pascual's looking at the basin, and the burnt hand in it, and back at Reggie's face. "That's what you did just now. With Esme. You made me into your man in England."

"Yes."

"And the lamp?"

"He doesn't know it's there." Reggie looks at it, burning on the table. "He's never seen it. Nobody's told him. It's a lamp on a table four thousand miles from anybody it could be for."

"Then what's it *for*?"

"So I've not stopped." It comes out flat. "If I put it out, I'd be a man who sent him away and got on with things, as though the *no* had cost nothing. And it has to cost something, or it wasn't a principle. It was just convenient." His mouth goes. "It's ridiculous. I know it's ridiculous."

"*Hombre*."

Reggie looks at the letter's corner showing at his breast pocket. "He writes twice a year in the form because the form's the only thing that's safe. And I sit up by the lamp. And I kissed you tonight with his letter in my pocket."

"I kissed you too."

"I know."

"I'd like that on the record. It wasn't only you, on that step." Pascual's voice is rough. "I've wanted to since May. Every night of you leaning on me, I've wanted to."

"Tonight —"

"Tonight you put your mouth on my neck and I turned round. I didn't do it by accident."

Reggie looks at him across the five feet.

"Pascual." He has to be careful. He has to be so careful. "I don't know what this is. I don't know what I am. An hour ago I'd have told you I was a man with a lamp lit for somebody in Norway, and that was all there was, and I'd have been quite sure."

"And now?"

"And now I've been leaning on you since *May*." It comes out wretched. "I read everybody. I can tell you what the Colonel had for breakfast from the way he holds his fork."

"You can."

"And I didn't read *myself*. Six months, with *you* right in front of me. I didn't see it, because I wasn't looking there. I was looking at the lamp."

"Hester says you're slow about anything that isn't a fish."

Reggie stares at him.

"*Hester* says?"

"She's known since Christmas." Pascual rubs his face. "Possibly Easter. She said so. In July. In the office, over the veterinary schedule."

"*Hester*."

"She's known longer than I have. She saw you do the feed for me in February and said you were insufferable for a week."

"I was not insufferable."

"You left a *note*."

Reggie opens his mouth, and remembers the note, and shuts it. He can feel himself going red. He can't remember the last time he went red.

"Oh, *God*," he says. "Oh, God, Hester Bevan. I'll have to leave the colony."

"You'll have to go into the office in the morning and have her look at you over her glasses."

"That's worse."

"Much worse," says Pascual, and his mouth goes at the corner. And for a moment, across the five feet, they're both nearly laughing, and the hut's nearly ordinary.

Then it stops being ordinary, because Pascual stops smiling.

"I'll tell you what I'm frightened of," he says. "Since we're doing it."

"Tell me."

"I'm frightened I'm *instead*." He says it straight out, and his whole face goes with it, as it always does. "He's not coming up that path. You've just told me you'd turn him round. And an hour before, you were on the step with me."

"Pascual —"

"I'll not be the thing you have because he's in Ålesund. I'd rather go to Kingston. I'd rather be *shot*."

"It isn't instead."

"You don't know that. You've just said you don't know what you are."

"No," says Reggie. "I don't know what I am. I know what you aren't." He looks at the lamp. He makes himself do it properly this time, while Pascual waits, the way he'd go through a set of figures that had come out wrong. "I sit up by that lamp for him. Since 1957. I sit on the step for — I've been sitting on the step since about *January*."

"On Sunday nights."

"On Sunday nights, with a book, thinking I was reading. And I'd hear the late boat, and you on the path, and say *oh, you're back*. And go in and go to sleep."

Pascual's face goes very still.

"By the lamp, I'm keeping faith with a man I'd not let in," says Reggie. "The step's not the same place. It's not even on the same side of the door." He hears his own voice go, and lets it. "You're not instead of him. You're *as well*. And the book was upside down. You told me."

"I didn't tell you. I thought it."

"You thought it very loudly."

Pascual makes a sound. He puts his hand over his mouth and looks at the floor.

"That's a terrible thing to say to a man," he says through his fingers.

"Is it?"

"*As well*. You're telling me there's two of us."

"Yes. I'm sorry. I'm telling you there's two of you." Reggie's hands are shaking again. The burnt one's throbbing inside the handkerchief. "I can't take him out, Pascual. I don't think I'd want to. I sent him away and he went, and he's paid for it every day since. So have I."

"I know."

"The lamp's the only thing I've had since. He doesn't even know it's lit. And I don't know whether I can put it out. Not tonight. I'm not going to lie to you and say I can."

"I've not asked you to put it out."

"You'd be entitled to."

"I fill the bloody *thing*," says Pascual. "Every Saturday. For eight years. I thought I knew what it was for. Seems I didn't, quite. It doesn't matter." He takes his hand off his mouth. "I'm not asking you to put it out. I'm asking you what you want."

---

Reggie sits on the table.

It's a plain question. It's the kind of question he asks students, *what do you want to know*, and then waits while they go red. He's never been very good at it put to him. He doesn't think anybody's put it to him in eight years. Alec used to, now and then, in the old days. And then Alec had done a sum across this very table in lamplight, about a man in Southport, and not asked the one person it cost.

"There's something else first," he says. "Before I can answer. You ought to know it."

Pascual waits.

"There's a file. In Kingston. From the inspection in 1957."

"*Can't*," says Pascual. "You told me. Not won't. Can't."

"I still can't tell you the whole of it. If a man with a pen case ever asks you a question, you'll have to be able not to know." He makes himself look straight at him. "But there's a part that's yours. You've a right to it before you decide anything."

"Go on."

"There's a sentence in that file with my name on it. I wrote it to make a man stop looking at something. The thing he was looking at was you."

Pascual's face goes through it all, one thing after another, fast.

"Me."

"You were by the window. In the office. With the veterinary schedule. He looked at you, and then at me, and he wrote something down. And I made him stop." Reggie's voice is quite steady, which surprises him.

"*That's* why. *Can't*."

"That's why. And it hasn't gone away because it's 1965. A thing like tonight, if it were seen, isn't a scandal on this island. It's that file, open again."

Pascual's quiet for a long time.

"You've been carrying that," he says at last. "Eight years. For me. And not told me."

"Yes."

"*Hombre*." It isn't angry. That's the worst of it. It's something much worse than angry. "You stupid, stupid — you *idiot* —"

"I know."

"You didn't know. You didn't even know what I *was* to you. And you carried it anyway."

"I knew you were my friend," says Reggie. "That was enough to carry it for."

Pascual gets up off the bed.

It's two steps. He comes across the hut and stands in front of the table, where he was an hour ago, and doesn't touch him.

"I'm not frightened of a file," he says. "I'd like that on the record too. They can have my licence and I'll go and do horses somewhere. I'm frightened of *one* thing, and it isn't Kingston."

Reggie looks up at him.

"You've told me the sum. Good. Now it's mine as well, and I get to say." Pascual looks at the lamp, and back. "So answer me. What do you want?"

Reggie looks up at him.

He doesn't reach for anything. He waits to see if the voice comes, and it doesn't come. There's nothing to hide. It's all out on the table, the lamp and the letter and the file and the burnt hand.

"I want you to come here," he says.

Pascual comes.

"I want —" His hand's on Pascual's jersey, the good hand, flat. He can feel the heart under it. "I want to go on doing what we were doing. Knowing I'm doing it. I haven't known about a single thing I've done for six months."

"No."

"I don't want to go to bed with you tonight. Not with his letter in my pocket and my hand like this." He hears how that sounds and doesn't take it back. "I'd like to do that properly, when I do it. When I know what I'm doing. If you'll wait."

"I've waited since May."

"I know. I'm asking you to wait a bit longer. It's a great deal to ask."

"It is," says Pascual. "It is a great deal to ask." But his face has gone soft all over, round the eyes and at the mouth, the way Reggie's watched it go for the calf and never, he thinks, for anybody else. "What else?"

"I want you to stay. I don't want you lying in your hut ten yards away through the wall knowing all this, while I lie here knowing it." He stops. "I'd like to go to sleep leaning on you. On purpose. Knowing it's you."

Pascual looks at him for a long moment.

"And the lamp?"

Reggie looks at it.

He'd like to put it out. It'd be a lovely thing, a clean thing: to reach over and turn it down to nothing and say *there*. And it'd be a lie. He'd be doing it for Pascual, to show him. And tomorrow night he'd come in and stand in the dark and not know what to do with his hands.

"I'm going to turn it down," he says. "Not out. Down."

Pascual watches him do it. He reaches over with his left hand, because the right's in the handkerchief, and turns the wick down till the flame's a small blue bead at the bottom of the chimney, hardly anything. The hut goes nearly dark, with a little light left at the table.

"That's honest," says Pascual, very low.

"It's what I've got."

"It'll do." Pascual's hand comes up to his face, slowly, giving him every chance. It rests along his jaw, over the rough place where his stubble dragged it raw. "May I?"

Reggie laughs. He can't help it. It comes out shaky.

"You're asking?"

"I'm asking. We've done it the other way. The other way you burnt your hand."

"Yes," says Reggie. "Yes. *Please*."

It isn't anything like the step. Pascual bends his head and kisses him slowly, the way you'd put your hand on an animal you'd frightened, and keeps his mouth there, and doesn't press. Reggie's good hand goes up behind Pascual's head, into the hair at the back of his neck. He holds him there, not pulling this time, only holding, and kisses him back. He knows exactly what he's doing, every second of it. He knows whose mouth this is. He knows he wants it. He can't remember being so sure of a thing he couldn't measure.

---

The bed was built for one man in 1950 by a carpenter who didn't hold with comfort. It turns out to be possible, just. Pascual lies on his back against the wall with his arm out. Reggie lies along him on his side with his head on Pascual's shoulder, where it goes, and his burnt hand held up on Pascual's chest out of harm's way, and his knee over Pascual's knee, because there's nowhere else for it.

They're both still dressed. Pascual's got the blanket back from the step and put it over them. Outside, the norther goes down the channel and rattles the shutters. The lamp's a bead of blue on the table.

Reggie lies there and knows it. He knows exactly where he is, and whose shoulder, and that he's leaning with the whole of his weight on purpose. It's quite different. He hadn't known it could be quite so different.

"Pascual."

"Mm."

"You wrote it down. The inches."

A long pause.

"On the back of a feed sheet," says Pascual, into the dark. "In the drawer. With the note about the guava."

"You *kept* the note about the guava."

"Go to sleep, Reggie."

"I'd like to see it. The sheet."

"You'll not."

"It's data."

"It's *mine*," says Pascual, and his chest shakes under Reggie's cheek, and his arm tightens. "Go to *sleep*. You've a hand to mind, and Hester in the morning."

Reggie shuts his eyes.

He isn't going to sleep. He's sure of that. There's the letter in his pocket, pressed flat between them, four lines long, and a sentence in a file, and a lamp turned down on the table, and a man under his cheek whose heart is still going far too fast. He'll lie here till it's light and go through all of it, one thing at a time.

He's asleep inside a minute.

---

Hester looks up from the quarterly return at a quarter past eight.

She looks at Dr Tar, in the doorway, with his right hand in a clean bandage. She looks at Dr Pobil behind him, about a foot behind him, not the usual three. Then she looks back at Dr Tar, over her glasses, for rather a long time.

Pascual watches Reggie go red to the ears. It's the first time he's seen that happen since 1952. He'd have paid a great deal of money to see it, and here it is for nothing, at a quarter past eight on a Wednesday.

"Good morning, Hester," says Reggie.

"Good morning, Dr Tar." Hester's eyes go down to the bandage. "You've burnt yourself."

"The lamp."

"Ah." She considers it. "I believe they do that, if you knock the table."

Reggie makes a very small sound.

"I gather," he says, with enormous dignity, "that you've known since Christmas."

Hester picks up her pencil.

"Possibly Easter, Dr Tar," she says. "I shall want the acknowledgement to the depositor for Tuesday's boat." She makes a small neat mark on the return. "In the form."

"In the form," says Reggie.

"Of course." Hester turns a page. "Dr Pobil, the veterinary schedule. And you'll want to put some gentian violet on that hand, not butter, whatever the Colonel says. Close the door on your way out. There's a draught."

They go out. Pascual closes the door. They stand on the verandah in the cold clear morning after the norther, with the channel gone flat and silver and the calf blowing in the north pen. Reggie's got his bandaged hand held against his chest.

He leans. He leans on Pascual's arm, on the verandah, at a quarter past eight in the morning, in full view of the long room windows. He does it quite deliberately, for about three seconds, and Pascual feels him know it the whole time.

Then he straightens up, and says *well*, and goes down the steps to the pens.

Pascual stands where he's been left, with the cold coming in down his left side where the man was.

It's no better. It's a great deal worse. He'd like it on the record.
