# Material Facts

Esme's letter comes on the Tuesday boat in the third week of November 1967. She writes every two months, in a small upright schoolroom hand, about her girls and the St Andrew heat and the headmistress of the school across the road, who is a fool. This one's shorter than usual. She's going to marry a surgeon at the University Hospital called Everald Mair. He's a widower, and very kind, and makes her laugh, and doesn't talk at dinner at all, which she finds she misses less than she'd expected. She hopes Pascual will come to the wedding at Easter. And there's a postscript, in the same hand, a little smaller.

*Have you told them yet? I'm not going to wait for you to be happy before I am, you know. I did wait two years, which I think was generous.*

Pascual sits on his bed in his hut with the letter on his knee and says a word in Aragonese that his grandmother would have washed his mouth out for.

It's been two years and three months since the church path. He's counted.

He's told himself every month of it that he'd do it the next month. There's always been a reason not to. In 1966 the inspector came, the young one in a hat, and read the file on the second morning. Reggie sat through the look, and came out of the office afterwards with nothing on his face, and went down to the north pen with no bucket. You don't tell a man a thing like that the week the file's been read. That winter the calf's mother was ill, and you don't tell a man a thing like that when he's sleeping on a camp bed by the pen. In the spring there was a dead calf at the south end, the first in six years. In the summer Jean Leslie wrote from the Menai Strait to say she'd got her doctorate and was coming home, and Reggie walked round the station for a fortnight with a face like Christmas. You don't take a thing like that off a man.

And all the while, every night, Reggie on the step.

He's still no idea. That's the part that makes Pascual want to throw a bucket at him. Two years. He's still waiting up on Sunday nights, and pushing the marmalade across, and putting his hand on the back of Pascual's neck for a moment too long, and looking as though he doesn't know it's been there. In June Pascual came back from the Bay with a cut hand, nothing, a gaff, and Reggie took one look at it on the jetty and went white. He sat Pascual down on a crate, dressed it himself with his mouth in a line, and then was short with everybody all afternoon without the faintest idea why.

He reads *everybody*. He read a young man in a hat in 1966 in about a minute and a half, Pascual watched him do it through the office door. He reads students and Colonels and men from the Admiralty. He's never once been pointed at the man in the next hut.

Pascual folds Esme's letter up and puts it in the drawer with the note about the guava.

Saturday will be fifteen years since he came off the Kingston boat with two suitcases and no English worth the name, and walked up the jetty. There'd been a man at the top of it with a clipboard, who'd looked at him for about four seconds and said *you'll be the vet, thank God, there's an animal in the north pen that won't take fish and I don't know why*. Pascual had known why inside ten minutes, and said so, and the man had said *you're wrong*, and he hadn't been.

Fifteen years. Reggie will have remembered. Reggie remembers every date that isn't his own birthday.

*Saturday*, Pascual thinks. *Saturday, and I don't care what's wrong with the calf.*

---

Reggie has remembered. He comes out onto the step at nine on the Saturday with a bottle of the Colonel's brandy that the Colonel doesn't know about, and two glasses, and sits down beside Pascual and pours.

"Fifteen years," he says. "To the vet."

"To the vet."

They drink. It's very good brandy. The row's dark except for the students' lamps, and the channel's flat under a moon that's nearly full. There are lights at the Bay, and the calf's mother blows every forty seconds in the north pen, and somewhere up the row somebody's playing a gramophone badly.

"You said I was wrong," says Pascual. "The first day. About the animal in the north pen."

"You were wrong."

"I was right. It was the teeth."

"It was the teeth *and* the water temperature."

"It was the teeth," says Pascual, "and you were too proud to say so in front of Hester, and you've been saying *and the water temperature* for fifteen years so as not to lose."

Reggie laughs. It's the real laugh, the short one, from the chest.

"Probably," he says. "Yes. Probably." He turns his glass round in his fingers. "Fifteen years. God. I'd have been lost without you. I'd have been dead, very likely, in about 1955. You know that."

"I know that."

"Good," says Reggie, simply, the way he said it on this step two years ago, and leans back against the doorpost, pleased with the brandy and the moon and the date. His shoulder's against Pascual's, and he hasn't noticed.

Pascual looks at the shoulder.

"Reggie," he says.

"Mm."

"You're in love with me."

---

There's a very long silence.

The gramophone up the row gets to the end of its side and scratches, and somebody takes the needle off. The calf's mother blows. Reggie sits on the step with his glass halfway to his mouth and doesn't move at all.

Then he laughs. It isn't the real laugh. It's a startled noise, a little high.

"I'm *what*?"

"You heard me."

"Pascual, you've had one glass of the Colonel's brandy, it's not *that* —"

"May 1965," says Pascual. "At dinner. I said I was going to Sunday dinner with Esme and her mother, and you did the thing."

"What thing?"

"*The* thing. The thing you do. The lamp. The shoulders." Pascual doesn't look at him. He looks at the moon, because it's easier. "The Governor and the Nunc Dimittis. Ten minutes by the clock over the door. I timed it. Hester saw it too."

"*Hester* —"

"It comes on for one thing, *hombre*. It's come on for one thing since 1957, and I've watched it fifty times. You did it for Esme Bourne's mother. And then you looked up and smiled at me over your fish as if you'd been talking about the weather."

Reggie's gone very still.

"You're on this step every Sunday night," says Pascual. "When I come back on the late boat. You've been on it since January 1965. You say *oh, you're back*, as if you're surprised."

"I like the step."

"You had a book upside down on your knee the night Esme went to Kingston. You push the marmalade at me when I didn't have any the day before. You don't know what *you* ate the day before."

"That's not —"

"In June I cut my hand on a gaff and you went the colour of the wall. You dressed it yourself and then bit the head off every student on the island till dinner."

"It was a deep cut."

"It was a *scratch*."

"Pascual —"

He makes himself go on. "And the night she went, you breathed out like a man they'd let off a hanging. And you said you'd have minded a great deal. And then you went to bed happy, and you had no idea. You've had no idea for two and a half years."

He runs out. He sits on the step with his glass in both hands and his face doing whatever it's doing, which he can't help and isn't going to try to.

Reggie says nothing for a long time.

Pascual doesn't look at him. He can't. He hears him put the glass down on the step, very carefully, and he hears him breathe.

And then he feels it happen.

He's watched it happen to other people a thousand times. He's watched Reggie turn round in a room and be pointed at somebody, a student, a Colonel, a man in a hat, and read them. It takes about four minutes. You can see it go through him like a current. He goes very quiet and very still, and his eyes go a little unfocused, as though he were looking at something written a long way off. And then it's done, and he knows, and he's never once been wrong.

Pascual has never been on the far end of it. Nobody's ever been on the far end of it who was also sitting right next to him.

He's not on the far end now either. That's the thing. Reggie isn't reading Pascual. Pascual can feel him not doing it. He's turned it round. He's sitting on his own step, pointed at himself for the first time in his life, reading.

It takes about four minutes. Pascual counts it. He can't help it.

At the end of it Reggie says, in a voice Pascual has never heard him use, "Oh, *God*."

"Yes."

"Oh, God. Pascual."

"*Yes*."

"The *marmalade*," says Reggie, in a sort of despair.

And Pascual, who has been more frightened in the last ten minutes than at any time since he came off the Kingston boat, laughs out loud, and can't stop, and has to put his glass down on the step.

---

Reggie sits on the step and looks at the moon and doesn't hear Pascual laughing, or hears it from a long way off.

He's looking at everything. It's all there. It's been there the whole time, and he walked past it every day like the Colonel's plaque. The step on Sunday nights. The marmalade. The gaff, and the shaking hands he'd put down to the heat. The book. He remembers turning the book the right way up, and not knowing why it had been upside down. He remembers the Governor. He'd thought it was a good story. He'd thought everybody laughed.

He remembers breathing out.

He remembers, going further back, a note on a bench. *Missed you. Bring back a guava.* He'd written it in February 1965, and gone back up the row pleased with himself, and thought nothing of it. It was the plainest sentence he'd written in eight years.

And before that. He goes on back, now he's started, because he can't stop. He goes back through the tank drawings, and the pen taken out of his hand at midnight, *no*. He goes back through the Saturdays, the lamp filled, a plate put down in front of him and a man standing there till something had gone off it. There's 1954, and a man shouting at him across the north pen in two languages, and Reggie thinking *thank God, somebody who'll tell me*. There's November 1952, and a man coming off the Kingston boat and being right about the teeth.

Fifteen years. And he'd known the whole of it, every day, and never once read it.

He turns his head and looks at Pascual.

Pascual's stopped laughing. He's sitting on the step with his hands round his knees, looking at him, and his face is doing everything. Reggie has looked at that face every day for fifteen years and never once let himself see what's on it. He sees it now, all of it at once. It's too much. It's like looking at the sun.

"And you," Reggie says. It isn't quite a question.

Pascual's ears go red. In the moonlight they go black.

"Since the church path," he says. "In August 'sixty-five. Don't look at me like that. I didn't ask for it either. I'd *done* this, Reggie. I got over it properly in 1956, and I checked, twice."

"You *checked*?"

"Twice. And then you went and did the Governor at me, and I had to look, and there it was again, and it wasn't even the same thing. It's worse. The first time I mostly wanted to hit you."

"You did hit me. In 1954. With a bucket."

"It was a *glancing* bucket." Pascual stops. "This one I don't want to hit you. Mostly. That's how I knew it was bad."

Reggie looks at him.

He wants — he finds it's the simplest thing he's wanted in eleven years. He wants to put his hand on the back of Pascual's neck, and not take it away. He can feel his hand wanting to do it. He can feel, now he's pointed at it, how many times it's wanted to and gone and done half of it and come away.

And then he goes cold all through.

He goes cold the way he went cold in the office in March 1957, with the inspector's pencil stopped over the pad, and the man's eyes going round the room, and stopping at the window.

"I can't," he says.

Pascual's face goes still.

"Not —" Reggie hears himself. He makes himself say the whole of it. "Not won't. Can't. Pascual, I can't. Not till I've told you something. And then you decide. You. Not me."

Pascual looks at him for a long time.

"*Not won't, can't*," he says slowly. "You said that in 'fifty-seven. The morning after the solicitors. With the buckets."

"Yes."

"I've been waiting ten years for you to tell me why."

"I know. I know you have." Reggie's hands have gone flat on his knees. "And I'm going to tell you now, because I've got to. If I don't, I'm doing what I did to Frank. I'm letting somebody pay for something without asking him first."

"Reggie —"

"And I won't. Not to you. I'd rather this didn't happen at all."

"*Reggie*."

"Let me," says Reggie. "Let me do it properly. Please. It's the only thing I've got."

Pascual shuts his mouth.

---

So he tells him.

He tells it the way he'd give a result, in order, plainly. He's been doing that since he was twenty-two, and it's the only way he knows to tell a thing that matters without the other voice getting into it.

The office in March 1957. Hester by the filing cabinet with her pad. Pascual by the window in the good chair, with a veterinary schedule on his knee, because the inspector wanted the vet present for the custody arrangements. The man in the hat, Halloran, an Ulsterman, polite, very thorough. The question.

*Did you know, Dr Tar, about Dr Spilsbury's disposition, when you brought him onto this station?*

"There were three answers," says Reggie. "If I'd said no, it was a lie, and he'd have known it was a lie, because it was his job to know. If I'd said yes, that was the licence gone, and the Institute, and everything on it."

"And the third?"

"If I'd said it was none of his business, he'd have written that down. And then he'd have looked round the room for somebody else to ask."

Pascual's sitting very still.

"He did look," says Reggie. "I watched him. He looked at Hester, and went past her. And he looked at the window."

"At me."

"At a man who'd been my friend for four years, and lived the other side of my wall, and had no wife, and whose name was on the licence under mine. As if you were a column that might add up."

Pascual's face has gone white under the brown.

"So I said it," says Reggie. "The true thing. *There's always been something about him, Mr Halloran.* In the voice. Leaning back. Doing the thing with my mouth. It was true, and it kept the licence, and it gave him the whole answer, so he'd got nothing left to look for. He wrote it down and said *that'll do*. And he never looked at the window again."

"He never asked me anything," says Pascual. His voice is quite small. "I thought it was because I was the vet."

"He wrote in the minute that he was satisfied no member of staff was implicated." Reggie makes himself look at Pascual. "That's what I couldn't tell you. If I'd told you in 'fifty-seven you'd have gone into that office and told him to put your name in instead."

"I'd have —"

"You'd have spent it. And it was bought. It was paid for with the worst thing I've ever said about the man I loved, on the record, for ever. It was worth it. I'd do it again tomorrow. But it was bought."

The calf's mother blows in the north pen. Pascual doesn't move.

"And this," says Reggie, "is exactly what he was looking for. Us. The man by the window. On a licensed station, with a file that gets read every three years, with my sentence in it in his writing."

"I'm still not married," says Pascual, quite quietly. "I know."

"And your name's still on the licence. And in April it'll be Jean's on the top and yours under it. If it ever comes out, it isn't only the licence. It's the one thing I bought that day. It's you."

He stops.

"So that's it," he says. "That's the material fact. I'm not asking you to carry it. I'm telling you what it weighs. And whatever you say, I'll take it."

---

Pascual doesn't say anything for a very long time.

Then he gets up. He walks off down the row, into the dark, as far as the end of the Colonel's hut, and stands there with his back to the step. Reggie sits and watches him. He doesn't follow. He's asked the question. The man's entitled to the time.

After about five minutes Pascual comes back. He doesn't sit down. He stands in front of the step in the moonlight with his fists shut, and his face is furious.

"Ten *years*," he says.

"Yes."

"You carried that for ten years. Me. You carried *me*, in your pocket, for ten years, and never said. Every time the man in the hat came. 'Sixty, 'sixty-three, 'sixty-six."

"Yes."

"I *saw* him look up at you, the young one last year, through the door. I thought it was about Spilsbury. It *was* about Spilsbury. And you sat there, and let him look at you like that, so he wouldn't look at *me*."

"Yes."

"*Hijo de* —" Pascual stops himself. He stands in front of the step shaking. "You should've *told* me."

"You'd have spent it."

"I'd have — *yes*. Yes, I would've. Of course I would've! Because it was *mine* to spend, you — you *English* —" He runs out of words in both languages, and stands there, breathing.

Reggie sits on the step and waits.

"All right," says Pascual at last. His voice has gone quite quiet. "All right. Now listen to me. You asked. So listen."

"I'm listening."

"I'm fifty-four," says Pascual. "I'm not a boy by a window with a schedule on my knee. You bought me ten years on this island, and I've had them. They've been the best ten years of my life."

"Pascual —"

"I'll thank you for them properly one day when I'm not so angry. But you don't get to go on buying them. You don't get to keep me safe in a drawer for the rest of my life like one of Spilsbury's slides. I'm not a specimen. I'm a man. It's my name on the licence and my neck and my risk, and I'm choosing it. That's what you asked me for. So I'm choosing."

Reggie opens his mouth.

"I haven't finished," says Pascual. "We'll be careful. We've been careful for fifteen years without knowing it, God knows, you didn't even know it *yourself*. Separate huts. Separate lamps. I'm the vet next door."

"You are the vet next door."

"And if anybody looks twice they'll see a man who shouts at you across the north pen." He takes a breath. "And Jean gets asked. Properly. The way you just did me. You don't put her name on the top of that licence without telling her what's underneath it."

"Yes," says Reggie. "That was the next thing."

"Good." Pascual's fists come open. "And the other thing."

Reggie waits.

"Him," says Pascual. "In Norway. You love him."

"Yes," says Reggie. He doesn't dress it up. He can't, not now, not with Pascual standing there after that. "I love him. I've loved him since 1946, and I'm going to go on. I don't know how to stop. I've tried, and I can't, and I'm not going to tell you I have."

"I know," says Pascual. "I've watched you not stop for ten years. I'm not asking you to stop. I'd think less of you. I'm asking if there's room."

And Reggie sits on the step, and looks at him standing there in the moonlight: fifty-four, grey at the temples, furious and frightened and red to the ears, with his face showing every single thing he's ever felt. He's shouted at Reggie in two languages, and thrown a bucket at him, and taken the pen out of his hand at midnight, and filled his lamp every Saturday for ten years without being asked. And Reggie reads, because he's pointed now and can't stop, how much room there is. He finds it's the whole of the rest of him. He'd never looked, because it had never once occurred to him that anybody might be standing in it.

"I didn't know there was," he says. "Until about a quarter of an hour ago. There is. There's —" His voice goes, and he lets it. "Pascual. There's a great deal."

Pascual looks at him for a long moment.

Then he sits back down on the step, beside him, shoulder to shoulder, the way they've sat every Sunday night since January 1965. And he puts his own hand on the back of Reggie's neck, where the hair wants cutting.

He doesn't take it away.

---

It's Reggie's hut, because it's the nearest, and because Pascual has been in it every Saturday for ten years with the oil can and knows where everything is.

The lamp's lit on the table. It's always lit. Pascual looks at it as they come in, and then looks at Reggie, and Reggie can see him decide to say it.

"Do you want it out?"

Reggie looks at the lamp.

Ten years. It's burnt every night since the spring of 1957. He'd never known he left it lit until somebody told him, and he still doesn't know how to not. He looks at it now, the small steady flame behind the glass. He finds that he knows exactly what it's for, and that he can't, not tonight, not yet, not even for this.

"No," he says. "Not yet. I'm sorry. I don't — I can't yet."

"All right," says Pascual. He doesn't say anything else about it. He just goes and turns it down, not out, till it's a small low light on the table. The room goes gold and brown and full of shadows. "There. That's for him. The rest's for me."

Reggie laughs. He can't help it. It comes out of him wet.

"That's *very* fair."

"I'm a fair man," says Pascual. "I've been told."

And then neither of them knows what to do next, for rather a long moment, and they stand in the middle of Reggie's hut looking at each other. They're two men of forty-nine and fifty-four who have known each other for fifteen years, in every possible condition. They've seen each other in waders and oilskins and pyjamas, covered in fish and blood and a great deal worse. And neither of them has the least idea how to begin.

"This is ridiculous," says Reggie.

"Yes."

"I've seen you with your arm inside a cow."

"That was a *horse*. In 1958. And you were sick in the bucket."

"I was *not* sick in the —"

Pascual kisses him.

It's not what Reggie expected. He hadn't known he'd expected anything, and then it's happening, and it isn't that, whatever it was. It's firm, and quite certain, and a little cross. It's the way Pascual says *no* at midnight and takes the pen out of his hand. Pascual's hand's on his jaw, broad and warm and rough from the gaff and the fish and the ropes. His mouth tastes of the Colonel's brandy. And Reggie makes a sound he hasn't made in eleven years, and puts both his hands on Pascual's shoulders and holds on.

"*There*," says Pascual against his mouth, as though he'd won an argument. "That's how you begin, *hombre*."

"You've been planning that."

"Two and a half years. I had it drawn. In pen."

Reggie laughs into his mouth and kisses him back. Then, because he's pointed at the man at last and can't stop reading, he learns things very fast. The back of Pascual's neck is hot, and the hair there is short and bristly where he's had it cut by Alleyne's boy's mother. He makes a low startled noise when Reggie puts his mouth to the side of his throat, under the ear. His hands, which have taken splinters and fish hooks and a calf's tooth out of Reggie over fifteen years with total steadiness, are shaking at the buttons of Reggie's shirt.

"Let me."

"I can do a *button*, Reggie."

"You're not doing a button. You've done one and a half."

"Then *help*, instead of reading me like a *paper*, I can *feel* you doing it —"

"I can't stop," says Reggie, and finds his own voice has gone quite unsteady. "I didn't know you were there. Fifteen years, and I didn't — and now I can see all of you at once, and there's so *much* of it, Pascual, I don't know where to —"

Pascual stops with his hands on the third button.

He looks at Reggie. His face is doing everything. It's doing it inches away, in the low gold light, with nothing in the way.

"Slowly," says Pascual. Quite gently. "Do it slowly. We've got time. I'm not going anywhere. I didn't go to Kingston."

"No," says Reggie. "You didn't."

"So read slowly."

He does.

He learns Pascual's back under his hands, broad and solid, with a long ridge of old scar low on the left side. A horse did that in 1958, the same horse. Reggie finds it with his fingertips and follows it, and Pascual says *that was your fault too*, and Reggie says *everything's my fault*, and Pascual says *yes*. He learns that Pascual's chest is grey where it was black in 1952. He learns that he laughs when he's nervous, low, in the chest, a sound Reggie has heard a thousand times across the long room without once knowing it was that. He learns that Pascual, lying back on the narrow bed with his arm under his head and the low lamp making him gold, looks at Reggie as though he were the calf coming up to breathe after a bad night. He looks as though it were the most ordinary miracle in the world, and one he'd sat up for.

"Don't look at me like that," says Reggie. "I'll say something stupid."

"You always say something stupid."

"Something *more* stupid."

"Say it, then."

Reggie's got his hand flat on Pascual's chest, over his heart. He can feel it going under his palm, fast and heavy. He's measured a thousand hearts, and never once one he wanted to keep his hand on.

"You're the best man I know," he says. "You've been the best man I know for fifteen years. I said it at the long table for your tenth, in front of everybody. I thought I was being *funny*."

Pascual's face does something extraordinary.

"You weren't funny," he says. "You were never funny about that. I sat there and died."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Come here."

He goes there.

It's not graceful. Neither of them is young, and the bed was built for one man in 1950 by a carpenter who didn't hold with comfort. At one point Reggie's elbow finds the wall and he swears, and Pascual laughs so hard he has to stop. At another Pascual's knee does something it shouldn't, and he says a word in Aragonese, and Reggie asks what it means, and Pascual tells him, and Reggie has to stop. But it doesn't need to be graceful. It needs to be this. It's Pascual's hands on his hips, holding on, broad and certain. It's his own mouth at the hollow of Pascual's throat, where the pulse is going like a hammer. It's Pascual saying his name, not *hombre*, not *Reggie* across the north pen at the top of his voice, but his name, low, into his shoulder, as though it were a word in a language he'd only just learned to speak. And it's Reggie finding that he's saying Pascual's, over and over, and can't stop that either.

"Don't read me," Pascual says at one point, into his neck, not meaning it.

"I'm not reading you."

"You *are*."

"I'm not," says Reggie. "I'm not. I've stopped. I've got it by heart."

And Pascual makes a sound Reggie will think about for a long time, and holds on to him hard enough to hurt.

---

Afterwards they lie in the narrow bed, which is much too narrow, with Pascual's arm under Reggie's neck going slowly dead and neither of them willing to say so. The lamp's still burning low on the table.

Reggie looks at it.

"It's all right," says Pascual, into his hair. He's half asleep. "Leave it. I'll fill it Saturday."

"You're sure."

"I've filled it for ten years. I'm not going to stop now you're sleeping with me. It'd be rude."

Reggie laughs, very quietly, into Pascual's shoulder.

"Pascual."

"Mm."

"Thank you. For telling me. For — the marmalade."

"*Ay*, the marmalade." Pascual's voice is slurring. "You'd have gone to your grave. With the marmalade. And no idea."

"Very probably."

"*Stupid*," says Pascual, with enormous tenderness, and is asleep. It happens all at once, the way it happens to Reggie, with his mouth a little open and his arm still under Reggie's neck.

Reggie lies awake a while longer. He looks at the lamp, and then at the man asleep beside him, and back at the lamp. He finds that he can hold both of them in his head at once. He'd never have thought that possible. It doesn't feel like a contradiction. It feels like room.

He goes to sleep with the lamp lit, the way he always does. It's the first time in ten years he's gone to sleep with anybody seeing it.

---

Jean Leslie has been home for three weeks when the Director of Research and the station vet ask to see her in the office, together, on a Friday afternoon. They shut the door.

She's known what it's about since they walked in. She knew from the way they came across the verandah: not together, carefully not together, three feet apart, like two men who've spent some time on the stairs deciding not to walk up them side by side. And from Pascual's ears, which were red before he'd said a word. And from Reggie's face, which was doing nothing at all, very deliberately. She hasn't seen him do that since the spring of 1957.

Reggie sits down across the desk from her. Pascual doesn't sit. He stands by the window.

Jean looks at the window, and at Pascual standing in it, and has to look down at her blotter for a moment.

"Jean," says Reggie. "I'm going to give you the directorship in April. Before you say yes, there's something you need to know about what you'd be signing for. It's going to take a while, and I'd like you to let me finish."

"All right."

He tells her. He tells it plainly, in order, the way he'd give a result: the office in March 1957, the three answers, the window. The minute in Halloran's hand, and what's in it, and who's in it. And then the last part, which she'd guessed from the verandah, and which he tells her anyway, all of it, in a level voice, with his hands flat on his knees. He doesn't look at Pascual once. Pascual doesn't look at him either. He's looking at the ceiling as though he'd like it to fall on him.

"So that's what's under the licence," says Reggie. "If you sign it in April, your name's on the top, over his, and mine's still in the file. If anybody ever puts two and two together, it isn't just us. It's you, and the Institute, and the deposit."

"I can count, Reggie."

"I know you can. I'm not asking you to carry it without knowing. I did that once. A man paid for it who was never asked." He stops. "If you say no, I'll keep the directorship. Or I'll go. Whichever you like. It's your decision. Not mine."

Jean sits back in her chair.

She has known Reggie Tar since September 1953, when she came off the Kingston boat at twenty-three with a trunk of books and a letter of introduction. She has seen him do a great many things. She's seen him be brilliant and cruel and wrong and funny and very kind, often in the same afternoon. And she has never once, in fourteen years, seen him sit in a chair and ask somebody for permission.

"How long have you known?" she says to Pascual, at the window.

Pascual jumps.

"Me? About —" He goes redder. "About him? Since the Governor. May 'sixty-five. About me, the church path. August. He's known since November."

"*November*," says Jean.

"He's very slow," says Pascual, "about anything that isn't a fish."

"So Hester said." Jean looks at Reggie. "Hester wrote to me. In 1965. To the Menai Strait. She said she thought I ought to know that the station vet had come into her office with a face like a wet week, and that Dr Tar had told a story about the Governor at dinner that nobody should have to hear twice, and that she didn't expect anything to come of it for a very long time, because Dr Tar was Dr Tar." Jean's mouth goes, very slightly, at the corner. "I've been waiting two and a half years for the rest of the letter."

Reggie is staring at her.

"And me?" he says. "Before that? Did Hester write to you about —"

"No," says Jean. "I didn't need Hester. I've known about you since about Easter 1954, Reggie. I'm a biologist. I watch animals for a living."

Nobody says anything for a moment. Pascual, at the window, makes a sound that might be a laugh or might not.

Jean picks up her pen. She puts it down again, square, the way she's seen a man she admired do once, a long time ago, at a table in the long room.

"Right," she says. "Here's my answer, and then I'd like you both to go away, because I've got transects. I'll sign in April. I read the file before I came home, I asked the Board for it, and I've read the minute. I know what it says and who's in it, and whose voice it was said in. I'd guessed a good deal of the rest. I'd have signed anyway. I'll sign now, knowing."

"Jean —"

"I haven't finished. Three conditions. One, you're careful. Not frightened, careful. There's a difference, and you both know it, and I'll know if you forget it. Two, you don't frighten the Colonel. He's seventy-eight and he thinks you're the finest scientist in the West Indies, and the finest vet, and he's right, and I won't have him upset. And three."

She looks at Reggie. He's sitting across the desk from her with his hands flat on his knees, waiting to be told what he may and may not have, as though he'd never once in his life simply taken a thing.

"Three," says Jean, rather more quietly, "is that you let him fill that lamp for as long as you need it lit. And the day you don't need it, you put it out yourself. Not him. You."

Reggie looks at her for a long moment.

"How did you —"

"Everybody on this island knows about that lamp, Reggie," says Jean. "You can see it from the Bay." She picks up her pen again. "Go away. Both of you. I've got transects, and I've waited two and a half years for this conversation, and it was even worse than Hester said."

They go. She watches them through the window going back along the verandah. They're three feet apart, carefully. At the top of the long room steps Pascual says something she can't hear, and Reggie laughs, the real laugh, and puts his hand on the back of Pascual's neck. He takes it away again at once, remembering, with a glance at the Colonel's hut.

Jean watches them go down the steps. Then she sits in the office with the door shut, and puts her face in her hands for a minute, and does not cry, very nearly.

Then she does the transects.
