# Quittance

The reply had been one line, by air, in the hand he had not seen on an envelope for nine years.

*Yes. Thursday the 15th, at three, wherever you say. R.*

He had said the Aquarium. He had thought about it for a day and a half, which was a day and a quarter longer than it deserved. A hotel was not possible. The Society's rooms were full of men who had known them both at the Labs and would come across the carpet with their hands out. A park was possible but it was September, and it would rain. The Aquarium was under the Mappin Terraces, and dim, and on a Thursday afternoon in term it was empty but for a keeper and a few mothers with prams. It had been built in 1924 by men who believed that a fish ought to be looked at in the dark. It was the one public room in London where two marine biologists could stand side by side for an hour in front of a tank of wrasse and nobody would think anything of it at all.

He has been there since a quarter to three. He had meant to arrive at five to. He had left the Society at twenty past two to walk it, and walked it faster than he had allowed for, and so he has been standing for a quarter of an hour in front of the second tank on the left. That is the one with the lobsters, and he could by now give a fair account of the moulting condition of every one of them.

He has worked out that he will know the step. He has not heard it for nine years and he will know it. Short, and quick, and a little uneven on the left, from the shin at the Labs that Reggie has never once explained. He stands with his hands behind his back and listens for it under the hum of the pumps.

At four minutes to three he hears it on the stairs.

He does not turn round. He finds he cannot. He stands with his eyes on the lobsters, and hears it come down the last three steps, and stop at the bottom. It does not come on. There is a pause long enough for a man to have seen the back of another man's head in a dim room and to be standing quite still looking at it.

Then it comes on, the whole length of the gallery, and stops beside him.

"You're early," says Reggie.

"I was delayed at the Society, and then found I was not."

"How long have you been here?"

"A quarter of an hour." He does not look round. "I can tell you a good deal about these lobsters."

"I don't doubt it."

He turns his head.

And the first thing he sees, before he sees anything else, before the face or the glasses or the grey gone right through at the sides, is that Reggie has been fed.

It is in the face. The hollows under the cheekbones he could have laid a finger in, in 1957, are filled. There is colour in the skin under the brown, and flesh on the jaw, and the collar of the shirt sits against the throat as a collar should. He has been fed for some time, steadily, by somebody who stood over him until it was done. Spilsbury looks at it for longer than he looks at anything else, and says so.

"You have been fed."

Reggie's mouth goes at the corner.

"I have been fed."

"Then he has done as he was told."

"He'd been doing it since 1957," says Reggie, "before anybody told him. He stands over the plate. He folds his arms." His voice is not entirely steady. "I've put on a stone since November. He weighs me. He says it's for the calf's charts and needs a control."

Spilsbury looks at the lobsters again.

"Good," he says.

---

Reggie stands beside him in front of the lobster tank and cannot, for a minute or two, do anything else.

Alec is fifty-six. He'd known that. He's known it every March since 1957, when he's thought *he's so many this year* and gone down to the pens. He hadn't known what fifty-six would look like on him, and it looks like this. It's the same long narrow face, like a blade of ash, and the same glasses, and the hair gone white rather than grey, all of it, and cut very short. There are two lines from the nose to the corners of the mouth that weren't there. It's the same one expression, which Alec has used for everything since the Labs, and which Reggie can read now as he could read it then, all the way down.

It's the old one. That's what stops him. It's the expression Alec wore at the tank-hall door in 1947 and on the jetty in 1957. And under it, if you know where to look, and Reggie has always known, the whole face is doing everything at once.

"You're thin," Reggie says.

"I have been told."

"By whom?"

"By Fru Rasmussen, on Sundays, since 1957." Alec's eyes haven't left the lobsters. "And by Fru Brekke, on the other six days, since 1961. I am fed. I am fed a great deal. It does not seem to take."

"You should let it."

"I am aware that I should."

Reggie laughs. It surprises him. It comes up out of him in the dim room with the pumps going, and Alec's head turns an inch at the sound, and there's something in the one expression that Reggie hasn't seen since a jetty in April 1957. He has to look away from it.

They walk. There's nothing else to do in an aquarium, and it's easier walking. They go down the length of the gallery past the wrasse and the dogfish and a very old conger in a pipe, and they don't look at each other, and they talk about the tanks. They talk about the filtration, which is antique. They talk about the conger, which Alec says was here in 1947. Reggie says it can't have been, and Alec says it has a scar on its left side in the shape of a figure seven, and he noted it in 1947, and there it is. And there it is. And for ten minutes, walking the length of the Aquarium, they're two marine biologists on a Thursday afternoon, arguing about a fish. It's so exactly what they were that Reggie can hardly bear it and can't bring himself to stop.

At the end of the gallery there's a bench, in the dark, in front of the big tank. A shoal of sprats goes round and round in it like a thing being stirred. There's nobody else in the room. The keeper's gone up the stairs for his tea.

Alec sits down on the bench. After a moment, Reggie sits down beside him, a foot off. Not three; not nothing. A foot.

"I have something to tell you," says Reggie.

"I know you have. You have had it on your face since the lobsters."

"The last thing I said to you that meant anything," says Reggie, "was in the spare hut with the wrong roof. That I couldn't come up your path again." He makes himself go on. He makes himself do it in his own voice, looking at the sprats. "And I meant it. I'd mean it tonight. If you walked up from the jetty tomorrow, I'd go down the step to meet you at the bottom, and I'd turn you round."

"I know."

"I know you know. It's why you've not come."

"It is why I have not come," says Alec. His voice is quite level. He's looking at the tank. "I did not wish you to have to do it twice. Once was sufficient, for both of us."

Reggie can't say anything for a moment.

"And I've let him up mine," he says at last. "The path. I shut it on you over Frank, and nine years later I've let a man up it, and he's the man you made me keep out of the file." He hears his own breath go. "I couldn't do that to you by post, Alec. Not the whole of it. It's the worst thing I could do to you, and I wasn't going to do it where I couldn't see your face."

It's quiet on the bench. The sprats go round.

"Who pays for it?" says Alec.

Reggie turns his head.

"It is a plain question. I have learnt to ask it." Alec takes his glasses off. He holds them in his lap, both hands, and looks at the tank without them. "You shut the path because a man in Southport was paying for what was on the other end of it, and had not been asked. So I ask. Who is paying for this one?"

"Nobody." It comes out rough. "Us. He was asked. I was asked. He's been asked about everything since November, till he's sick of it."

"Then it is not the same path." Alec's voice doesn't change, and his hands on the glasses don't move, and Reggie can read all of it anyway. "You did not shut a path on *me*, Reggie. You shut it on a man who would have had you on what Frank paid, with his arms round you, and not minded. I was that man, in 1957. I had done the sum and found I could afford it, and I had not asked the one person it cost." He stops. "I should like to think I am not that man now. I have had nine years to find out what he was. But I was him then, and you were right, and you would be right again."

"*Alec* —"

"I did the sum without asking him. I made it so that you could not come up my path and remain yourself. You asked me not to, at the table, and I said no." He puts his glasses back on, carefully. "I am sorry. I am not saying it as a noise. It is a real apology, and you should have it."

The pumps hum. The sprats go round in the big tank like a thing being stirred.

"You're a *bastard*," says Reggie, very quietly.

"You said that to me once before," says Alec. "In November 1946. In a rather different tone."

Reggie stares at him.

Then he laughs. It comes up out of him cracked and wet. He puts his hand over his mouth on the bench in the Aquarium and laughs into it, and his shoulders shake. He's laughing and not laughing. Alec sits a foot away from him and doesn't touch him, and Reggie can see, in the green light from the tank, that the long narrow face is doing a great deal more than it ever lets anybody see. It's doing it in the dark, where Alec thinks nobody is watching, and he's letting it.

When he can, Reggie wipes his eyes under his glasses with the back of his hand.

"Thank you," he says.

"Tell me about him," says Alec.

"You know him."

"I knew him in 1957, for nine months, and liked him. He told me I had the dentition of the cow wrong on my first afternoon, and I had." Alec looks at the tank. "He has signed a letter to me with both his names. I should like to know who he is now."

Reggie thinks about what to tell him. He does it carefully, because it matters what goes across this bench and what doesn't. There's a step, and a knot in a plank, and a basin of cold water, and none of it is Alec's to carry home. So he tells him the work.

He tells him the calf, who's nine and the size of her mother and has never once been called anything but the calf. He tells him Pascual's dentition charts, the whole pen, every animal, drawn in pen and ink from casts he takes himself with dental wax he orders from Kingston. The *Proceedings* took four of the plates last year and asked for more. He tells him about the winter of 'sixty-three, when the young male off the second head came in with his flipper cut to the bone by a propeller. Pascual sat in the water with him for three nights, and the flipper healed, and the animal's out past the reef edge now with a scar you can see from the launch. He tells him the calf's mother has gone grey round the muzzle, and Pascual weighs her every week and has her on a softer feed, and argues with the Colonel about it.

And he tells him about the lamp.

"He fills it," Reggie says. "On Saturdays. He has since the spring of 1957. He comes along the row with the can at eight, and fills it, and sits on the bed and says *well?*, and I tell him the week. He's never said a word about what it was for. He measures the oil. He can tell you what it's burnt any week since 'fifty-seven."

Alec doesn't say anything for a long time.

"Every Saturday," he says at last.

"Every Saturday."

"For a lamp he knew was lit for me."

"Yes."

Alec looks at the sprats going round.

"I did not know that," he says. "I should have. I costed him as a risk, in February. *There is another man on this row.* And I looked at him for nine months across the long table, and I did not see it, which is not like me." His mouth goes at one corner. "I think I did not want to."

"It wasn't there, then. Alec, in 1957 it was *you*. It was you every day. It wasn't there."

"No," says Alec. "I don't suppose it was, in you. It was there in him, once. He'd got over it by the time I came, I think. I'd have seen the getting over, if I'd looked. It has a particular look." He's very still. "I have seen it in a mirror."

Reggie turns his head.

"Alec —"

"I am trying," says Alec. He says it to the tank, quite evenly, as he'd give a reading. "I should like you to know that I am trying. So that you do not have to wonder about it on a Sunday night. I do not expect to manage it. I shall go on trying anyway. It is the least I can do, and it is also the most."

Reggie can't answer that. He sits on the bench and looks at the side of Alec's face in the green light from the tank, and can't say anything at all.

"Is he good to you?" says Alec.

"Yes."

"Tell me how."

"He shouts at me," says Reggie, when he can. "He tells me when I'm wrong, at the top of his voice, in front of the students, in two languages."

"Good."

"He weighs me. He says it's for the calf's charts and he needs a control."

Alec makes a small sound that might be a laugh.

"And I burnt my hand on a lamp chimney in November," says Reggie, "and he made me hold it in a basin of cold water for five minutes before I was allowed to say a word. He stood over it. He held my wrist under."

"Five minutes is correct."

"He said it was what he'd do for the calf."

"Then it is doubly correct," says Alec, "and I am glad of him."

---

They go up into the air at half past five, because the keeper comes down with his tea finished and his keys out. It's raining a little, fine and grey, the way it rains in London in September. They walk up the Broad Walk under the plane trees with their collars up, a foot apart, like two men who've been to a meeting.

"You're not asking me for anything," Reggie says, at the top, by the fountain.

"No."

"You said in the letter. You said you had nothing to ask for."

"I have nothing to ask for. I have had what I came for." Alec has his hands in the pockets of his raincoat. He's looking straight ahead, up the walk. "I wished to see your face when you told me. I have seen it. I have been expecting, since your letter, to see something I should have to carry home."

"And?"

"You are fed. You laughed. You told me I was a bastard. You talked for twenty minutes about a man who measures lamp oil, and your whole face did what it does." His voice goes, very slightly. "It is all right. That is what I came for."

They walk.

"There's the lamp," says Reggie.

Alec doesn't say anything.

"I've lit it every night since 1957. I told him I'd know what it was for when I'd seen your face." He stops by the boating lake. There's nobody on it. The boats are pulled up under a tarpaulin, and the rain's making rings on the water. "It's been turned down to a bead since November. It's burning half what it did. He measures it."

"Of course he does."

"You didn't know it was there. You've never seen it. It's a lamp on a table on a rock, for a man four thousand miles off who'd no idea." Reggie's mouth goes. "It's ridiculous. I've known it was ridiculous for nine years. I couldn't put it out."

"Why not?"

"Because then I'd be a man who'd sent you away and got on with things. As though it hadn't cost anything." He looks at the rings on the water. "I don't know what to do with it, Alec."

Alec stands by the lake with his hands in his pockets and looks at the rings on the water.

"It is ridiculous," he says at last. "It is also the most exact thing I have ever heard of you doing. You have been paying a bill nobody sent you, in paraffin, every night for nine years, where nobody could see the receipt." His voice goes, very slightly, and comes back. "I did not know. I should like you to know that I am more moved by it than I can say by a boating lake in the rain."

Reggie can't answer.

"A lamp," Alec says, "is for somebody who is expected."

"Yes."

"I am not expected. You have lit a lamp every night for nine years for a man you would meet at the bottom of the step and turn round. And I have known you would, and not come, so that you should not have to." His mouth goes. "We have been very careful of each other, across four thousand miles."

"I knew. Under everything."

"So did I." He turns his head. "You have been keeping faith on the rock with a man you would not let in, and I have been waiting in Ålesund, without grounds, for the form to change back. It has made two men thin. I should like to be something else now."

"What?"

Alec considers it. He takes a long time. Reggie watches him do it and knows every stage of it, the way he'd know the steps of a dissection.

"Kept," says Alec. "I should like to be kept. Not waited for. There is a difference." He looks back at the water. "One keeps a letter. One keeps a photograph, face down if one prefers. One keeps a man in a drawer, and opens it when one likes, and shuts it again, and goes to bed. One does not sit up by a drawer."

Reggie can't speak.

"Put it out," says Alec. "Not on my account. I have no account on that island any more. I closed it in 1957, and I have been sending you statements on it twice a year since, out of habit." His mouth goes at the corner. "Put it out on yours. And on his. He has filled it long enough."

"*Alec*."

"Tell him that. Tell Dr Pobil, with my regards, that he has filled it long enough. And that he is to put it out himself. He has more right to it than either of us. He has paid for the oil."

---

It gets dark early under the rain. At the Gloucester Gate, where the park comes out into the street and they'll have to go different ways, Reggie stops.

"I have a message for you," he says. "From him."

Alec waits.

"He said to tell you he'll see that I eat. That he's got your regards, and he knows you meant it." Reggie's voice is doing something. He lets it. "And that you were right about the dentition."

Alec looks at him.

Then he laughs. Reggie's heard it perhaps a dozen times in twenty years. It's short and cracked, like something breaking under a boot, and he puts his hand over his mouth in the Gloucester Gate in the rain.

"He *said* that."

"He made me promise. He said it twice."

"I was *not* right about the dentition. There was a retained premolar on the left side and I missed it. He found it with his thumb in about four seconds, and was kind to me about it for nine months." Alec takes his hand away. "He is telling me I was right so that I shall go home with something. He is a very decent man."

"He is."

"Then tell him —" Alec stops. For a moment he doesn't seem to know how to finish. "Tell him I was wrong about the dentition. And that I know he knows. And that I am obliged to him."

"For the lie?"

"For the *kindness*. You will have to learn to tell the difference, Reggie. You have been living with a kind man for nine years and not noticed. It is apparently a habit."

Reggie laughs, in the rain.

And then there's nothing more to say, and they both know it. They stand under the gate with the rain coming down and the traffic going past on the Outer Circle, a foot apart.

"May I have your wrist," says Alec.

Reggie looks at him.

"Not your hand. Your wrist. For a moment. I shall not ask for anything else."

Reggie holds it out.

Alec takes it in two fingers, the way he's taken it in the tank hall and the hut. He holds it, and doesn't look at it. He looks at the gate, and the rain. And Reggie can feel him counting. He can feel it in the fingers, as he feels Pascual do it at night, a man tapping out a rhythm on a table.

"Seventy-eight," says Alec, at the end of the minute.

"Pascual says seventy-two."

"Pascual has not just seen me." He lets go of the wrist, and puts his hand back in his pocket. "Seventy-eight is a perfectly good figure. It is a figure for a man who has been fed, and has walked up the Broad Walk in the rain, and is going home. It is not a figure for a man who is waiting."

"No."

"Go home, Reggie."

"Alec —"

"The boat train is at nine from Waterloo. You will want to eat first. There is a place in Albany Street that does a chop." He's already turning. "Eat the chop. I shall know if you have not. I shall write to Dr Pobil in the form."

"And you," says Reggie.

Alec stops.

"You eat one. Tonight. All of it." Reggie's voice isn't steady. "You're thinner than I am, and I've been on a rock for nine years. Promise me."

"You will not know if I do not."

"I'll know. I'll read it off the typewriter in March."

Alec looks at him for a long moment in the rain.

"Very well," he says. "I give you my word. A chop."

And he goes, up the Outer Circle, in the rain, with his hands in his pockets and his collar up, at the pace he's always walked at. It's very exact and a little too fast, and it's the walk of a man who knows exactly where he's going and would rather not be watched getting there.

Reggie watches him till he's round the curve.

Then he goes to Albany Street and eats the chop.

---

Spilsbury walks to the Society's rooms, a mile and a quarter, and does not stop.

He goes up to the room they have given him, which looks over the gardens. He does not put the light on. He takes off the wet raincoat and hangs it on the back of the door, and sits down in the chair by the window in the dark, in his jacket, with the rain going down the glass.

He has done the sum.

He does it again, in the chair, because he cannot help it. He has done it every day since the ninth of February 1957, and he does it now, with the figures he has had since three o'clock this afternoon.

A man alive, and on the licence, and in post, with his work and his animals. The other man behind the wall, who was not asked. That was the one side, in 1957. On the other side was the cost, and Spilsbury had taken it, and had it deferred until Easter, and found he could pay it, just, if he did not look at it too long. He has looked at it every day for nine years. He has never once known whether what was on the other side of the page had been worth what it cost. The only man who could tell him had been put out of reach by the sum itself.

He knows now.

He has seen the face. It was fed. It laughed in an aquarium and called him a bastard and laughed again in the rain at the Gloucester Gate. It talked for twenty minutes about a man who draws teeth in pen and ink and measures lamp oil, and its whole self did what it does, and did it without the smallest idea that it was doing it. That was the finding. The man had not known he was showing it. He had been, for twenty minutes on a bench in the dark, simply happy, while talking about somebody else.

It was worth it.

Spilsbury sits in the dark with that. He is not sure he has ever held a result so exactly, or had it cost so much, or been so glad of it.

His hand goes to his inside pocket. It has done that since the seventh of February 1957, for a thing that is not there. He had burnt the green books on the island, in the incinerator behind the sick bay, the last week. He has kept the entries since in the place behind the eyes where he has found he can keep a surprising amount, if he is careful.

He composes this one. He has declined to compose several since 1957. This one he composes, exactly, with the full stops.

*15.ix.66. R. T. Seen. Fed. Laughed.*

And then, since he is being exact, and since there is nobody to see, in the column where the cost has gone for nine years:

*Nil.*

He sits with it for a while.

Then he gets up, and puts the light on, and draws the curtains, and goes down to the Society's dining room, where they are still serving. He orders the chop. He eats it, all of it, slowly, because he gave his word, and he finds that he wishes to be a man who keeps his word about a chop.

In March he will write to the Institute in the form. He will say that the depositor of accession 197 remains resident at Kongens gate 18, Ålesund, and may be reached there. It will be quite true, and it will go by sea. And it will be answered in two hands, one above the other, and he will read both names every time. He will mind, a little, every time, and he will find that he can carry it. It is a very good thing to be kept.
