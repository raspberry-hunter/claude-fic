# By Return

"That one's not mine," says Jean.

She puts it down by his elbow on the bench in the long room and doesn't go away. It's addressed in Alec's hand, and there's no *The Director* on it at all.

*Dr R. Tar*
*William Martin Institute for Marine Studies*

"I haven't opened it," says Jean. "It isn't addressed to the Institute." She stands there with her hands in the pockets of her coat. "Do you want me to fetch him?"

Reggie looks at the envelope.

"Yes," he says. "Please."

---

He reads it at the table in the hut at the end of the row with Pascual standing in the doorway. Pascual doesn't come in. He leans on the frame with his arms folded, and looks at the row, carefully not at the letter.

*For two years the Institute's acknowledgements have been signed by Dr Leslie.*

Reggie reads the whole of it. *I have found that I have to ask you rather than go on measuring it. I should like to see you. That is the whole of it. No answer is an answer, and I shall read it as one. I am sixty this year.* There's a full stop after the *A*.

He reads it a second time. Then he holds it out behind him without turning round.

Pascual takes it. Reggie hears him read it, slowly, his lips moving a little on the long words. It takes him some time. Then Pascual comes in and sits down on the bed, which creaks, and holds the letter on his knee.

"He's sixty," says Pascual.

"Yes."

"He'll look it, I'd think. He looked it at forty-six." Pascual turns the letter over. "He's going to sit in that hotel."

"Yes."

"All week. Facing the door. Like a — like the calf's mother when there's a boat coming." Pascual looks up. "You know he is."

"I know he is."

Pascual's quiet for a while. The calf's mother blows in the north pen. There's the long room's generator coughing into life for the evening, and a student laughing on the verandah.

"Go, if you want," says Pascual. "I'd not stop you."

Reggie turns round in his chair.

"I mean it," says Pascual. His ears have gone red, but his face is quite steady. "I've thought about it. On the path, just now, coming along. Go to London. Sit in his hotel. Tell him whatever you need to tell him. I'll be here when you come back. I'm not a boy, Reggie, and I told you there was room. I meant the room."

"Pascual."

"I *meant* it."

"I know you did." Reggie looks at him. He looks at him the way he's been able to look at him since November 1967, pointed, the whole of him, with nothing in the way. And he finds, which he'd half expected, that it isn't a question of room. "I'm not going."

"Because of me."

"No. Not because of you." He turns back to the table and puts his hand flat on the letter where Pascual's put it down. "Because of Frank. Because of what I said in the office, and whose account it was said on. I can't be the man who said that and go and sit across a table from him in a hotel bar, as if it had cost nobody anything. I couldn't in 'fifty-seven and I can't now. That hasn't changed. It's not going to." He stops. "He knows that. He knew it before he wrote. I think he wrote anyway so as to be told."

Pascual's quiet.

"So tell him," he says. "You've got to tell him."

"Yes."

"Don't let him sit there. *Hombre*. Don't do that to him. Whatever you do, don't let him sit there all week reading the door."

"No," says Reggie. "I'm not going to. I'm going to answer it."

"Tonight?"

"Tonight. By return." He picks up his pen. "It'll catch the Tuesday boat."

Pascual gets up off the bed. He stands behind Reggie's chair for a moment, and puts his hand on the back of Reggie's neck, and leaves it there. Reggie leans back into it, very slightly.

"D'you want me to go?"

"No," says Reggie. "Stay. Sit on the bed. Don't read over my shoulder."

"I can't read your writing anyway," says Pascual, and sits on the bed, and doesn't.

---

He writes it by hand. He doesn't draft it. He hasn't written anything to that address in his own hand since 1956. He's typed thirteen years of *Dear Sir*, and then watched Jean type two more, and the whole time his hand has known exactly what it wanted to put, and he's never once let it.

He lets it now.

> *William Martin Institute*
> *20th April 1970*
>
> *Dear Alec,*
>
> *I've got your letter. I'm answering it, because you said no answer was an answer, and I won't have you sitting in the Charing Cross for five nights reading one off the door.*
>
> *I'm not coming. It isn't the margin. You measured that right; it's Jean's name on the top now and she knows exactly what she signed. It's what I said in the office, and whose account it was said on. I can't be the man who said it and also sit across a table from you as though it hadn't cost anybody anything. That was true in 1957 and it's still true, and I think you knew it before you wrote. I think you wrote so as to be told. So I'm telling you.*
>
> *The other thing I can't put plainly, and you'll know why. I'm not on my own at the end of the row any more. I haven't been since November 1967. You'll know who. You said once, in the long room, that he looked at me as though I were a mistake he intended to correct. He still does. He knows about you, all of it that's mine to tell. He's sitting on my bed while I write this and not reading over my shoulder, and he told me not to let you sit in that hotel.*
>
> *You know what I'm not going to write. It hasn't stopped. It isn't going to. He knows that too, and there's room.*
>
> *The water off the jetty was twenty-six degrees this morning. I took it myself at the feed. I've been meaning to tell you since March 1960.*
>
> *Yours,*
>
> *R.*
>
> *P. says you're to eat something. He says you looked like the ash.*

He reads it through once. He doesn't change anything.

He puts the full stop after the *R*, and looks at it, and leaves it.

"Where'd you put me?" says Pascual from the bed.

"You're in the postscript."

"What'd I say?"

"You said he's to eat something."

"I *did* say that," says Pascual, pleased, "in 'fifty-seven. On the jetty." And then, after a moment, less pleased, "That's all I said?"

"No," says Reggie. "You're in the rest of it too."

He addresses the envelope himself, in his own hand, with the ring on the *Å* put on quickly, the ordinary way. Then he gives it to Pascual to take up to Jean's tray for the Tuesday boat, because Pascual's standing there holding out his hand for it, and because he wants somebody else to carry it the first few yards.

---

It comes to Kongens gate on the second of May, a Saturday, with the morning post.

Fru Brekke brings it up herself, which she doesn't do, and puts it on the table by the window. She says only that it's from the West Indies and that the hand is not the usual one, and she hopes that is not bad news. Spilsbury thanks her, and waits until she's gone down the stairs, and then stands and looks at it.

It's addressed in Reggie's writing hand, not the capitals. The ring on the *Å* has been put on quickly, by someone who didn't care whether it was right.

He doesn't sit down. He opens it standing at the window with his thumb, and reads it once for what it says.

*I've got your letter. I'm answering it.*

He has spent four weeks deciding what the silence would say. He had costed it most carefully. He had known the probable answer, and allowed for the improbable one, and made himself ready to read a closed door in the absence of any letter at all. He had meant to sit in the bar at the Charing Cross in September in any case, facing the door. He'd known it was irrational, and had fully intended to do it.

He had not costed an answer.

*I'm not coming.*

He finds that he's sitting down after all, on the edge of the bed, without having decided to.

*It's what I said in the office, and whose account it was said on.* Yes. He knew that. He knew it in the spare hut in February 1957. He knew it when he wrote *I have found that I have to ask you*. The man is quite right. He wrote so as to be told. He has been told, plainly, in the right voice, and it is the first time in thirteen years anybody has told him anything.

*I'm not on my own at the end of the row any more.*

He reads that sentence and stops.

He sits on Fru Brekke's candlewick bedspread with the letter on his knee and does, very fast, the arithmetic he has not once in thirteen years let himself do. He had costed everything. The licence, and Frank, and the office, and Easter, and his own fifteen years on a quay. He had costed the man at the end of the row, lying awake, for the rest of his life, on the far side of a wall, alone.

He had never once put anybody on the near side of the wall.

*You'll know who.*

He does know. He knows it before he gets to the next line. Pobil. Pobil on the jetty in April 1957, taking him by both shoulders hard, saying *you look like the ash*. Pobil at the long table in 1956 correcting Reggie's water chemistry to his face in front of the Colonel, and Reggie going pink with pleasure and not knowing why. *He looked at me as though I were a mistake he intended to correct.* Spilsbury said that. He'd forgotten. He'd said it at the bench in the long room in the autumn of 1956, as an observation, not unkindly. And Reggie had laughed and said *he's the best man on the island*, and meant it, and hadn't known what he meant.

*He's sitting on my bed while I write this and not reading over my shoulder.*

Spilsbury puts his hand over his eyes.

He stays like that for some time. He is aware of a number of things at once and cannot, for once, put them in order. There is grief, very large and quite plain, and not new. There is something under it that is new, and that he takes some time to identify, because he has not felt it in so long.

It is relief.

Not that Reggie will not come. He had expected that. He'd known it, if he's honest, since before he wrote. It is the other thing. For thirteen years he has done the sum every morning on the quay, with the thermometer, and it has always had the same term at the end of it. A man alone at the end of the row, under a lamp, not sleeping. It is the one term he has never been able to do anything about. He has carried it to the quay and back every day since 1957, and there has been no instrument for it, and no reply.

And it is not there.

There's a man sitting on the bed. There's somebody on the near side of the wall. It is a man Spilsbury liked, very much, from the first week, who corrected Reggie to his face and filled his lamp without being asked, and who had told him on a jetty to eat something.

*You know what I'm not going to write. It hasn't stopped. It isn't going to. He knows that too, and there's room.*

*The water off the jetty was twenty-six degrees this morning.*

He takes his hand away from his eyes. He reads the postscript.

*P. says you're to eat something. He says you looked like the ash.*

And Alec Spilsbury, sixty years old, sitting on a candlewick bedspread in a rented room in Ålesund at a quarter past eight on a Saturday morning, makes a sound that Fru Brekke, on the stairs with the coal scuttle, will afterwards tell her sister was very nearly a laugh.

---

He burns it at noon.

He has burnt everything since 1957, and he burns this too, in the stove in Fru Brekke's kitchen, while she's at the market. He does it not for his own sake. There is nothing on that page that could cost him anything now. He does it for Pobil's. It is a page in Reggie's writing hand saying *I'm not on my own at the end of the row*, with a date on it and an initial, and it names nobody. And it would take a man in a hat about four minutes to work out who.

He reads it once more before he does it. He does not need to. He has it by heart.

Then he puts it in, and watches it go, and puts the kettle on, and eats two slices of Fru Brekke's bread and cheese standing up at her table, the whole of both, because he has been told to.

In the afternoon he goes down to the quay with the thermometer and lets it down into the Brosund on its string, and waits three minutes, and brings it up.

Six degrees.

He goes back to Kongens gate and sits at the table by the window, and takes a sheet of the station's paper, and writes, by hand, without drafting it.

> *Kongens gate 18*
> *Ålesund*
> *2nd May 1970*
>
> *Dear Reggie,*
>
> *Thank you. I shall not sit in the bar. I shall read my paper on the Tuesday and come home on the Saturday, and I shall not look at the door.*
>
> *I am glad. I find that I mean that entirely, which I had not expected, and I have checked it twice.*
>
> *Tell P. that I have eaten. Tell him also that I was right about him in 1956, and that I am glad to have been right, which is not a thing I have often been able to say.*
>
> *The sound was six degrees this afternoon.*
>
> *Yours,*
>
> *A.*

He looks at the *A.*, with the full stop after it, for a while. Then he addresses it, and takes it to the post office himself before it closes. On the way back he stops at the baker's on Kongens gate, which he has passed every day for ten years, and buys a cake.

---

It comes on the sixteenth, a Saturday, on the morning boat.

Jean brings it down to the north pen, where Reggie and Pascual are doing the calf's mother's teeth, and puts it on the wall and goes away again without saying anything. Reggie wipes his hands on his trousers and opens it standing in the pen with the water to his knees, because he can't wait, and he reads it out.

He doesn't mean to read it out. He finds he's doing it. He reads it in his own voice, plainly, into the morning, with Pascual standing beside him holding the calf's mother's jaw open with both hands and a wedge.

"*I am glad*," Reggie reads. "*I find that I mean that entirely, which I had not expected, and I have checked it twice.*"

Pascual makes a noise.

"*Tell P. that I have eaten.*"

"Good," says Pascual, very gruffly, holding the jaw. "*Good*. About time."

"*Tell him also that I was right about him in 1956, and that I am glad to have been right, which is not a thing I have often been able to say.*"

Pascual doesn't say anything to that at all. Reggie looks up from the letter, and Pascual is standing in the north pen with his hands in the calf's mother's mouth. His face is doing everything. It's wet, and he can't wipe it, because if he lets go of the jaw she'll close it on the wedge.

"What was he right about?" Pascual says. "In 'fifty-six?"

"You. That you look at me as if I were a mistake you meant to correct."

"You *are* a mistake I mean to correct."

"I know."

"*Checked it twice*," says Pascual, and his voice breaks right down the middle. "He checked it *twice*, *hombre*. Like me. With the — the first time, in 'fifty-six, I checked it twice —"

"I know."

"Take the wedge," says Pascual. "Take the wedge, I have to — take it, Reggie, *please*, before she bites it."

Reggie takes the wedge. Pascual lets go of the jaw, and stands in the north pen with the water to his knees and puts both his hands over his face. The calf's mother, who has been extremely patient, looks at them both with one large dark eye. Then she sinks down into the water with a long, offended sigh and blows bubbles.

---

That night, in the hut at the end of the row, Reggie turns the lamp down, the way Pascual taught him to in November 1967, till it's a small low light on the table.

He looks at it for a long time.

Pascual's in the bed already, on the side by the wall, because the bed's still much too narrow and the wall side is Pascual's. He's got his arm behind his head and he's watching Reggie watch the lamp, and he doesn't say anything. He hasn't said anything about the lamp since the night in November. He's filled it every Saturday, and turned it down every night he's been here, and never once asked.

The letter's on the table beside it. Reggie won't burn it. He's never burnt anything. Alec's other letters are in the Institute's file, in Hester's order, *in accordance with the terms of deposit*. This one's going in the drawer, with the other one, the April one. *I am glad. I have checked it twice.*

"Pascual," Reggie says.

"Mm."

"Jean said I was to put it out myself. The day I didn't need it."

"I remember."

"Not you. Me."

"I'm not likely to forget, Reggie. I was there. I was the one at the window."

Reggie puts his hand on the little brass wheel.

He doesn't know what he expects. He's been leaving it lit since the spring of 1957, the whole of every night, and not known he was doing it. He went to sleep under it for ten years alone and for two and a half not. He supposes he'd expected, when this came, that it would feel like something. Like a door. Like the end of something, and a great weight going off.

It doesn't. It feels like turning down a lamp.

The man in Ålesund isn't sitting in the dark. He's eaten something. He's going to read his paper on the Tuesday and not look at the door, and he's glad, and he's checked. There's nobody out there across the water waiting for a light. There's nobody on the far side of anything. There's just the room, and the bed, and the man in it.

He turns the wheel. The flame goes down, and down, and out.

The hut goes dark. It's very dark. He can't see a thing for a moment. Then the shutters come up grey with the moon, and the shape of the table, and the letter on it, and the bed.

"There," he says.

"*There*," says Pascual, in the dark. His voice is not quite steady. "Come here. You're letting the cold in."

"It's twenty-six degrees."

"You're letting the *cold* in, Reggie."

He gets into the bed, on the outside, where there's about eight inches. Pascual puts his arm over him and pulls, hard, the way you'd pull a man out of the water. Reggie ends up against the whole warm length of him with his face in Pascual's neck, and he laughs, and then he isn't laughing.

"It's all right," says Pascual into his hair. "*Hombre*. It's all right."

"I know it is," says Reggie. "That's the — I *know* it is. That's what I can't —"

"I know."

"He *checked* it."

"I know he did. Go to sleep."

He goes to sleep. It happens all at once, the way it always does, with his arm flung up over his head and his mouth a little open. For the first time since the spring of 1957 he does it in the dark.

---

On the Saturday after, Pascual Pobil goes into the hut at the end of the row at ten in the morning with the oil can, the way he has every Saturday for thirteen years.

Reggie's at the pens. The hut is full of sun. The bed's made, badly, which means Reggie made it, and there's a green notebook open on the table with the week's water temperatures in it in Reggie's small neat hand, twenty-six, twenty-six, twenty-seven. There's a cup with a ring of cold tea in it, and the drawer is shut.

The lamp is on the corner of the table where it's always been.

Pascual picks it up, from habit, and shakes it, and stops.

It's full. It's full to the brim, the way he left it last Saturday. It hasn't burnt a drop all week. Not one night.

He stands in the middle of the hut with the lamp in one hand and the oil can in the other, and looks at it.

Thirteen years. Every Saturday. He's filled it through four inspections and a dead calf and a hurricane. He filled it in the spring of 1957 when the man couldn't eat, and in 1960 after *four degrees*, and every week of the two and a half years he sat on the step trying to find a way to say it. He'd have gone on filling it till one of them died. He'd have been glad to. It was the one thing, for most of those years, that he was allowed to do.

He puts the lamp down on the corner of the table where it was. He puts the oil can down beside it on the floor.

Then, because there's nobody to see, and because he's a man from Zaragoza and his face has never in fifty-seven years been able to keep anything off it, he sits down on the badly made bed for a minute and has a good cry.

After that he gets up and goes down to the north pen. The calf's mother's teeth still want looking at. Reggie's standing at the rail in his shirtsleeves with his glasses pushed up on his forehead and a bucket in his hand, arguing with a student about water chemistry. He looks up as Pascual comes down the path.

"There you are," says Reggie. "Where've you been? You're late."

"I've been filling your lamp."

Reggie looks at him. He looks at him the way he's been able to since November 1967, the whole of him, pointed. He reads it in about a second and a half.

"Oh," he says.

"It's full."

"Yes," says Reggie. "I know." His face does something at the corner of the mouth. "Sorry. I should've told you. You wasted a trip."

"I didn't waste anything," says Pascual. He takes the other bucket off the wall. "Give me the wedge. And you're wrong about the water chemistry, I heard you from the path."

"I'm not wrong."

"You're wrong," says Pascual. "You've been wrong about water chemistry since 1952. It was the teeth."

And Reggie laughs, the real laugh, short, from the chest, and hands him the wedge.
