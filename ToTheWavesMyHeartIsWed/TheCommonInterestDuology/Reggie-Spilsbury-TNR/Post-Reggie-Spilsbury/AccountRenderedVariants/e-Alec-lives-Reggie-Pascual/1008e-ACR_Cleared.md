# Cleared

The first thing Pascual does, the morning after Reggie's boat goes, is weigh himself.

He doesn't know why. He goes into the long room at six, before the students, and stands on the platform scale they use for the calf's feed and for the students when they're being stupid about diving. He reads it, and writes it down on the back of a feed sheet, and puts the feed sheet in the drawer of his bench. Then he stands there with his hand on the drawer and feels like a fool.

*A control*, he'd said to Reggie in March, standing him on that scale every Friday. *For the calf's charts.* It wasn't for the calf's charts. They'd both known it wasn't. It was so that on Friday mornings there'd be a figure going up in pencil on the back of a sheet, a quarter of a pound and a half a pound at a time. He could look at it on a bad night.

There's nobody to weigh now. So he weighs himself. It turns out that's what you do with the habit when the thing it was for has gone to London.

He's fine for the first week.

He does the calf. He does the old cow, who's gone off her fish a little and has to be coaxed with the good mullet. He does the young males off the second head in the launch with a second-year at the tiller. The boy puts them within a yard of the reef edge, and Pascual shouts at him in two languages and feels a great deal better. He eats in the long room with the students and the Colonel. He tells the Colonel that Dr Tar is at the Zoological Society in London on the Institute's business, which is true, and the Colonel says *quite right, show the flag*. Hester looks at Pascual over her glasses and says nothing.

On Thursday the fifteenth of September, at about the hour a man would be sitting down on a bench in London, Pascual finds he's standing in the north pen to his waist with his hand flat on the calf's flank. He's been there twenty minutes and can't remember going in.

On the Saturday he fills the lamp.

He'd said he would. *Till then*, he'd said, in the bed from the Bay, with a bead of blue light through the slats. So at eight o'clock he goes along the row with the can, and lets himself into the hut at the end with the key Reggie gave him in June, and fills it. It's burning half what it did. It takes almost nothing. He turns it back down to its bead. And then he stands in the dark hut with the can, where nobody is sitting on the bed to say *well?* to, and finds he can't leave.

He sits down on the bed himself. It's too narrow, and too hard, and smells of Reggie. He sits there for some while with the can on the floor between his feet, looking at the bead of light, and thinks about the other man, who's never seen it.

*It's his*, he'd said. *He's been decent.*

He's frightened. He can admit that on the second Saturday, sitting on the bed. He's not frightened of the man in London. He liked him, and the letter made him cry in the long room, and he'd put money on the man having done nothing on a bench in Regent's Park but behave perfectly. He's frightened of the thing he can't put his hand on, the way he was in the launch. He's frightened of the sea between here and there, and the boat, and the seven weeks, and of a man who might come back up the path knowing something he doesn't.

He doesn't say any of this to anybody. He doesn't need to. On the second Monday Hester stops him in the passage outside the office.

"Dr Pobil. You've lost weight."

"I've not."

"You have. Four pounds, I should think." She looks at him over her glasses. "I've kept the books on this station since 1938. I know what a man looks like when he's not eating his lunch."

"I've been eating my lunch."

"You have been *moving* your lunch," says Hester, "about your plate, with a fork, for nine days. Even the Colonel's noticed." She turns to go back into the office. "He'll be home on the sixth. He'll want to see you've eaten. It seems to be what you both do."

---

The telegram comes on Tuesday the fourth of October, from Kingston, by the Institute's boat. Alleyne's boy brings it up to the long room in his cap and Pascual opens it at the bench.

*ARRIVED KINGSTON. BAY THURSDAY. LATE BOAT. ATE CHOP. TAR.*

He reads it four times.

He doesn't know what *ate chop* means. He sits at the bench with the telegram in his hand and turns it over. At a shilling a word from Kingston, Reggie Tar has spent a shilling on the information that he ate a chop, and that's the part Pascual can't get past. He's never known the man send an unnecessary word by any means that charged by the word. He puts it in his breast pocket, and takes it out again at dinner, and reads it under the table.

*Ate chop.*

He finds, somewhere around the pudding, that he's grinning like a fool, and that he's hungry, and that he's eaten everything on his plate and would like more.

Hester, at the end of the table, passes him the dish without being asked.

---

Thursday he's on the step at nine.

The late boat from the Bay comes in when it comes in, which is between ten and midnight depending on Alleyne's boy and the wind. Pascual knows that. There's no reason on earth to be on the step at nine. He's on the step at nine, in his shirtsleeves, with a book.

He doesn't know what the book is. He took it off his shelf on the way out. It's one of the Spanish ones, and he's been sitting with it open on his knee for a quarter of an hour. At a quarter past nine he looks down, and it's upside down.

He looks at it for a long moment. Then he laughs, out loud, alone on the step at the end of the row, and doesn't turn it the right way up.

He sits there with it upside down till half past eleven.

He hears the boat before he sees it: the little engine coming round the second head, and then the change in the note as it throttles back for the channel. He sees the lamp at the bow. He hears it bump the jetty and Alleyne's boy say something, and a voice answer. It's short and dry and entirely familiar. Pascual's whole body goes loose and hot at once, sitting on the step, so that he has to put his hand flat on the planking.

Then there are feet on the path. Short, quick, a little uneven on the left.

He doesn't get up. He can't. He sits on the step with the book upside down on his knee and watches the small shape come up the path in the moonlight with a case in one hand. It stops at the bottom of the step.

Reggie looks at him. Then he looks at the book.

Pascual looks up.

"Oh," says Pascual. "You're back."

Reggie stands at the bottom of the step for a long moment with his case in his hand. His face in the moonlight does something Pascual has seen perhaps twice. Then he puts the case down, very carefully, on the path.

"I'm back," he says.

---

He doesn't go up the step. He goes down on his knees at the bottom of it, on the path, in his travelling suit, so that his face is level with Pascual's where Pascual's sitting. He takes Pascual's face in both hands. He's aware that the row might be awake and finds, for about five seconds, that he doesn't care, and then that he does. He kisses him once, hard, and lets go, and sits back on his heels on the path.

"Your book's upside down," says Reggie.

"I know it is."

"You *know*?"

"I've known since a quarter past nine." Pascual's voice is doing something. "I left it. I thought it was fair."

Reggie laughs. It comes up out of the whole of him after seven weeks, and he has to put his hand over his mouth for the row. Pascual's laughing too, with his face gone to pieces, sitting on the step with a Spanish book upside down on his knee.

"You've lost weight," says Reggie, when he can.

"I have *not* —"

"You have. Four pounds. Five. I can see it in your face." He puts his hand up flat against Pascual's jaw and feels it. "You've not been eating."

"Hester said four."

"Hester's generous." He keeps his hand there. "I'll weigh you in the morning."

"What *for*?"

"The calf needs a control," says Reggie, and Pascual makes a sound that isn't a word and leans his face into Reggie's palm and shuts his eyes.

"You ate a chop," says Pascual, into the palm.

"I did."

"You sent a telegram about it. From Kingston. A shilling."

"I'd promised."

"Promised *who*?"

"Him," says Reggie. "He told me to eat a chop before the boat train. And I made him promise to eat one too. So I sent you the chop. I thought you ought to know it had been eaten." He hears how that sounds. "It made sense in Kingston."

Pascual opens his eyes.

"He made you eat a chop."

"We made each other. It's the kind of thing we do. It's the kind of thing *you* do." Reggie takes his hand back. "He's handed it over, Pascual. In writing and in person. You're in charge of the chops now."

Pascual looks at him for a long time on the step in the moonlight. His face goes through everything, one thing after another, all the way down.

"Come inside," he says. "Tell me."

---

He tells him in the hut at the end of the row, with the door bolted, sitting on the narrow bed. The lamp's on the table, burning its bead of blue, as it has been every night since November. Pascual sits beside him, not a foot off, with his shoulder against Reggie's.

He tells him most of it. He tells him the Aquarium, and the lobsters, and the conger with the figure seven on its side. Pascual laughs at the conger. He tells him the bench in front of the sprats, and what he said there, about the path.

"And what did he say?" says Pascual.

"He said he knew. He's always known I'd turn him round, if he came." Reggie looks at his hands. "That's why he never came. So I'd not have to do it twice."

Pascual's quiet for a long moment.

"And then?"

"And then he asked me who was paying." Reggie's mouth goes. "The same thing you asked me in the launch. Near enough the same words."

"*Hombre*," says Pascual at last. "The pair of you."

"I know."

"Nine years. You keeping a lamp lit he didn't even know about, so you'd not be a man who'd stopped. Him staying away so you'd not have to put him out in the rain."

"I know."

"And each of you knowing what the other was doing, and neither saying."

"Yes."

"That's —" Pascual puts his hand over his eyes. "That's the stupidest thing I've ever heard, and the most decent, and I don't know which, and I don't think you do either."

"No," says Reggie. "I don't."

He doesn't tell him everything. He's decided that on the boat, somewhere south of the Azores, lying in a bunk. He'd kept things back on the bench from Alec, the step and the plank and the basin, because they weren't Alec's to carry home. He keeps a thing back now from Pascual for the same reason. *I have seen it in a mirror. I am trying.* That isn't his to hand on. It's Alec's, and it was said to him, and he'll keep it where Alec asked to be kept.

Pascual reads that something's been kept. Of course he does. He looks at Reggie sidelong, and Reggie sees him see it, and sees him decide not to ask.

"Is he all right?" is all Pascual says.

Reggie thinks about it properly.

"He's thin," he says. "He's fed on Sundays by a woman who puts more on his plate than he eats. He has the West Ice seals. He laughed twice." He stops. "He'll be all right. Not quickly. But he means to be."

"Good," says Pascual. His voice isn't steady. "Good."

"And he sent you a message."

Pascual goes still.

"I gave him yours," says Reggie. "About the chops and the regards and the dentition. He laughed at the dentition. Out loud, in the Gloucester Gate, in the rain. I've heard him laugh like that perhaps a dozen times in twenty years."

"He *laughed*."

"He said he wasn't right about the dentition at all. He said you found the premolar with your thumb in about four seconds."

"It was —" Pascual stops.

"And were kind to him about it for nine months." Reggie watches Pascual's face go red to the ears in the lamplight. "He said you were telling him he was right so he'd go home with something. He said you were a very decent man."

"I —" Pascual's mouth works. "It was the *left upper*. The deciduous had never come out. He'd not have seen it without his hand in her mouth, nobody would, it wasn't —"

"He said to tell you he was wrong about the dentition," says Reggie, "and that he knows you know. And that he's obliged to you."

Pascual can't say anything.

"I asked him if that was for the lie," says Reggie. "He said it was for the kindness, and that I'd have to learn to tell the difference, because I'd been living next to a kind man for nine years and hadn't noticed."

Pascual puts both hands over his face.

He sits on the narrow bed in the hut at the end of the row with his face in his hands. Reggie sits beside him and doesn't say anything, and puts his hand on the back of the man's neck, flat, the way you'd steady a man on a deck. He leaves it there. He knows he's doing it.

---

"There's another message," Reggie says, after a while.

Pascual takes his hands off his face. His eyes are wet.

"About the lamp."

Pascual looks at it.

It's on the table where it's always been, the brass base and the glass chimney. There's a black mark on the inside of the chimney from the night in November, and the bead of blue at the bottom of the wick, hardly anything. The oil's half down. Pascual filled it on Saturday.

"I told him," says Reggie. "About the lamp. That I'd lit it every night since 1957. That you'd filled it every Saturday. That you measured the oil and never said a word about what it was for."

"You told him *that*?"

"It was the one thing about you I thought he had a right to." Reggie keeps his eyes on the lamp. "He didn't know. He said a lamp is for somebody who's expected, and he isn't. He said I'd lit it nine years for a man I'd turn round at the bottom of the step."

"No."

"He doesn't want to be waited for any more. He'd like to be kept." He stops. "The way you keep a letter in a drawer. You open it when you like, and shut it, and go to bed. You don't sit up by a drawer."

Pascual doesn't say anything.

"And he said —" Reggie's voice goes, and he lets it, and gets it back. "He said to tell Dr Pobil, with his regards, that you've filled it long enough. And that you're to put it out yourself. He said you've more right to it than either of us. You've paid for the oil."

It's very quiet in the hut. Through the shutters there's the sound of the calf blowing down in the north pen, and the sea on the rocks at the second head.

Pascual looks at the lamp for a long time.

"*Me*," he says.

"You."

"It's *his*."

"He's given it you. He says it's yours." Reggie turns his head and looks at him. "Pascual. I'm not asking you to. If you'd rather I did it. If you'd rather it stayed lit another week, or a month, or —"

"No." Pascual's still looking at the lamp. "No. Don't ask me that. He's right. That's the worst of it, he's always *right*, that man, it's unbearable —" His voice cracks straight across. "Nine years I've filled that thing. I used to stand here with the can and think *one day it'll go out*. And I thought it'd be because he'd come back. Or because you'd — I didn't know. I never thought it'd be *me*."

"He thought it should be."

"He's never seen it."

"No," says Reggie. "He's never seen it. You're the only one who ever saw it from the other side. Every Saturday."

Pascual gets up.

He crosses the hut, which is four steps, and stands at the table and looks down at the lamp. Reggie sits on the bed and doesn't move. He's decided that on the boat too: he'll sit, and not move, and not help, and let it be Pascual's.

Pascual puts his hand on the brass wheel of the wick.

He doesn't turn it at once. He stands there with his hand on it, big and brown and scarred across the back. And Reggie watches his face in the blue light from below, and can read every line of it, and doesn't need to. There's the nine years in it. There's a can on the path every Saturday at eight, and a man sitting on this bed saying *well?* There's a thin white-haired man in a grey stone building on a quay who didn't know, and should have. And there's something else under all of it that's just Pascual's, and is private, and Reggie lets it stay private.

Then Pascual turns the wheel.

The bead goes down. It goes smaller, and smaller, and blue to nothing. There's a faint smell of paraffin and hot brass, and a thread of smoke going straight up in the chimney, and then it's dark. The shutters show grey where the moon's behind them, and there's nothing else.

Neither of them says anything.

Reggie sits on the bed in the dark in the hut at the end of the row. It's the first time since the spring of 1957, and he waits for what it'll be. He'd thought, on the boat, that it might be grief. He's grieved in this hut before. He knows the shape of it. He'd thought it might be a terrible emptiness, like the day after a funeral.

It isn't either of those. It's quiet. That's all. It's dark, and quiet, and there's a man standing at the table with his hand still on the wheel, breathing hard. And there's nobody coming up the path, and nobody expected, and nobody to sit up for.

"Pascual," he says.

"Give me a minute."

"Take it."

Pascual takes it. He stands at the table in the dark with his hand on the cold wheel, and his shoulders go, once, and then again. Reggie sits and lets him. After a while Pascual takes his hand off the wick. He picks up the lamp, the whole of it, brass and glass, in both hands, as though it were an animal he was lifting, and carries it across the hut. He puts it on the shelf over the washstand, beside the shaving things, where you'd put a lamp you meant to keep but not to light.

Then he comes and sits down on the bed.

"There," he says. His voice is wrecked. "*There*. Kept."

Reggie puts his arm round him in the dark.

---

Later, he gets up. He finds the letter in his breast pocket, the one on blue airmail paper that came on the fourteenth of June. He's carried it to London and back. He takes it out, and opens the drawer of the table where the form letters are, eighteen of them and one more, in their creases. He puts the blue one in on top, with the others, and shuts the drawer. He leaves his hand flat on it for a moment.

Then he takes it away, and goes back to bed.

---

The bed is still too narrow. It was built for one man in 1950 by a carpenter who didn't hold with comfort, and Pascual lies on it and says so.

"I'll not buy one from the Bay," says Reggie.

"I didn't ask you to."

"You were going to."

"I was *going* to say," says Pascual, "that we've got a perfectly good bed ten yards up the row."

"We have."

"And that you've always said you'd not sleep anywhere but your own hut."

"I did say that."

"So?"

Reggie lies half on top of him in the dark, because there's nowhere else to lie. He considers it.

"So I'd like to be in my own hut tonight," he says. "With the lamp out. I've not been in it with the lamp out since I was thirty-eight. I'd like to know what it's like. With you in it."

Pascual's quiet for a moment under him.

"Then we'll manage," he says, and his voice has gone rough. "We've managed on worse. We managed on a camp bed in the long room."

"We didn't manage on the camp bed. We stopped on the camp bed."

"We're not going to stop tonight," says Pascual.

"No," says Reggie. "We're not."

It's different in the dark. He'd known it would be. He hadn't known how. In June, in the bed from the Bay, there'd been a line of blue light through the slats of the shutter all night, and he'd lain there with it on the edge of his eye. Now there's nothing. There's the grey of the moon behind the shutters, and a man under him whose face he can't see and whose every breath he can feel.

He doesn't need to see it. He finds that out almost at once. He's read that face for fourteen years in every light there is. He finds he can read it in the dark with his hands. He finds the jaw, and the mouth, and the place under the ear, and the wet at the corners of the eyes that isn't gone yet. He finds the heart going under his palm like a pump.

"You're shaking," Reggie says.

"You've been gone seven weeks."

"I know."

"Seven *weeks*. I weighed myself. Every morning. I didn't know what else to do with it." Pascual's hands have come up to his back, under the travelling shirt he's still got on, and they're spread wide, and holding on. "I sat on your step with a book upside down. I stood in the north pen for twenty minutes with my hand on the calf and couldn't remember going in."

"When?"

"Thursday. The fifteenth. About four in the afternoon." Pascual's voice cracks. "About the time you'd have been sitting down."

Reggie can't say anything to that. He puts his face down into Pascual's neck, in the dark, and breathes there. There's soap and salt and linseed and the calf, and the thing under all of it that's just Pascual. He's smelt it for fourteen years on jerseys and in doorways, and he knows its name now.

"I was on a bench in front of the sprats," he says, into the man's throat. "At four. Telling him. And I thought about you standing in the north pen. Just for a second. I thought, *he'll be doing the calf*."

Pascual's arms tighten round him until he can hardly breathe.

"Take this off," says Pascual. "This shirt. It smells of the boat."

"It's been on the boat for a fortnight."

"I *know*. Take it off."

Reggie takes it off. It's hard, lying on a man on a bed built for a ferret, and he gets an elbow in Pascual's ribs, and Pascual says *ay*. They're both laughing, in the dark, and then the shirt's gone, and he comes back down and there's skin. There's the whole broad warm length of it under him, all the way, chest to chest. The laughing stops.

"*There*," says Pascual. "That's — there. You."

"Me."

"Don't go to London again."

"I'll not go to London again."

"Don't go anywhere. Not for seven weeks. I'll not do it. I'm fifty-two, I've not got it in me, I'll weigh myself to *death* —"

"Pascual." Reggie gets his hand on the man's face in the dark and holds it. "I'm not going anywhere. I've been to London and told him, and eaten the chop, and come back. It's done." He kisses him, slowly, knowing it. "I'm in my own hut, with the lamp out, with you. And I'd like to stay here a very long time. Is that asking?"

Pascual makes a sound under him that's half a laugh and half the other thing.

"That's asking," he says. "*Yes*. Now shut up."

Reggie shuts up.

It's slow, because it has to be on that bed, and because it's been seven weeks, and because neither of them wants it fast. Pascual's hands learn his back all over again in the dark. They go down it one vertebra at a time and stop at the small of it, and spread there, and pull him in. His own hands learn Pascual's face and throat and chest, the grey hair and the ridge of the scar under the ribs and the heat of him. And he talks, because Pascual asked him to in June and has gone on asking. He says *there*, and *yes*, and *I missed this*, and *I missed you*, and *I stood at the rail of that boat every evening south of the Azores and thought about this bed*. It all comes out plain, in his own voice, in the dark, with nothing at all to hide.

Pascual talks back, in two languages, low, with his mouth at Reggie's ear. Most of the Spanish Reggie doesn't know. Some of it he's starting to. He knows *así*, and he knows *no pares*, and he knows his own name, which Pascual says over and over as though he's been saving it up for seven weeks and has a great deal to get through.

Pascual's not clumsy now. He hasn't been since the second night in June. He's a man who learns bodies with his hands in the dark. He's learnt this one, all of it, carefully, over the summer, the way he'd learn the calf. Reggie can feel him use every bit of it now, slow and exact. He can feel Pascual find the places he found in July and August and go back to each of them in turn, as though he were checking they were still there after seven weeks. They are, every one. And Reggie hears himself make the sounds he makes, in the dark, in his own hut, where there's nobody left to wait for and nothing to keep from being seen.

Pascual goes first. Reggie feels it coming in him before Pascual knows it himself: the breath catching and catching again, the big hands on his back going still and then gripping, hard, as if the bed were a deck in a sea. Then Pascual shakes all along Reggie's length, and says *Reggie* in the dark, quite low, the way you'd say something you'd found safe on a shelf where you'd left it. Reggie holds him through it, with his mouth at the man's temple, and feels every bit of it go through him. It's that that undoes him. It comes over him a moment after like the tide coming in over the reef flat, all at once, lifting everything. He holds on to Pascual with both arms and his face in the man's shoulder, and doesn't say anything at the end but Pascual's name. He says it into his skin as though it were the only word he'd brought back.

---

Afterwards they lie in the dark on the narrow bed, tangled because there's no other way to lie on it. Reggie's half on top of him with his head on Pascual's chest. Pascual's arm is round him and his hand is flat over the middle of Reggie's back, holding on.

Neither of them says anything for a long time.

Out in the north pen the calf blows. Reggie counts it without meaning to, and then stops counting, because there's no reason to count it.

"It's very dark," says Pascual at last.

"Yes."

"I've not been in here in the dark. Not once. Since 'fifty-two." His hand moves on Reggie's back. "It was always lit. Even the night in November with your hand. Even that."

"I know."

"It's a different hut."

"It's the same hut," says Reggie. "It's the first time either of us has seen it."

Pascual laughs, very low, and his chest shakes under Reggie's cheek.

"That's a terrible thing to say."

"You always say that."

"You always *say* them."

And then he's quiet, and after a while his breathing changes, and his arm goes heavy. He's asleep. He's asleep in the hut at the end of the row, in the dark, with his hand flat on Reggie's back, holding on even in his sleep, the way he said in June. Reggie lies on him and listens to him breathe.

He isn't going to sleep. He's quite sure of that. He's been on a boat for a fortnight. He's been to London and sat on a bench and heard a thing he'd carried nine years turn out to have had another end. He's had a message put in his hands and handed it on. He's sat on this bed and watched the best man he knows put out a lamp. And there's a drawer in the table with nineteen letters in it, kept.

He'll lie here and go through all of it.

He's asleep inside a minute.

---

On the Saturday Pascual comes along the row at eight with the oil can.

He doesn't mean to. He's halfway along with it swinging in his hand before he knows he's got it, and then he stops on the path outside the Colonel's hut and looks at it. It's the same can, the one with the tin handle that squeaks on the forward swing. He's carried it along this path every Saturday since the spring of 1957.

He stands there with it for a long moment in the evening light.

Then he goes on anyway, because it's Saturday, and it's eight, and it's where he goes. He lets himself in with the key. Reggie's at the table with the calf's weight sheets and his glasses pushed up on his forehead, and he looks up, and sees the can. His face does something small and complicated that Pascual can read all the way down.

"You've brought the can," says Reggie.

"I've brought the can."

"There's nothing to fill."

"I know there's nothing to fill." Pascual looks at the shelf over the washstand, where the lamp stands dark beside the shaving things. He looks at the can in his hand. "I brought it anyway. It's Saturday."

Reggie takes his glasses off, and folds them, and puts them on the weight sheets.

"Put it on the shelf," he says. "By the lamp. Next to it."

Pascual goes and puts it on the shelf. The can and the lamp stand side by side over the washstand in the evening light, brass and tin, neither of them wanted. That turns out to be the right place for them. Pascual can't have said why. He stands and looks at them for a moment.

Then he comes back across the hut and sits down on the bed, where he's sat on Saturdays since 1957.

"Well?" says Pascual.

And Reggie tells him the week.
