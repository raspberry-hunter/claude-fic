# Not for Circulation

It's Crane who notices, and she notices on a Tuesday in February at lunch, on the verandah, with her mouth full.

"Has anyone," she says, and swallows, "ever actually talked to Tar about Spilsbury?"

Ansell has. Obviously he has. He opens his mouth to say so.

Crane is in her third year. She's Dr Leslie's student, from Bangor, and she came out in October 'sixty-seven, and she has the particular air of a woman who's been on a small island for two and a half years and has read every book in its library, some of them twice. She doesn't ask things idly. She's sitting on the verandah rail with her plate on her knee and her feet on the bench, looking at the long room door as if it had just said something to her.

"Course I have," says Ansell. "In November. About the decline curve."

"What did he say?"

Ansell thinks about it.

It was a good afternoon. He remembers that quite clearly. He'd been at the bench next to Tar's with the oxygen figures, and he'd said something about the Spilsbury decline curve, how elegant it was, how he'd like to do something like that one day with the dive data, and Tar had leaned back on his stool and smiled at him. Tar doesn't often smile at him. Then they'd talked for three quarters of an hour, and it had been the best supervision Ansell had had since he came out. Tar had pulled his oxygen figures to pieces and put them back together in a better order, and been funny, and asked about Leeds, and told him a story about a skate in somebody's bath. Ansell had gone to bed that night thinking that Dr Tar was a great deal nicer than people said.

"He said my dive data wanted a log scale," Ansell says.

"About Spilsbury."

"We talked about —" Ansell stops. "We talked about my oxygen curve."

"Yes," says Crane. "Baptiste?"

Baptiste is sitting on the step below the rail with her back against a post and her notebook on her knee, because Baptiste always has her notebook on her knee. She came out in October, the same boat as Ansell. She's from Port of Spain by way of the university at St Augustine, and she's the cleverest person he has ever met and the most frightening, and she writes down the time of everything.

"I asked him about the hooded seals," Baptiste says. "In December. At dinner. Whether the West Ice series was really twenty years or whether the early years were patchy."

"And?"

"And we talked about my grandmother." Baptiste frowns at her notebook, as though it had let her down. "He wanted to know about her boat. She had a pirogue at Carenage. He asked very good questions about the pirogue."

"How long did you talk about the pirogue?"

"I don't know. I didn't — " Baptiste stops. "Ten minutes? About ten minutes."

Crane puts her plate down on the rail.

"I've been here two and a half years," she says. "I've been to every dinner. I've been in the long room most days. I've heard Spilsbury's name at that table perhaps a hundred times. Visitors say it. Dr Leslie reads out his releases from Ålesund when a paper comes in. The deposit's half the reason anybody applies here." She looks at them both. "And I've never once talked to Tar about him. Not once. And I didn't notice till about ten minutes ago."

"They worked together," says Ansell slowly. "At the Labs. After the war."

"They worked together for ten years. He sat on the wall at the north pen for fifty-one days. That's his specimen in the deposit. He *took the animal*. It's on page six." Crane spreads her hands. "And I can't remember one conversation. Not one. Can you?"

Ansell tries.

He can remember the name being said. He can remember a great many pleasant evenings. He can remember Tar smiling at the head of the table, and people going up to bed saying *what a delightful evening*. He can't remember a single thing Tar has ever said about the man.

"Wait," he says.

"Yes," says Crane.

"No, but, *wait*."

"I know," says Crane. "That's what I said. About ten minutes ago."

Baptiste has turned to a clean page at the back of her notebook. She's ruling columns on it with the edge of her plate.

"What are you doing?" says Ansell.

"I'm going to measure it," says Baptiste.

---

The columns are *Date*, *Stimulus*, *Speaker*, *Latency*, *Duration*, and *Terminal Topic*. Baptiste explains them on the verandah that evening, with the tilley lamp between them and the insects banging on the glass, and Ansell has the strong feeling, as he often has with Baptiste, that he's been enrolled in something without being consulted.

"The stimulus is the name," she says. "Latency is how long before his face changes. Duration is how long before the conversation's somewhere else. Terminal topic is where it ends up."

"His face changes?"

"You've seen it. His shoulders drop about half an inch and he looks pleased. Like somebody's given him something." Baptiste taps the column. "I've seen it twice since lunch, now I'm looking. I didn't see it for four months before that. I think it happens every time."

"This is a bit much, isn't it?" says Ansell. "It's the man's private — it's his business."

"It's a behaviour," says Baptiste. "It's reproducible. Somebody should write it down."

"You can't just run *trials* on a *Consultant*."

"Why not? He'd run them on us."

That's true, and Ansell knows it's true. He's watched Tar time how long a student takes to label a jar. He's watched him stand at the north pen with a stopwatch and count how many seconds the calf's mother takes to come up, and then turn the stopwatch on the student who's supposed to be counting, and count how long *he* takes to notice. There's no answer to it.

"Anyway, I'm not running trials," says Baptiste. "I'm recording observations. That's different."

It isn't, and within a fortnight it's definitely not, because by then she's making Ansell say the name.

---

Trial four is on the ninth of March, at dinner, with the Americans from Woods Hole. Ansell is the stimulus. He's been told what to say. He's to be natural about it.

He's not natural about it. He's never been natural about anything in his life, and he knows as he opens his mouth that his voice is going to be too loud, and it is.

"Of course, Dr Tar," he says, "you'll have known Spilsbury very well."

Crane, across the table, closes her eyes.

And Tar leans back.

It's exactly as Baptiste said. Ansell has never watched it properly before, and now he watches it from about four feet away, and it is uncanny. Tar's shoulders come down, not much, and his face goes open and warm. He smiles at Ansell with enormous pleasure, as though Ansell were the most interesting person at the table, and says, "Oh, everybody knew Spilsbury, he made sure of it. He told half the Linnean they were wrong. Speaking of which, Ansell — that log scale. Did you try it?"

And Ansell, who knows perfectly well what's happening, who has been *briefed*, finds himself saying that he did try it, actually, and it helped, but the variance at the deep end was still very ugly. And Tar says *show me after dinner*, and turns to the man from Woods Hole and asks whether it's true they've got a new boat. And the man from Woods Hole talks about his boat for twenty minutes. Ansell sits there with his soup going cold and understands that he's been handled, beautifully, by an expert, and that he still wants to show Tar the variance after dinner.

He does show him. It takes an hour. Tar finds the problem in the first ten minutes and spends the other fifty showing Ansell why it was a problem. It's the most useful hour Ansell has spent on the island.

Baptiste, on the verandah afterwards, writes it up by lamplight.

*9.iii.70. Stimulus: P.A., direct. Latency: approx. 2 sec. Duration: 1 min 40 sec to terminal topic. Terminal topic: P.A.'s variance (then Woods Hole boat). Subject affect: warm. P.A. affect: enchanted.*

"I wasn't *enchanted*."

"You showed him your variance for an hour."

"It was useful."

"Yes," says Baptiste. "That's what makes it good."

---

By the end of April there are seven entries, and the figures are very consistent. The latency is always about two seconds. The duration is somewhere between a minute and a half and four minutes, depending on how hard the stimulus pushes. It's never longer than ten minutes before the name's gone down like a stone and doesn't come up. The terminal topic is always the stimulus's own work. Or their boat, or their grandmother, or a skate in a bath.

"He knows what everyone wants to talk about," says Crane. "Everyone. That's the trick. He gives everybody the thing they most want, and they're so pleased they don't notice he's taken something."

"That's a bit sinister," says Ansell.

"It's not sinister, it's *kind*. It's the kindest thing he does. He's never that nice any other time." Crane frowns. "That's what's odd. Why would you be at your nicest to get rid of a name?"

Nobody knows.

So Crane goes to the library, because that's what Crane does with a question, and on a Saturday in May she comes down the steps to the verandah with the 197 paper open in her hands and her face very serious.

"Look," she says. "Look at the title page."

It's the paper everybody reads in their first week. It's the one on the specimen in the deposit, the reason the deposit exists. *R. Tar*, it says on the front, and under that Dr Leslie's initials, very small, and nothing else.

"Now look at page six."

Page six is the methods. Ansell has read it. The specimen was taken by Dr A. Spilsbury on such a date, at such a place, in such a manner, and it goes on for a whole paragraph, very plain.

"He took the animal," says Crane. "It's his specimen. He's in the methods, by name, for a whole paragraph. And he's not on the paper."

They look at each other.

"Oh," says Baptiste.

"Oh, no," says Ansell.

"A dispute," says Crane, "about authorship."

It's awful. It's the most awful thing any of them can think of. Ansell has heard stories about it at Leeds, in the pub, about men in other departments who took somebody's name off something. Men who put their own name first when it should have been second. Men who never spoke again for forty years and went to each other's funerals to make sure. It's the thing you're warned about in your first term. It's the one thing, more than being wrong, more than being scooped, that nobody ever forgives.

And it explains everything. That's the horrible part. It fits all the data.

"It explains the letters," says Baptiste slowly. "Twice a year, March and September. *In accordance with the terms of deposit.* Dr Leslie reads them out. That's not a friend writing, that's a *condition*. That's a *settlement*."

"There was a solicitor," says Crane. "Old Mr Purdie at the Bay. He drew up the deposit. Mrs Bevan told me."

"Lawyers."

"Lawyers," says Crane. "Over a title page."

Ansell sits down on the step.

He thinks of Tar leaning back, and his face going warm, and the voice he uses. He thinks of the best conversations he's had on the island, every one of them started by somebody saying a name that made Tar want to be somewhere else. He feels suddenly and quite horribly as though he's been reading somebody's letters.

"We have to stop," he says. "The trials. We have to stop."

"Yes," says Baptiste, straight away, and closes the notebook.

"It's his —" Ansell doesn't know how to put it. "Whatever happened, it's his. And it's obviously still — he obviously still minds. Twelve years. We can't go poking it with a stick at dinner to see how fast he twitches."

"No," says Crane. She's gone rather pink. "No, you're right. That's — no."

So they stop. They agree it on the verandah, the three of them, solemnly, like a treaty. Nobody's to say the name at the long table again when Tar's there. If a visitor says it, they're to help. And nobody's to mention page six to anybody, ever. It's the most decent thing Ansell has done in his life, and he feels quite proud of it, and slightly ashamed of the three months before.

---

It turns out that the helping isn't difficult, because somebody's been doing it for years.

Ansell notices that in June. He's in the long room when a new student, a boy from Glasgow who's been on the island for four days, comes in with the Ålesund hooded seal release in his hand and a face full of questions and heads for Tar's bench. And Dr Pobil, who's at the sink with his sleeves rolled up and his reading glasses on the end of his nose doing something unspeakable to a fish, says without turning round, very pleasantly, "Ah, that's Jean you want. She's got the deposit. Office, second door." The boy says *thank you, sir*, and goes, and never gets within six feet of the bench.

Tar doesn't look up from his weight sheets.

Dr Pobil goes on with the fish.

Ansell stands very still with a beaker in his hand and thinks, *he knows*. *Pobil knows. He's been doing it for years.*

Later that week he watches Mrs Bevan do it. She's retired now and lives at the Bay, but she still comes over on the Tuesday boat to see about things nobody's asked her to see about. A visitor at lunch starts on the Spilsbury deposit, and Mrs Bevan, from the end of the table, asks the visitor about his wife's health in a voice that could stop a clock. The visitor talks about his wife's health until the pudding.

So it's true, then. It's all true. Everybody who's been here long enough knows about the dispute. They've closed ranks round it, the way people do round something shameful that happened to somebody they're fond of. Ansell finds it rather moving.

He brings it up with Dr Leslie in July, which is a mistake.

He doesn't mean to. He's in her office handing in a draft, and she's reading it with her pencil, and he's standing about in the way you do, and it comes out. He asks — very carefully, he thinks — whether Dr Spilsbury ever comes across to the Institute. To use the deposit. Since it's his.

Dr Leslie looks up.

She is a small, sharp, dark-haired woman who has been addressed as *Miss Leslie* by very nearly every visitor to the Institute since 1953, doctorate and all, and corrects none of them out loud that Ansell has heard, and he's afraid of her in a way that he isn't afraid even of Baptiste.

"No," she says. "He's not permitted on the Institute's premises."

She looks back down at his draft and makes a small mark with her pencil.

"Your third paragraph's in the wrong place," she says. "Was there anything else?"

"No, Dr Leslie."

"Then shut the door, please."

He shuts the door. He goes straight down to the verandah, where Crane and Baptiste are.

"*Not permitted*," he says. "On the *premises*."

Crane puts down her book.

"By whom?"

"She didn't say. She just said it. She said it like —" He tries. "Like reading a minute."

"A minute," says Crane. Her eyes have gone very wide. "The Board. It went to the *Board*."

"Or the Colonial Office," says Baptiste. "The licence comes from Kingston. If it was about the licence —"

"It was about a *title page*," says Ansell.

"People have gone further for less," says Crane. "Look at Newton and Leibniz. Look at Owen. Owen had a man's fossils taken off him and *sat on the committee*." She stands up and walks to the end of the verandah and back. "Somebody complained. Formally. And it went all the way up, and somebody in Kingston wrote *remove from the premises* in a file, and he can't come back. To his own specimen. Ever."

It's the most dreadful thing Ansell has ever heard.

"Who complained?" says Baptiste, in a small voice. "Which of them?"

Nobody answers that. Nobody wants to. They sit on the verandah as the sun goes down with that question in the middle of them, and none of them will pick it up.

---

The first time it comes up at dinner after the treaty is the last week in August. They make a hash of it.

The visitor is a man from the Plymouth laboratory called Hartley, a nephew of the Hartley who kept a skate in his bath. He's pink and cheerful and has come for a week to look at the calf's growth figures. On the second night he puts down his fork and turns to the head of the table with a pleased, expectant face.

"I say, Tar. Spilsbury. Is it true he sat on a wall for fifty-one days? My uncle swears it was —"

"Oh, you'll want Dr Leslie," says Crane, from halfway down the table, much too fast. "She handles the deposit."

"How's your boat?" says Baptiste, at the same moment. "Somebody said you'd had her refitted."

"Didn't your uncle keep a skate in his bath?" says Ansell, louder than either of them, because he's panicked. "Was that true?"

Hartley looks from one of them to another, blinking, with three questions arriving at once from three directions. Then he laughs, because he's a nice man, and says he'd no idea about the skate, he'd never thought to ask, what a marvellous thing to wonder about. The boat, though, the boat he can tell them about. He tells them about the boat until the pudding.

Ansell sits with his ears burning and doesn't dare look at the head of the table.

He looks anyway, over the pudding.

Tar isn't listening to the boat. He's sitting with his hands either side of his plate, and he's grey. He's been grey all summer, and it's worse by the lamps. He's looking down the table at the three of them. He looks at Crane, who's gone pink, and at Baptiste, who's looking fixedly at Hartley, and at Ansell, who can feel his face doing everything at once. He looks at each of them for a long moment.

It isn't the face Baptiste wrote down in February, with the shoulders coming down and the lamp lit behind it. Ansell would know that face anywhere now. This is a different one. It's quite still, and tired, and something is happening at the corner of the mouth that doesn't finish. Tar looks down at his plate for a second, as though he's lost something on it. Then he looks up and asks Hartley a question about the boat's keel, quite ordinarily, and the moment's gone.

"He saw," says Ansell on the verandah afterwards. "He *saw*. We were terrible."

"We weren't terrible," says Crane. "We were *enthusiastic*."

"We all went at once. Like a — like a fire drill."

"It worked," says Baptiste. "Hartley talked about his keel for forty minutes."

"It worked on *Hartley*."

---

The next morning Ansell is at the bench next to Tar's by half past seven, because he couldn't sleep, and Tar is there already with the calf's weight sheets as he is every morning.

They work for about twenty minutes without saying anything. Ansell is running his deep-end samples again and hating all of them.

"It was a thornback," says Tar.

Ansell looks up. Tar hasn't. He's writing a figure in the margin of the weight sheet in his small neat hand.

"Sir?"

"Hartley's skate. The old man's. It was a thornback, about two foot across. He kept it three weeks, and his wife made him take it back to Plymouth on the train in a bucket." He writes another figure. "In case it comes up again. It's the sort of thing that does."

Ansell stands at his bench with a sample tube in his hand.

Tar doesn't look up. He finishes the column. Then he pushes Ansell's deep-end figures back across the bench towards him without looking at them either, as though he'd had them the whole time, which he must have.

"Still wrong," he says. "Go and find out why."

"Yes, sir," says Ansell.

He goes and finds Crane and Baptiste in the library, where they're not supposed to be at eight in the morning, and tells them, word for word.

"*In case it comes up again*," says Crane.

"He knows," says Baptiste.

"Knows what? That we know? We don't *know* anything, we've got a title page and a theory."

"He knows we're doing it," says Baptiste. "Whatever he thinks we think. He knows what we were doing, and he gave you the skate." She sits back in her chair with her hands in her lap. "He gave you something to *use*."

They sit in the library and think about that.

"Oh," says Crane at last, quite softly. "Oh, that's *nice* of him."

Ansell doesn't say anything. He's thinking about the corner of the mouth that didn't finish, and about Tar looking down at his plate as though he'd lost something on it. He finds that he'd do it again tomorrow, fire drill and all, and every night after that, for as long as he's on the island.

---

In September a man called Ferris comes from Canada. He's very clever and very nice, and he's just come through London, and on the evening of the fifteenth he sits down at the long table full of Spilsbury.

Ansell sees it coming ten seconds before it arrives. So does Baptiste, beside him. So does Crane, across the table, who puts down her fork. They've had their treaty since May, and a skate since August. They've done it twice more since Hartley, more smoothly each time. They're quite good at it now. But Ferris is at the other end of the table, next to Tar, and he's fast, and he's already talking.

"I heard Spilsbury this morning," says Ferris. "The seal paper. The hooded seals, the whole series from the West Ice. It was extraordinary. You were colleagues, weren't you, Dr Tar? At the Labs? Someone said he'd been out here. Are you going over for the second week?"

And Ansell waits for the shoulders.

They don't come down.

Tar sits at the head of the table, looking at the young man from Canada. His face doesn't open. It doesn't go warm. It doesn't do anything at all. He's the wrong colour, Ansell thinks suddenly. He's been the wrong colour all summer. Ansell's been looking at him for six months to see his face change and he hasn't seen what colour it is.

"No," Tar says. "I'm not going. I'm too tired, I think."

He says something else, about how good the paper must have been. He says *you should read everything he's written*. And he gets up, with his dinner half eaten, and goes past the students' end of the table and out of the door.

Ansell hears him on the verandah steps. One at a time.

Then the steps stop.

Dr Sealy is up from the table and out of the door before anybody else has moved. Dr Pobil goes after her, and then Dr Leslie, and the door swings shut behind them. The rest of the table sits there with its forks in its hands, and Ferris says *oh God, was it something I* — and nobody answers him.

Baptiste has her notebook on her knee under the table. Ansell sees her open it at the back, from habit, and look at the columns she ruled in February. Then she closes it again without writing anything, and puts both hands flat on top of it, and keeps them there.

---

After that Tar is ill, and everybody knows he's ill, and nobody knows what with.

He's at the General on a Thursday. He's at Dr Sealy's surgery on the Monday after, and the Tuesday after that he's at the General again, and comes back on the evening boat with a plaster in the crook of his elbow and his sleeve rolled down over it. He's grey. He's been grey since the stranding last year, if Ansell's honest, and he goes greyer. He doesn't eat at dinner. He sits at the head of the table and moves things about on his plate, and says very little, and nobody says anything to him. The long table's quieter than Ansell's ever known it.

"It's his heart," says Crane, on the verandah, very low. "It must be. The steps."

"It's not his heart," says Baptiste.

"How do you know?"

"Because they'd have kept him in. They don't send you home on the boat with a heart." Baptiste has her knees drawn up and her arms round them. "My uncle had a heart. They kept him in three weeks."

Then it's the first Sunday in October. Tar goes across to the Bay on the Saturday evening to have dinner with the Pobils, the way he's done every Sunday lately. He comes back on the Monday morning boat.

And he's *whistling*.

Ansell hears him before he sees him. He's coming up the jetty path with a bag over his shoulder, whistling something tuneless and cheerful through his teeth. He comes into the long room and puts the bag down on his bench and says, "Morning, Ansell. Morning, Baptiste. Is that the dive series? Bring it here, let's look at it." And he looks at it, for an hour. He tells Ansell in a clear carrying voice, in front of the whole long room, that the deep end is still wrong, and that it's wrong in a very interesting way, and that Ansell's to go away and find out why before lunch.

He eats lunch. The whole of it.

At four o'clock he and Dr Pobil have a row on the verandah about whether the calf's mother's teeth want looking at. It's a proper row, loud, with both of them on their feet. Tar says *you don't know that* and Pobil says *I do know that, I've got eyes* and something in Spanish. Everybody in the long room stops working to listen. Crane, at the next bench, puts her pipette down and says, very quietly, "They used to do that all the time. When I first came. I haven't heard it in a year."

He's still whistling on the Tuesday. On the Wednesday he goes out on the jetty at six in the morning with a thermometer on a string and takes the water, which Ansell has never seen him do. He stands there for a long time looking at the thermometer in the grey light before he writes anything down.

"It was the blood," says Crane, on the verandah that night, with enormous relief. "The General. They gave him blood. It must have been anaemia. You can fix anaemia. My aunt had anaemia, she's fine."

"It wasn't the blood," says Baptiste.

"Of *course* it was the —"

"The blood was the twenty-ninth." Baptiste opens the notebook. It's a different page, not the columns. She's been keeping dates all the same. She can't help it. "Tuesday the twenty-ninth, the General. He was still grey on the Wednesday. He was grey Thursday, he was at the surgery on Thursday and came back looking like death. He was grey Friday. Saturday he went across. It's the Monday." She shuts the notebook. "It's not the blood. Something happened on the Sunday."

"What happens on a Sunday?"

Nobody knows.

---

On the Friday Dr Leslie calls the students into her office, all six of them. She shuts the door and tells them.

She does it standing behind her desk, with her hands flat on it. She says it plainly, the way she says everything, without any softening at all, and it's the worst thing Ansell has ever had said to him in a room. It's in his stomach, she says, and it's gone to his liver, and it can't be operated on. Dr Sealy thinks six months. Perhaps a year. He's asked her to tell them so that nobody has to wonder, and so that nobody asks him. He's going to go on working for as long as he can. He'd like them to go on working too, and to be as stupid in front of him as they usually are, so that he's got something to correct.

The boy from Glasgow starts crying. Dr Leslie comes round the desk and puts her hand on his shoulder, briefly, and goes back.

"That's all," she says. "Go and have your lunch."

They don't have their lunch. They sit on the verandah, the three of them, in a row on the step, and don't say anything for a long time.

"He knew," says Baptiste at last. "On the Sunday. He'd known since the Thursday. The surgery on the Thursday. That's when she told him." She stops. "And on the Monday he came back whistling."

"People do that," says Crane. She doesn't sound sure. "It's a thing people do. They get it over with, and they're relieved to know. My aunt —"

"Your aunt had *anaemia*."

"I know my aunt had anaemia, I'm trying to *help*."

Ansell sits with his elbows on his knees. He thinks about Tar on the jetty on the Wednesday at six in the morning, holding a thermometer and looking at it for a long time. He thinks, for no reason at all, of the columns in the back of Baptiste's notebook, *Stimulus*, *Latency*, *Terminal Topic*.

"That's not how it works," he says. "You don't get better the week you find out you're dying."

Nobody has anything to say to that, because he's right. And across the long room, through the open door, they can hear Tar at his bench telling the boy from Glasgow, who has stopped crying, that his labels are a disgrace and that he wants them done again, all of them, before tea.

---

He moves to the Bay in the middle of October. Dr Sealy makes him, Dr Pobil says. He comes across every morning on the seven o'clock boat and goes back every afternoon on the four o'clock. The lamp in the last hut at the end of the row, which Ansell's never once seen dark since he came, is dark every night.

On the twenty-second, a Thursday, Ansell and Baptiste are sent across to the Bay with the launch to meet the Kingston boat, because there are four crates of formalin on it and a new still, and Alleyne's boy can't manage the still alone.

The Kingston boat is late.

They wait for it on the quay, behind a stack of their own empty crates in the shade of the customs shed. The whole Bay's there, the way it's always there for the Kingston boat. There are women with baskets, and a man with a goat, and the priest.

And at the end of the quay, sitting on the one iron bollard, is Tar.

Mrs Bevan is standing at his shoulder in a hat, like a sentry. Dr Sealy is on his other side. Dr Pobil is walking up and down behind all three of them with his hands in his pockets, and every so often he takes them out and puts them back again.

"What's he doing here?" Ansell says. "He should be in bed."

"He's meeting somebody."

"Who?"

"I don't know," says Baptiste. "But look at Dr Pobil."

Ansell looks. Dr Pobil's face, as everybody on the island knows, shows everything. It's showing so many things now that Ansell can't read any of them.

The boat comes round the point at a quarter to four, low in the water, with a crowd on the rail. And Ansell watches Tar watch it come in. He watches it the whole way, from the point to the quay, without moving. It's the way he watches the north pen when an animal's gone under and hasn't come up yet.

Then, before the ropes are even on, Tar stands up.

"Reggie," says Dr Sealy, quite sharply.

Tar says something to her Ansell can't hear. He doesn't sit down.

The gangway goes down, and the crowd comes off. The women with baskets, somebody's cousin, two sailors. And a tall man in a dark suit, much too heavy for the heat, with a hat and two cases. He's older, grey, very upright, and he comes down the gangway and along the quay without hurrying, the way a man walks who's making himself not hurry. He stops about a yard from the bollard and puts the cases down.

Baptiste has gone completely still beside him.

"Peter," she says. "That's him."

"Who?"

"Look at his *face*. That's *him*. There's a photograph. In the library. The Linnean. Nineteen thirty-something, the back row." She grabs Ansell's arm hard enough to hurt. "That's Spilsbury."

Ansell's stomach turns right over.

It's the funeral, he thinks. It's the men who don't speak for forty years and then go to each other's funerals to make sure. Only he's come early. He's come all the way from Norway, to a quay he's not allowed past, to — to what? To have it out? To say something unforgivable to a dying man on a bollard, in front of the Bay and the priest and the goat? He looks round wildly for Dr Leslie, for anybody who'd know what to do, and there's only Mrs Bevan, at Tar's shoulder, not moving at all.

Tar says something. The tall man answers. They're too far off to hear, and the wind's the wrong way. Tar says something else, and the tall man looks at him. He looks at him from the top to the bottom, very slowly, the way Tar looks at an animal, and says something short.

Then he steps over his own cases and puts both his arms round Tar.

Ansell stares.

It's not a handshake. It's not the thing Englishmen do with one arm and a slap on the back and a lot of coughing. It's the whole of him, round Tar, hard. Tar's face goes into his shoulder. The tall man's hand comes up to the back of Tar's head and stays there. They stand like that on the quay at the Bay at four in the afternoon, and don't move.

Mrs Bevan turns round. She turns her back on them, squarely, the way you'd turn your back on a window with the blind up. She says to the priest, in a voice that must carry to Kingston, that the new wharf is a *disgrace*, and she's written to the Governor, and she'll write again.

"Claudette," says Ansell. "*Claudette*."

"I see it."

"They're —"

"I *see* it."

Tar's shoulders are shaking. For a terrible second Ansell thinks he's crying. Then he hears it, faint across the quay, over the wharf and the Governor and the goat, and it's Tar laughing. It's his real laugh, the short one from the chest that catches at the end. He's laughing into the tall man's coat, and the tall man is holding on to him, and doesn't let go.

Dr Pobil picks up both the cases, and puts them down again, and takes the tall man by both shoulders and says something to him, and picks the cases up again. He's crying openly. He doesn't seem to mind in the least.

They go off up the hill all together, very slowly, at Tar's pace, the tall man with his hand under Tar's elbow.

Ansell and Baptiste stand behind the formalin crates.

"How long," says Ansell eventually. "How long was that? The — how long did they —"

Baptiste looks down at her hands. She's not holding her notebook. It's in her bag, on the crates, and she hasn't taken it out.

"I didn't time it," she says.

---

"*Hugged*," says Crane.

"Yes."

"Hugged *how*?"

"Like —" Ansell tries to think of anything. "Like my mum, when I came back from Leeds the first Christmas. Like that. Only for longer."

"Bitter rivals," says Crane, "don't *hug*."

"I know they don't. I know. I was there."

It's ten o'clock on the verandah, and they're whispering, though there's nobody on the cay who could hear them. Tar's at the Bay. So is the tall man, who is Spilsbury. It was Spilsbury. Baptiste was right about the photograph. Dr Pobil confirmed it the next morning in the long room, when Ansell asked, with a face like a sunrise. *Yes, that's Dr Spilsbury, he's come to stay for a while, he's at the house.* Then, before Ansell could say another word, *Your deep end's still wrong, Ansell, Reggie told me to tell you*, and gone.

"It's a reconciliation," says Crane slowly. "Isn't it? It must be. He's dying, and he wrote. And the other one came. From Norway, straight away. To make it up before —" She stops.

They all think about that.

"Whoever complained," says Baptiste. "Whichever of them it was. It doesn't matter now."

"That's the Sunday," says Ansell.

They look at him.

"The Sunday. At the Pobils'. He'd just found out. He'd have thought, I've got six months, I'm not going to — I'm not going to die with this. And he sat down and wrote." Ansell's throat has gone tight, and he doesn't care. "Twelve years. Over a title page. And he wrote, and Spilsbury got on a boat."

"And he came back whistling," says Baptiste. She says it very softly. "Because he'd written. Because he'd put it down."

"*Oh*," says Crane, and puts her hand over her mouth.

They sit on the step. Ansell finds that his eyes are wet, and that Crane's are too, and that Baptiste is looking hard at the sea. It's the most beautiful thing he's ever heard of. Two old men who'd fallen out over the worst thing you can fall out over, and one of them dying, and making it right.

It's a great deal more wrong than they know. But the bits that are right are right.

---

And he gets *better*.

That's the part none of them are ready for. It's the part Crane, with her books, says is simply not possible, and the part Baptiste writes down anyway because she can't not.

He comes across on the seven o'clock boat every morning and he's pink. He isn't well. He's still thin, and he still takes the verandah steps one at a time, with his hand on the rail. But he's pink, which he wasn't, and he eats lunch, all of it. Dr Pobil stands at his elbow and watches him do it, which is new. Pobil used not to look at what Tar ate.

He's back at the bench by eight and he's *unbearable*. He's sharper than Ansell's ever known him. He tears the boy from Glasgow's labels up in front of the whole long room and makes him do them in front of him, and then tells him they're very good, and means it, and the boy goes pink to the ears. He corrects Crane's statistics, loudly, and Crane argues, loudly, and he laughs and says *all right, you're right, I'm wrong, go and do the rest*. He has a row with Dr Pobil every afternoon at four, on the verandah, in front of everybody, about the calf's mother's teeth, about the dive schedule, about whether a man with his blood count ought to be standing in the north pen in waders. Pobil wins that one. Tar stops going in the pen, and grumbles about it for a week.

And at four o'clock he goes down to the jetty and gets on the boat back to the Bay. He doesn't miss it. He goes down the jetty steps at five to four the way a man goes home from the office.

Spilsbury never comes across. Not once. They know why, now.

But Baptiste goes over to her aunt at the Bay for a Saturday in November and comes back with a report. At six in the morning, on the harbour steps below Dr Sealy's house, she saw the two of them. Spilsbury was on the bottom step letting a thermometer down on a string, and Tar was sitting on the step above with a green notebook on his knee. Spilsbury read the thermometer and said a number. Tar wrote it down. Then Spilsbury said something else, and Tar laughed and hit him on the arm with the notebook.

"They take the water together," says Baptiste. "Every morning. Mrs Joseph's niece says. Six o'clock."

"Mrs Joseph's niece?"

Mrs Joseph is the cook. She's had the long room kitchen since before the war, and she knows everything that happens on the island about a day before it happens.

"She's the girl at Dr Sealy's," says Baptiste. "She does the rooms."

"I thought," says Crane, "it would be — you know. Stiff. Formal. Two old men being very polite to each other at breakfast. Making it up."

"They're not polite to each other at all," says Baptiste. "He hit him with a notebook."

"The *dying* one hit the *other* one?"

"I think he's allowed," says Baptiste.

---

It's Mrs Joseph who does it, in the end, and she does it without meaning to at all.

It's the first week of December, a Wednesday. Ansell's in the kitchen after dinner because he's washing the boats' flasks, and Baptiste is at the table because she's on dishes, and Mrs Joseph is at the stove talking. Mrs Joseph always talks. Mostly it's about her niece at Dr Sealy's, who has been run off her feet, she says, since the gentleman from Norway came. Morning, noon and night. Washing, washing. The doctor's a good woman, a good doctor, but that house wasn't built for four, not with only the one spare room.

"One," says Baptiste.

Ansell looks up from the flasks.

"Only the one," Mrs Joseph agrees, stirring. "With the big bed. It was the old doctor's. Beverley says it takes her an hour to make, it's so big. Four posts and a net and all." She taps the spoon on the side of the pot. "And now there's two gentlemen, and more washing, and Doctor Pobil in and out, and she's not paid for two gentlemen. I've told her to speak to Doctor Sealy."

"Yes," says Baptiste. "Yes, she should."

Ansell has stopped washing the flasks.

He finishes them. He finishes them with enormous care, one at a time, and dries them. Baptiste finishes the dishes. They thank Mrs Joseph for dinner, which they don't usually, and go out onto the verandah, and Crane's sitting on the rail with a book.

Baptiste stands in front of her.

"Dr Sealy's house has one spare room," says Baptiste.

Crane looks up from her book.

"Yes? So?"

"One. With one bed in it."

"So Tar's in the spare room and Spilsbury's —" Crane stops.

There's a long pause.

"Where does Spilsbury sleep?" says Ansell.

Both of them turn and look at him. He's aware, as he says it, that he has asked the question in the voice of a man who has already worked out the answer and would very much like somebody else to say it out loud first.

"Oh," says Crane.

"Yes," says Baptiste.

"*Oh*."

"Yes."

"Oh, my *God*," says Crane, and puts her book face-down on the rail, and then picks it up again and looks at it as though she's never seen a book before, and puts it down.

And then it all goes, in about a minute, the way a wrong curve goes when you finally see the variable. The name. The shoulders coming down. The ten minutes, every time, of being the nicest man on the island to get it out of the room. The letters twice a year, *as required*, that Tar never wrote back to himself. *Not permitted on the premises*, which is — Ansell's stomach turns over again — which is almost certainly a real minute in a real file, and not about a title page at all. Ferris, and the face that did nothing. The Sunday, and the whistling. The quay. The hand on the back of the head. The water, every morning at six, one thermometer, one notebook.

"Oh, *God*," says Crane again. "The *authorship*."

"I know."

"We thought it was about *authorship*. We had a — we had a *treaty*."

"I know."

"We sat here and said *Newton and Leibniz*."

"*You* said Newton and Leibniz," says Baptiste. "I said the Colonial Office."

"We were *right* about the Colonial Office," says Ansell. "That's the worst part. We were right about the Colonial Office and wrong about *why*."

Crane puts her face in her hands and makes a noise into them. Ansell thinks it's laughing, and then he's laughing too. He sits down on the step because his legs have gone, and Baptiste sits down beside him, and they sit on the verandah and laugh like idiots. It isn't at Tar. It isn't at anybody. It's at the three of them, with a ruled page and a library and a theory, being so earnestly and completely and scientifically wrong for ten months.

"It's sweet," says Crane, when she can talk. She takes her hands off her face. It's pink and wet. "Isn't it? It's rather sweet."

"It's more than rather," says Baptiste.

"Twelve *years*," says Crane. "Twelve years, and letters in the *form*, and he was in *Norway*, and they couldn't — and then he wrote, and the other one just got on a *boat* —"

"It's illegal," says Ansell. He doesn't mean it the way it comes out. He means it as a fact, the way you'd say the tide's out.

"It's not in England," says Crane. "Not since 'sixty-seven."

"We're not in England."

"Who's going to tell?" says Baptiste. She says it quite quietly, and looks at each of them in turn. "Us?"

Nobody says anything.

Ansell thinks about Dr Pobil at the sink, saying *that's Jean you want* without turning round. He thinks about Mrs Bevan asking a visitor about his wife's health in a voice that could stop a clock. And Dr Leslie, reading a minute across her desk with a pencil in her hand. And Mrs Bevan again, on a quay in a hat, turning her back squarely and talking about the wharf to the priest in a voice to carry to Kingston. He thinks of twelve years of a whole island doing it, quietly, pleasantly, every day, without ever once saying why. He thinks, with a strange lift under his ribs, that they've been doing it too since May. They just had the reason wrong.

"No," he says. "Nobody's going to tell."

"No," says Crane.

Baptiste nods once, as though that settles a matter of procedure. Then she takes her notebook out of her pocket and opens it at the back, at the columns she ruled in February. She looks at them for a while. She doesn't tear the page out, as Ansell half expects her to. She writes something at the bottom of it in pencil, small, and closes the book, and puts it back in her pocket.

"What did you put?" says Ansell.

"*Hypothesis rejected*," says Baptiste.

---

They are not, the next day, subtle.

Ansell knows they're not subtle. He can feel them not being subtle all afternoon, and can't do anything about it. At twenty to four Baptiste goes over to Tar's bench and reminds him that the boat is at four, which he knows, and which he has known every day since October. At a quarter to four Crane appears with his bag, which he didn't ask for, and holds it. At ten to four Ansell discovers that he needs to go across to the Bay for the post, which isn't his job and never has been, and says so, loudly, to nobody.

Tar looks at the three of them. He puts his pen down and takes his glasses off and rubs his eyes, and puts his glasses back on, and looks at them again.

Then he gets up and takes his bag from Crane, and says *thank you, Crane* in a voice Ansell can't read at all, and goes out to the verandah steps.

They follow him out. They don't mean to. They end up in a row along the verandah rail, the three of them, watching him start down.

He's on the third step when Crane says it.

"Give our regards to Dr Spilsbury, sir."

She says it quite clearly, the way you'd say anybody's name. Nobody's said it to his face in the long room since February without somebody moving to catch it before it landed. Nobody moves now. It lands.

Tar stops on the step, with his hand on the rail. He turns round and looks up at the three of them.

He looks for a long time. Ansell has no idea what his own face is doing. He suspects, from Tar's, that it's doing a great deal.

"I will," says Tar.

The corner of his mouth goes, and this time it finishes. Then he turns round and goes on down the steps, one at a time, to the jetty.

---

Ansell's on the four o'clock boat, going across for the post, and so is Tar.

He sits in the stern with his hat over his eyes the whole way across, and doesn't say anything, and Ansell doesn't either. About halfway over, Ansell sees his shoulders start to shake under the hat, and stop, and start again. At the Bay he gets off first, slowly, with a hand on the rail. And Spilsbury is waiting on the quay, the way he's waited every afternoon since October. He's in his shirtsleeves now, and has given up the hat.

Ansell stays in the boat, handing up the post sacks, and doesn't look. He looks anyway.

Tar goes up to him, and says something, and gestures back at the boat, at Ansell, very slightly, with his chin. Then he says something else, at length, with his hands.

Spilsbury listens. He listens with his head bent and nothing on his face at all, very carefully, as though he were being told a result. Ansell hasn't seen the man smile yet, not on the quay in October and not on the harbour steps. At the end of it he puts one hand slowly over his eyes and holds it there.

And Tar laughs so hard that he has to sit down on the bollard.

He sits there with his hat in his hand, laughing, and Spilsbury stands over him with his hand still over his eyes, shaking his head. After a while he takes it away and says something to Tar that Ansell can't hear. It makes Tar laugh harder. Then Spilsbury holds out his hand, and Tar takes it, and lets himself be pulled up, and they go off together up the hill towards the house above the harbour, at Tar's pace.

Ansell hands up the last of the post sacks to Alleyne's boy.

When he gets back to the cay it's nearly six, and the sun's going down behind the second head. The lamps are coming on along the row. The students' huts. Dr Leslie's in the office. Dr Pobil's old hut, which somebody's using for the still. And at the far end of the row, the last one, dark.

Ansell stands on the jetty and looks at it for a while.

He'd thought it would look terrible, a lamp that had always been lit and wasn't. He'd thought that in October, the first night. It doesn't look terrible. It's just dark. He knows where the man is now, and who with, and that he's laughing, and he finds, standing there on the jetty with the post, that he doesn't mind the dark at all.
