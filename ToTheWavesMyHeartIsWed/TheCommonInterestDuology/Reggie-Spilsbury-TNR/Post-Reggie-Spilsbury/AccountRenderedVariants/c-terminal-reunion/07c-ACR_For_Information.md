# For Information

He notices the pencil the second time.

It's a Thursday in February, at dinner, and there's a man from Bermuda at the long table talking about spiny lobsters. Halfway through the fish he says Alec's name, the ordinary way, as a thing to talk about. Reggie feels it arrive the way he always does, the way you'd feel a change in the weather. His shoulders come down. His face does whatever it does. He hears his own voice go warm. He doesn't decide any of it, any more than he decides to blink. And somewhere in the middle of asking the man from Bermuda about his moulting figures, which are genuinely interesting, he sees Baptiste's hand move under the table.

It's one of the three new ones, or new-ish. Crane's been here since 'sixty-seven, and is Jean's, and is clever, and argues. Ansell and Baptiste came out on the same boat in October. Until about November he'd have had trouble saying which of the two was which, not because they weren't any good but because he hadn't got round to them. Then Ansell said something about the decline curve at the bench, and Reggie spent three quarters of an hour on the boy's oxygen figures. He came out of it rather liking him, which was a surprise, and he's no idea how they got from the one to the other.

Baptiste he got round to in December, over a pirogue.

She's writing something. She's got her notebook on her knee under the edge of the table, the way he used to keep his in meetings at the Labs when Hartley was talking. Her pencil moves when he starts talking about moulting. It moves again, about two minutes later, when the man from Bermuda has gone off happily on to the subject of his own boat.

Reggie watches her do it over the rim of his glass.

She's timing something.

He doesn't know what. It could be anything. She could be timing the lobster man, who is a long talker. She could be timing how long it takes Mrs Joseph's girl to clear, which is interesting in its own right. He files it, the way you'd file a noise from a pump that might be nothing, and forgets about it until the ninth of March.

---

On the ninth of March there are two men from Woods Hole at dinner, and Ansell says it.

"Of course, Dr Tar," says Ansell, much too loudly, "you'll have known Spilsbury very well."

He says it like a boy in a school play who's been told his line on the way in. Across the table Crane shuts her eyes. Beside him, Baptiste doesn't move at all, except that her right hand has gone under the table.

And it comes anyway. That's the thing he'd tell anybody who asked, if anybody ever did. He can see exactly what's happening. He could draw the diagram. And his shoulders come down, and his face goes open, and he smiles at the boy with enormous warmth. He hears himself say *everybody knew Spilsbury, he made sure of it*, and *speaking of which, Ansell, that log scale*, and all the while, somewhere behind his own face, he's watching Baptiste's pencil. It moves at *log scale*. It moves again when he turns to the man from Woods Hole and asks about the boat.

About a minute and a half, he thinks. From stimulus to terminal topic.

He's being measured.

He sits at the head of his own table, being charming to a man from Massachusetts about a research vessel, and realises that three of his postgraduates have designed an experiment and are running it on him at dinner. He very nearly laughs out loud. He doesn't. It would contaminate the trial.

It isn't a bad design, either. That's what he keeps coming back to, afterwards, in the long room at ten with Ansell's variance spread out in front of him. The stimulus is badly trained. Ansell couldn't deliver a line naturally to save his life, and Crane shouldn't have shut her eyes, because a subject could read that from across the room. But the observer's good. Baptiste sat through the whole thing without once looking at him directly. She kept her eyes on the Woods Hole man the entire time, and took her readings off the conversation rather than off his face. That's the right way to do it. He'd have done it that way himself.

He'd like very much to see her columns.

He can't ask. He sits with Ansell's variance and understands that he can't ask, because asking would mean saying what they're measuring. It would mean that he knew what it was, and knew why it happened, and nobody on the cay but Pascual and Hester has ever been let that far in. So he looks at the deep-end figures instead. They're a mess. Somewhere in them is a very interesting mistake, and he spends an hour showing the boy why, because it's true, and because the boy's face when he sees it is worth the hour.

At the end of it Ansell says *thank you, sir, that was —* and stops, because he's a well-brought-up boy from Leeds and doesn't know how to finish.

"Go to bed, Ansell," says Reggie. "You were very loud at dinner."

He watches the boy go red to the collar and flee.

---

By April he's worked out roughly what they're getting. He can't see the notebook, but he can see the pencil, and he can count. Latency, a couple of seconds. He knows that from the inside; it's how long the weather takes to change. Duration, anything from a minute and a half to four or five, depending on how hard the stimulus pushes. The terminal topic is always somebody's own work, or their boat, or their grandmother.

He'd tell them the mechanism if they asked. That's the funny part. It isn't difficult. You find what the other person most wants to talk about, and give them that, and they're so pleased to be given it that they don't notice what's gone. He learnt it at school, where everybody learnt it. He was good at it at the Labs, and it was the worst thing he ever did with it. He has not, since 1957, been able to stop it on this one subject, and he's stopped trying.

He doesn't tell them. He goes on being measured. He finds that he rather looks forward to it. There aren't many things in 1970 that he looks forward to, and he's not sure what it says about him that one of them is being run as a trial by three people in their twenties at dinner. But it's the truth.

And then in May they stop.

It happens between one week and the next. There's a man from the Plymouth laboratory with a beard at dinner on the Tuesday, and he says the name, and Reggie waits for Baptiste's pencil. It doesn't come. Baptiste is looking at her plate. Crane is looking at her plate. Ansell is looking at Reggie, with an expression Reggie can't at first identify, and then can.

It's pity. It's a very young, very decent, very embarrassed sort of pity, the kind you'd give a man at a funeral whose name you'd forgotten.

He goes down to the long room the next morning and finds the 197 paper on Crane's bench, open at page six.

He stands and looks at it for some time.

*The specimen was taken by Dr A. Spilsbury.* Page six, a whole paragraph, and nothing on the front but his own name and Jean's initials. He doesn't need anybody to tell him what three postgraduates have made of that. He can see it on the bench as plainly as a result. A dispute about authorship. The one thing every student in the world is warned about in their first term, the thing people don't speak for forty years over.

They think somebody took Alec's name off his own work.

The name's off the front because the man himself wanted it off, for three good reasons, and argued Reggie down in the long room one night in 1957 with his hands flat on the bench. Reggie lost, and has minded ever since. And now three children have looked at it and concluded that one of them stole from the other. They've concluded that the man at the head of the table might be the sort of man who'd do that.

And then they stopped measuring him, out of decency, and were kind.

He doesn't know what to do with that. He stands at Crane's bench with his hand flat on the page and finds that he doesn't know, and that his eyes have gone hot, which is absurd.

He shuts the paper and puts it back where it was, square on the bench, the way she'd left it. He goes and does the calf's weights.

---

"Ansell asked me whether Spilsbury ever comes over," says Jean in July.

She's in the office doorway with a draft in her hand, Ansell's. It has a great many pencil marks on it, most of them in the third paragraph.

Reggie looks up from the weight sheets.

"What did you tell him?"

"The truth," says Jean. "He's not permitted on the Institute's premises."

"*Jean*."

"It's true." She doesn't come in. She leans on the door frame and looks at him with the face she's worn for him since 1954, which is fond and entirely without mercy. "You'll want to know what they've made of it. I heard them through the window."

"I'm sure I don't."

"They think it went to the Colonial Office."

Reggie puts his pen down.

"It did," he says.

"Over a title page," says Jean. "Crane says look at Newton and Leibniz. Crane says Owen sat on the committee."

And he laughs. It surprises him. It's the first time he's laughed properly since April. It comes up out of his chest short and hard and catches at the end, and he sits at the bench with his hand over his eyes. Jean waits in the doorway until he's finished, without any expression at all.

"Which of us," he says, when he can, "do they think complained?"

"They haven't decided. They're too polite."

"Of course they are. They're very well brought up." He wipes his eyes under his glasses. "Thank you, Jean."

"I didn't do it for you," says Jean. "I did it because he asked a question and I'm not having a student think I don't know the answer." She looks at him a moment longer. "You look terrible, Reggie. Eat something."

She goes back into the office and shuts the door.

---

Pascual is less amused.

"They think you're a thief," he says that evening on the step. He's heard it from Mrs Joseph, who's heard it from God knows where. He's been red about it since the four o'clock boat. "*Hombre*, they think you pinched his name off a paper. Or he pinched yours. They've got it going to Kingston."

"It did go to Kingston."

"That's not what I mean." Pascual's hands go up. "I'm going to tell them."

"No, you're not."

"I'm not having them think —"

"Tell them what?" says Reggie. "That it wasn't a title page? Then they'll want to know what it was. They're scientists, Pascual. You take away one explanation and they'll go looking for the next one. And the next one's the right one." He leans his head back against the post. "Leave them. They've got a story. It's a worse story than the real one, and it's theirs, and they're being kind about it. Let them have it."

Pascual looks at him for a long time.

"You like them," he says. "Don't you."

"They're all right."

"No, you *like* them. Don't tell me you don't, I've known you eighteen years." Pascual's face, as always, is doing everything. "*Madre de Dios*. You've got a soft spot. For *students*."

"I've got three good postgraduates," says Reggie, "who can't deliver a line and run a perfectly decent trial. Leave them alone."

"Fine," says Pascual. "*Fine*. I'll leave them alone." He gets up to go down the steps to the boat, and stops on the top one. "They're doing it too, you know. What Hester does. What I do. I've seen them at it."

"I know."

"You've not said a word to me about it. Twelve years, and I've done it every week."

"No," says Reggie. "I didn't."

Pascual looks at him, and his face does something complicated, and he goes down the steps without saying anything else.

---

The last week in August there's a man called Hartley at dinner. He's the nephew of old Hartley at Plymouth, the one with the skate, and he's pink and cheerful and good at his job. He's here to look at the calf's growth figures. Reggie is at the head of the table with the letter in his inside pocket on the left, where it's been since April. It has gone soft at the folds.

On the second night Hartley puts his fork down.

"I say, Tar. Spilsbury. Is it true he sat on a wall for fifty-one days? My uncle swears it was —"

Reggie feels the weather start to change.

And three voices go off down the table at once, like a fire drill. Crane, too fast, *oh, you'll want Dr Leslie, she handles the deposit*. Baptiste at exactly the same moment, *how's your boat, somebody said you'd had her refitted*. And Ansell, louder than either of them because he's panicked, *didn't your uncle keep a skate in his bath? Was that true?*

Hartley looks from one of them to another, blinking, with three questions arriving from three directions. Then he laughs, because he's a nice man, and says he's no idea about the skate, what a marvellous thing to wonder. But the boat, he can tell them about the boat.

He tells them about the boat.

Reggie sits at the head of the table with his hands either side of his plate.

The weather hasn't changed. It had started to. He'd felt it start, the way he's felt it start every time for thirteen years. And then it didn't have to, because by the time it would have got there the name was gone. Three people in their twenties had grabbed it out of the air and thrown it somewhere else before it landed, clumsily, all at once, with their ears going red.

Pascual does it well. Hester does it beautifully. He's watched them do it since 1958, and never thanked either of them, because thanking them would mean saying what for.

These three did it badly. That's what gets him.

They'd no idea what they were doing. They'd no idea what the name *was*. They had a title page and a theory and a treaty, and they'd decided in May, without asking him, that whatever it was, it was his and it hurt. And that nobody was going to poke it with a stick at dinner, and that if anybody did, they'd see to it. And they had. Very badly. With a skate.

He looks down the table at them.

Crane has gone pink to the hairline. Baptiste is looking fixedly at Hartley with her hands in her lap, taking no readings of anything. Ansell is looking at his pudding. Reggie can see from the back of his neck that he's longing to look up and won't let himself.

Something happens at the corner of his mouth. He can feel it start, and he can't finish it, not at the head of the table, not in front of Hartley. He looks down at his plate for a second, as though he'd lost something on it. He supposes he has.

Then he looks up, and asks Hartley a perfectly sensible question about the boat's keel, and lets the evening have him.

---

He doesn't sleep much. He sits on the step of the hut at the end of the row with the lamp lit behind him, and watches the students' lamps go out along the row one by one, and thinks about what he could say.

*Thank you* is out. You can't thank somebody for a thing without saying what the thing was. He'd have to say *thank you for keeping his name off me*, and then it'd be said, and they'd know there was a thing to keep off. They'd know it wasn't a title page. They're too clever not to.

He can't say anything. He's a man who has made his living for thirty years out of saying things plainly, and there's nothing he can say to three postgraduates at a bench.

At about two he thinks of the skate.

---

Ansell's at the next bench by half past seven, which he never is, which means he hasn't slept either. Reggie lets him get twenty minutes into the deep end before he says it. He doesn't look up. It's easier not to.

"It was a thornback," he says.

He can feel the boy look up.

"Sir?"

"Hartley's skate. The old man's. It was a thornback, about two foot across. He kept it three weeks, and his wife made him take it back to Plymouth on the train in a bucket." He writes a figure in the margin of the weight sheet. His hand's quite steady. "In case it comes up again. It's the sort of thing that does."

The boy doesn't say anything at all. Reggie finishes the column. He pushes the deep-end figures back across the bench without looking at them. He's had them since seven, and read them twice, and knows exactly where the mistake is, and isn't going to tell him.

"Still wrong," he says. "Go and find out why."

"Yes, sir," says Ansell. His voice isn't steady at all.

Reggie hears him go out of the long room at something very like a run. He knows, as surely as if he'd been told, that the boy's going to the library, where the other two will be, where they're not supposed to be at eight in the morning. He sits at the bench with the weight sheets. After a while he takes his glasses off and puts his hand over his eyes, and keeps it there until Pascual comes in from the pens and asks him what the matter is.

"Nothing," says Reggie. "Hay fever."

"There isn't any hay on this island."

"Then it's something else," says Reggie, and puts his glasses back on.

---

At the Ferris dinner in September he sees them try.

He sees Crane put her fork down ten seconds before it comes. He sees Baptiste's mouth open, and Ansell half out of his chair, too far down the table and too late. Ferris is at his elbow and fast, and it's London, and the seal paper, and *are you going over for the second week*. And the weather doesn't change at all, for the first time in thirteen years. There's nothing there to change it with.

He'd like, going down the steps, to tell them that it wasn't their fault, that nobody could have caught that one. He doesn't get to the bottom of the steps to tell anybody anything.

---

In October he asks Jean to tell them.

He asks her in the office, the day after Marguerite gives him the number. He doesn't want to do it himself. He knows exactly how it would go. He'd stand in the long room and say it, and they'd look at him, and he'd see Ansell's face, and he'd go on in the voice he uses for bad results, and they'd hear the voice and not the result. He wants them to hear the result.

"Tell them so nobody has to wonder," he says. "And so nobody asks me. And tell them —" He stops. "Tell them to go on being stupid in front of me. I'll want something to correct."

Jean looks at him across the desk for a long time.

"I'll tell them you said that," she says. "In those words."

"Good."

She does it on the Friday. He stays at his bench in the long room with the door open and the Glasgow boy's labels in front of him, because he's not going to hide in the hut. He hears the office door shut, and some time later open. He hears feet on the verandah, three sets, and then nothing. They're sitting on the step. He can see a shoulder of Crane's through the door, and the back of Ansell's head.

For a long time none of them says anything. Then Baptiste does. He hears her through the door, low, working it out from her dates the way he knew she would. *He knew. On the Sunday.* And Crane tries to help with her aunt, and Baptiste snaps at her. And then Ansell, very quietly, in the voice of a boy who's just seen the answer and wishes he hadn't:

*That's not how it works. You don't get better the week you find out you're dying.*

Reggie sits at his bench with a label in his hand.

The boy's right. It's the best observation anybody's made about him all year, and it's quite correct, and Reggie can't tell him why. He can't tell him about Marguerite's verandah, or the sum, or the letter that went on the early boat on Monday. Or that he has been, since about midnight on Sunday, for the first time since 1957, a man waiting for the post.

He sits with the label. Then he does the only thing he can think of. He turns round to the Glasgow boy at the next bench, who has been crying and has stopped, and tells him in a clear, carrying voice that his labels are a disgrace. He's to do them again, every one, before tea. He says it so they'll hear it on the step. He says it so they'll have something to be annoyed about.

He hears Crane make a noise that's half a laugh and half something else.

Good, he thinks.

---

He sees them on the quay on the twenty-second, but not till afterwards.

He isn't looking at anything on the quay but one thing. Then Alec lets go of him, or starts to, and Pascual's got the cases, and Hester's turned round. Going up the hill at his own pace with Alec's hand under his elbow, Reggie says, "Were there people watching? I didn't look."

"The whole of the Bay was watching," says Alec. "And a goat."

"Anybody I'd know?"

Alec considers.

"Two young people," he says. "Behind a stack of crates by the customs shed. Formalin, from the labels. A young man with his mouth open, and a young woman who had a notebook in her bag and did not take it out."

Reggie stops walking.

"Tall boy? Fair? Looks as though he's about to apologise for something?"

"Yes."

"Ansell." Reggie starts walking again. "And Baptiste. Oh, *God*. She'll be furious. She'll never forgive herself."

"For what?"

"For not timing it," says Reggie.

Alec looks down at him. Reggie can see him decide not to ask, and file it for later. He can see him file it with the same small movement of the mouth he used to make at the Labs, over something in a report he'd want to take apart at leisure.

---

He gets better.

He knows it, and Marguerite knows it, and everybody on the cay knows it, and none of them can make it add up. Marguerite says the blood's doing its job, and it is, and it isn't only the blood. He eats. He crosses on the seven o'clock boat every morning and goes back on the four, and in between he's at the bench. He's more at the bench than he's been in two years. He rows with Pascual on the verandah every afternoon at four, about the calf's mother's teeth, about the dive schedule, about the waders. He loses the waders.

He is, Pascual tells him, unbearable.

"The students think you've made it up with him," Pascual says. He's leaning on the long room door, beaming. He's been beaming for three weeks. It's quite difficult to have a row with somebody who won't stop beaming. "Over the title page. A reconciliation. Mrs Joseph says Crane cried."

"Crane did not cry."

"Mrs Joseph says she did, and Mrs Joseph's not wrong about that sort of thing. On the verandah. All three of 'em." Pascual's face goes, briefly, quite serious. "They think you wrote to him because you were dying and you didn't want to go with it. That you put it down. That's why you got better."

Reggie looks at the weight sheets.

"Do they," he says.

"Yes." Pascual pushes himself off the door. "They're not wrong, *hombre*. That's the thing. They've got the reason all wrong and they're not wrong."

He tells Alec that evening at Marguerite's table. He tells all of it, from the pencil under the table in February. He tells it the way he'd tell a result, in order, with the figures where he's got them. Marguerite has to put her fork down twice. By Newton and Leibniz, Alec has stopped eating altogether.

"A dispute about authorship," Alec says.

"Yes."

"Over the 197 paper."

"Yes."

"My own name. Which I took off myself, in the long room, over your objections. You argued for an hour."

"An hour and a quarter. They don't know that."

"And they have not decided," says Alec slowly, "which of us complained to the Colonial Office."

"They're too polite."

Alec sits for a moment with his hands either side of his plate.

"I should like it entered in the record," he says, "that it was not me."

Marguerite laughs so hard she has to leave the table.

---

On the first Thursday in December, Baptiste's notebook is on her bench, open at the back.

He doesn't go looking for it. He's passing, at a quarter past nine, with the calf's sheets, and it's open, face up, and she's out at the pens. He reads whatever's put in front of him. He reads it upside down without breaking step, and then breaks step, and stands there.

It's ruled in columns with the edge of something, a plate, he thinks, from the slight curve at the top. *Date. Stimulus. Speaker. Latency. Duration. Terminal Topic.*

*9.iii.70. Stimulus: P.A., direct. Latency: approx. 2 sec. Duration: 1 min 40 sec to terminal topic. Terminal topic: P.A.'s variance (then Woods Hole boat). Subject affect: warm. P.A. affect: enchanted.*

There are seven entries. The figures are very consistent. She's got his latency within a second of where he'd have put it himself, from the inside, and she took it from the far end of the table, off the conversation, without once looking at his face. He'd have given a great deal, in March, to see this page. He's seeing it now, in December, and his hands aren't quite steady on the weight sheets.

Under the seventh entry there's a gap, a long one, the rest of the page from May. At the very bottom, in fresh pencil, small, a single line. It's dated yesterday.

*Hypothesis rejected.*

He stands at the bench and reads it several times.

Then he goes out to the north pen, rather fast, and sits on the rail in the sun and watches the calf's mother come up for air every forty seconds. He counts it, because it's a thing he can count, until he can go back in.

---

They are not, that afternoon, subtle.

At twenty to four Baptiste comes over to his bench and reminds him the boat's at four. At a quarter to, Crane appears holding his bag, which he didn't ask for. At ten to, Ansell announces to the long room at large that he's going across to the Bay for the post, which isn't his job and has never been, and goes red.

Reggie looks at the three of them.

He's known since a quarter past nine. They don't know he knows. They're standing round his bench with their faces doing everything at once, like three people carrying a very full tray across a room, and every one of them thinks they're being perfectly natural.

He takes his glasses off and rubs his eyes, and puts them back on, and looks at them again. He'd like to laugh. He'd like, rather more, to do the other thing. He does neither. He gets up and takes his bag from Crane.

"Thank you, Crane," he says.

He goes out to the verandah steps. They follow him, of course. He can hear them line up along the rail behind him as he starts down.

He's on the third step when Crane says it.

"Give our regards to Dr Spilsbury, sir."

She says it quite clearly, the way you'd say anybody's name.

He stops, with his hand on the rail, and waits for the weather.

It doesn't come. It doesn't need to. The name comes down the steps to him in a young woman's voice, ordinary, clear, in front of the long room. It stops beside him and stays there, and nobody moves to catch it and nobody needs to. There's nothing wrong with it. It's just Alec's name, and somebody saying it to him, and wanting him to carry something back.

He turns round and looks up at the three of them along the rail.

"I will," he says.

His mouth goes, and this time he lets it finish. Then he turns round and goes on down to the jetty, one step at a time, before any of them can see the rest.

---

He manages the first half of the crossing. Ansell's in the bow with the post sacks, looking at the sea very hard. Reggie sits in the stern with his hat over his eyes and gets as far as the second head before he goes. He sits there shaking under his hat, in silence, and gets it back, and then thinks *P.A. affect: enchanted*, and goes again.

Alec is on the quay, the way he's been every afternoon since October.

Reggie gets off first, with a hand on the rail. He goes up to him, and doesn't wait to be asked. He tells him the whole of it, there on the quay: the columns and the curve at the top from the plate, *hypothesis rejected*, the tray, the bag, *give our regards*. He gestures back at the boat with his chin, at Ansell, who's handing up post sacks and trying not to look. He tells it with his hands.

Alec listens with his head bent, very carefully, as though he were being told a result. At the end of it he puts one hand slowly over his eyes and keeps it there.

Reggie laughs so hard he has to sit down on the bollard.

He sits with his hat in his hand. Alec stands over him with his hand still over his eyes, shaking his head slowly from side to side. Presently he takes the hand away. He looks down at Reggie with an expression Reggie has seen on him in front of a particularly beautiful section.

"I should like," Alec says, "to see her figures."

And Reggie has to hold on to the bollard with both hands.

---

He does the presents on Christmas Eve, in the morning, before anybody's up.

Alec wrapped them. He did it at Marguerite's table the night before, in brown paper from the stationer's by the church, with the corners folded square. Reggie watched him do it and said that no postgraduate in the world was going to notice the corners, and Alec said *Baptiste will*, and Reggie had to concede it.

He puts them on the three benches in the long room at seven, with the sun just coming over the second head, and goes out to the pens so as not to be there.

On Baptiste's bench, the stopwatch. It's his, the one he's timed animals with since 1954, and timed students with. It's the one the whole cay knows by sight. There's a card under the string, in his own hand.

*For the latencies. R. T.*

On Ansell's, a reprint of the decline curve, from the second of Alec's two cases, which turned out in October to have very few shirts in it and a great many reprints. On the title page, in a hand that doesn't vary, it's signed.

*For P. Ansell, with the author's compliments. A. Spilsbury.*

The boy asked him about that curve at the bench in November 1969, and Reggie talked about his oxygen figures for three quarters of an hour instead. It's taken him thirteen months to answer.

On Crane's, a card. Just a card, small and white, in the same hand as the reprint, and very formal. Alec drafted it three times at the table, and Reggie told him he was being absurd, and he was, and it was exactly right.

*Dr Spilsbury thanks Miss Crane for her regards, and returns them.*

Reggie stands at the north pen rail with his back to the long room and waits. He watches the calf's mother come up, and go down, and come up.

He hears the long room door at a quarter to eight. He hears a pause. Then a sound from inside that could be anybody, and a second sound that's certainly Crane, and a third that's Ansell saying something that ends in *sir*, rather loudly, to an empty room.

He doesn't turn round.

Baptiste comes out of the long room a minute or two after, onto the verandah, and stands at the top of the steps. He can feel her there. He watches the calf's mother go down.

He hears the stopwatch click.

He counts to forty in his head, and the calf's mother comes up, blowing, into the sun. At the top of the steps behind him, he hears it click again.

He doesn't look round. He puts both hands on the rail, and looks at the water, and lets his face do whatever it likes, since there's nobody on this side of him to see it.
