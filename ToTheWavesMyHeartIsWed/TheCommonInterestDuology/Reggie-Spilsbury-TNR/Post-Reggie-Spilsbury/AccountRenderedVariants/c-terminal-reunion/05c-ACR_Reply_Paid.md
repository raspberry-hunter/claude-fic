# Reply Paid

The tracing comes back from the General on the Friday, and it's wrong.

It isn't wrong the way she'd braced for. She reads it in her surgery with the door shut, and then again with the report from the General laid beside it, and it shows a heart that has been working too hard for some time and hasn't come to any harm from it. There's no dead muscle and no scar. The rhythm she hadn't liked on the step is there on the strip, fast and a little ragged, the way a heart runs when it's being asked for more than it's got. The heart itself is sound.

She sits with that for a while.

A sound heart doesn't put a man down on the fourth step from the bottom the colour of a fish. Something is asking it for more than it's got.

She has him back on the Monday. This time she doesn't start with his chest. She sits him on the couch under the window with the blind right up, in the full light, and pulls his lower eyelid down with her thumb, and looks.

It's white. It should be the wet pink she'd see on Pascual, and it's white, like the inside of a shell.

"Look up," she says. "Now down."

"What is it?"

"Your blood. Or rather, where it's got to."

She takes some and sends it across on the noon boat, and while they wait she asks him about the beans. Only now she knows which beans she wants. She asks how much of a plate he gets through before he's full, and he thinks about it properly, the way he thinks about anything, and says about half, lately. Since when? Since about Easter. Does it hurt? No. Then, because she waits, there's an ache under the ribs after he eats that he's been calling indigestion since Christmas, and a heaviness, like having swallowed a stone. She asks the one other question she asks everybody, plainly, and watches him understand why she's asking it, and he answers it plainly too.

Then she has him lie back, and puts her hand flat on his belly under the right ribs, and asks him to breathe in.

She has felt this perhaps two dozen times in thirty years. The edge of the liver comes down to meet her fingers as he breathes, and it's too low, and it isn't smooth. It's hard and knotted, like a rope with knots in it under a blanket.

She keeps her hand there one breath longer than she needs to. Then she takes it away and pulls his shirt down, and doesn't let anything happen to her face, because he's watching it.

"Well," says Reggie.

"I want you at the General on Tuesday. They'll give you a drink of something white and take pictures of it going down." She writes on the card. "And some blood. You've about half what you ought to have. That's your steps."

"Not my heart?"

"Your heart's been doing the work of two. It's fine. It's tired."

"And the other thing? The thing you felt?"

"Tuesday," says Marguerite. "Then I'll tell you."

He looks at her from the couch for a long moment, and she knows he's read the whole of it off her anyway, and is letting her have Tuesday because she's asked for it.

---

She tells him on the Thursday, the first of October, in the surgery, with the pictures from the General on the lightbox and the blind down.

She's done this before. She has a way of doing it, which is to say the thing first and plainly and then stop talking, because whatever she says after the thing is only noise the patient has to get past. She doesn't use the way with him. She finds she can't.

"It's your stomach," she says. "There's a growth. Here." She puts her finger on the film, on the grey ragged shape where the white should run smooth. "It's been there a while, and it's gone to the liver. That's what I felt on Monday."

"Can they take it out?"

"No. Mr Aris has looked at all of it, and he won't operate, and he's right."

He looks at the film. He takes it the way she's seen him take things before, like a man catching something thrown at him hard without letting his hands move.

"How long?"

Three weeks ago she wouldn't give him a number.

"Six months," she says. "Perhaps a year. I'd be surprised by much more."

"You wouldn't tell me before."

"Before it was a target. You'd have used it to see how close you could cut." She sits down on her stool, facing him. "This isn't a target. It's a timetable. You're entitled to know when the boat goes."

"Nearer six or nearer twelve?"

"Some of that's up to you. I'm not going to pretend it's much." She makes herself go on. "The blood will help. It'll give you your breath back for a good while. You'll have good weeks, and some good months, and then fewer."

"And then?"

"You'll get tired, and thinner, and you'll sleep a great deal towards the end. I'll keep you comfortable. I'm good at it."

"Can I work?"

"Until you can't."

"Good," says Reggie.

She waits. He sits on the edge of the couch with his hands on his knees and looks at the shape on the film for a long time.

"Does Pascual know?"

"No. That's yours to tell him."

"Tonight," he says. "On the step."

"Tonight, or I'll know."

"You would, too." Something happens at the corner of his mouth. "Anybody would think you'd read my file."

"Is there anyone else?" says Marguerite. "Anyone you need to tell yourself. Family. Anyone at all."

"No."

His hand goes to the inside pocket of his jacket, on the left, as he says it, and rests there flat against the cloth, and comes away.

She watches it go. She says nothing. She's asked about the beans for three weeks, and there aren't any beans left now, and what's left is that.

---

Hester goes home at half past nine on the Sunday, with the Colonel's lantern and Pascual's arm. She has spent the whole of dinner at Reggie's elbow watching him eat, and she has said nothing about why she's watching, and at the top of the verandah steps she stops and looks back at him over her glasses for a long moment, and says, "Dr Tar," and nothing else, and goes.

So she knows. Nobody's told her. Reggie supposes she read it off the fish.

Pascual comes back up the hill alone at ten, and doesn't sit on the rail. He pulls a cane chair round and sits in it facing Reggie, knee to knee, which he doesn't do, and Marguerite comes out from the kitchen and doesn't go in to see about the spare room. She sits on Reggie's other side with her feet tucked up under her.

Reggie looks from one of them to the other.

"Oh, *God*," he says. "It's a case conference."

"It's the letter," says Pascual. "That's what this is."

"Pascual."

"No. I told you in 'fifty-eight. If it ever came on with me, I'd say it anyway, and you'd have to put up with me. It wasn't a bargain. It was a warning." He's gone red to the ears already, and hasn't said anything yet. "I've kept out for twelve years, *hombre*, and I'm going in."

"Consider me warned."

"You've had Marguerite's timetable since Thursday. I've had it since Thursday night." Pascual's voice goes, and he gets it back. "So I'll tell you what happens. You die, and Jean writes to him."

"Pascual —"

"She'll have to, it's in the deed. She'll sit at Hester's old machine and type *Dear Sir*, and *died at the Institute on*, and a date, and *Yours faithfully*. That's how he finds out. From a typewriter."

Reggie sits very still.

"That's what you want?" says Pascual. "Tell me that's what you want, and I'll stop."

"He said no answer's an answer."

"He said that in April," says Marguerite. She says it quite gently, from his other side. "To a man he thought was going to outlive him by twenty years. He was asking a question you're not being asked any more."

"It's not the —" Reggie stops. "It isn't the file. It was never the file. I said a thing in the office in 'fifty-seven, and it's still true, and I can't be the man who said it and go and sit in a hotel with him."

"I know. You told me."

"I didn't tell you all of it."

"I know you didn't," says Pascual. "There was a name. The inspector said it. I was by the window, I've got ears. Nicholson." He watches that go into Reggie. "I don't know what it's got to do with you. But whatever it is you think you owe that man —"

"Pascual."

"— you're *paying* it." It comes out hot, and he doesn't pull it back. "Look at you. Marguerite's put the date on it. Whatever you think you'd be having on his account, *hombre*, you're not going to be having it by next summer. You've paid. Look at you, you've paid the whole thing."

Reggie opens his mouth, and doesn't say anything.

He sits in Marguerite's cane chair on the back verandah under the vine, with the harbour dark below and the cay across the channel, and does the sum. He does it Alec's way, because Alec's way is the only way he's ever seen it done properly, and he finds the man's voice doing it in his head, level, with the full stops.

In 1957 there was a man on one side of the page, alive and on the licence and in post, with his animals and his friend behind the wall. On the other side there was Frank, who had paid, and Reggie, who would have been having what Frank paid for with his arms round it every night for as long as he went on living. That was the whole trouble. It was the *going on*. It was twenty years, thirty, a lifetime of being the beneficiary.

He isn't going to have a lifetime. He's going to have until about next summer.

Whatever he takes now, he takes on account, and the account closes when he does. There's nobody left at the end of it having anything. Nobody holding the proceeds. It doesn't make it square. Frank's still somewhere, and still paid, and nothing Reggie does will give him back the bench or the room. But nobody will have been sitting in a hotel bar for thirty years on what Frank lost. Nobody's going to be sitting anywhere for very long.

That's one side of it. The other side's the lift.

He'd sat at the table in the hut in February 'fifty-seven and seen, quite clearly, the man he'd be if he stayed. It was the man in the lift in 1949, with the voice turned up, getting what he wanted before the ground floor and letting a decent man walk off and carry what it cost. He'd been that man once, at thirty, in four days. He wasn't going to be him every night for thirty years. That was what he'd refused, going out of the spare hut with his face wet. Not Alec. The man he'd have had to go on being to keep him.

And you can't become a man in ten months. There isn't time to turn into anything. You can only be the one you already are, in front of the people who know you, until the boat goes.

And the ones who'll pay, this time, are sitting either side of him with their knees against his. Pascual, red to the ears. Marguerite, with two fingers on his wrist. They've come and sat down and asked. Nobody's having anything done to them in a front room in Southport without being told. And the one who'll pay most is in Norway, and doesn't know yet, and the letter is how he'll be asked. It's the thing Reggie said himself, at the table, with his hands over his face, and Alec said no to: *not without asking him*.

It's Alec's arithmetic, with the one line in it that Alec left out. It's the first of Alec's sums he's ever liked the look of.

He finds that he's laughing. It's the short one, down in the chest, and it catches at the end, and Marguerite puts her hand on his wrist without asking and counts through it.

"What?" says Pascual. "*What*? What's funny?"

"Nothing's funny." He wipes his eyes under his glasses. "You're right. That's all. You're quite right. I've paid."

Pascual stares at him.

"I'm right?"

"Yes."

"You're not going to argue?"

"I'm too tired to argue with you," says Reggie, "and you're right, and I'd lose. Marguerite, what's my pulse?"

"Too fast," says Marguerite. "Write the letter."

"Now?"

"Now. Tonight. At my table. I'll sit with you." She doesn't let go of his wrist. "If you don't, I'll write it myself. My handwriting's appalling and I'll put in everything, including your blood count."

"You don't know where he lives."

"Kongens gate eighteen," says Pascual. "Ålesund."

Reggie turns his head.

"Hester," says Pascual. He's gone red again. "She files things where I can see them. She's done it since 'sixty. I thought it was on purpose. You never know, with Hester."

---

He writes it at Marguerite's dining table, under the lamp, at about midnight, on a sheet of her writing paper with her address at the top in blue.

Marguerite sits at the other end of the table and reads a medical journal, and doesn't turn a page. Pascual stands on the verandah just outside the open door with his back to the room, where he can hear if he's wanted and doesn't have to watch.

Reggie finds the first line has already been written, in his head, some time on Friday, without asking him.

It takes him about twenty minutes, which is less than it took him to type *Yours faithfully* in 1960. He doesn't draft it. He writes it straight down in his writing hand, not the capitals, and when he gets to the close his hand does what it's wanted to do since the second of September 1957, and he lets it.

> *The Bay*
> *4th October 1970*
>
> *Dear Alec,*
>
> *You said no answer was an answer, and you'd read it as one. I let it be one. I'm sorry. It wasn't the answer you'll have read.*
>
> *I've got your letter. It's been in my pocket since April.*
>
> *Marguerite Sealy, who's Pascual's wife and my doctor and a great deal cleverer than either of us, has found a growth in my stomach. It's gone to the liver and it can't be taken out. She says six months, perhaps a year. I asked her for the number and she gave it me, and she doesn't give numbers she hasn't checked. I'm telling you exactly, because you'd want it exactly, and because you ought to know what it'll cost before you decide anything. I'm asking. I didn't, last time, and nor did you.*
>
> *I couldn't answer you in April because of what I said in the office, and whose account it was said on. I've done the sum again tonight, your way, on Marguerite's verandah. The account closes when I do. That doesn't make it square. It means there's nobody left at the end of it having what was paid for, and I find I can live with that, for as long as the living goes on.*
>
> *I should like to see you. That is the whole of it. I have no other reason, and I shan't pretend to one.*
>
> *The water off the jetty was twenty-six degrees this morning. I took it myself. I've been meaning to tell you since March 1960.*
>
> *Yours,*
>
> *R.*

He looks at the *R.* for a while, with the full stop after it.

Then he addresses the envelope, in the same hand. He hasn't written that address before. He's watched Hester write it twice a year from 1957 until she retired, and he writes it now as though it were a name he'd known all his life, which it is.

*Dr A. Spilsbury*
*Kongens gate 18*
*Ålesund*
*Norway*

He has to put the ring on the *Å*. He's done it once before, on a parcel label, in 1957, too carefully. He does it the ordinary way this time, quick, the way you'd write it if you wrote it every day.

"Airmail," says Marguerite from the end of the table, not looking up from the journal she isn't reading. "Pascual will take it over on the early boat. It'll be in Norway in ten days."

"Ten days."

"He'll be here in three weeks." She turns a page, finally. "Men like that don't write back."

It comes on a Thursday, the fifteenth of October, with the morning post.

Fru Brekke has it on the hall table. She's still low about the canary, and has not yet had the heart to take down the cage, and she says only that there is one from the West Indies, and that she would be glad of the stamp, if he did not want it. Then she goes back into her kitchen and shuts the door, because she's a woman who has had lodgers for thirty years.

He stands in the hall and looks at it.

It's addressed in Reggie's hand.

It's the writing hand, not the capitals, and the ring on the *Å* has been put on quickly, the way anybody would put it on who wrote it every day. He has not seen that hand on an envelope since the autumn of 1956. For thirteen years every envelope from that island has been in Hester Bevan's hand, and then in Dr Leslie's, and the man's own hand has been inside, in the form, three lines long, with a gap after the *R*.

He takes it upstairs to his room, where the photograph is propped against the wall on the table, and sits on the edge of the bed, and opens it with his thumb.

He reads it once for what it says.

He does not read it a second time for how it has been built. He sits with it in his hands, and discovers that he isn't able to.

*It's gone to the liver.*

He looks up at the photograph. The man at the rail, side-on, with his glasses on his forehead and his hand flat against his chest. He has taken the measurement off that print every morning for five days, with his thumb against the posts, and got a stone and a half, and read the hand as a hand on a heart. He had the weight right. He had the reason wrong. It was not the heart. It was lower down, and it had been there since Easter, and the hand had been over the pocket all along, over his own letter, which had been in there since April.

*I've got your letter. It's been in my pocket since April.*

He sits on the bed.

For thirteen years every sum he has done has had the same thing on the far side of the page. A man on a cay in the West Indies, alive and on the licence and in post, with his animals and his friend behind the wall and a lamp at the end of the row. Everything on this side of the page was spent to keep that standing. The form, twice a year. The stove. The quay in all weathers. The bar at the Charing Cross, facing the door, until half past ten, five nights running. Six words in April, and then not writing again, because he had said he would not, and because it was a line, and the man at the other end of it was the one thing he had left to protect.

The man at the other end of it has put a date on himself, in his own hand, exactly, because Alec would want it exactly.

He looks at the far side of the page, and there is a date on it.

He finds that the sum does not need doing. That is the whole of what happens in Fru Brekke's upstairs room at a quarter past eight on a Thursday morning. There is nothing to protect the man *for*. Every line he has refused to draw for thirteen years was refused so that the man should have his life, and the man has told him how much of it there is left, and it is not enough to protect.

He reads the last lines again.

*I should like to see you. That is the whole of it. I have no other reason, and I shan't pretend to one.*

His own sentence. In April he had written it out four times before it was plain enough, and here it is, back, in the other hand, with one contraction in it. The man had kept it by heart. Of course he had. He keeps everything by heart that anybody says to him properly, and cannot remember where he put his glasses.

*Twenty-six degrees.*

He puts his hand over his eyes.

He stays like that for some time. Then he gets up, and puts the letter in the inside pocket of his coat, on the left, and buttons the coat over it, and goes down the stairs and out without his coffee. Fru Brekke calls after him about the stamp. He says, over his shoulder, that she shall have it, and does not stop.

---

Rasmussen is in his office on the first floor with the window open on the Brosund and a hooded seal's skull on the desk, which he is using as a paperweight.

"I must go to the West Indies," says Spilsbury. "Today, if it can be done. Tomorrow if it cannot."

Rasmussen looks at him over the skull.

He has been Spilsbury's Director for thirteen years. He has not asked him one question in that time about anything but seals, and Spilsbury has been grateful for it every week of the thirteen. He looks at him now for rather a long time. Spilsbury does not know what his face is doing. He suspects, from Rasmussen's, that it is doing something.

"For how long?" says Rasmussen.

"I do not know. Until it is finished."

Rasmussen nods slowly, as though that were a perfectly ordinary term for a leave of absence.

"Then go until it is finished," he says. "Vik will take the cellar. Fru Aarseth will tell him what to do." He gets up and comes round the desk, which he does not do, and holds out his hand. "Solveig will want to give you food for the journey. If you do not go to the house, she will come to the aeroplane with it."

"I shall go to the house."

"Good." Rasmussen keeps hold of the hand a moment longer. "You have been a long time here," he says, in his careful English, "with something the matter. Solveig and I have always said so. It is not our business. But I am glad it is going to the West Indies, whatever it is, and not staying on the quay."

Spilsbury finds he cannot answer that. Rasmussen does not appear to need him to.

---

He sends the cable from the telegraph office on the quay before noon, to Dr Sealy at the Bay, because it is the address at the top of the letter in blue. He writes the form out twice before he gets it plain enough.

*ARRIVING THURSDAY 22ND KINGSTON BOAT STOP SPILSBURY*

The clerk counts the words and asks whether it is to be reply paid. It is a small sum extra. It means that the man at the other end may answer without paying anything at all.

"Yes," says Spilsbury. "Reply paid."

The reply comes to the station at nine the following morning, while he is showing Vik the barrel labels for the last time. The boy brings it down to the cellar in its yellow envelope.

*YES R*

The telegraph has no stops. He looks at the *R* for a while, standing among the barrels of salt, with no full stop after it and nothing to be read off the spacing. It is the least that could have been sent for the money. It is the whole of what he had asked for, and he has been given it, and he had paid in advance so that it should cost the man nothing.

Fru Aarseth, who is checking the manifest at his elbow, says without looking up that she hopes it is good news.

"It is an answer," says Spilsbury.

She considers that, and appears to find it sufficient, and goes on with the manifest.

He's on the quay at the Bay when the Kingston boat comes in, because Marguerite said he could be if he sat.

He sits on a bollard. It's the only one on the quay, iron, painted black, warm from the afternoon, and it's a bollard, and he's thought of that and decided not to mind. He had two pints of somebody else's blood at the General on Tuesday and he's got his breath back, most of it. He could have stood. Marguerite said *sit*, in the voice, and Pascual said *sit*, and Hester, who has come down from the white house by the church in a hat and is standing at his shoulder like a sentry, didn't say anything, and he sat.

The Kingston boat is late.

"The boat is always late," says Hester, to nobody.

It comes round the point at a quarter to four, low in the water, with a crowd on the rail. Reggie watches it come the whole way in. He knows he's doing it and does it anyway, the way you'd watch a tank for an animal you know is in there.

He sees him before the ropes are on.

He's at the rail, near the gangway, a head taller than anybody round him, in a dark suit that's too heavy for the Caribbean and a hat. He's thinner, too. He's greyer. He's standing the way he stood in the lift and at the fuse box and on the jetty in 1957, very upright, with nothing on his face at all, and he's looking at the quay the way he'd read a column, from the top, and Reggie watches him get to the bollard.

It's quite a long way. Perhaps forty yards. Reggie can see him stop reading.

He stands up.

"Reggie," says Marguerite.

"I know. I'm standing up. I'll sit down again in a minute."

The gangway goes down, and the crowd comes off it, women with baskets and a man with a goat and a priest. Alec comes off in the middle of them with two cases, one in each hand, the same two, and walks down the quay. He isn't walking fast. He's walking the way a man walks who's decided not to run and is having to keep deciding it.

He stops about a yard off, and puts the cases down.

Reggie waits for the voice. He's waited for it every time anybody's said this man's name for thirteen years. It's always come, on this one subject, whether he wanted it or not. He waits for his shoulders to come down the half-inch and his face to go warm and the lamp to come up behind his voice.

Nothing comes. There's just his face, and whatever it's doing.

"You're late," he says.

"The boat was late."

"The boat's always late. You used to allow for it."

Alec looks at him. He looks at him the way he looks at anything, all of it, top to bottom, and Reggie watches him take the measurement and get a figure and not like it, and not hide that he doesn't like it, which is new.

"You are two stone lighter than you were," Alec says.

"How on earth —"

"I will tell you later."

And then Alec steps over the cases, on a public quay at the Bay at four in the afternoon, with Hester Bevan at one shoulder and a man with a goat at the other, and puts both arms round him.

It isn't a colleague's anything. It's the whole length of him, and hard, and Reggie's face goes into his shoulder, into wool that smells of an aeroplane and a boat and under that, very faintly, of tobacco. Alec's hand comes up to the back of his neck, where the hair wants cutting, and holds on. He's shaking. Reggie can feel it. Alec Spilsbury, whose hands were perfectly steady at the fuse box in 1946 and on every section he ever cut, is standing on a quay with his arms round a man and shaking like a leaf.

"People will talk," Reggie says into his shoulder.

"Yes."

"Half the Bay's watching."

"Let them watch." Alec's voice is not level. "There is nothing further they can do to you."

Reggie hears it arrive. Thirteen years ago, at a table in the hut at the end of the row, *there is nothing further they can do to him that has not already been done*, about Frank, in the lamplight. And he knows Alec's heard it too, a second after he said it, because his arms go tighter.

He laughs. He can't help it. It comes up out of him into the wool, and it catches, and he goes on laughing.

"That's *dreadful*, Alec."

"I know. I heard it."

"That's the worst thing you've said to me in thirty years."

"No," says Alec. "It is not in the first ten."

They stand there. Reggie doesn't know how long. At some point he's aware that Pascual has come round the bollard and picked up both the cases, and that the man with the goat has gone, and that Hester has turned her back on them squarely, and is facing the harbour, and is telling the priest in a voice that would carry to Kingston that the new wharf is a disgrace.

Then Alec lets go, or starts to, and doesn't let go of his arm.

"Sit down," he says. "Dr Sealy is looking at you."

"She's been looking at me for three weeks."

"Then she has had long enough. Sit down."

He sits down.

Pascual comes up with a case in each hand, and puts them down again, and looks at Alec for a long moment with every single thing he feels going across his face and none of it hidden. Then he takes him by both shoulders, the way he did on the jetty in 1957, briefly and hard.

"You've still got a face like the ash," he says.

"I have been told."

"Didn't you eat anything? In Norway? In thirteen years?"

"A woman called Solveig Rasmussen saw to it."

"Good. Marguerite's going to see to it now. She's worse." Pascual lets go of him, and then doesn't, and says something low in Spanish that Reggie doesn't catch and Alec plainly doesn't understand, and then lets go properly and picks up the cases. "Come on. It's up the hill. He's slow on it. Don't hurry him."

"I had not intended to."

Hester has turned round. She looks at Alec over her glasses for a long moment, from under the hat.

"Dr Spilsbury," she says.

"Mrs Bevan."

"You've been very regular," says Hester. "March and September. I always said so." And then, in exactly the voice she used for the fish accounts: "It's ten years since I addressed you. I shan't need to again, I take it."

"No," says Alec. "I think not."

"Good," says Hester, and takes the Colonel's arm, since the Colonel has arrived from somewhere, and goes off up the quay without another word.

---

Marguerite's house has one spare room.

She says so at the top of the verandah steps, with her hand on the door. She says it the way she'd tell a patient the surgery shuts at six. "There's one spare room. It's got the big bed in it; it was my parents'. I'm not making up another, the girl has enough to do and so have I. Supper's at eight. The walls are wood. Good night." And she goes in.

Pascual stands on the verandah with a case in each hand and his mouth open.

"*Marguerite* —"

"Good *night*, Pascual."

So they're in it, at ten, with the lamp turned low and the shutters open on the harbour, and the cases still not unpacked, and Alec is sitting in the only chair and Reggie is sitting on the end of the bed, which is very wide and very old and has four posts and a mosquito net tied up in a knot above it.

Neither of them has said anything for a minute or so.

Spilsbury has thought about what he will say in this room on four aeroplanes and a boat, and has thrown all of it away, and finds there is nothing in its place. He sits in the chair and looks. He cannot stop looking. He had not known that he would not be able to stop.

Reggie takes the letter out of his pocket.

It is his own. He knows it from the fold, top third a quarter of an inch short. It has gone soft along the creases and there is a brown mark at one corner, and Reggie holds it on his knee in both hands and does not give it to him.

"You said you'd read it as an answer," Reggie says. "If I didn't write."

"I did."

"What did you read?"

"That you would not come. And that you would not say why, because I knew why." Spilsbury looks at the letter on the man's knee. "I read it in the bar at the Charing Cross, five nights, facing the door, until half past ten. I knew what I was doing. It did not seem to help."

Reggie shuts his eyes.

"I knew you'd sit there," he says. "I knew in April. I didn't let myself look at it. Pascual made me look, in the end. That was the start of it."

"Pobil."

"He threatened me. He's been threatening me since 'fifty-eight. It turns out he meant it."

"I had not thought he would not."

Reggie opens his eyes and looks at him, and for the first time since the quay, something happens at the corner of his mouth.

"You've got fewer contractions than ever," he says. "Did you know? Thirteen years of Norwegian. It's worse."

"I have been told that as well," says Spilsbury. "By a student. She said I spoke Norwegian like a man reading a will." He hears his own voice, and it is not quite level, and he finds he doesn't mind. "Reggie. Why now?"

"You had the letter."

"I had the letter. I want it from you. Not on paper." He leans forward in the chair. "Nicholson is still wherever he is. Nothing about that has changed. In April it was the reason. On the verandah it was not. I should like to know what happened on the verandah."

Reggie looks at the letter on his knee.

"I did your sum," he says. "Pascual said I'd paid. He was right. I'd have been having it on Frank's account for the rest of my life. And the rest of my life's until about next summer. It closes when I do."

"That is half of it."

"You'd know."

"I should. I wrote the other half, at the table. What is it?"

"The lift," says Reggie.

Spilsbury waits.

"In 'fifty-seven I looked across that table and saw who I'd be if I stayed. Him. The man with the voice, getting what he wanted, letting somebody decent walk off with the bill." He turns the letter over on his knee. "I'd been him once. I wasn't going to be him every night till I was seventy."

"And now?"

"There isn't time to turn into anybody. I can only be what I am, for ten months."

"And the cost."

"Is yours," says Reggie. "And Pascual's, and Marguerite's, and Hester's, she'll mind more than she lets on. But you've been asked. All of you. That's the difference." He looks up. "*Not without asking him.* That was me, at the table. You said no."

"I did."

"So I asked. In writing. With the number in."

"And I answered," says Spilsbury, "reply paid."

"You paid for my answer." Something happens at the corner of Reggie's mouth. "Of course you did. You couldn't even let me have the telegram."

Spilsbury sits in the chair.

He can see the whole sum. He can see it the way he sees a curve once the axes are in, and every line of it is correct, and he had not once thought of it. In thirteen years, in every version, on every page, he had not once put the man on the other side as the one with the date on him. There were nine years between them. The arithmetic had always assumed that he would be the one to go, and the man would be the one left, with his animals and a lamp at the end of the row.

"I costed everything," he says. "In February 'fifty-seven. At the table. I costed Frank, and you, and the office, and Easter. I costed my own fifteen years on a quay. I did not cost the order."

"No."

"I was to go first. In every sum. It did not occur to me that it could come out otherwise."

"I know," says Reggie. "I read it off you on the quay."

"What did you read?"

"That you'd done it on the boat coming in, and it came out wrong, and you'd done it again to check, and it came out the same." Reggie's voice is very gentle. "And that you haven't got a book to put it in, so you're carrying it."

Spilsbury looks at him for a long time.

"Yes," he says. "That is what you would have read."

---

"There's something else," Reggie says. "You won't like it."

"I shall like almost nothing about the next year. You may as well go on."

"You wrote to Rasmussen from Kingston. Pascual saw the envelope. You're not going back."

"I am not going back while you are alive."

"Fine. Good. I want you here. That's not the thing." Reggie puts the letter down on the bed beside him. "You'll want to come across. To the cay. To the hut. You've been thinking it since the quay. There's nothing left to protect me for, so why not."

Spilsbury says nothing, which is an answer.

"You can't," says Reggie. "Halloran's minute says *remove from the premises*. It's still in the file, and Jean's name's on the licence now. Not mine. Hers. Pascual's under hers."

"And the calf."

"And the calf's mother's teeth." He holds Spilsbury's eyes. "There's nothing left to protect me for. There's plenty left to protect them for. You stay at the Bay. I'll come to you."

Spilsbury looks at him.

He had sat on four aeroplanes and a boat with nothing on the other side of the page, and had thought that meant the page was blank. It was not blank. There were other names on it. He had not been looking at them, because for thirteen years there had only been the one.

"Yes," he says. "You are right."

"Say that again."

"I shall not. You heard it."

Reggie laughs. It's short, and it catches, and he puts his hand flat over his ribs on the right side while he does it, low down, not over the pocket, and Spilsbury watches the hand go there and reads it, correctly, and says nothing.

"And there's an account," Spilsbury says, "in a bank in London, in my own name. Fifteen pounds each quarter day since midsummer 'fifty-seven. The draft came back in May of that year marked *gone away*. I had them put it on deposit. I have not stopped."

"Of course you haven't."

"It is about eight hundred pounds."

Reggie looks at him, and his face does something complicated, and he shakes his head.

"Later," he says. "That's later. Don't — not tonight."

"No," says Spilsbury. "Not tonight."

---

After a while Reggie holds out his hand, palm up, on the blanket.

Spilsbury looks at it.

"Your pulse," Reggie says. "Give it here. I haven't counted it in thirteen years."

He gets up out of the chair, and it is further across the room than it looked, and he sits down on the end of the bed beside the man and gives him his wrist. Reggie takes it round the bone, the way he would take hold of an animal to count it, and looks at the watch on his other wrist, and his lips move very slightly.

"Ninety-six," he says.

"That seems high."

"It's lower than last time. Last time it was a hundred and four."

"Last time you were leaving me."

"Yes," says Reggie. "Well."

He doesn't let go of the wrist. He turns it over, instead, and puts his thumb in the palm, and holds it there. And Spilsbury, who has not been touched on purpose by anybody but Solveig Rasmussen's handshake in thirteen years, sits on the end of a four-poster bed in a doctor's spare room with his palm open under the man's thumb and discovers that he cannot say anything at all.

"Can I say something?" says Reggie. "Something I've said before. In the wrong voice. I'd like to say it again in the right one."

Spilsbury knows what it is before it comes. He nods.

Reggie looks at him. His face doesn't open or go warm or do anything. There's no lamp behind it. It's just his face, thin, grey at the temples, with the glasses on, looking.

"There's always been something about you," Reggie says.

It is quite quiet. It is said the way the man says a result he is pleased with, to whoever is standing there, without noticing that saying it to somebody is a thing he has done.

Spilsbury has carried the other version of that sentence for thirteen years, in an Ulsterman's square small hand, and heard the voice in it every time. He finds that he can put it down. It goes, as he sits there. He feels it go, the way you would feel a weight taken off a bench.

"Yes," he says, when he can. "That is the right voice."

"And the other thing. I said I'd not say it in a room again."

"You said you would not say it in a room with a pad in it."

Reggie looks round, deliberately, at the chair and the cases and the shutters and the mosquito net in its knot.

"No pad," he says.

"No."

"I love you," says Reggie. "I've loved you since you told me my curve was wrong in a corridor, and I've loved you every day since, including the ones I spent not answering your letters, which I'm sorry for. There. That's said. It's in a room."

Spilsbury puts his free hand up to the man's face, to the jaw, which is sharper than it was, and holds it.

"I have loved you since a passage at four degrees," he says. "I told you so in the spare hut. It has not changed. I have had nowhere to put it for thirteen years, and I have put it on the quay at Ålesund every evening and walked home without it, and it was there again in the morning. I should like to put it here now. For as long as here lasts."

"That's until about next summer."

"Then I shall put it here until about next summer," says Spilsbury, "and after that I shall see."

---

It is a long way from the end of the bed to the pillows. They go the whole of it slowly, because the man is tired, and because Spilsbury finds he does not want to go any faster than he can see.

He takes the glasses off first, and folds them, and puts them on the table by the bed, because somebody has to, and Reggie will put them down anywhere at all and look for them for an hour. Reggie watches him do it and says *thank you* in a voice that isn't quite steady, and then reaches up and takes Spilsbury's off too, and puts them down beside his own, and the two pairs lie there side by side on Marguerite's parents' bedside table.

"Now neither of us can see," says Reggie.

"I can see you perfectly well at this distance."

"Can you? What do you see?"

"A man who needs his hair cutting."

Reggie laughs into his mouth. He kisses him the way he kissed him at four degrees, the first time, in the dark of a stairwell with the boiler gone, as though he'd been meaning to for some while and had just this minute decided. His mouth is dry and a little cracked at the corner, and tastes of Marguerite's lime and something under it that is only him. Spilsbury holds the back of his neck, where the hair is too long, and feels the whole of the man lean into it.

He undoes the shirt. It is the same shirt, or one very like it, that was loose across the shoulders in the photograph, and it is looser now. He had measured a stone and a half off the rail with his thumb, and it is two, and he finds it under his hands before he finds anything else. The ribs are all there to be counted. The collarbones stand up like a bird's. When he puts his palm flat on the man's back, between the shoulder blades, he can feel each one of the vertebrae under the skin, the way you would feel them on an animal that has not been eating.

He does not take his hand away. He does not let anything happen to his face either, and then finds that he is letting it anyway, because Reggie is looking at it, and there is no reason in this room why he should not see.

"Don't," Reggie says softly. "Alec. Don't measure me."

"I am not measuring you."

"You are. I can feel you doing it. You're doing it with your thumb."

Spilsbury stops. He takes his thumb off the rib where it had been, of its own accord, finding the width of the intercostal space, and lays his whole hand flat instead, over the ribs and the heart, and leaves it there.

"I am sorry," he says. "It is the only instrument I have."

"I know. I know it is." Reggie puts his own hand over it, holding it there. "Use it tomorrow. You can measure me all you like tomorrow, in daylight, and write it down. Just not tonight."

"Not tonight," Spilsbury agrees.

After that it goes more easily. He finds he can simply look, which he has not done since 1957 without also reading. The man is thin and grey at the temples and there is a new hollow at the throat, above the collarbones, where the pulse shows. There is the old scar on the forearm from the lip, which he watched being made. There is the scar on the shin from the Labs, which he knows the whole history of and has never told anybody. He puts his mouth to that one, and Reggie makes a sound he hasn't heard in thirteen years and says *oh, Alec, really*, and doesn't mean *stop*.

He is careful. He has never needed to be careful with this man before. Reggie, in 1946, in 1950, on the island in 1956, took up the whole of a bed and a great deal of the air in any room and asked for things in a clear carrying voice as though they were items on a requisition. Now he tires at the shoulders when he lifts his arms, and has to stop, and laugh at himself, and go on. And Spilsbury, who has spent his life being given whatever this man asked for, finds that the man is not asking tonight. He is only holding on. He holds the back of Spilsbury's shirt in a fist, the way he held it in the spare hut, as though keeping his footing on a deck. His hips come up against Spilsbury's and stay there, and his breath goes against Spilsbury's neck, uneven, warm, catching. He says *you're here* twice, in a voice of the purest astonishment, and the second time Spilsbury says *yes*, and puts his mouth to the hollow at the throat, where the pulse is, and feels it go under his lips, too fast and a little ragged and entirely alive.

"Alec."

"I am here."

"Don't stop."

"I have no intention of stopping."

"The walls are wood," Reggie says, into his hair, shaking with something that is half laughing. "She said so. On purpose. Pascual's the other side of that one."

"Then Pobil will have to bear it," says Spilsbury, "as I have borne a great many things on his account," and Reggie laughs outright, and then doesn't, and then says his name in quite another voice, low, against his collarbone, the bone of his brow pressed there hard as it was in 1957 and for a different reason entirely.

---

Afterwards they lie in the middle of the wide bed, because it is wide enough to lie in the middle of.

Reggie is on his back with one arm flung up over his head, the way he has always slept, and Spilsbury is on his side against him with his hand spread over the man's ribs, not measuring, only there. The lamp is nearly out. The shutters are open, and there is a moon over the harbour, and across the channel he can see the long low shape of the cay against the stars.

There are lamps along the row. The students'. Jean Leslie's, in the office. Pobil's old hut.

The one at the far end is dark.

"That's mine," says Reggie. He's watching it too. "The last one. I lit that every night for thirteen years. Pascual filled it on Saturdays. Went to sleep with it burning. I didn't know I did it till this month."

"Why?"

"You know why."

Spilsbury does know. He looks at it for a while. He remembers standing in the doorway of the hut at the end of the row at two in the morning, in 1956, with the lamp out because the man inside was asleep beside him. He had said once, on the island, that the only nights Reggie Tar slept properly were the nights the light was out.

"It is out now," he says.

"I've been here since Tuesday week. Marguerite made me. Pascual goes across and doesn't light it." Reggie's voice is getting slower. "It's dark every night now. You can see it from here. First thing I looked at, the first night. I thought it'd be awful, and it wasn't. It was only dark."

"Go to sleep," says Spilsbury.

"I'm going."

"You are talking."

"I'm going to sleep talking," says Reggie. "It's efficient."

And then he isn't talking, fairly suddenly, the way he goes, out like a lamp himself with his arm over his head and his mouth a little open. Spilsbury lies awake beside him and does not count his breathing. He finds that he is counting it anyway, round about the fortieth. He stops himself. He starts again. He lies with his hand on the man's ribs and feels them go up and down under his palm, and at some point between the twentieth and the thirtieth he stops counting, not because he has stopped himself, but because he is asleep.

---

Reggie wakes at six to an empty bed and the shutters full of grey, and has one bad moment before he's properly awake, and then hears the verandah door.

Alec comes in with his collar off and his sleeves rolled and the thermometer on its string in his hand, wet to the bulb.

"You went without me," says Reggie.

"You were asleep. I was told on the quay not to hurry you." Alec holds up the thermometer. "It is twenty-seven in the harbour. At the steps. Six o'clock."

"Twenty-seven."

"Yes."

"I said twenty-six in my letter."

"That was a fortnight ago, and off the jetty at the Martin. It is warmer in the harbour. It is sheltered." Alec sits on the side of the bed. "I have been standing on the harbour steps for a quarter of an hour, wondering where to put it."

Reggie looks at him.

Then he pushes the blanket back, carefully, and gets his feet on the floor, and gets across the room to the case he brought from the cay on Tuesday week, and gets it open. It takes him longer than it would have done in the spring. Alec doesn't help him. Alec sits on the bed and watches him do it, and doesn't help, and Reggie loves him for that more than for anything he's done since the quay.

It's at the bottom, under his shirts, wrapped in brown paper from the stationer's by the church. He bought it on Tuesday week, walking back from the General slowly after the second lot of blood, before there was any cable, before there was anything. He had walked past the window and seen it and gone in.

He gives it to Alec without saying anything.

Alec unwraps it. He does it the way he opens everything, and the brown paper comes off square.

It's a notebook. Octavo, green cloth boards, feint ruled. It's as near as the stationer at the Bay could come to the two he'd burnt in a brick incinerator behind a sick bay in February 1957, and it's nowhere near, the green is too bright and the paper's too thin. Alec sits with it on his knee and doesn't open it for a long time.

"You had no way of knowing I would come," he says.

"No."

"You bought it before you wrote."

"I bought it before I'd decided to write." Reggie sits down on the bed beside him, because his legs would like him to. "I thought if I didn't, I'd post it to you. After. Jean could put it in with the notice. You'd have something to write in."

Alec looks at the notebook.

Then he takes a pencil out of the pocket of the shirt, because there has always been a pencil in the pocket of his shirt, and opens the book at the first page. He writes on it, small, in the hand Reggie has read on the backs of envelopes and in the margins of other men's papers and in two green books he was never allowed to see, and was not allowed to see now, and looks at anyway, over his shoulder, because he's sitting right there.

*The Bay, harbour steps, 23.x.70, 06.00. 27°.*

He stops with the pencil above it.

"Initials," says Reggie.

"It was I who took it."

"I bought the book."

Alec considers that, and appears to find it sufficient, and writes, after the full stop:

*A. S., R. T.*

He looks at it. Reggie looks at it. It's one reading, from one place, at one hour, with two sets of initials after it, and there's nothing on the other side of it, and there's nothing that needs to be.

Alec closes the book on the pencil and holds it on his knee. Across the channel through the open shutters, the cay has come up out of the grey, long and low, with the row along the top of it. There's smoke from the kitchen chimney where the cook's lit the stove, and a boat going out from the jetty, and at the far end of the row the last hut with its shutters closed and its lamp dark, as it's been every night since Tuesday week.

"Breakfast," says Alec.

"I'm not hungry."

"Dr Sealy will be looking at you."

"She's always looking at me."

"Then she has had long enough," says Alec, and gets up, and holds out his hand, and Reggie takes it, and lets himself be pulled up off the bed, and goes to breakfast.
