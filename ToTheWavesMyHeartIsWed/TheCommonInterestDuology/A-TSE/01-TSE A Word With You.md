# A Word With You

The rain starts while he is walking back from the pens, so lightly that for the first minute he takes it for spray.

He goes the north way, past the generator. It is longer. He tells himself it is because the south path floods at the culvert and he has no wish to ruin his shoes, and he keeps telling himself that all the way past the generator, where the noise is such that a man could scream twenty feet off and you would hear nothing at all. Jean established that. Jean went and stood there with a watch and established it, and then took it to Hester Bevan instead of to him, which he has not decided yet whether to mind.

So he cannot hear anything from the beach. That is the whole of it. He would have to be looking, and he is not looking.

He counts the drums outside the fuel store as he goes past, which is nine, the same as it was this morning, and then he counts the steps up to the old sick bay, which is five, and then there is nothing left to count and he is standing in front of a door with a light under it.

---

Pascual has the lamp pulled right down over the bench and his back to the door, and he does not turn round when it opens.

"It's late," he says.

"It is."

"If it's about the morning, it's ready. The files are ready. I did them twice." A page turns. "I'm not doing them a third time because you've thought of something."

Reggie shuts the door behind him. The room smells of formalin and hot lamp glass and, under it, of the sea, which everything here does. On the bench there is the buff folder with the specimen tag pasted on the corner, and beside it a fresh sheet with a ruled column down the left in Pascual's blunt hand: the ten days of water samples, colour and volume, port wine to straw to clear. He has been making it beautiful. He has been sitting here at eleven at night making the file beautiful for the Americans, because the animal that was dying is not dying, and it is his, and he wants it seen.

"Pascual."

Something in it makes him turn round. The lamp is under his chin and it does what lamps do to that face, which is nothing subtle.

"Charles has taken Beta out," Reggie says. "Tonight. He'll be past the reef within the hour, if he isn't already."

Pascual looks at him. The pen is still in his hand.

"Out," he says. "Out where? Out of the pen — out of the *pen*? What do you mean, out? Where is he?" He is up off the stool by the third question, and the stool goes over behind him and neither of them looks at it. "What have you done?"

"I told him to do it."

"You —" Pascual takes one step and stops as if he has walked into the edge of something. "*Say it again.*"

"Charles is putting Beta into the sea. I gave him the order and the chart and he's doing it now."

For a moment there is only the generator, and the rain starting properly on the iron roof, small and fast, like sand thrown.

"Eleven days ago," Pascual says, very low, "that animal was passing water the colour of port. Eleven days. He couldn't turn his own shoulders. I have been putting bicarbonate into his fish twice a day with my own hands —"

"I know."

"You don't know, you have never once come and *looked* —"

"He's fit," Reggie says. "Two and a half knots sustained. Charles timed him round the pen and I checked the arithmetic myself. Twenty-five nautical miles in a day and a bit more if he pushes. You told the boy on the fifth day that he could swim."

"I said he could swim, I did not say he could go to *sea* —" and then Pascual stops, because he is an honest man and he has heard what he has just said. He stands there with his mouth open on the rest of it. Reggie watches him put it down. It is not a graceful thing to watch; it never is with him. The heat goes out of his face in patches, like a lamp cooling.

"He is fit," Pascual admits. "The stiffness went on the fifth day. The water was clear on the ninth. He's nine pounds up and he's been eating like a man at a wedding." He rubs a hand down his face. "He is fit. *Ay.*"

He bends and rights the stool and sets it square, and does not sit on it.

"You didn't ask me," he says.

"No."

"You have never once asked me. Not for the trial, not for the boy, not for this. Do you know what I am for, Reggie? Do you actually know?"

"You're the only man on this station whose word I'd have taken for it," Reggie says, "and I didn't ask you, because if I'd asked you at six o'clock this evening you'd have said no, and by seven you'd have said yes, and I hadn't got the hour."

Pascual's mouth is open again. He shuts it.

"That is not an apology," he says.

"No."

"I did not think it was." He folds his arms. Under the shirt his forearms are a mess of old scars from things that bit him before Reggie ever met him. "Why."

---

"Spilsbury," Reggie says.

"That is a name. It is not a reason."

"He comes in with the Navy party at ten hundred. Officially he's an observer, the same as the last one." Reggie takes his glasses off, finds them wet, and puts them back on without doing anything about it. "The last one looked at the log book and walked away again. This one will want two things. He'll want the specimen that beat the mortality tables, and he'll want the man who did it, and he'll want to know why it worked. He isn't interested in keeping either of them."

"You are guessing."

"I'm not."

"Then how do you know?" Pascual says. "How do you know what a man will want? You have never met a man you could predict in your life, you can't predict *Jean* —"

"I know him."

"How?"

"I am not going to answer that."

It comes out flat and level and wrong, and Reggie hears it come out wrong, every word of it standing separate like something read off a card, and there is nothing whatever to be done about that now.

Pascual does not say anything. He is looking at him the way he looks at an animal that has gone quiet in a manner he does not care for: the whole face working, thinking, deciding what to touch first. He is an anatomist. He has spent a year across a table from this face at three meals a day and he is not a fool, and Reggie stands there and lets himself be read, because the alternative is to walk out, and he finds he is not going to walk out.

"All right," Pascual says at last.

That is all. *All right.* It goes through Reggie like the first mouthful of anything hot.

"There is still the boy," Pascual says. "You'll have to hide him. He can't be here in the morning with his face doing what his face does, they'll have it out of him in ten minutes —"

Reggie says nothing.

It is a fraction of a second too long and Pascual is on it like a dog.

"Reggie."

"He's going with him."

"He is *what*?"

"He's going into the water with the animal and I don't expect him back."

Pascual makes a noise. It is not a word in either of his languages. "You have put a boy — that boy, that boy who cannot remember to eat, who I have to send to bed like a child — you have put him into the open sea, at night, in the dark, with no boat, with no —"

"He has water. And food, and zinc, and lines, and hooks, and a knife."

"*How do you know what he has?*"

"Because I watched him pack it," Reggie says, "over the last five days, and I didn't stop him."

The rain fills up the room. Pascual has both hands on the edge of the bench behind him and is gripping it, and the tendons stand out from wrist to elbow, and Reggie thinks, quite clearly and from some distance off, that if the man decides to hit him it will be a considerable event.

"You gave him a chart," Pascual says.

"Yes."

"You gave a man a chart with the fresh water marked on it and the ordnance marked on it and you're telling me you didn't know."

"I knew," Reggie says. "I knew when I put it in his hand. I've known for a week. He's going to hold onto a wild animal in open water four hundred miles from anywhere his own kind lives, and the odds are very poor, and I gave him the chart."

"*Why.*"

"Because I would rather he drowned," Reggie says, "than that Spilsbury had him on a table to find out how it works."

He hears himself say it and finds that it is true, and that he has not previously said it in that order, even to himself.

Pascual lets go of the bench.

He does not shout. That is the thing about him that nobody outside this room would credit: the shouting is the small stuff, the shouting is a bad blood sample and a wet tarp and a man who did not fetch him at two in the morning. When it is real he goes quiet and he goes and stands nearer.

"He'd have died here," Pascual says. "The animal. If Hester's arrangement had not come off, and it might not have. He'd have gone the way Alpha went and the way Gamma is going." He says it as if it costs him money. "And the boy would have died with him. Slower. That is my opinion. You may put it in your file."

"Thank you."

"It is not for you, it is what I think." He glances at the bench, at the ruled sheet with its ten beautiful days on it, and away again. "Have you eaten?"

"What?"

"Have you eaten anything, tonight, since the verandah."

"No."

"*Dios.*" He is already moving. There is a tin locker under the bench that Reggie has never once wondered about, and out of it comes bread, and a tin of something, and a bottle a third down with no label, and it is all put in front of him on the clear end of the bench with the flat competence of a man laying out instruments. "Sit down. Not there, that's fixative. There."

Reggie sits down. He is handled. He is aware of being handled, in the way one is aware of the tide, and he eats the bread, and it is very good, and he has a mouthful out of the bottle and it takes the top of his head off politely.

Pascual leans against the bench with his arms folded and watches him do it and says nothing at all, and this — this precisely, this exact procedure, food produced and put down and not discussed — is what Reggie has been watching him do to other people for a year.

"The material from Alpha," Reggie says. "Jean's blocks. Are they still in the store?"

"Yes. Both boxes. Why?"

"No reason."

Pascual's eyebrows come down. It is not a subtle face and it is not doing anything subtle now.

"No reason," he repeats.

"Not tonight."

And Pascual — who does not let anything go, who asks four questions where one would do — looks at him for a long moment and then says, "Eat your bread," and lets it go, for tonight, in the manner of a man setting something aside within easy reach.

---

Eleven months ago the generator failed.

It was February and there was no moon to speak of, and they walked out along the jetty with one hurricane lamp between them to see what the pumps were doing, and at the seaward end there is a bollard that is not where anyone would expect a bollard to be. Pascual put a hand on the back of his neck and steered him round it.

That is the whole of the incident. A hand on the back of the neck, warm, absolutely certain, for the length of about three steps, and then gone, and no remark made, and the pumps were fine.

What told Reggie was not the hand.

What told him was that in the eleven months since, Pascual Pobil has not touched him. Not once. Not a hand on the arm at the rail, not a palm between the shoulder blades going through a door, not the grip on the elbow he gives Jean, or John, or the men off the boats, or the boy, whose glasses he has taken off his face and cleaned on his own shirt in front of the entire station. Reggie has kept the record. There is nothing else in his life he has kept a record of that he did not intend to publish.

He puts the last of the bread down and squares the plate to the edge of the bench, because his hands want something to do.

"I don't want to go back to my room," he says.

"Then don't," Pascual says. "There's the cot through there, it's not much, but —"

"That isn't what I meant."

The rain comes down harder. Somewhere along the roof a nail has gone and there is a thin sound under the loud sound.

"I have a hypothesis," Reggie says. "I've had it about eleven months. It's the only one I've ever had that I haven't tested, and I find that tonight I'd rather like to know, because there's a fair chance that after ten o'clock tomorrow I shan't have the leisure."

Pascual has gone completely still. Everything shows on that face; the trouble is that a great deal is showing at once.

"You'll have to say it out loud," he says. "I'm not the one who reads minds."

Reggie stands up.

"February," he says. "The pumps. You put your hand on my neck to get me round the bollard and you haven't touched me since. Not once. You touch everyone. You had your hands in Charles' hair this morning getting a fish hook out of it. And you have been extremely careful with me for eleven months, and I don't believe it's because you don't like me, because you're incapable of concealing it when you don't like someone —"

"Reggie —"

"— and I would like you to stop being careful. That's the hypothesis. I can put it more plainly if you'd rather."

"No," Pascual says. "*No.* You've — no. That's plain."

He has not moved. He has gone the colour of a man who has been out in the sun, from the throat up, and he is looking at Reggie as if he has been handed something breakable and wet.

"Do you know what it would cost," he says.

"I know exactly what it would cost. I've known what it would cost since I was fifteen. I have a fair estimate of the odds and I've decided I'll have them." Reggie takes his glasses off and folds them and puts them down on the bench, which is the only thing he can think of to do with his hands. "You may say no. Say it in a sentence and I'll go and sit in the store with Alpha until it gets light and we'll neither of us mention it again, and I'd think no worse of you, and it would make no difference to anything tomorrow."

Pascual crosses the room.

He does it in three steps, the same as the jetty, and stops with about a foot to spare, which at his size is not a great deal of spare.

"You are the most infuriating man I have ever worked with," he says.

"Yes."

"You *know* that. You know it and you don't care —"

"No."

"— and I have wanted to put you against a wall since the second week."

"Well," Reggie says. His voice does something. "There's a wall."

---

He reaches up, and puts his hand flat against the side of Pascual's face.

He does it deliberately, knowing what it is. Jean told him what she saw at the pen, and he went to Hester and Hester recited him three lines of Andersen from memory in her sitting room with her hands folded, and he has thought about it more than he intends to admit to anybody. So he knows the shape of it. He makes it on purpose, with his eyes open, an Englishman of thirty-four in a sick bay that smells of formalin, borrowing the vow of a species that is not his.

Nothing comes across. Of course nothing comes across; he is not Charles and this is not water and the man in front of him has no more of that in him than the bench has.

He learns everything anyway.

He learns it from the jaw going tight under his palm, and the breath that stops and then does not start again for a noticeable interval, and the small sound Pascual makes, which is the same sound he made on the verandah before he clamped his mouth shut, the one that got away from him. He learns it from the heat coming off the man like a stove.

And then Pascual raises his own hand and puts it against the side of Reggie's face, and holds it there.

Reggie's eyes shut. It is not a decision.

*You don't know what you've just done,* he thinks. *I shall have to tell you, and then you'll be furious, and I shall enjoy it.*

Then Pascual's mouth is on his and there is nothing organised about any of it after that.

---

The bolt on the inner door is a good one; it is Admiralty, it went in in 1943 to keep men out of the drug store, and it takes Pascual two goes to shoot it because he will not let go with the other hand.

The room beyond is eight feet by ten, iron cot, mosquito net tied up in a knot, one small high window with the rain coming through the corner of it onto the floor, and nobody who is not looking for it comes within thirty yards of it after dark. Reggie takes all this in, in the way he takes in a room, and files it, and then is put against the wall as advertised, and stops filing anything.

He had expected — insofar as he had let himself lay out an expectation, which he had, in some detail, at intervals, for eleven months — he had expected the size of him. What he had not properly costed was the *carefulness*: the hand that comes up behind his head first, before anything else, so that the wall does not have him; the mouth that goes to the corner of his jaw and stops there, waiting, until Reggie makes a noise that could not be read as anything but yes.

"Don't," Reggie says, "don't be gentle with me, for God's sake —"

"*Quieto*," Pascual says into his throat, "I will be exactly as I like," and goes on being both at once, and it turns out that this is the thing, this is precisely the thing, and Reggie has to hold on to a fistful of the man's shirt and think about the Colonel's boat schedule for several seconds in order to remain a going concern.

He gets the shirt off him, eventually. There is a lot of him and he is built like the men who unload the fish, and there is a white seam of scar under the left collarbone that Reggie has never seen and cannot ask about tonight, and he puts his mouth on it anyway. Pascual says something in Spanish against his hair that Reggie does not catch and does not need to.

They get to the cot in stages. The cot is a disgrace and complains bitterly and neither of them cares. There is a period during which Reggie is on his back with a hand spread flat on his chest holding him down, not hard, just *there*, the whole weight of the man's attention behind three ounces of pressure, and being looked at — that face, which conceals nothing, which has never in its life concealed anything, looking down at him and letting him see all of it at once — is very nearly more than he can stand. He shuts his eyes and it is worse. He opens them.

"You're laughing," Pascual says, outraged.

"I'm delighted," Reggie says. "There's a difference. Come here."

There is heat and rain and iron and the noise of the generator through the wall covering everything, which is a mercy, because Reggie is not quiet and discovers this about himself at some length. Hands, and hips, and the small of his back, and his own mouth on a shoulder to muffle himself and the shoulder being offered for exactly that purpose, and being held, at the end, held down hard and held on to, and a voice saying his name in a register he has never heard from him and will now hear whenever he closes his eyes for the rest of his life.

Afterwards he lies with his face against a wet collarbone and his heart going like a bird's and cannot for some minutes assemble a complete thought.

The first one that arrives is: *eleven months.*

The second is: *and I have got nine hours.*

---

The rain settles into something steady. It is a fortnight early, and he thinks: good. No boat goes out looking in this. No aircraft either.

Pascual has an arm over him and is running his thumb, absently, up and down the ridge of Reggie's spine, the way he strokes an animal he is keeping calm, and has quite certainly not noticed he is doing it.

"You should eat more," he says.

"I've just eaten your bread."

"You are like a bird. I've watched you at that table for a year, you eat what a bird eats and then you talk for two hours." The thumb goes on. "Tell me what you asked about Alpha for."

"No."

"Reggie."

"In the morning."

Pascual is quiet for a moment. Then he says, "You will ask me to write something."

Reggie does not answer.

"You'll ask me to write something that is not true, and put my name under it, and give it to an American at ten o'clock."

"In the morning," Reggie says.

"*Ay, Dios.*" But the thumb does not stop, which is interesting, and which he files. "Ask me now, then, and get it over with."

"No."

"Why not?"

Reggie considers the several things he could say, which include *because you'd say yes tonight and mean it, and I want you to have slept on it*, and *because if you say no I should rather it happened after breakfast than now,* and *because I've had this for one hour and eleven minutes and I am not spending it on Spilsbury.* He is not accustomed to being in a room where he would say any of them. It is a novel difficulty. He has spent his whole life not being in the room.

"Because I'd rather have tonight first," he says.

The thumb stops. Then starts again.

"All right," Pascual says.

They lie there. Somewhere out past the reef, in water that Reggie has never once been into and does not intend to, there is a man holding onto an animal in the dark with a knapsack on its back, going west or south or nowhere at all, and there is nothing further that anybody on this island can do about it.

"That thing you did," Reggie says. "With your hand. On my face."

"Mm."

"You ought to know what it was."

"It was my hand on your face."

"It's a mermaid vow," Reggie says. "Andersen has it, and Wilde has it. Both of them talked to naturalists. One of them puts a hand to the other's face and if it's answered, that's the thing done, and it seems it's more or less permanent. Hester says so. Charles did it to Beta some weeks ago and I imagine that's the whole reason we're all in this mess."

There is a silence of really excellent quality.

"And you did that to me," Pascual says, "*first*, on purpose, without telling me, in my own sick bay."

"I did."

"You unbelievable —" He gets up on one elbow. His face is doing several things and every one of them is legible from the door. "You could have said."

"I've just said."

"You —" And then he stops, and looks at him a moment longer, and lies back down and hauls Reggie in against him with an arm like a spar, which Reggie permits, in the interests of the experiment.

"Then it's done," Pascual says to the ceiling, in the tone of a man closing a file. "There. It's done, it's your own fault, and I'm not doing it again in the morning, so don't ask me."

"Ten hundred hours," Reggie says.

"Go to sleep."

He does not, particularly. He lies and listens to the rain going hard on the iron and the generator under it, and he counts, out of long habit, the hours between now and the boat: nine, and then eight, and then a bit less than eight. Somewhere in the seventh he stops counting, and puts his hand flat on the warm broad plane of Pascual's chest, over the ribs, where it can be felt, and leaves it there.
