# A Likeness

The mail boat comes in on the fourteenth of July with the glassware Pascual has been writing letters about since May, a crate of stores, two drums of paraffin, and four letters for him.

He counts them on the jetty before he opens any of them, which is a habit he has had since he was nine and has never troubled to give up. Four. One with the Society's die-stamp. One from the *Journal*. One in a hand he does not know, with a Shropshire postmark. And one from a man called Marchant at Plymouth who he last saw in 1949 and who has no reason on earth to write to him.

He takes them up to the office and shuts the door and sits down and opens the *Journal* first, on the reasonable principle that one eats the worst thing on the plate before it goes cold.

---

They are printing the retraction in the December number. They would like it cut by a third and they would like the word *erroneous* left in, which he had tried to take out on the grounds that it was doing the work of four other words and doing it at his expense.

The Society's letter is two paragraphs of exquisite regret and contains the phrase *in the circumstances* twice.

Marchant's is four pages long.

Marchant, it turns out, has a bad set of numbers on grey seals in a tank at the aquarium going back to 1938 that he has never known what to do with, and has read the retraction in proof because somebody sent it round, and has worked out inside a week what took Reggie two years, which is that he has been measuring his own housekeeping. *I have been running the figures again since Thursday and they are frankly disgraceful and I am delighted. Tell me what your anatomist found in the adrenals. Tell me at once.*

Reggie reads it twice and then puts it down and sits back and finds that he is grinning at a wall like a fool.

Then he goes and finds Pascual, who is in the sick bay with the new glassware out of its straw and spread across the bench, holding a flask up to the window with an expression of naked greed on his face.

"Marchant's got grey seals," Reggie says.

"Who's Marchant?"

"Plymouth. Pinnipeds. Sixteen years of tank records he thinks are rubbish, and he's right, and he's worked out why." He puts the four pages down on top of the straw. "He wants the histology."

Pascual puts the flask down and reads it standing up, moving his lips slightly, which he does with English and does not know he does.

"He says *at once*," he says.

"He does."

"*Ay.* Then I'll want the whole block series photographed, and I've no camera worth the name, and you'll have to ask Bevan for money."

"I'll ask him at supper."

"Ask him before supper. He'll say yes before supper and no after it." Pascual is already sweeping straw off the bench with his forearm. "Sixteen years. In a *tank*."

"In a tank."

"And nobody's put two of them in it."

"Nobody ever puts two of them in it," Reggie says.

There is a short silence in which both of them think the same thing and neither of them says it, and then Pascual says, "Photographs. Go away and get me my money," and Reggie goes away.

He is at the door when Pascual says, "Which was the fourth letter?"

"Shropshire."

Pascual stops sweeping straw.

---

*Dear Dr Tar,*

*My sister and I have had your letter and Mrs Bevan's letter and the crate, and I am sorry to have taken seven weeks to answer you. Victoria answered at once and I could not. I am the younger one. Charles wrote to me and not to her, which was not fair on her and which I did not do anything about at the time.*

*I do not want to ask you anything about the water. Mrs Bevan's letter was very clear and I have read it a number of times and I do not think there is anything further that would help.*

*What I am writing to ask is whether there is anything of his at the Institute with his writing on it.*

*We have four letters. They are all from his first month there and they are all about the weather and the food and a man called John, and after that he stopped, and I did not chase him, because I was cross with him for going. The books have come and there is nothing written in them except his name in the front, twice, and once a pencil mark against a passage in the Andersen which I do not understand.*

*I know he kept notes. He kept notes about everything from the age of about nine; he had a book about a pond at home and he weighed things in it. If there is a log or a day book or anything at all in his hand that the Institute does not need, even a page, even a list, I should be very glad of it. I would pay for the postage and I would send it back if it were wanted.*

*I am sorry to trouble you with this. I expect you have a great deal to do.*

*Yours sincerely,*
*Lettice Cholmondeley*

---

He reads it three times and then he takes his glasses off and sits in the office for some while with them in his hand.

Forty yards away, in a house with a Colonel in it, in a place nobody has found in forty-one years, there are four hundred pages of a young man's handwriting, dated, consistent, with the failures left in.

*He kept notes about everything from the age of about nine.*

---

He gets Pascual after the pens are done, at nine, and hands him the letter and lets him read the whole of it, and then says, "One page. The fan worm, or the first entry, or the list of fish on a Tuesday. I don't care which."

"No."

"Pascual —"

"*No.*" He puts the letter down flat on the bench and squares it, which is a thing Reggie has taught him and which he does now when he wants his hands doing something. "Not one page."

"She wants a list. She'd take a list."

"She'd take a list and she'd put it in a frame and show it to her vicar."

"Lettice Cholmondeley isn't going to —"

"In nine years somebody asks her where her brother died, and she'll say the William Martin, and out comes the page." He is not shouting. He has gone the other way. "And it'll have a date on it. Everything that boy wrote has a date on it. He couldn't not put one."

"Then cut the date off."

"Then it's a page with no date, out of a book that doesn't exist, sent by a man who wrote to her that the boy drowned on the nineteenth of May."

"The handwriting proves nothing on its own —"

"The handwriting's the only thing that has to be proved." Pascual has both hands on the edge of the bench now. "Spilsbury asked at my table for that book. He said it was the most valuable object on this station, and he was *right*."

"And John told him it went into the sea."

"And that's holding up because there's no paper anywhere in the world in Charles Cholmondeley's hand after the seventeenth of May." He lets go of the bench. "You want to put some in a post office."

"I know what I want to put in a post office."

"Then don't."

The generator does what it does.

"She has four letters about the weather," Reggie says.

"I know."

"He wrote to her and not to the other one, and she was cross with him for going, and she didn't chase him, and now that is the whole of what she has."

"I *know*," Pascual says, "and it's the worst letter I've read, and my answer's still no," and then, more quietly: "Ask me again in the morning if you like. I'll say it again."

Reggie stands there with his hands behind his back.

"No," he says at last. "You'd say it again and you'd be right again and I'd have made you say it twice."

"That's the first time you've done that."

"Don't get used to it."

---

He goes to Hester in the morning because there is nowhere else to go, and because he has worked out somewhere around four o'clock that he has been trying to solve the wrong problem for a night and a half.

She reads the letter at her desk with her spectacles on and does not say anything for a long time.

"She's the younger one," she says.

"Yes."

"The younger one is always the one who writes." Hester takes her spectacles off and folds them. "What did Dr Pobil say?"

"No. Twice, and correctly, and I made him do it the second time, which I regret."

"Good." She puts the letter down. "He's right, and you know he's right, and I'm not going to spend the morning proving it to you. So let us have the other question. What is it she actually wants?"

Reggie opens his mouth to say *a page*, and does not say it.

"She wants to know what he was like," he says.

"Yes."

"And she thinks the way to have that is a list of fish, because she has four letters about the weather and a pencil mark in a book she doesn't understand."

"Yes," Hester says. "And now tell me why you wanted to send her one."

He does not answer that.

"Because you've read the book," she says, "and I've read the book, and Dr Pobil hasn't."

"That isn't —"

"Between the three of us there's one person with any business deciding what leaves this island, and it isn't the one who's been awake all night." She puts her hand flat on the desk. "Come back at six."

---

At six she gives him three sheets of notepaper in her own hand.

It is not a copy. He can see that in the first line. She has gone through four hundred pages and taken out the things that could not hang anybody — no dates, no figures, no pens, no bicarbonate, no hand on any face — and written them out one after another in the third person, as a woman writes when she is telling somebody about a person they both knew.

*He gave the animal a name in his sixth week because it made a sound at him and he could not bear to go on calling it by a letter. He chose the name badly and knew he had and used it anyway. He wrote down every single time he was wrong, which almost nobody does. He thought the creature thought of him as a fan worm, the sort that comes out of its tube to feed and goes back in, and he wrote that he did not know what to do with this. He was not afraid of anything in the water.*

"You can't send her this," Reggie says. "It's in your hand."

"It is in the hand of the chief administrator of this Institute, who is writing to the sister of a member of staff about his work and his character, which is precisely the thing I am for." Hester is already addressing the envelope. "There is no fact in there that could not have come out of a supervisor's recollection, and the one thing in it that is unusual is a fan worm, and if anybody ever asks me where I got a fan worm from I shall say that Dr Tar told me, and you will say that you did, and that will be the end of it."

"And the book?"

"Stays where it is for thirty years." She blots the envelope. "She's thirty-one, Reginald. She'll be sixty-one."

"That's a long while to be given nothing."

"She's the one who'll be alive when it comes out. She'll read it, and she'll find out that he was happy." Hester sets the blotter down. "That's the part worth having, and it doesn't fit in a post office this year."

Reggie looks at the three sheets for a while.

"You did that in a day," he says.

"I did that in forty-one years," says Hester Bevan. "Go away."

---

The covering letter takes him four drafts, which is three more than the paper took.

Pascual comes in on the Monday evening with his hands still smelling of fixer and reads the fourth one over his shoulder, because it does not occur to him to ask.

*Dear Sir — I enclose a paper which I hope may be of some small interest to you. You will notice that my own name stands fourth, and I should like to say at once that this is not modesty but arithmetic; I have contributed the least of the four and it seemed absurd to pretend otherwise. I am conscious that the Society has been very patient with me this year, and I hope —*

"Take it out," Pascual says.

"Which part?"

"All of it. Start again at *I enclose a paper*."

Reggie puts the pen down. "It's a covering letter. It's meant to —"

"It's the voice."

"It's the usual voice for a covering —"

"It's the voice you used on Bevan on Wednesday." Pascual has not moved from behind the chair. "And I've had five days deciding whether to say so."

Reggie sits back.

"I got you a camera," he says.

"You got me a camera in four minutes, out of a man who'd already decided no, before supper, with his own joke handed back to him improved."

"You wanted the camera."

"I wanted the camera. I'd want it again tomorrow." Pascual comes round and leans on the corner of the desk. "That isn't the question."

"You haven't asked one."

"No." He folds his arms. "Where did you get it?"

Reggie takes his glasses off and looks at them, and thinks, without any particular distress, that he would rather be doing almost anything else, and that this is not a good enough reason.

"Where does anybody get anything," he says. "Eton at thirteen. Before that my mother's drawing room on Thursdays, which was worse. Then Cambridge, which is the same place with punts."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the whole answer." He turns the glasses over. "It's a dialect. Five hundred boys in one building, no money changing hands, and nothing whatever to be had except by being pleasant to somebody who has it."

"And you were good at it."

"I was ordinary at it. Everyone I grew up with has one and about a third of them have a better one." He shrugs. "I'm sorry. You want a thing that happened to me, and nothing did. It came with the territory, like the vowels."

Pascual is quiet for a moment.

"Something happened," he says.

"Something happened in my twenties, which is a different matter. I found out what I'd been using it for." Reggie puts the glasses back on. "That's not a confession. It's a date."

"*Ay.*"

"Quite."

"I'm not asking you to stop it."

"Then what?"

"Your face goes somewhere else." Pascual says it flatly, which is how he says the things he has decided about. "I watched it from four feet away on Wednesday."

"And?"

"And you were entirely charming and you weren't there. I couldn't find you in it." He is not being kind about this and is not pretending to be. "Worst four minutes of my week. Then he gave you the two hundred pounds."

Reggie sits with that for a bit.

"You've got one, you know," he says.

"I haven't."

"You've a very good one and it runs in Spanish."

"That's not the same —"

"Salt Cay. The ice woman. Juan's mother, who doesn't like scientists and has fed you twice." He picks the pen back up. "You got a second refrigerator out of the quartermaster in March by standing in his doorway being enormous and delighted about refrigerators, and you never wrote him a line."

"That's — *no.* That's only me."

"Yes," Reggie says. "That's the difference. Yours is you turned up. Mine is me stepped out of."

The fan is off. Pascual looks at him for rather a long time.

"I didn't know that," he says.

"No. You wouldn't. That's more or less the point of having a good one."

"Then tell me first."

"Tell you what?"

"When you're going to do it. A word, a look, I don't care." Pascual pushes off the desk. "Before, not after. Then I'm watching a thing you're doing, instead of a thing happening to you."

Reggie does not answer at once, because what he is feeling is disproportionate and he would like a moment with it before he says anything out loud.

"All right," he says.

"That's it?"

"That's it. It costs me nothing, and it's the first useful thing anybody's suggested about it." He pulls the fourth draft towards him and rules a line through nine-tenths of it. "You're also right about the letter."

What goes to London on the Wednesday is four lines long.

*Dear Sir — I enclose a paper. The plate sequence is the record and the text is commentary upon it; I should be grateful if it could be set that way round. The names stand in order of contribution. Yours faithfully, R. Tar.*

---

The paper goes off on the twenty-first.

*Cohabitation of two specimens of Nereopithecus aestuarius, with observations on captive decline.* Plates 1–34, J. C. Weald, from the plate sequence, running as the primary record and not as illustration; §4, the salinity data and the surfacing intervals of the eighth of June, J. M. Leslie; §5, the histology of Specimen Alpha, P. Pobil, with a note that the material was cut in 1954 from blocks taken in 1952 and that the original necropsy was Miss Leslie's. Reggie's name is on it fourth and on the covering letter alone, because somebody has to sign the covering letter.

John looks at the title page for rather a long time.

"That's the wrong way round," he says.

"It's the right way round. You have the sequence, Jean has the water, Pascual has the tissue, and I have a wrong table and a theory. Fourth is generous."

"People will think you've been pushed out."

"People will think what they like." Reggie takes the sheet back off him and squares it. "It's your record, John. I've spent two years calling it illustration and I was wrong about that as well, and I'd rather be wrong about it in print than at supper."

John does not say anything. He goes rather red, and picks up his portfolio, and goes off down the path to draw Chumley, who is eight pounds up and has recently learned to get a fish out of the bucket, which nobody taught him and which Gamma very plainly did.

---

On the verandah that evening the Colonel opens something he has been keeping.

He pours seven glasses, which he has not done since the night the Institute opened, and he stands up, which is worse, and everybody stops talking.

"Seven weeks ago," he says, "this station lost a specimen and a young man, and had the United States Navy on the jetty at the time. I am aware that I have not been told everything about that, and I have not asked, and I do not intend to."

Nobody moves. Beside Reggie, Pascual has gone extremely still.

"I am told there is a paper going to London on Wednesday with four names on it," the Colonel says, "which is four more than this Institute has managed in two years."

Nobody has touched a glass.

"Two of them are people I should have been advised in 1952 not to put on anything. Mr Weald. Miss Leslie." He raises his glass about an inch. "That's all. Sit down, Tar, you're not being congratulated."

"Sir."

"Hester tells me the pens want re-lining before the November rains and that it will cost about twelve hundred pounds, and that I am going to find it." The Colonel sits down. "So I shall."

And that is the whole of it, and Jean laughs out loud, and Hester looks at the tablecloth with an expression Reggie will not attempt to describe, and the evening goes on being an evening.

---

John gives him the drawing on the Monday, at the pen, without any ceremony whatever, in the way one hands somebody a spare pencil.

"That's yours," he says. "I've got two."

It is a half-sheet, in soft pencil, done from the rail on the night of the fresh water: five men round a fire pump at four in the morning with a torch going, and it is very funny — Juan's patience is in it, and his brother's shoulders, and the pump is drawn like a large animal that has been asked to do something undignified — and at the left of it there are two figures who are not with the others.

One of them is inside the housing to the shoulder. The other one is holding the torch.

John has drawn the torch-beam wrong on purpose or by accident, so that it goes rather wide of the pump. And he has drawn the face above it looking down and not out, and the angle of the shoulders over the man on the ground, and the whole of the rest of the sheet is a joke about a fire pump, and this corner of it is not a joke at all.

Reggie stands there with it in both hands with the sun coming off the concrete.

"It's not numbered," he says.

"No. It's not in the book."

"John —"

"It's a fire pump," John says. He is looking at the water and not at Reggie, and he has his pencil going on the next sheet and has not stopped it once. "It's five men and a fire pump and it's funny and I like it, and I've kept the other one. And if anybody ever asks me about it, it's a fire pump."

Then he says, "You can do what you like with it. Obviously."

---

*Undated,* Reggie thinks, walking up the path with the sheet held flat against his thigh where it cannot be read from either side. *Unnumbered. Not in any sequence. Proves nothing whatever to anybody, and there is exactly one thing it could be used to do.*

There is a burn behind the store twice a week and nobody has ever once looked at it.

He gets as far as the corner of the fuel store, where there are nine drums, the same as there were in May.

He stands there for what he later works out must be four or five minutes.

Then he folds it in half, and in half again, and puts it in his shirt pocket, and goes on up the path.

---

"You will want to say something," he tells Pascual that night, "and I'd like you to take as long over it as you need, because I intend to be extremely annoyed by it and I'd rather it was thorough."

Pascual looks at the drawing. Then he looks at Reggie. Then he sits down slowly on the low chair and puts his hand over his mouth, and his shoulders start going.

"Don't."

"I haven't said one word."

"Your face is saying a paragraph."

"*Ay, Dios mío* —" He has to stop and wipe his eyes with the back of his wrist. "Eight weeks. Eight weeks ago you stood in an office and told me it was all of the cost and none of the value."

"I did."

"And you *burned mine*."

"I burned the study. You wouldn't give me the other one."

"And you haven't given me this one."

"No," Reggie says.

"No." Pascual gets his breath. He is still shining about the eyes and is not troubling to do anything about it. "So. Now we both know what you are, which we didn't before, and I find I don't mind it at all." He holds his hand out. "Give it to me."

"What for?"

"Because I'm going to put it where the other one is, and you're going to let me, and then we're both going to be people who couldn't do it, which is better than one of us standing about being right."

Reggie gives it to him.

It is a great deal harder than he expects and takes about two seconds longer than it should, and Pascual watches him do it and does not say anything, and that is the last of it.

---

"Say something," he says, later.

"You're always wanting me to talk."

"I am aware of what I want. Do it."

So Pascual does, and it is nothing whatever, that is the point of it — the state of the microtome, a slander against the fire pump, something about Marchant and grey seals that turns into something that is not about grey seals at all — low and unhurried against the side of his throat, in two languages, while his hands do what they are doing, and Reggie stops being able to attend to the words at some stage and does not need them.

"Slower."

"I'm going slower."

"You are not, you're —" and then he has to stop, because a hand has gone flat between his shoulder blades and pressed, and his own opinion of the matter has become irrelevant, which is precisely what he came here for.

"Now?"

"Yes. God. Yes."

He is not quiet. He gave up any pretence about that on the first night and has not troubled about it since, and the only concession either of them makes is the bolt and the rain; and there is a period in the middle of it where he can hear his own voice saying a number of things that are perfectly specific and would finish him in any other room in the world, and the man above him is taking each one in turn and doing it, and looking at him the whole time.

"You're watching me."

"I am."

"You've been doing that since May."

"Since February," Pascual says, "and I'm going on, so get used to it," and puts his mouth to the hinge of Reggie's jaw and stays there.

Afterwards Reggie lies with his face against a shoulder and his hand flat on the ribs where the heart is, and counts it, because he cannot help himself, and gets ninety-four and then loses it and does not start again.

"Reggie."

"Mm."

"The Colonel stood up."

"He did."

"He hasn't stood up in two years."

"No."

"He knows," Pascual says.

"He knows something. He's a soldier; he'll know as much as he can use and not an ounce more." Reggie shifts, and gets his arm round properly. "And that's what he was doing on his feet with a glass in his hand. Go to sleep."

"In a minute."

"Now."

"*Quieto*," Pascual says, comfortably, into his hair, and does not go to sleep either.

---

In the morning he is on the verandah at half past five with the tea and the December proof and the word *erroneous* in it, and down at the pens there is a female past breeding condition teaching a four-year-old to get a fish out of a bucket, and John is already at the rail, and Jean is walking down the path with the hydrometer swinging from her hand.

It is the fourteenth week of the wet season and nothing whatever is wrong.

Somewhere south-west of here, four hundred miles from anywhere his own kind lives, there may be a man in the water, or there may not be, and there is no possible way of finding out, and Reggie Tar has stopped putting the question to himself every morning, which took until about the middle of June and which cost him more than the retraction did.

He drinks his tea.

Then he gets up and goes down the path to see what the animals are doing.
