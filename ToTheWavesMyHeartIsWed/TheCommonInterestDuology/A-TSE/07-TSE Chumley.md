# Chumley

The boat is at the jetty at ten to eleven and the boxes are aboard and the manifest is signed in two hands, and there is a moment at the top of the path where Spilsbury stops and turns round and looks back up at the pens with his hat in his hand, and Reggie watches him do it and thinks, with a clarity that arrives too late to be useful: *he is sorry to be going.*

Not sorry about the pen. Sorry to be leaving. He has had four days of the best material of his life and he is being put on a boat.

"Walk down with me," Spilsbury says.

---

He does it in about ninety seconds, on the jetty planks, quietly, with his hat in his hand and the launch idling ten feet away.

"I'll tell you what I think and then I'll get aboard, and you needn't say anything at all, and I'd rather you didn't.

"I think that animal was in good condition on the nineteenth. I don't think it was in crisis and I don't think it died in that pen. I think your anatomist's file stops on the seventeenth because there is nothing on this earth that would make that man write a false line, so somebody took the true ones out instead — and I noticed, and so would anybody, and you had better think about that. I think your illustrator lied to me at breakfast yesterday and did it extremely well and hasn't the least idea what he's got himself into. I think Mrs Bevan could put a document into the Colonial Office and lose it for a hundred years and enjoy doing it.

"And I think Cholmondeley walked that animal down to the water because you told him to, and that he went in after it because he wanted to, and that you knew he would, and that you let him.

"I think you did all of it because you knew what I was coming for."

The launch idles. A pelican goes over.

"And I think you were right," Spilsbury says.

Reggie does not move.

"I can't prove any of it. There's nothing in those boxes that proves any of it and I shan't pretend otherwise in a report, because a report has to be defensible and mine will be." He turns his hat over in his hands. "But you know what I am, Tar, and I know what you are, which is the same thing wearing a different coat, so let us not be coy about the rest of it. I shall go away and I shall think about it. And in about ten years, when there's money again and somebody wants a reason, I shall come back — or somebody very like me will, and he'll be worse, because he won't have known any of you. That is how this always goes and you know it as well as I do."

"Yes," Reggie says.

"Good." He puts the hat on. "You've done something extraordinary here, you know. That pairing is three days old and it is already the only record of its kind anywhere on earth, and you got it on a wet Friday afternoon with a borrowed derrick and eleven men off a fishing boat, while I stood at the rail and watched you do it. I shall be thinking about that for a long time as well."

"Pobil had it."

"Yes," Spilsbury says, and turns, with one foot on the gunwale, and smiles at him, perfectly pleasantly, in front of eleven people. "Do give him my regards. He's a very fine man and you are extremely lucky."

Then he steps down into the launch and sits, and takes his hat off again for the sun.

"Goodbye, Alec," Reggie says.

It is the only time in eleven years he has used it.

---

They stand on the jetty and watch the launch out to the head, the whole station in a row, and nobody says anything.

When it goes round and the engine note drops away, Jean lets out a long breath through her teeth and says, "*Christ.*"

"Miss Leslie," says the Colonel.

"Sorry, sir."

"Quite understandable," says the Colonel, and walks back up the path with his hands behind his back, and Hester goes after him, and at the turn she looks back once at Reggie and gives him about a quarter of a nod.

That is all any of them will ever say about it.

---

He finds John at three, at Gamma's pen, drawing.

The calf is up in the top half of the water for the first time, going round and round in a manner that can only be described as showing off, and Gamma is lying near the surface at the shallow end being enormous and tolerant, and every so often the calf swings past her at speed and she lifts a hand out of his way with the air of a woman moving a teacup.

John has three sheets going at once and pencil all over his face.

"That's not bad," Reggie says.

"It's not finished."

"No. It's still not bad." He puts his forearms on the rail. "The pairing paper. I want the plate sequence to run as the primary record, not as illustration — the whole run, from the day he went in, with your dating. And your name on the plates, first, before mine."

John does not look up. "You already said that."

"I said it on Thursday when I wanted something from you. I'm saying it now when I don't." He watches the calf turn. "There's a difference and you're entitled to it."

The pencil keeps moving for a while.

"He needs a name," Reggie says. "We can't write six years of papers about Specimen Delta and it's bad practice anyway; Charles was right about that and I was wrong, and I told him he was being sentimental. You've drawn him more than anybody. You name him."

That does get John's head up.

"Me."

"You."

John looks at the calf for a long moment. Then he says, "Chumley."

Reggie does not say anything at all for a second or two.

"It's how you say it," John says, defensively, going red. "Chol-mon-de-ley. Chumley. It's — nobody outside would ever — and we'd have to *say* it. Every day. In the log and at supper and in the paper, for thirty years. And nobody would know why except us." His jaw sets. "And I called him Fish Boy for two years, so."

Down in the pen the calf comes up, breaks the surface, blows, and goes down again.

"Chumley," Reggie says. "Enter it in the day book in your own hand, and I'll countersign it."

---

Supper is very nearly a normal supper. The Colonel opens something. Jean laughs so hard at her own story about the salt ponds that she has to put her glass down. Pascual reports that Gamma is up four and a half pounds and gets a round of applause and goes crimson and grumbles about small sample sizes.

Reggie sits at the end of the table and drinks two glasses of wine and does not once do the voice, and finds that his hands are steady for the first time in five days, and that he is looking at a man forty feet away across a verandah with an intensity that would be visible from the sea.

He gets up at half past nine and says goodnight.

At a quarter to ten he is at the sick bay door.

---

"You said Wednesday," Pascual says.

"I did."

"It is Monday."

"Yes."

Pascual shoots the bolt behind him and does not sit down, and neither of them makes any pretence of it. The room smells of formalin and lamp glass. Outside it is warm and black and the generator is going and there is nobody on this island now who is looking at either of them.

"Tell me about Gamma first," Reggie says. "You've been sitting on it since Sunday and I have thought about nothing else for two days."

So Pascual tells him: the corner of the pen, the squared shoulders, the calf jammed into the angle of the wall behind her, a female with no relation to that animal putting her body between him and a stranger at the ladder, at nine days off her own death.

Reggie hears it out and then stands in the middle of the floor and says, "Say the last part again."

He says it again.

"Nobody has that," Reggie says. "Nobody has ever had that. Nobody has ever had two of them in one place to *see* it. That is a protective response between unrelated adults and juveniles, in a species everyone in the field describes as solitary and territorial, and it is the reason Reggie Tar has been wrong for six years and the whole literature with him —" and he is walking, he notices, up and down a room ten feet long, "— and the mortality tables are not mortality tables at all, they're a record of what happens when you put a social animal in a box on its own, and I *built* those tables, I built them and I published them and every man in this field has been reading them for four years as though they described the species and they describe *us* —"

"Reggie."

"And you knew that on Sunday morning at half past five and you sat at my table twice and said nothing —"

"I was saving it," Pascual says, "and you know precisely who I was saving it from, and you are shouting."

Reggie stops.

"Yes," he says. "Sorry."

"Do not be sorry." Pascual is leaning against the bench with his arms folded and his eyes very bright, and he is not hiding a single thing that is happening on his face, and never has been. "That is the first time in a year I have watched you be pleased about something in a room with only me in it. Do it again whenever you like."

---

There is a silence after that, of a slightly different kind.

"He told me he'd come back," Reggie says. "In about ten years. Or somebody like him and worse."

"Yes."

"You don't seem surprised."

"I am not surprised." Pascual gets the bottle down and finds the beaker and pours about an inch and gives it to him. "And I am going to say one thing about that man and then we will be finished with him tonight. On Saturday you told me he was perhaps a person who does not know what lands."

"Yes."

"That is the only stupid thing you have said to me all week." He says it quite gently. "He knew what he was doing at that table. He knew when he read the Andersen out in that hut with the boy standing there. He knew at half past five in the morning when he got into my pen after two men had told him not to, and he knew exactly what he made that animal do, and I will not forgive him for it if I live to be ninety. He is not a man who blunders, Reggie. He is a man who has decided that finding out is worth whatever it costs, and he has been that way a long time, and you have been telling yourself the other story for eleven years because it is easier to have nearly kissed a fool than a man who knows what he is."

Reggie stands there with an inch of rum in a beaker.

"And I told him," Pascual says. "On Sunday. He asked me straight out whether the animal died and I said yes, and he looked at my face while I said it, and he had it. That is what I have been carrying since Sunday and I would rather you had it from me."

"I know," Reggie says. "I knew on Sunday night. It's why he stopped asking anybody anything."

"You *knew*—"

"I knew there was nothing to be done about it, and that you'd tell me when you could." He puts the beaker down on the bench without drinking any of it. "Pascual. Sit down. I'm going to tell you about the labs and I would rather do it now than think about it for another two days."

---

He tells him.

He does it standing up, in the end, because he finds he cannot do it sitting, with his hands behind his back and his eyes on the dark square of the window, in the flattest voice he has: what the place was, and what it was for, and what he did there for four years, and what he was very good at, and what he went on being good at for eleven months after the point at which any decent man would have stopped.

It takes about a quarter of an hour. He does not soften any of it and he does not explain any of it, because there is no explanation, there is only the fact that he was curious and that being curious was enough. He hears his own voice going on and on in a small hot room and he keeps waiting for it to break and it does not break, which is somehow the worst part; it comes out level and ordered and complete, like a paper being read aloud, because he has been rehearsing it silently for eleven years and never once said a word of it out loud to a living soul.

At the end he stops, and the generator goes on doing what it does, and he stands there with his back to the room because he cannot at any price turn round.

There is a very long silence.

Then Pascual says, "How many?"

Reggie tells him that too.

Another silence, shorter.

"Turn round," Pascual says.

He turns round.

Pascual is sitting on the low chair with his elbows on his knees and his hands together and his face — his terrible, legible, hopeless face — showing everything, and what is on it is not horror and it is not pity and it is worse than either, because it is grief, and none of it is for Reggie.

"I am not going to forgive you for that," he says. "I could not if I wanted to. I am not one of the people who could, and some of the people who could are not anywhere at all now, and you know that better than I do."

"Yes."

"So I will not insult you with it." He stands up. "That is the first thing.

"The second thing is that you have carried it about for eleven years by yourself and made yourself into a man who cannot be in a room, and every single person on this station thinks you are cold, and I have watched you be looked at by all of them for a year and thought it as well." He crosses the floor. "And you have just handed the whole of it to me in a quarter of an hour, standing up, without one word of excuse, on the day you had the chance to say nothing at all. Do you know how few men could do that? I have never met one. Not one."

"That is not a virtue —"

"I did not say it was a virtue, I said it was a *fact*, and I am going to have my say and then you may argue." He has Reggie by the front of the shirt now, both fists, not hard. "Here is the third thing. I am not going anywhere. Not tonight and not because of that and not ever, and if you have been standing there for a quarter of an hour waiting for me to open that door and put you through it, then you can stop, because it is not going to happen, and if you make me say it again I shall be extremely angry."

Reggie discovers that he cannot see very well.

"Say it again," he says.

"*Ay, Dios* — I am not going anywhere," Pascual says, and kisses him.

---

It is not like the first night.

The first night was eleven months of arithmetic coming due all at once, in a hurry, against a wall. This is something else. This is a man who has just put the worst of himself down on the floor of a room and not been made to leave it, and there is a period of several minutes during which Reggie is not entirely in command of himself and is held through it and does not have to be.

"Come here. Come *here* —"

"I am here."

"You're not — " His hands are shaking. He gets one of them into Pascual's hair, at the back, and holds on. "Say something."

"What shall I say?"

"Anything. Keep talking. I don't care what it is."

So Pascual talks — into his mouth, into his throat, into the side of his neck, half of it in English and half of it not, and Reggie catches perhaps one word in three and does not need the rest, because the register is the whole of the message: it is the voice the man uses to a frightened animal, low and rough and absolutely unhurried, and Reggie has watched it work on a mermaid, and it works on him too, and he is exactly as insulted by this as he is undone by it.

"You are shaking."

"I'm aware."

"Good," Pascual says, and gets him by the hips and moves him, and there is nothing gentle in it at all, and that turns out to be what he needed.

The bolt goes across on the inner door. Eight feet by ten and an iron cot and one high window, and the rain begins again on the roof while Reggie is getting a wet shirt over a man's head and forgetting entirely which of them it belongs to. He puts his mouth on the winch scar under the collarbone because it is his now, apparently, that is a thing he is allowed. He gets his hand flat over the ribs where the heart is going and holds it there and is looked at while he does it.

"You're not to be careful."

"I am never careful."

"You are always careful, that's the entire —"

And Pascual takes both his wrists in one hand and puts them up over his head against the iron of the cot; and then, with the other hand, unhurried, moves the folded shirt under him so that the rail is not against the back of his head; and then puts his mouth at the join of Reggie's neck and shoulder and does something there that is not gentle in the slightest.

The sentence stops in the middle of a word.

"You were saying," Pascual says, into his throat.

Reggie says something. It is not English and it is not Spanish and it is not, strictly speaking, a word, and he does not try the sentence again for some time.

Afterwards there is a great deal of noise from the roof and none from anywhere else, and he is lying half across a man who is going heavier by the minute, with his face against a shoulder, thinking about nothing whatever, which is a condition he can remember experiencing perhaps four times in his life.

"Reggie."

"Mm."

"On Sunday," Pascual says, into his hair, "I told you it would do for thirty years."

"Yes."

"You did not say anything about that at the time."

There is a pause during which the rain does all the work.

"No," Reggie says. "I heard it."

"Ah."

"I've thought about very little else since." He shifts, and gets his arm round properly, and shuts his eyes. "We'll come back to it."

"*Bueno.*"

"Not tonight."

"No," Pascual agrees comfortably. "Not tonight. We are a pair of people who put things in a drawer."

"We are," Reggie says. "It's an excellent drawer. There's a great deal in it."

And Pascual laughs — down low, so that Reggie feels it more than hears it, all the way through his own ribs — and puts a hand on the back of his neck and leaves it there, and the rain goes on, and out at the pens two animals who are not alone move about in the dark, and a mile east of the island there is nothing to be found, and nobody is looking.
