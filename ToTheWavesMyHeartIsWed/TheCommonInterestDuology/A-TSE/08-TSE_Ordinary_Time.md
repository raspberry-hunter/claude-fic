# Ordinary Time

At ten past five on the Tuesday there is nothing whatever for him to do.

He notices it at the rail with the torch off. Gamma is at the shallow end with the calf tucked in against her ribs and one hand loose across him, and both of them are asleep, and neither of them is dying. There is no sample wanted at dawn. There is no bicarbonate to get into anything. There is no boy on a pad on the concrete with his hand trailing in the water and his glasses folded on his chest.

He goes back up the path and makes tea and sits on the step of the sick bay and drinks it while the light comes, and thinks that this is what the work is meant to be like, and that he is not certain he cares for it.

At six he goes down again and takes the residue out with the net, and weighs it, and writes the figure in the book.

Nobody asked him to. There is a column for it because a postgraduate with terrible handwriting decided four months ago that somebody ought to be measuring what came out as well as what went in, and Pascual has been filling it in himself since Thursday, because if he does not fill it in then the column stops. He has looked at a column that stops, recently, and he did not care for that either.

---

Washing day is Friday. He has been getting round it since the twentieth of May by the simple method of wearing other shirts.

There are four pages in the pocket. They have been through rain and sweat and the inside of a pen and they have gone soft at the folds like cloth, and on the Friday morning he sits on his cot with the shirt across his knees and takes them out and lays them on the blanket, and looks at them for a while, and then gets up and goes to find Reggie — because it turns out he does not want to do this on his own, which is a piece of information about himself he has acquired within the last fortnight.

Reggie is in the office with the door open and the fan off, because the fan makes the papers go. He looks up. He looks at Pascual's face, and then at his hand.

"Shut the door," he says.

"It's a hot day."

"Shut it anyway and I'll open the shutter."

Pascual shuts it. Reggie opens the shutter and comes round the desk and clears a space with his forearm, and Pascual lays the four sheets out in a row and smooths them flat with the side of his hand, and does not say anything, because there is nothing to say that the paper does not say better.

Two of them are his own: the tenth and the eleventh days. Weight up nine pounds. Surfacing intervals four and five minutes. Water clear. *No stiffness observed. Animal playing.*

One is loose and unnumbered and came out of the wallet at the back of the portfolio: a working study, half a sheet, a hand and a jaw and the waterline and nothing else finished. John drew the thing twice, the way a man does when he is finding out whether it can be true.

And one is plate 214 itself.

Reggie stands over it for a long moment with his knuckles on the desk.

"He wasn't there," he says.

"No."

"He was thirty feet off and behind a rail."

"And sent away. He drew it afterwards, in the hut, out of what he worked out."

"From thirty feet and a rail."

"He's got the water at the right height," Pascual says. "And he's put the boy's spectacles on the rail behind, which is where they'd have been." He clears his throat. "I've had it in a shirt for a fortnight."

Reggie looks at it a while longer.

Then he straightens up and says, "Burn it."

"*You.*"

"Me."

"A fortnight ago you stood in that hut with four hundred pages in your two hands and told me you can't burn a result because it's awkward —"

"That was a record," Reggie says. "This is a picture."

"It is the *same boy* —"

"It isn't data, Pascual. It's undated, it's unnumbered, it's not from life, and the man who drew it says so himself."

"It's a record of —"

"Of nothing. There isn't one thing in the world it could be used to prove." He has got his glasses off and is pointing with them, which is what he does instead of raising his voice. "And there's one thing it could be used to do, which is hang Hester and John and you and me off the same rope."

"I know what it could do."

"Then you know it's all of the cost and none of the value. I'd keep your two in a bank and I'd put the other pair on the fire tonight."

The fan is off. Outside, somebody is doing something to a boat.

"You're right," Pascual says.

"Then give them to me."

He gives him the study. He watches it go into the office grate with a match under it, and it takes about four seconds, and he finds he can stand there and watch it and feel nothing much at all.

Then Reggie holds his hand out for the other one.

"No," Pascual says.

Reggie stops.

"I said you're right, and you are, and I'm not arguing with a word of it." He is aware his voice has gone; he does nothing about it. "I can't. That's all."

"Pascual —"

"I've been on my own cot with it since six, and I had a fortnight before that, and I can't put it in a fire, and I'd like you to stop looking at me as though there's a better answer coming."

"I wasn't looking at you like that."

"You were looking at me exactly like that."

"I was thinking," Reggie says, "that being right at you has a very poor record, and that I keep doing it, and that it's beginning to look like a defect in my method."

Pascual snorts, which he had not intended to do.

"Hester," he says.

"Yes." Reggie puts his glasses back on. "Take the other two while you're at it, and tell her I said so, or she'll have them off you anyway and enjoy it more."

---

She receives him at half past seven on the Saturday with the tea already poured, which by now he understands is not hospitality but a way of saying *I know why you have come and you may sit down*.

He puts the two file sheets on the table first and explains them in about four sentences. She takes them without comment and puts them under the blotter, and he thinks: *so that is where they are going to live, then, under a blotter, in a house with a Colonel in it* — and finds he minds a great deal less than he expected to.

Then he puts plate 214 in front of her.

Hester Bevan looks at it.

She does not put her cup down. She looks at it for what must be half a minute, and does not move at all, and Pascual — who reads faces for a living, and has had nothing out of this one in fifteen months that she did not intend him to have — sits in a chintz chair at half past seven in the morning and watches a woman of sixty-odd be looked at by a drawing.

"I've never seen one," she says.

"Ma'am?"

"A picture." She says it perfectly steadily. "There's the Andersen, and the Wilde, and a stanza of LaMotte, and I've had all three by heart since I was nineteen. Words. Anybody at all could have made up words." She turns the sheet a very little, to the light. "Nobody has ever drawn it."

"No."

"It's the right height. The water. That's exactly where it comes to."

"Yes," Pascual says.

"Yes," she says.

She puts her cup down then, and puts her hand flat on the table beside the drawing, the way she did in this room on the twenty-first when she asked Reggie whether the boy was alive; and after a moment she takes it away again and picks the sheet up by two corners.

"Does Reginald know you've brought me this?"

"He told me to burn it."

"He would. He'd be right, too."

"He would be right," Pascual says, "and I've told him so."

"And he sent you here instead." She is already at the door. "That's the only useful thing anybody has told me this week."

She goes out with the drawing held in both hands, and comes back some minutes later without it, and pours him another cup he has not asked for.

---

He picks the other thing up on the Sunday, in the store, among the boxes, because he has been carrying that one for a fortnight too, and it is lighter, and one leaves the lighter thing till last.

"Alpha," he says.

Reggie is on the second rung of the steps with the inventory board. "What about him?"

"On the night of the nineteenth you came into my sick bay wet through and ate my bread and asked whether Jean's blocks were still in the store. And when I asked you why, you said *no reason*."

"I did."

"I put it down within reach. I have been reaching for it for a fortnight. Why did you want them?"

Reggie comes down the steps.

"Because at about half past nine that evening I worked out I was going to need a dead mermaid by morning and hadn't got one."

"And?"

"And it occurred to me there were two boxes of one in the store, with another animal's name going spare to put on the labels."

"*Ay.*"

"Yes."

"And at seven the next morning you asked me to do it, and I said no, and you dropped it inside four seconds."

"You gave me a better reason than I had," Reggie says. "Also you were extremely frightening."

"I was not frightening, I was *right* —"

"You were both. It's a thing you do." He sets the board down on a crate. "And I didn't drop it, as it happens. I dropped the labels."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning I've wanted those boxes open since Wednesday, and I've been working out how to ask you without it sounding like the same request."

Pascual looks at him.

"Ask badly," he says. "I'll cope."

"Right." Reggie takes his glasses off. "If Gamma was dying of solitude, then Alpha died of solitude. And Jean wrote *pneumonia* on him because pneumonia is what she found."

"Pneumonia is what everybody finds."

"Pneumonia is what everybody has always found, and I've got four years of a table built on the assumption that what everybody found is what killed them."

"Ah."

"So it's the last event and not the cause. I want to know what was going on in that animal for the four months before it."

"You want the adrenals."

Reggie's head comes up so fast that the glasses swing in his hand.

"There's a Canadian," Pascual says. "Montreal. He's had rats under every miserable condition a man can devise for a rat — cold, restraint, the lot."

"And?"

"And they come out the same on the table, every time. Adrenals up, thymus down, ulcers in the stomach. It doesn't matter what you did to them." He shrugs. "The body's only got the one way of being badly treated for a long while."

"When did you read this?"

"Two years ago. I thought it was interesting and I'd nothing to try it on."

"And now you've got Alpha in two boxes."

"I've got Alpha in two boxes, and Jean's notes, and she weighed the organs, because she's Jean."

"You'd want the weights out of the book."

"I'd have them in a book with nobody's name anywhere near it." He is talking faster than he means to. "It isn't proof. One animal is one animal."

"But."

"But if that cortex is twice the depth it's any business being, and the thymus is gone in a four-year-old, then what killed Alpha was four months in a box on his own, and pneumonia was the door he went out by."

There is a silence in the store of a quality he has come to look forward to.

"Cut it," Reggie says.

"I'll want the microtome."

"The microtome is a disgrace."

"The microtome is a *disgrace*, and I'll want two weeks, and I'll want Jean — they're her blocks, her hands did the gross, and she'll have half of it in her head and not in the book."

"Take her."

"And what'll you be doing?"

"Writing to the *Journal of Mammalogy*," Reggie says, "to tell them my mortality tables describe my own arrangements and not the animal."

"*Ay, Dios.*"

"It'll be the least pleasant afternoon of my professional life, and I want it done before you hand me a figure. If I do it afterwards it looks as though I was made to."

"You could wait for the figure."

"I could."

"It's better science with the figure in it."

"It's better arithmetic with the figure in it," Reggie says. "It's better *manners* without."

And he picks the inventory board up and goes back up the steps, and Pascual stands among two boxes of a dead animal and finds that he is grinning at a ladder.

---

The rains come in earnest on the Thursday night, and by midnight it is not rain, it is weather.

He is woken at twenty to one by Jean hammering on the sick-bay door with the flat of her hand, in oilskins, with a torch and a hydrometer and her hair plastered to her head.

"The basin," she says. "Have you looked at the basin?"

"I looked at it at ten."

"Look at it now."

He looks at it at ten to one, in the rain, with Reggie arriving from the other direction pulling a jersey over his head and his glasses already useless. The whole back of the cay is running. It comes off the ridge and off the roofs and off the concrete apron and goes down the old slipway into the seaplane basin in a sheet, and the basin feeds the pens.

Jean has the hydrometer on a line. She drops it, reads it by torchlight, hauls it up, and says a word he has not previously heard her say.

"Top eighteen inches is under five," she says. "It was thirty-four this afternoon."

"How long does it stay?"

And Jean — soaked, at one in the morning, with the water coming off the end of her nose — takes a breath and answers the question she has spent two years being able to answer.

"It sits. That's the whole thing about fresh on salt: it doesn't mix itself, it lies on top like oil, and it'll lie there for days if nothing shifts it. Pond Three does it every wet season and I've got two years of counts on it. The top foot goes to nothing inside four hours of a real rain and the bottom doesn't move at all. The only things that break it are a hard wind down the long axis or the tide getting under it — and the pens haven't got a long axis, and the gate's been shut since the Navy came."

"Then open the gate," Reggie says.

"Not at the top of the tide, you'll put more on."

"At the ebb."

"At the ebb it drains over the sill, and the sill's high, and what goes over a high sill is the top of the water. Which is the part we want rid of."

"When's the ebb?"

"Twenty past four."

"Three and a half hours," says Pascual.

"Three and a half hours."

"He has a raw flank."

Nobody says anything for a second, because all three of them know what three and a half hours of fresh water does to an open lesion on an estuarine animal, and two of them could give the histology.

"Then we mix it ourselves," Reggie says. "Where's the fire pump?"

---

The fire pump is behind the store under a tarpaulin. It was made in 1943 for the United States Navy and it has not been asked to do anything since.

It takes the two of them, and Juan, and Juan's brother, and two lengths of hose that are the wrong lengths, and forty minutes.

Reggie ends up inside the housing. This is not a figure of speech; he gets the cowling off and goes in as far as his shoulder with a spanner between his teeth, in the rain, in a jersey, five feet six of senior scientist with his arm in a wartime pump up to the elbow and paraffin running down inside his sleeve — and he is, Pascual observes, with the torch in one hand and water going down inside his own collar, completely and visibly happy.

"Hold the light *still*."

"I am holding it still. It's the rain that's moving."

"The rain isn't moving the light, Pascual, you are moving the light —"

"Then you hold the light and I'll do the spanner."

"You can't get your arm in."

"I can get my arm in."

"You cannot get that arm into this housing. It's an arm for lifting animals —"

"*Dale*," says Juan, out of the dark, in a tone of great patience, and reaches past the pair of them and does something with his thumb, and the pump starts.

It runs for four minutes and thirty seconds, throws a fitting off the delivery side, and empties about ninety gallons of the seaplane basin over the three men standing nearest, one of whom is holding the torch.

There is a silence.

"Well," Reggie says, from somewhere inside it.

Pascual gets the torch up.

Reggie Tar is sitting on the wet concrete against the wheel of the pump with his legs straight out in front of him, black to the shoulder down one side, glasses gone entirely opaque, hair flat to his skull — and he is laughing. Not the dry noise he makes at the table, not the one-syllable thing he does when Jean says something cutting: properly, helplessly, with his head back against the tyre, the way a man laughs at four in the morning when he has been up for twenty hours and something has just come off in his hand.

Pascual stands in the rain and holds the torch on him and does not say a single word, because he would like this to go on.

---

At half past three, having got nowhere, he goes down to the pen to see how bad it is.

It is not bad at all.

Gamma is on the bottom at the deep end. The calf is under her arm against the wall, six feet down, well beneath the fresh; and every three or four minutes she takes him up to the surface with one hand at the back of his neck, and he blows, and she has him down again inside two seconds. Up, blow, down — the whole thing quicker than a man could do it — and then she settles again in the dark water at the bottom and waits.

He crouches at the rail with the torch off and watches her do it nine times.

Then he goes and gets Reggie and Jean, and the three of them crouch at the rail in the rain and watch her do it a dozen times more, and nobody says anything at all until Jean whispers, "She's holding him under."

"She's holding him *out of it*," Pascual says. "She's got the same reading you've got, and she got it two hours before you did."

"With what?"

"Her skin, probably. Ask me in a year." He wipes his face with his hand, which achieves nothing. "Somebody take the interval. For the love of God, has anybody got a watch that isn't full of water?"

Jean has a watch that is not full of water. Jean writes it on the inside of a cigarette packet, holding it under her oilskin, in the rain, at four o'clock in the morning: surfacing interval, assisted — three minutes forty, three minutes twenty, four minutes.

At twenty past four the tide turns and Juan cracks the gate, and the top of the basin goes over the sill into the sea for the best part of two hours; and by seven the hydrometer says nineteen at eighteen inches and climbing, and by noon it says thirty-one.

The calf's flank is unchanged. Pascual has it dry-dressed twice that day anyway, with Gamma lying alongside within arm's reach the whole time, watching him do it and not interfering, which is a thing he also writes down.

---

They sleep most of Friday, in shifts, like men off a boat.

Jean comes to the sick bay at four in the afternoon with her hair still wet and a notebook, and puts the notebook down, and says: "I want the salinity work written up with the pairing paper. As a section of its own. Mine."

"Yes."

"I haven't put it to Reggie."

"Put it to him. He'll say yes before you've finished the sentence, and then he'll ask you for the pond data for the next thirty years — so decide what you want out of him first and say it while he's still pleased."

Jean looks at him for a moment with her head on one side.

"You've got him worked out," she says.

"I've got *nobody* worked out."

"Mm," says Jean, and picks up her notebook, and stops at the door. "Pascual. I'm not going to ask you anything."

"I know."

"I want that on the record. Not about the boy, not about the chart, not about any of it." She is not looking at him. "I did what I did and I'd do it again, and I don't want to be told whether it came off."

"All right."

"And neither of you is to thank me, either. John says you've all been trying to thank him and he's found it unbearable."

"He said that?"

"He said it was *worse than the marmalade*," Jean says, "which I've decided not to ask about," and goes out.

---

John takes the residue column back on the Monday.

He does not make anything of it. He comes down at six with his portfolio under his arm and his sleeves already rolled, takes the net off the hook, and says, "I'll do the weights — you've got a microtome to swear at." He does it wrong twice and then correctly, and writes the figure in the book in his round hand under four months of a small terrible one, and does not say a word about whose column it is.

Pascual leaves him to it. Three-quarters of the way up the path he turns and looks back, and the boy is sitting on the rail with the book on his knee and the pencil going, drawing the pair of them in the early light, with his tongue between his teeth.

---

It is the second Monday, the seventh of June, when Reggie tells him what Spilsbury said on the jetty.

He does it in the sick bay at about ten at night with his shoes off, sitting on the bench with his back to the wall and a beaker on his knee, and he says it the way he says everything he has been carrying: all at once, flat, and a fortnight late.

"*Do give him my regards. He's a very fine man and you are extremely lucky.*"

Pascual stops what he is doing.

"In front of eleven people," Reggie says. "With one foot on the gunwale. Smiling."

"And you've had that in your pocket since the twenty-fourth of May."

"I have."

"*Ay, Dios.* Why?"

"Because for the first four days I couldn't decide whether it was a threat."

"And after that?"

"After that I decided it wasn't. Then I spent a week and a half not able to work out why I minded so much that it wasn't."

"And now you've got it."

"I got there yesterday, and I'd rather tell you now than have you find it in me in six months."

"Then tell me."

"He liked you." Reggie turns the beaker round on his knee. "He was on that jetty having lost every single thing he came for, and he stopped to tell me I'd done well for myself. He meant it. It's the only sentence he said to me in four days I'm sure he meant."

Pascual puts down the flask.

He had expected to be frightened. He has been braced for a fortnight to be frightened, and had got as far as working out what he would say to keep this man from doing anything stupid about it; and now the thing is in the room, and what he feels is not fear at all.

"Well," he says. "He's right."

"Pascual —"

"He *is* right, and I'm not spending the next thirty years frightened of the only accurate remark the man made." He takes the beaker out of Reggie's hand, drinks some of it, and gives it back. "He can see it. All right."

"That is not a small thing —"

"So can Hester. So can Jean, I should think, and John'll get there by Christmas, and not one of them is going to a police sergeant in Kingston about it."

"That isn't the same as the world seeing it."

"No. It isn't. And you know that better than I do." He picks the flask back up. "But I'd rather be looked at by a man who's seen us than spend a fortnight being managed by one who hasn't."

Reggie does not say anything.

"Say it in a sentence," Pascual says. "Do you want to stop?"

"No."

"Then that's the whole of it."

"That," Reggie says, "is the second time you've asked me that, and I notice you keep the question in the same box you keep the bread in."

"It's a good box."

---

"Thirty years," Reggie says, some while later, into the dark.

Pascual has very nearly gone under. "Mm."

"You said it on the Sunday. In this room. And I said we'd come back to it, and then I didn't, for a fortnight. Which you'll have noticed."

"I noticed."

"I've been doing the arithmetic."

Pascual opens his eyes.

"I'm afraid," Reggie says, in exactly the voice he uses for reading a figure off a page, "that I went to the shelf in the office and got down the *Registrar-General's Statistical Review*, and looked up the expectation of life for males in England and Wales, and did the sums — and then did them again on the Thursday against the American tables, because I wanted a second source. Sixty-five for me. Seventy-one for you. Thirty years takes us both there and leaves a margin at your end, and I've allowed for the fact that you eat like a Spaniard and drink like a sailor, which is worth something; and against that you've been bitten by a very great many animals."

There is a silence.

"You looked up *mortality tables*," Pascual says.

"I did."

"For us."

"I did."

"You. The man who built the mortality tables. You went and got a book down off a shelf and worked out how long I —"

And then he cannot finish it, because he has started laughing and it has got hold of him somewhere below the ribs, and he has to sit up on the edge of the cot with his face in his hands and his shoulders going, in the dark, at eleven o'clock at night; and it goes on for some time, and turns partway through into something that is not entirely laughing. Reggie puts a hand flat between his shoulder blades and leaves it there and does not say one word about it, which is a mercy he has not earned and takes anyway.

"*Thirty years*," Pascual says at last, wetly.

"Thirty-one, if you're careful."

"You appalling man."

"You said it first." Reggie's voice has gone quiet. "You said it at me across a room in a voice like a bill being presented, and then went straight on to something else."

"I did."

"And I've carried it about ever since like a stone in a shoe. I'd like it now, please. Properly. With both of us awake."

Pascual turns round.

In the dark the man is propped on one elbow and he can just make out the shape of him; and he thinks about a wet shirt, and a stool going over backwards, and fifteen months of taking his hands off doors.

"Thirty years," he says. "Yes. All right. *Sí.* Come here."

---

And it is the ordinariness of it that undoes him.

Nobody is coming. That is the whole of what has changed, and it changes everything: there is no boat in the morning, there is no lamp burning at the end hut, there is nothing to be got through — and so there is no reason on God's earth to hurry, and he finds he does not know what to do with that, and has to be shown.

Reggie shows him. Reggie is not shy about any of this and never has been; he is the most articulate man Pascual has ever had his hands on, and he uses it, flat and dry and specific, in the same voice he uses to ask for a figure. *There. Do that again, slower.* The first time he did it Pascual nearly had to leave the room. Now it goes through him like the first mouthful of anything hot.

"Say that again."

"I said slower."

"You did not say slower. You said —"

"I know precisely what I said," Reggie says, "and so do you, and I'd be obliged if you'd act on it instead of conducting a review."

So he acts on it.

He takes his time over the throat, which is thin and takes a mark and always has, and over the jaw, and the bone behind the ear; and Reggie's hands come up into his hair and shut there, and there is a noise he makes when it is the back of the neck that Pascual has been hearing in his sleep since the twentieth of May. He puts his mouth to the inside of the wrist, where the pulse is, and holds it there long enough to count, because he is what he is.

"*What* are you doing?"

"Eighty-eight."

Reggie laughs, and then stops laughing rather abruptly.

Shoulders. The hollow under the collarbone. The ribs, which are too near the surface, and which he intends to do something about over the next thirty years, one meal at a time.

"You're going to tell me to eat."

"I'm not saying one word about eating."

"You're *thinking* it. You've gone quiet in a particular way —"

"I'm thinking," Pascual says, into his sternum, "that you've the ribcage of a bird and the constitution of a goat, that it isn't fair, and that I'd like you quiet for about a minute."

"Make me."

So he does that, too.

There is a point, some while after, with his hand spread flat on the small of Reggie's back holding him exactly where he is, and Reggie's forehead down against his shoulder and the breath going out of him in pieces, when Reggie says his name — not the noise, the name, the whole of it, in a register that does not get used at a table — and says, "*don't stop*," and then, unbelievably, "please."

Pascual has to hold entirely still for a second with his eyes shut.

"I'm here."

"I know. Don't —"

"I'm not going anywhere. I'm here. *Quieto.* I've got you."

And goes on, and it is not gentle, and it is not hurried; and he watches the whole time, because that face gives him almost nothing in daylight and gives him everything in this room, and he isn't going to waste any of it.

Afterwards Reggie lies half across him, going heavier by the minute, with one hand flat on Pascual's ribs over the heart where it can be felt, and does not move it.

The rain has stopped. The generator is doing what it does. Somewhere down the path two animals who are not alone move about in the dark, and every three minutes and forty seconds one of them takes the other up to the surface and brings him back down again.

"Pascual."

"Mm."

"The adrenals."

"*Ay, no.* I'm not discussing adrenals."

"When will you have the sections?"

"Thursday. If the microtome behaves, and if you go to sleep."

"Right," Reggie says, and does not go to sleep.

And Pascual lies in the dark with a thin arm across his chest, listening to the man beside him not going to sleep, because he is thinking about a four-year-old animal that died in a box in 1952; and finds that he is entirely content, and that he would not hand any part of this back.

---

On the Thursday the sections come off the microtome cleanly at six microns, which they have no right to do.

The adrenal cortex is very nearly twice the depth it should be in an animal of that age. The thymus, in a four-year-old, is a scrap of fat and fibrous tissue with almost nothing left in it at all.

Pascual sits back from the eyepieces and takes his glasses off and rubs his eyes with the heel of his hand. Then he gets up and goes to the door of the sick bay and stands in the sun, looking down the path towards the pens, where a female past breeding condition is lying near the surface with a calf going round and round her showing off, and John is sitting on the rail drawing the pair of them with pencil all over his face.

Then he goes to find Reggie, who is at the office with a letter to the *Journal of Mammalogy* in front of him and has not written a word of it since Tuesday.

"Come and look down my microscope," Pascual says.
