# Plate 214

At ten past five he is at Gamma's pen in the dark with a torch, because he has been awake since four and there is only one thing on this island he wants to look at.

They are both on the bottom, at the shallow end, about a foot apart.

He crouches at the rail and watches for a long time with the torch off. Gamma has not slept off the bottom in nine days; she has slept jammed into the corner with her tail curled under her like something spoiled in a tin. Tonight she is out from the wall. The calf is beside her with one hand loose on her forearm, the way a child sleeps with a fistful of its mother's sleeve, and every so often one of them makes a small sound and the other one moves.

*Ay.* He puts the back of his wrist against his eyes for a second, which is ridiculous, and he is forty-one years old, and there is nobody here.

Sixteen hours ago that animal was dying and today she is not. He has been a veterinarian for nineteen years and he has been given perhaps four mornings like this one, and this is the best of them, and he is going to hold on to it for as long as he possibly can, because he has a suspicion about how the rest of the day is going to go.

At half past six the light comes up grey and pink, and John comes down the path with his easel and his stool and his box, whistling, at an hour when John has never in his life been awake.

"Morning," John says. "Dr Spilsbury wants to see the whole portfolio after lunch. Everything, all of it, right back to Alpha." He is setting up the easel with his tongue between his teeth. "He says the plate record is the most valuable thing on the station. He says people don't understand what a sequence is *worth*."

"Mm," Pascual says.

"So I'm getting the pairing down first thing, and then I'll go through the book and put it in order, because some of it's loose —"

"Mm," Pascual says.

He walks away up the path at an ordinary speed, and gets round the corner of the fuel store, and stops, and puts his hand flat on the corrugated iron.

*He draws the animal every other day. He dates them. He numbers them.*

And on the eighteenth and the nineteenth of May, when Pascual Pobil's clinical file has nothing in it at all, John was sitting at that rail with a pencil, putting down an animal in condition.

---

He finds Reggie at seven, shaving, in the doorway of his own room with a mirror hung on a nail.

Pascual says it in about eleven words. He watches the razor stop.

There is a silence of maybe four seconds, and in it he can see the man work, which is a thing he has begun to enjoy watching in a way he intends to think about at some later date when he has an hour to spare.

"Numbered," Reggie says.

"Numbered and dated and he is putting the book in order this morning to show that man after lunch."

"Then it can't go missing." He wipes the razor on the towel over his shoulder. "Nothing can go missing from a numbered sequence. It has to go somewhere, and it has to go somewhere on paper, and the paper has to be older than the question." He is talking to the mirror and not to Pascual at all now. "The launch goes at noon."

"Yes."

"Hester."

"Yes," Pascual says. "That was my thought also."

Reggie turns round with half his face still lathered and looks at him for a moment with an expression that Pascual can only describe as *appetite*, and which he would very much like to do something about, and cannot, because it is seven in the morning and there are sixteen strangers on this island and one of them is awake at the end hut with his lamp still burning.

---

Hester Bevan receives them in the sitting room at half past seven with the tea already poured, which means Jean has been and gone.

Reggie explains it in the flattest voice he has: that the report on the loss of Specimen Beta and the presumed death of Mr Cholmondeley goes out with the Commander's launch at noon, that the Colonial Secretary's office will want the visual record appended as it is the only pictorial documentation of the specimen's condition, and that the plates from the final week ought therefore to be enclosed.

Hester looks at him over the pot.

"Which plates," she says.

"The eighteenth and the nineteenth."

"Only those."

"Only those."

She does not ask why. She pours a third cup nobody has asked for and puts it in front of Pascual, who takes it, and she says: "The Colonial Secretary's office will file them with the quarterly fisheries return, where they will be seen by a clerk and nobody else, and where they will remain, and where a man who wanted them would have to make an application in writing which I would hear about within the week." She sets the pot down. "I'll have John bring me the book at eight."

"Thank you," Reggie says.

"Reginald." She has not raised her voice at all. "Is the boy alive?"

The room gets very quiet.

"I don't know," Reggie says.

"But he wasn't dead when he went in."

"No."

Hester Bevan puts her hand flat on the table beside the tray, and looks at it, and after a moment takes it away again.

"Thank you," she says. "That's all I wanted."

And Pascual, sitting there with a cup he did not ask for, understands something about that woman that he has not previously understood, and has the sense not to look at her while he understands it.

---

He gets the portfolio to himself at nine, in the sick bay, with the door open because a closed door is a thing people remember.

He works fast, because John has been told the plates are wanted for the report and John will be back for his book inside the hour, and because there is a man on this island who reads everything properly.

Plate 209. *Specimen Beta, 18.v.54, a.m.* The animal on his side at the surface with his weight back on him, and the shoulder muscle drawn in with that soft blunt thumb-work John does, and in the corner, small, a study of one hand. It is a picture of an animal in excellent condition and it is dated the day before the day he died of renal failure.

Plate 212. *19.v.54.* Worse. He is *playing*. John has caught him mid-roll with the water coming off him, and he has written along the bottom in his round hand, *very lively, wd not keep still*.

Pascual sits and looks at that for slightly longer than he can afford.

He takes them both out, squares them, and puts them in the buff envelope Hester has sent down with her own hand on the front, and seals it, and it is done, and it took forty seconds and it was easy, which is a thing he is going to have to have some sort of opinion about eventually.

Then, because he is a thorough man and because he intends to know exactly what is in that book before an outsider does, he goes back through the rest of it.

And on the third-last leaf he finds plate 214.

---

It is undated. That is the first thing he notices, because everything else in the book is dated, and the second thing is that it is not drawn from the rail.

It is drawn from inside. From the water, from a yard away, at eye level: a man and a mermaid, face to face, both of them up to the collarbone in the pen. And Charles has his hand flat on the animal's cheek with his fingers back into the hair, and the animal has his own webbed hand up against Charles' face in exactly the same way, and neither of them is looking at anything else in the world.

John never saw this. John was thirty feet off and behind a rail and was sent away, and he has drawn it anyway, afterwards, from what he worked out, the way a man draws a thing in order to find out whether it can be true.

He has got it perfectly right.

Pascual sits in his own sick bay at ten past nine in the morning with the flat of his hand over his mouth.

He knows the shape. He has been taught the shape, thirty-three hours ago, in the dark, on an iron cot, by a man who did it to him first without telling him what it was. He has made it three times since. He made it last night at a quarter to midnight with his forearms still wet and somebody's hand in his hair.

*Boy,* he thinks. *Ay, boy. And I made you eat, and I made you sleep, and I took your glasses off your face and cleaned them on my shirt, and I never once asked you what it was.*

He thinks: and he went into the water with him, and nobody on this station will ever say so out loud again, and I have signed a paper saying he drowned.

He thinks, quite separately and very coldly: and if that man sees this sheet he will have all of it in about four seconds, because he is not a fool and he has read Andersen the same as everybody else.

He does not put plate 214 in Hester's envelope. He cannot; it is not dated, it will not go into a sequence, and there is no honest paper anywhere in the world that it belongs on.

He folds it in half, and in half again, and puts it in his shirt pocket with the other two.

Three pages now. Four, counting both his own.

---

The launch goes at noon with the Commander and the lieutenant and two ratings and one buff envelope, and Pascual stands on the jetty and watches the paper leave the island and thinks: *there. Now it is true. Now nobody can go back and make it be anything else.*

Beside him, Reggie is being charming.

It is the second time and it is worse than the first time, because the first time it was an ambush and this time Pascual can see it coming, and can see the exact moment the thing goes on — the shoulders drop about half an inch, the head comes round, the voice changes register — and can watch the Commander of a United States Navy vessel be pleased about a joke concerning the reef.

The Commander shakes his hand and says he hopes to come back. He means it. Nobody in fifteen months has left this jetty meaning it.

Pascual looks out at the water and works on his face.

---

Spilsbury gets him at three, at Gamma's pen, over the calf.

He has been perfectly pleasant all afternoon. He has been through the portfolio, and made no remark whatever about numbering, and has spent two hours at the rail with a watch in his hand recording surfacing intervals like a postgraduate, which he did not have to do and which was, Pascual concedes bitterly, competently done.

"Dr Pobil," he says. "What do you want?"

"Excuse me?"

"You've been at this a year. You've got one pen, a paraffin bath from about 1930, no microtome worth the name, no assistant, no pathologist to argue with, and you are doing clinical work on an animal nobody has ever kept alive — which you cannot publish, because Tar publishes, and you're the man who takes the samples." Spilsbury leans on the rail with his forearms and looks down into the water, entirely friendly. "I can give you a department. Material, proper instruments, a technician, and your name on it. And you would never again in your life have to ask a man like Tar for permission to do your own work."

The water moves. Down at the bottom of the pen the calf comes up the wall and butts Gamma in the shoulder and she moves her tail out of the way of him.

"No," Pascual says.

"Think about it for a week."

"No."

Spilsbury turns his head, and there is a pause that Pascual does not like at all.

"That's interesting," he says.

"It is not interesting. It is an answer."

"It's interesting," Spilsbury says, "because yesterday morning, in front of six people, you told me that Reggie Tar took your only competent assistant away, ran an experiment that half killed your animal, wouldn't stop when you told him to, and got a man drowned." He says it mildly. He is not accusing anybody of anything; he is genuinely turning something over, and that is a hundred times worse. "You were extremely convincing. I've thought about it a good deal since. And I've just offered you the exact opposite of every one of those things, and you didn't take four seconds over it."

*Ay, Dios mío.*

The thing they did to save themselves yesterday is the thing that is going to hang them, and he has just walked into it holding the rope.

He does the only thing he has, which is to be, absolutely and without any decoration whatsoever, himself.

"Come here," he says, and points down into the pen. "Look at that animal. Nine days ago she was in the corner with her tail under her and she had stopped eating, and every mermaid ever taken has died like that, in a box, on its own, and everybody in this business has always said it was pneumonia. It is not pneumonia. It is *solitude*, and we know that now, and we know it because of what happened in this station in the last four months, and yesterday I put a calf in there and she came up off the bottom and touched him." He is louder than he means to be and does not care. "There is one place in the world where that has been done and I am standing in it. You want to give me a microtome. I would not cross that path for a microtome. I will be here when she is fat, señor, and I do not care whose name goes on it, and if you think a man like me stays anywhere for a *reason to do with Tar* then you have not met very many veterinarians."

Spilsbury looks at him for a moment.

Then he laughs — delightedly, with his head back — and says, "No. No, that's fair. That's entirely fair," and claps him on the shoulder, and goes back to his watch and his surfacing intervals.

Pascual stands at the rail with his hands shaking very slightly and thinks: *I have got away with that. I think I have got away with that. I said nine true things and one of them was the shape of a lie and I do not know which one he kept.*

---

John comes to the sick bay at nine in the evening, after supper, when Pascual is cleaning glassware, and stands in the doorway with his hands in his pockets.

"Why did they want my plates."

"For the report."

"Two of them. Out of four hundred and six." John's chin is up. He is twenty-four and he has had two rums and he has been slighted, and he is not stupid, and this is the third time this week that somebody on this island has decided he was a fool. "Dr Tar told me yesterday morning that my Beta theory would finish my career. He was very kind about it. He sat on Charles' bed and he was *kind* about it. And then last night Dr Spilsbury told me it was the most interesting thing he'd heard in a year and asked me to write it up, and offered to put it in front of people who'd read it."

Pascual puts down the flask.

Here it is, then. He can hear Reggie's voice in his head telling him precisely what to say, and he can hear how well it would work, and he stands there in his own sick bay with a cloth in his hands and finds that he is not going to do it. He has done as much of that as he is going to do this week.

"Sit down," he says.

"I don't —"

"Sit down, John."

John sits on the low chair, which is not what he expected.

"I am not going to tell you that your idea is stupid," Pascual says, "because I do not think it is stupid, and I think you know that I do not think it is stupid, which is why you have come to me and not to Jean." He pulls the stool round and sits on it, and puts his forearms on his knees, and looks at the boy properly, which he now realises he has not done in two years. "So instead I am going to ask you a question, and you are going to be honest with me, because you are a great deal cleverer than anybody in this place has ever bothered to tell you.

"That man came here with the Navy for two days. He has sent his Commander home. He is sleeping in the guest hut with a lamp on until three in the morning, and today he stood at a rail for two hours with a watch, doing work a postgraduate could do. Four hundred miles from anywhere. Ask yourself what he is still doing on this island.

"And then ask yourself why the most interesting thing he has heard in a year is a thing about a man's *mind*, and not a thing about an animal. And then ask yourself what he would want next, after the paper. Not from you. From the man in the water."

John has gone very still.

"He's a scientist," he says.

"Yes," Pascual says. "So am I. So is Tar. That is not a character reference, it is a job." He picks the cloth up again because his hands want something. "I will tell you one more thing and then you may go to bed. Everything in your book is true and it is the best work anybody has done at this Institute and it will be worth something one day. Keep it. Do not give it away in pieces to a man who is nice to you at supper."

There is a long silence. Down at the pens the generator changes note the way it does at half past nine.

"Is that what Dr Tar told you to say?" John says.

"No," Pascual says. "He does not know I am saying it, and he is going to be extremely annoyed with me."

John looks at him for a moment longer. Then he gets up and goes to the door, and stops there with his back to the room.

"Charles used to talk to him, you know," he says. "For hours. Through the wall of our hut I could hear him practising it, before he'd go over. As though he were nervous." He shrugs, still not turning round. "Everybody thought he was a joke. I called him Fish Boy."

Then he goes.

---

Reggie comes at midnight and gets told all of it, standing in the middle of the floor with his hands behind his back like a man on a carpet.

Pascual watches him take it. He waits for the cold voice, and for the thing about how one does not hand a subordinate a weapon and hope he will be too flattered to use it.

"That may have been the only move on the board," Reggie says at last, "and I could not have made it. I don't have the — " he moves one hand vaguely, irritably, and gives up on the sentence, which from him is practically a sonnet.

"He may go straight to that man in the morning."

"He may. But he'd have gone anyway, in about three days, and gone happy." Reggie takes his glasses off and rubs the bridge of his nose. "You've made him uncomfortable instead. It's the only thing that's ever worked on John and I've never once thought of it, because I've never in my life wondered what he wanted." He puts the glasses back on. "It cost you something."

"Yes."

"I know," Reggie says, and crosses the room, and that is all he says about it, and it is enough, and by twenty past midnight the bolt is across and the lamp is out and neither of them says anything else worth writing down for some time.

Afterwards, in the dark, with the man asleep on his arm and going heavier by the minute, Pascual lies and listens to the generator and thinks about his shirt, hanging on the back of the door.

There are four pages in the pocket. Two of his own, and two — no. Three of his own now, and one that is not his and never will be: a man and a mermaid with their hands on each other's faces, drawn from memory by a boy who was never in the water, undated, unnumbered in any sequence that will ever be filed.

He has not told Reggie about it.

He turns it over for a while, in the dark, and finds he does not intend to tell him tonight, and cannot entirely account for that, and goes to sleep.
