# Effects

He has been at it since half past five and he has written eleven words, and three of them are *Dear*.

*Dear Miss Cholmondeley.* He does not know her Christian name. He had to go to Hester last night and ask for the next-of-kin form to find out that there are two of them and that they live in Shropshire, and he stood in her sitting room copying two women's names off a card with a pencil, having worked beside their brother for eleven months.

*Dear Miss Cholmondeley. It is my very painful duty*

No.

*Dear Miss Cholmondeley. I am writing to you about your brother, who*

He puts the pen down.

The rain has stopped again and the light is coming up off the water in that flat early way, and down at the pens a bucket goes over with a bang and somebody swears in Spanish, and Reggie Tar sits at the end of the verandah table with three ruined sheets in front of him and finds that he cannot produce a single sentence that is both true and possible.

---

At breakfast John does not sit next to Spilsbury.

He takes his plate to the far end and eats fast with his head down, and when Spilsbury says something pleasant to him about the light on the water he says, "Mm," and goes on eating.

Reggie makes himself look at his own plate.

Then Jean says, apropos of nothing at all, "Dr Spilsbury, has anyone shown you the salt ponds?"

"The salt ponds."

"Six ponds in the chain, all different salinities, and I've got two years of counts on the brackish fauna out of them. It's the only population data on an estuarine system anywhere in these islands, and if merfolk are estuarine animals then it's the only description of the ecosystem they're supposed to live in that anybody has." She is buttering toast and does not glance in Reggie's direction, not once, not for a fraction of a second. "It's an hour there in the truck and an hour back and I could show you the lot before lunch. But it wants doing before the heat."

Spilsbury puts his cup down. "Miss Leslie," he says, "yes, please."

They are gone by twenty past eight.

Reggie sits with his tea going cold and thinks: *she has given me ninety minutes and she has done it in front of six people and she will never once mention it.*

He has been on this island two years and he has been running it, more or less, and it is only this week that he has begun to understand that he has been living among a great many people who are cleverer than he had troubled to notice.

---

The hut is dim and smells of hot canvas and there are two cots in it, and one of them has been stripped, because Charles took his sleeping pad down to the pens in February and never brought it back.

Pascual stands in the doorway with his arms folded, blocking most of the light.

"Twenty minutes," Reggie says. "We're looking for anything in his hand that isn't a log sheet. Anything at all."

"And then?"

"Then we'll discuss it."

The kit is pathetic in the way a young man's kit always is. Two shirts. A tie he can never have worn here. A tin of tooth powder, a razor, a photograph of a dog. A stack of letters in a woman's hand, four of them still in their envelopes, one of them not opened at all. A crate on end for a table, with a lamp and a mug on it, and above the crate a plank shelf with eleven books.

Reggie goes along the shelf with one finger.

Andersen. Wilde. A Christabel LaMotte in a broken binding. Labat, in French, eighteenth century, foxed to hell. Walter Roth on the water spirits of the Guiana Indians. Three fisheries handbooks and a Cambridge zoology in two volumes.

And at the end of the shelf, spine out, indistinguishable from any other laboratory notebook on this station, a quarto hardback with a canvas cover.

It has been sitting there in plain sight for three days.

"*Ay,*" says Pascual, from the door.

---

Reggie takes it to the crate and sits down on the stripped cot and opens it.

It is not a log. He knew it would not be a log; a log is a thing you leave in the office. It is dense, and it is in Charles' small terrible hand, and it goes back to about November.

> *B. responds to name after 6 days. Vocalisation consistent: hum + click. Have started calling him Monty which is absurd but he turns to it.*

> *Third day. 11 min. before white spots. Yesterday 8. Note: no correlation with morning sea-bathing, so the sun hypothesis is dead and I ought to stop repeating it to myself.*

> *Fish with blue lines abt the face = Balistes vetula, triggerfish. Swaggering, highly territorial, will bite. This is what he is when he thinks of himself. I did not invent this fish. I did not know it was territorial until I looked it up afterward.*

> *silence. call. no answer. — I get this every time and I get it worst just under the surface. If I am inventing it I am inventing it very consistently and in a form I do not enjoy.*

> *Nudibranch, purple and yellow, like a flower. This is his answer to a question I had no business asking. I shall call her Iris, though he has never seen an iris.*

> *The double spiral fan worm is me. He thinks I am that. I do not know what to do with this.*

> *J. asked at supper how far M. could swim in a day. Twenty-five miles. She asked it in a particular way and did not look at me. I do not think she was making conversation.*

> *R. came down again this evening to look at M. He watches me more than he watches M. Two years I wanted him to look at me and now I would give a great deal for him to stop.*

Reggie sits very still on a dead man's cot with that on his knee.

He turns to the end. The last written page is dated the nineteenth of May, and it is four lines long, and it says:

> *M. 9 fish. Residue 2 lb 3 oz. Behaviour: no change (see everything above).*
>
> *Shoulders sore from the tarp business, still. Ridiculous.*
>
> *Tomorrow: ask P. about the bicarb — whether we can stop, or whether it is doing something I can't see.*

There is nothing after it. He never asked.

Reggie hears the sound Pascual makes behind him and does not turn round, because he is not able to.

---

"It goes on the fire," Pascual says.

"No."

"Tonight. The incinerator behind the store, with the dressings. There is a burn twice a week and nobody has ever once looked at it." Pascual has come in and shut the door, which is a thing they agreed not to do, and neither of them mentions it. "You give it to me and you do not think about it again."

"No."

"Reggie —"

"*No.*" He is holding it in both hands and is aware that he is holding it in both hands. "It's the only record in the world of the only thing anybody has ever found out at this Institute. Nine months of it, dated, consistent, with the failures left in — he wrote down every single time he was wrong. Do you know how rare that is? There will not be a second animal and there will not be a second Charles. You can't burn a result because it's awkward. That is the one thing you cannot do."

"There are four hundred pages in there about a man's *heart*," Pascual says, "and you are standing in his hut holding it like a jar."

"Yes."

"You say yes to me as though we were agreeing about the *weather* —" He stops himself, with both hands, visibly. "Listen to me. A book like that does not stay in a drawer; they never do. That book is Hester finished, and John finished, and you and me in a launch with a police sergeant. And if that man gets six pages of it he will not stop looking for the boy as long as he lives, and he will find him, because he is very good and he is patient. You told me in my own sick bay that you would rather Charles drowned than have Spilsbury take him. I believed you. And now you will not burn *paper*."

"No. Because paper can't be hurt and the boy can."

"That is not why."

"No," Reggie says. "It isn't."

And there it is, out on the floor of a hot canvas hut between the two of them, and neither of them picks it up.

Pascual breathes out through his nose.

"You would put every one of us in the water for that book," he says.

"Yes."

"At least you know it."

"I've always known it."

Somewhere off behind the store the generator changes note. Reggie stands there with a dead man's handwriting under both hands and finds that he cannot put it down on the crate — physically cannot — and that Pascual has been watching him not do it for some time.

"Give it to me," Pascual says.

"You said you'd burn it."

"I said a great many things." He puts his hand out, and it does not waver. "I have burned nothing at all this week and I have had every opportunity. Two pages out of my own file. Two plates. Other things." He shrugs, angrily, as though the shrug were an admission he had not intended to make. "I take things out. I do not destroy them. It is a defect and I have had it since I was a boy."

"Then what do you propose? It can't be in this hut at two o'clock, and it can't be in the sick bay, and it can't be in my room."

Pascual looks at him.

"Hester," he says.

---

She is at her desk with the quarterly returns and she does not put her pen down for a long moment after they come in, and then she does.

Reggie says what it is in as few words as he can manage. Pascual says nothing at all.

Hester Bevan takes the notebook and opens it somewhere in the middle and reads perhaps three lines.

Her face does not change. Reggie is watching it very closely and it does not change, and then she puts one hand up and presses the back of her wrist against her mouth for about a second and a half, and then she takes it away and goes on reading.

"He got it," she says at last. "The whole shape of it. Nobody has ever — " She stops. She turns two pages, and stops again at the fan worm, and Reggie watches an extremely competent woman of sixty-odd sit at a desk with her spectacles on and be, quite briefly, entirely somewhere else.

"There's a man in the guest hut," Reggie says. "I'd remind you of that."

"Yes, Reginald, I am aware of the man in the guest hut." She closes the book. "I have kept something in this house for forty-one years and no one has ever found it, including my husband, who knows it is there. I will not tell you where and you will not ask." She puts the notebook in her lap, both hands flat on top of it, which is not how one holds a document. "This does not leave the island and it does not leave me. And in thirty years, when everybody who could be hurt by it is dead, somebody will publish it with his name on."

"His sisters —"

"Will get his books and his shirts and his photograph of the dog," Hester says, "and a letter from you, which you have not written."

"No."

"Write it today." Her voice is not unkind and it does not admit of discussion. "Before you sit down to dinner with that man, because it will be harder afterwards and you will find a reason not to. Now go away, both of you, and take the inventory at two the way you would if there were nothing in this hut at all."

---

Spilsbury comes to the hut at two o'clock in his shirtsleeves, with a notebook of his own, and is perfect.

That is the thing about him that Reggie has been forgetting on purpose for eleven years: he is not a ghoul. He does not gloat. He takes off his hat, in a hut, for a man he never met, and he says, "Which cot?" and when John — who is here because it is his hut, and who is white about the mouth — points at the stripped one, Spilsbury says, "Right," and does not look at it again.

They do it properly. Reggie reads the items out and Hester's form gets filled in and John packs them into the crate, wrapping the razor in a shirt without being told to, because he has thought about the sisters opening it, which Reggie had not.

It takes forty minutes. The books go in last.

Spilsbury picks them up one at a time and passes them across, and reads out the titles as he goes because that is what one does. "Labat. *Nouveau voyage aux isles* — good Lord, that's a nice copy for a postgraduate. Roth, the Guiana thing. Andersen." He stops.

He does not stop dramatically. He stops the way a man stops when a book falls open in his hand, because it has been read in one place more than any other.

"Ah," he says.

And then, in a perfectly pleasant conversational voice, standing in a hot hut with a packing crate at his feet, he reads it out: the mermaid holding the prince's head above the water with her hand, and letting the waves drift them where they will; and, for a moment, the prince holding her head in his hand as well, before his limbs entirely fail him.

Nobody says anything.

Reggie makes himself write the word *Andersen* on the inventory in his ordinary hand, and does not look up, and is aware of John standing absolutely still by the crate, and of the fact that John has read that passage too, because everybody in this business has, and of the additional fact that John has drawn it.

"There's a pencil mark against it," Spilsbury says.

"He was a sentimental man," Reggie says.

"He was, wasn't he." Spilsbury turns the book over, and looks at the spine, and then puts it in the crate with the others, quite gently. "His sisters will like having that."

---

Dinner on the verandah.

Gamma has eaten twice today and has been up in the top half of the water for four hours, and Pascual, who cannot help himself, has been saying so all evening to anybody who will hold still, and the Colonel has produced a bottle of something better than usual, and for about twenty minutes it is very nearly an ordinary night.

Then Spilsbury, pouring, says, "Are you married yet, Tar?"

It is nothing. It is the most ordinary question in the English language and there are seven people at this table and four of them have asked it of somebody in the last month.

"No," Reggie says.

"Nor am I." Spilsbury sets the bottle down and settles back with his glass. "There isn't the time, is there. One tells oneself there'll be time."

"Mm."

"Do you remember Nicholson?"

"Yes."

"He got himself into a great deal of trouble," Spilsbury says comfortably, to the table at large, "about four years ago. Frightful business. Nobody saw it coming; everybody said afterwards they had. Extraordinary how that works." He drinks. "Anyway. He isn't at the labs any more."

Down at the other end of the table Jean laughs at something the Colonel has said. Hester asks Pascual a question about the calf's weight. Pascual answers it. His voice is perfectly steady and he does not look up the table, not once, which is exactly what he should do, and which Reggie will be thinking about at four in the morning.

"No," Reggie says. "I don't imagine he is."

---

At half past ten he sits down at the end of the verandah table with the lamp and a clean sheet.

He can hear the generator, and the sea, and two men's voices from the direction of the huts, and behind him, in a house with a Colonel in it, four hundred pages of a young man's handwriting in a place nobody has found in forty-one years.

He thinks about the letter he could write, which would say that Mr Cholmondeley was a promising researcher who died attempting to recover a valuable specimen, and would be signed, and would be a lie in every particular except the adjective.

He thinks about *Tomorrow: ask P. about the bicarb.*

Then he uncaps the pen and writes, in his ordinary hand, without stopping. Only one sentence in it is untrue, and he puts that one down in exactly the same hand as the rest.

> Dear Miss Cholmondeley,
>
> I was your brother Charles's supervisor at this Institute for eleven months, and it is my very painful duty to tell you that he is dead.
>
> On the night of the 19th of May one of our specimens broke out of its pen and got itself down to the sea. Your brother had the care of that animal. He went into the water after it, alone, at night, and neither of them has been recovered. The shelf here falls away to a hundred fathoms a short distance out and there is no reasonable hope. I would rather write it plainly than leave you to imagine it, though I am aware that plainness is cold comfort.
>
> Mrs Bevan, our administrator, will write to you separately about the official position and about his effects, which are being sent to you.
>
> I want to tell you two things that will not be in her letter, because I do not think anybody else will tell you and because they are the only two things I am entirely certain of.
>
> He was the best natural animal man I have ever seen, and I have worked with a great many. Nobody taught him it. He arrived with it.
>
> And he was not afraid of anything in the water. Not ever, not once, not of any of it.
>
> Yours very sincerely,
> R. Tar
