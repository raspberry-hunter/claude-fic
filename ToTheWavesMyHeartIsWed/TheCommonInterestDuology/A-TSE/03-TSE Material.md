# Material

At twenty to ten he goes and finds John in the hut, and does the worst piece of work of his life, and does it well.

He does it by sitting down on Charles' empty cot, which he has never done, and asking John's opinion, which he has never done either. He listens to eleven minutes on the subject of a mental connection between a postgraduate and a mermaid, and he lets it be a fine piece of deduction, because it is one. And then he explains, gently, with every appearance of regret, what will happen to a young man of twenty-four who puts that in front of the scientific press with his name under it: the reviews, and the dining-out, and Fleming's friends, and the fact that he will spend the next forty years being the fellow who believed in telepathic mermaids. He watches it land. He watches the boy's face go from bright to careful.

Then he gives him the other thing. Gamma and the new animal, together, the first cohabitation trial ever attempted — the whole visual record, John's alone, his name on the plates.

"Do you mean that?" John says.

"I never say things I don't mean," Reggie says, which is not true, and which has never in his life been less true than at eleven minutes past nine on the twentieth of May.

He goes down to the jetty feeling that he would like to wash.

---

The rain has stopped and the sun has come out into the wet, which on this island produces a light like the inside of a shell and a heat that goes straight through a hat. The whole station is drawn up on the jetty, because the Colonel likes a thing done properly: the Colonel and Hester, Jean with her hands behind her back, John with his portfolio, Pascual beside him with his shirt buttoned to the throat and his jaw set, and the men from the village standing off to one side by the derrick.

The tender comes round the head at nine minutes to ten with a lighter under tow, and on the lighter is a canvas tank, and in the tank is the fourth mermaid the Martin has ever had.

Pascual does not look at him. Pascual has not looked at him since half past eight and will not look at him for the rest of the day, which they did not discuss and did not need to.

"Bevan," Pascual says to the Colonel, apropos of nothing, "when they get that tank up, nobody puts a hand on it but me."

"Naturally," says the Colonel.

Reggie stands with his arms at his sides and finds that his heart is going. It has been going since about half past four this morning, when he woke on an iron cot with somebody's arm across him and lay there for twenty minutes not moving, and it has not entirely stopped since, and there are moments in the day when it stops being about Spilsbury at all and becomes something else altogether, and this is very inconvenient, and he has no intention of doing anything about it.

Then the tender comes alongside and there is a man on the rail in a linen jacket, no uniform, hatless, holding on with one hand and shading his eyes with the other, and Reggie's whole body knows him before his eyes have finished the job.

"*Tar!*" Spilsbury says, delighted, across ten feet of water. "Good God. You're brown."

---

It comes back on like a coat.

That is the thing he had not budgeted for, and he ought to have. He had budgeted for the man, and the questions, and the file. He had not budgeted for the fact that within forty seconds of Spilsbury's shoe touching the jetty, Reggie Tar is making conversation.

He is doing it beautifully. He is asking about the crossing and getting a story about the crossing and giving one back about the reef; he is turning slightly so that the Commander can be brought in and then handed on to the Colonel without anybody feeling handed on; he is being *charming*, fluently, in a manner he has not used since he was thirty-one, and he can feel Jean staring at the side of his head like a woman watching a dog talk.

He hates it in a way that makes his back teeth ache. It fits perfectly. That is the whole trouble with it; the damned thing still fits perfectly.

"Before you come up," he says, when there is a gap. "You should have it from me. We lost Beta in the night."

Spilsbury stops walking.

"He got out of the pen and made the water," Reggie says, "and we haven't recovered him. And one of my postgraduates went in after him and hasn't come back."

He waits for the face to do what a face does.

It does not do it. There is a flicker of something at the outside — *how dreadful* is put on, quite creditably, for about a second and a half — and underneath it, coming up through it like a light behind a blind, is pure appetite.

"Out of the *pen*," Spilsbury says. "Show me."

Not: *your man.* Not: *what was his name.*

Reggie says, "This way," and turns, and the coat fits, and it fits, and it fits.

---

Spilsbury goes over the deck on his hands and knees for twenty minutes with a pocket rule and a soft pencil, in a linen jacket, in the sun, humming.

He measures the drag mark from the lip of the pen to the edge of the concrete. He measures the scrape of epidermis on the lip and asks for a scalpel and takes some of it, and Pascual gives him the scalpel and stands over him with his arms folded like a man watching somebody handle his tools. He finds the old urine stain from the ninth, the port-wine patch, and goes at it with a swab and a bottle and is extremely happy.

Then he sits back on his heels.

"Sixty-one feet," he says. "Tar, that isn't possible. An animal in uraemic crisis does not haul sixty-one feet of dry concrete on its arms. I'd want a very great deal of persuading."

"It's the second time," Pascual says.

Spilsbury's head comes round.

"He did it on the ninth," Pascual says. "Forty feet, and he was in a worse state then than he was last night — he was down nine pounds on where he was yesterday and he had not begun to break down. There is a record of it. There is skin on the lip from that one too, if you know where to look, and I have the urine from that morning in the store, and there are drawings."

"Drawings," says Spilsbury.

And John, who has been standing at the rail all this time with his portfolio under his arm and no idea in the world that he is the most dangerous object on this island, says, "I did them that morning," and steps forward, pink with pleasure, and starts undoing the ribbon.

Reggie stands quite still and lets it happen.

They are good. They are better than good; John has drawn the animal on the concrete from three angles with the tarp underneath him and the light coming across, and there is a small one in the corner, done fast, of the tarp itself with the streaks on it. Spilsbury goes through them one at a time and says, "Who are you?" and John tells him, and then Spilsbury says, "These are excellent," and John goes the colour of a cooked shrimp.

*There,* Reggie thinks. *There, you fool. That is what you wanted. Take it and hold it.*

---

The sick bay at half past eleven is a small room with six people in it and a lamp on in the daylight.

Spilsbury reads the file. He reads it properly — that is the horror of him, he has always read things properly — turning each sheet, going back two, going forward again, saying nothing at all for eighteen minutes while the Commander shifts from foot to foot and the Colonel looks out of the window.

Pascual stands at the bench with a wooden box open in front of him and keeps up a low grumbling commentary that nobody has asked for: here is the serum from the eleventh, here is the sediment from the twelfth, here are the blood films, all dated, all labelled, here are the feed logs in the postgraduate's hand which will corroborate every ounce, here is the weight book. It comes out of him in a flood, aggrieved, exhaustive, the way a man empties his pockets on a counter to prove there is nothing left in them. There is more real material in that box than Spilsbury will be able to work through in a fortnight and every single item of it is genuine and every single item of it says the same thing, which is that an animal was in renal failure for eleven days.

Reggie watches Spilsbury's hands and thinks, not for the first time in his life, that he has never met anybody who wanted so much and hid it so poorly.

Then Spilsbury turns a page, and stops, and turns it back.

"Your last entry is the seventeenth," he says.

The room is very quiet.

"Yes," Pascual says.

"Then a death note on the twentieth. There's nothing here for the eighteenth or the nineteenth." He looks up over the top of the file, mild as milk. "Sixty hours, Dr Pobil, and this is the only animal of its kind in the Western Hemisphere. What were you doing?"

Reggie draws breath to answer.

He does not get the chance, because Pascual straightens up off the bench, and the whole of him goes, all at once, like a boiler.

"What was I *doing?*"

Jean's head comes up.

"I had a dying animal, and I had no hands, and I had this —" the arm comes out and it is pointing straight at Reggie "— this man running an experiment on the only useful pair of hands on this station, because he wished to know something. He took the boy off Beta for eleven days. He put him in the other pen and would not let him back. I told him. I told him on the verandah in front of everybody standing here and he did not stop it until the animal was on the *concrete*, and then he stopped it, and he said he *regretted* it —"

"That's enough," Reggie says.

"It is not *nearly* enough. You want to know what I was doing on the eighteenth and nineteenth, señor? I was in the water twice a day getting bicarbonate into an animal that his curiosity had half killed, and I did not sit down and write it up, no, and if that is a black mark against me then put it there, I do not care, put it in your report —"

"Dr Pobil is a fine anatomist," Reggie says, "and an indifferent colleague. Perhaps we might have this out later."

It comes out of him in the old drawl, cool and pared and public-school, exactly as it should, and he sees Jean flinch, and he watches Pascual's face contract as if he had been struck, and it takes everything he has left to keep his own perfectly still.

"Later," Pascual says thickly. "Yes."

He walks out. The door does not slam, which is worse; he closes it with great care, the way a furious man does when there are visitors.

There is a pause of the kind that gets into the walls.

"Well," Spilsbury says pleasantly, closing the file. "He isn't lying, at any rate."

---

He gets Reggie alone at noon, on the walk between the pens, with the sun coming off the concrete.

"You've got a lot out of very little here," Spilsbury says. "Four hundred miles from the animals, no money, a Colonel, and a vet who wants to hit you. That's rather your form, though, isn't it."

"We do what we can."

"Come back."

"No."

"It isn't the same place now, you know." He says it kindly. That is the thing about him; he is not cruel, he has never once in his life been cruel, he simply wants to know and cannot be made to stop wanting. "Half of it's gone. You'd have material, and money, and nobody standing over you. You could have had all of this and a proper pen and forty miles from the shoals instead of four hundred."

"No."

Spilsbury looks at him sideways, and something moves in his face that Reggie has been afraid of since the tender came round the head.

"You always did think you'd been let off," he says. "You hadn't, particularly. Nobody was let off. We were all doing the same work."

The sea goes on making its noise at the end of the path.

"Was your postgraduate any good?" Spilsbury says.

"He was the best natural animal man I've ever seen."

"Pity. Did you get anything off Beta before he went? Anything at all — you didn't take a hand, I suppose? Even a hand would have been —" He catches himself, and laughs at himself, and puts his hands in his pockets. "No. Of course not. Sorry."

"Quite all right," Reggie says.

---

The Commander wants to know about a search, because a mermaid is an asset and an asset has gone into the water.

They do it on the verandah with the chart on the table, and Reggie is aware that he is watching two professionals work who are not himself. Hester puts a form in front of the Commander, and then another one, and explains with enormous courtesy the position regarding territorial waters and the fishing rights of three separate villages and the licence under which the Institute holds specimens and the additional licence that would be required to recover one, and the Colonel says almost nothing at all and every twenty minutes says exactly the right thing.

And Spilsbury does the arithmetic himself and saves them all.

"He's dead," he says, straightening up from the chart. "I'd very much like him not to be, but he is. Look at the file: he was eleven days into a uraemia and he'd broken his own muscle down twice. He hauled himself sixty-one feet and that's the end of what that animal had. The shelf goes to a hundred fathoms two miles out and there was a surge running all night. There's nothing to find. If there were anything to find the sharks have had it by now."

"And the man?" says the Commander.

"Also dead," Reggie says.

He is very slightly surprised at how steady it comes out, and then he is not surprised at all, and then he would like to sit down.

They put one launch out for the afternoon anyway, because the Navy must be seen to do something, and it goes six miles down the coast and finds a great deal of water.

---

He has the row with Pascual at two, in the store, among Alpha's boxes, in whispers, and this one is entirely genuine.

"Today," Pascual says. "*Today.* You want to put a juvenile in with a declining adult, with sixteen strangers on the station, on the day I have already — no. No. Absolutely no."

"Gamma will be dead in three weeks."

"Then she will be dead in three weeks having been let alone —"

"She'll be dead in three weeks having been let alone in exactly the way that killed Alpha and nearly killed Beta, and there will never again be this many hands and this much boat on this island, and if it goes wrong today there are eleven men within thirty yards who can get an animal out of the water. Next month there'll be four of us and a bosun's chair." Reggie hears his own voice and lowers it. "And the juvenile is small and Gamma is a female past breeding condition and if there is one pairing in the world that doesn't fight it's that one. You know it. You told me so yourself in March, about *dolphins*, at dinner, at some length, while I was trying to read."

There is a silence among the boxes.

"You listened to that?" Pascual says.

"I listen to everything you say," Reggie says, and turns round and goes out, because there is only so much a man can do in a store in the middle of the afternoon.

---

They put the young one in with Gamma at four o'clock.

It takes half an hour to get the tank across on the derrick, and Pascual does it himself with the men from the village, swearing steadily in two languages, and there is a moment when the sling shifts and the whole thing lurches and eleven people make the same noise at once. Then the animal goes over the side into Gamma's pen, and everybody stops talking.

He is small. He cannot be more than four years old — a third of Beta's length, mottled, with his ribs showing and a raw place on his flank from the tank. He goes straight to the bottom and sits there against the wall with his arms round himself, and Gamma, who has not moved off the bottom of that pen in nine days except to be fed, who is a bag of bones in the shape of something that used to be magnificent, lifts her head.

Nothing happens for four minutes. Reggie counts them.

Then she comes across the pen — slowly, terribly slowly, with her tail doing about a third of what it should — and stops a yard from the calf. And the calf makes a sound, a thin one, the first sound anybody on this station has heard out of him, and Gamma answers it, and puts out her hand and puts it flat on the side of his face.

And the calf raises his own hand and puts it on hers.

Somebody says something. Reggie does not know who and does not care. What he is aware of is that his own eyes have gone hot and unreliable and that this is not acceptable in front of the United States Navy, and that beside him John has got his pencil going without looking down at the paper, and that from the other side of the pen, for the first time in nine hours, Pascual Pobil is looking straight at him.

He wants, very badly, to say something to him, and cannot, and so puts both hands on the rail instead and grips it, and lets the whole thing go up through him like current.

"Well," Spilsbury says softly, at his elbow. "*Well.* Look at that. Tar, why has nobody done that before?"

"Because I wouldn't let them," Reggie says.

---

Supper on the verandah is very nearly a success.

The Commander is human after his third rum. Jean gets the lieutenant explaining salinity gradients back to her and does not laugh once. Hester runs the table like a woman docking a ship. Gamma has eaten, for the first time in six days, and so has the calf, and Pascual has been up and down between the bungalow and the pen four times and has stopped being able to hide that he is happy about it.

Reggie sits with his glass untouched and does the arithmetic he has been doing all day: eleven hours since the tender, thirteen more before it goes, and then a fortnight in which nothing has to hold except paper.

Then John says, "Of course, there is a theory about why Beta did so well."

The verandah does not go quiet. That is the awful part; it does not go quiet at all. The lieutenant goes on talking. Jean's face does something very fast.

"Oh?" says Spilsbury.

"Mm." John turns his glass round on the table, not looking up, in the voice of a man who has been told he is a fool and has had four rums since. "I put it together myself, actually. But I'm told it isn't fit for publication."

"Most of the interesting things aren't," Spilsbury says.

"John," Reggie says, "take Dr Spilsbury's glass, would you."

"I'm not —"

"Take his glass."

And it works, because John is twenty-four and has been told what to do by Reggie Tar for two years, and he gets up and does it and the moment goes by, and the lieutenant is still talking about salinity, and Hester says something to the Commander about the mail boat.

But Spilsbury is looking at John's back. He looks at it for perhaps three seconds.

Then he turns to Reggie and smiles at him, in a friendly, interested, entirely unhurried way, and says, "Lovely evening."

---

He waits by the fuel drums until the last hut light goes out, which is twenty past eleven, because he is not going to be seen walking to the sick bay by anybody at all tonight.

Pascual has been at Gamma's pen since ten. He is in the low chair by the door, the one he keeps for people who have fainted, with his legs stretched halfway across the floor and his sleeves shoved up and his forearms still wet to the elbow, and his eyes shut. There is an inch of the unlabelled bottle in a beaker on the floor beside him, because there are no glasses in this room.

Reggie shoots the bolt and leans against the bench.

Nothing is going to happen here tonight. Neither of them has slept, and there are sixteen strangers on this island, and he came anyway, which he supposes is the whole of the information.

"An indifferent colleague," Pascual says, without opening his eyes.

"You pointed at me."

"I pointed at you because it was true, and because that man believes nothing he is told and everything he overhears." One eye opens. "You did it very well. That was the worst part of my day, if you would like to know. Watching you be able to do it."

"Yes," Reggie says. "It was the worst part of mine."

Pascual takes that, and turns it over, and evidently decides not to open it tonight, which Reggie will remember for a long time.

"He asked me to go back," he says.

"I know. I saw the two of you on the path." Pascual finds the beaker without looking and drinks out of it. "What did you say?"

"No."

"Good." A pause. "Was it hard to say?"

"No," Reggie says, and finds it true, and something in his chest lets go about half an inch.

Pascual puts the beaker down and takes the folded pages out of his shirt pocket — they have been in there for sixteen hours; they are damp and they are going soft at the crease — and looks at them, and puts them back.

"We are very good at this," he says. "That is what I have found out today. I did not know that about myself."

"We got away with it."

"That is not the same as being good at it, and it is not what I said." He looks up. "You gave John the cohabitation because you knew he would take it. You gave me a room with six people in it because you knew what I would do in front of six people. You knew I would go off before I knew it."

"I hoped."

"*Ay.*" Pascual shakes his head slowly. "And I let you. And I would do it again in the morning."

"You'll have to," Reggie says. "He's not going. The Commander goes at noon and Spilsbury's stopping four days to look at the pairing."

Pascual is quiet for a moment. Then he shifts his legs apart, which is not subtle and is not meant to be, and Reggie comes off the bench and crosses the room and stands between his knees.

Sitting, in that chair, the man's head comes to about the middle of his chest. It is the only arrangement in which Reggie is the taller of the two of them and he intends to make thorough use of it for as long as it is available. He puts a hand into the wet hair at the back of Pascual's head, and Pascual leans forward the last two inches and rests his forehead against Reggie's breastbone and stays there, breathing, with one hand shut round the back of Reggie's knee.

They hold still like that for some time. Reggie looks at the top of his head and the grey in it and does not say any of the four things he thinks of.

Then Pascual tips his head back, and reaches up, and takes Reggie's face in his hand — deliberately, eyes open, the way he has now been taught — and Reggie shuts his own and lets it happen, and puts his hand up to answer it, because apparently that is a thing he does now.

"Then we had better be good at it," Pascual says.

---

He goes out at midnight into a night that smells of wet stone. The generator is doing what it does. Down at Gamma's pen there is the smallest sound of water moving, twice, and then again: two animals, awake, in the dark, not alone.

The huts are dark. John's is dark.

The guest hut, at the end, has its lamp burning, and against the blind there is the shape of a man sitting up with a file on his knees, turning a page.

Reggie stands and watches it for a while, and then goes to bed.
