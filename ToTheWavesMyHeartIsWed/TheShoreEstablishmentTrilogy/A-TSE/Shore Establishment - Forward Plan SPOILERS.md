## 3. Arc two: structure

The register inverts. Arc one was three weeks of hiding one thing from one man. Arc two opens with the consequences of something they did **on purpose, in public, under their own names**.

### Part I — autumn 1954 into June 1955

Opens with the paper in proof and the first letters arriving.

1. **The paper lands.** Institutes asking how it's done. The first request the Martin has ever had to *supply* an animal rather than receive one.
2. **Money.** Success brings funding; funding brings the Navy back. The thing that saves the Institute is the thing that endangers it, and the Colonel has to take money from somebody.
3. **The Colonel.** The shield, and not young.
4. **John's open door** — still open, and John old enough to walk through it.
5. **Jean** — outgrowing the place, and knowing everything.
6. **The review, June 1955.** Happening inside all of the above.

**The review, as agreed:** they are both extremely intense men and at one year they take the thirty years outright. No re-optioning. What tips it is the three-words scene — Reggie discovers that Pascual took thirty years on the night and *then* sat up and sold him twelve months, and once that is out there is nothing left to review.

**The three words scene (June 1955, at the review).** Reggie asks what it meant. He is *furious* — in the bickering register, not the dangerous one, exactly as Pascual was about the mermaid vow. *You accepted thirty years, that night, out loud, and then you sat up and sold me an interim analysis?* Pascual's defence: you liked the interim analysis enormously, and one of us had to be sensible. Pascual enjoys this more than anything that has happened to him in a year.

**Proposed causing event for the jump** (author's call, this is my pick): the Martin takes money on condition it becomes the **training centre** — other institutions send men to learn the method, and Reggie and Pascual teach them, with their own hands. They seed the world themselves. Consequences arrive over years, it is entirely their own choice, and it is the same appetite a fifth time. Secondary structural bomb: the Colonel's succession.

### The jump — now occupied by the Interlude

Three or four years. **Working title for the middle arc: *The Common Interest* (alt: *A Working Relationship*).** Set 1956–57, self-contained, own numbering. It converts the gap from elapsed time into the most consequential thing that happens between the arcs, and it frees the 1964 endgame for Charles rather than making Spilsbury and Charles compete for the same slot.

## 3a. The Interlude — reluctant allies

**Model: Crais.** Pursuer becomes ally without becoming good. Never a change of heart. Every co-operative act is legible as his own logic, the alliance is contingent throughout, and he would defect the moment the arithmetic changed — which everyone in the room knows at all times.

### The bigger thing

A **programme**, not a person: a well-funded joint defence effort to take merfolk at scale, using Tar's own published method, for capability rather than knowledge. Bigger and more menacing than Spilsbury because it is impersonal, has authority, cannot be argued with — and exists *because of the paper*. It should be the training-centre fuse detonating: men Reggie taught, doing exactly what he showed them, at a scale he did not imagine.

The most frightening version wants not the animals but the **bond** — Spilsbury's 1954 negative result went into a report, and somebody with more money and less scruple read it and wants the positive case. Which makes Charles what they are ultimately looking for, without knowing his name.

### Why Spilsbury comes to them

Never conscience. Layered, all three true, none of them moral:

1. He has been cut out. His subject has been handed to people who will ruin it.
2. They will do it crudely, kill every animal, and close the field for a generation. He wants forty years of work; they want a capability by Tuesday.
3. He cannot stop it alone. He needs Tar's name, Tar's method and Tar's credibility.

He should say all of this in terms of the work, never the animals and never the people. **His price and his surety** are the thing he has been holding since 1954: what he knows about what they did. Whether he ever wrote it down is something they have to decide whether to believe.

### The sub-conflicts

**Reggie and Spilsbury working.** Two men at the same pitch, finishing each other's sentences, twenty hours at a stretch, producing something genuinely brilliant. It is efficient and complementary and it is a room Pascual cannot enter.

**Pascual.** Not jealousy, or not mainly. He knows they worked side by side for as long as he has known Reggie, and he knows about 1946, and he watches a version of the man he lives with appear for somebody else. The register to write is a man being extremely fair about something he hates.

**The turn, and the point of the whole series.** The plan Reggie and Spilsbury build *works*, and costs something they should not pay. Pascual says no. Reggie listens. That is the difference: **Reggie prefers Pascual by an enormous margin precisely because Pascual pulls him back — as he did with Charles — and Spilsbury pushes him on.** It is a large part of why he stayed at the labs as long as he did, and he is not proud of it, and this is where he says so out loud.

**Spilsbury's response to that** should be puzzlement, not malice. He cannot see why a man would want a brake. He may say so, helpfully, as an observation about efficiency.

### The two answers owed

**Nicholson.** What he was doing: establishing a fact he might need. Not to use — he would say — but because he likes to know, and a man who knows a thing has an option. Partly, too, to settle 1946, because he has wondered for eleven years and would like to have been right. The explanation must be worse than a threat would have been: *I have had it for two years and done nothing with it, which you may take as evidence or not.*

**1946 — the flashback, on the page.** Adjoining rooms, late, a piece of work going extremely well, and a moment. Nothing happened. The reveal is *who stopped it and why*: **Spilsbury stopped it**, and not from fear or decency, but because he had calculated that Reggie was worth more as a colleague than as anything else. An allocation decision. Reggie has kept it eleven years as ambiguity; on the other side it was arithmetic.

Which makes **Pascual the exact mirror** — he also declined, on a jetty in February, for fifteen months, and his reason was that he could not have stopped at once and there was nowhere to put it and it would have been a cruelty to hang it on the man. Two men decline Reggie; one out of calculation, one out of care; the difference is the whole book.

### Ground rules

- Spilsbury is never redeemed, never softened, and never apologises. He may be *useful*, warm, funny and genuinely helpful, and remain exactly as dangerous.
- The alliance ends the moment the common interest does. It should end that way.
- He does not learn anything about Charles that he did not already suspect in 1954.
- **Charles stays entirely off the page for this arc.** Ruled. He may be what the programme is ultimately reaching for, without anyone naming him; he does not appear, is not found, and is not present in any room Spilsbury is in. The standing ruling is that he is never taken by anybody who does not have his best interests at heart, and Spilsbury emphatically does not — so putting the two of them in proximity writes straight at the ruling and makes him a bargaining chip. It would also make arc three redundant.
- **The join to arc three:** the arc may end with Charles becoming *findable* — a trace, a rumour, a search pattern, a piece of evidence the programme itself generates — which detonates the next arc without him ever having been on the page in this one.

### Part II — 1958/59

- Pens in three countries, staffed by men Reggie trained.
- Chumley grown; Gamma alive years past every table.
- John somebody. Jean gone or going.
- Reggie and Pascual long-established: a quieter, different good, and the register change the jump buys.
- Later reviews handled in retrospect, probably in a line.
- Spilsbury's ten years more than half gone.

### Endgame — 1960 to 1964, and a long tail

With the Interlude occupying the gap, the endgame belongs to Charles.

- **By 1960–64 the shape becomes visible to the people who can see it** — Reggie, Pascual, Jean, and Charles, wherever he is: captivity expanding *because* of the method, and habitat loss starting to bite. They can see the two curves and cannot yet see where they cross.
- **The reversal — that the captive population is what saves the species — does not become apparent until the 1980s.** That is legitimate endgame material and lands inside the life of the costing sheet (1984 is underlined twice on it).
- Spilsbury is no longer a scheduled return — after the Interlude he is a known quantity who has been in their house. The endgame question becomes what he is *now* and what he wants next, which is worse.
- Hester's thirty-year plan for the notebook, and whether she lives to execute it.
- Charles and Monty alive, and **on the page in arc two** — the only living witness who could unmake all of it, and the one person none of them can protect and none of them can reach.
- The question *Waves* shelves — whether merfolk are people — which nobody is pressing now that Charles is gone.

## 4. Rulings and open questions

**Settled:**

- Part I opens autumn 1954.
- **The thirty years is taken outright, not re-optioned — and the three-words scene is what triggers it.** The moment Reggie finds out what Pascual said that night, they are committed; there is no second annual review after that. So the placement of the reveal *is* the placement of the commitment, and the two scenes are one scene. If it happens in June 1955, that is the only review that ever goes on the page.
- **John is not suborned.** If he takes Spilsbury's door he holds fast — or, if he lets something slip, he comes and tells them himself. He never becomes a leak they don't know about.
- Reggie's fury at the reveal is the bickering register, not the dangerous one, and Pascual enjoys it enormously.
- The second sister is Lettice.
- Pascual's three pages: put away after the first week of June 1954. Location unspecified and **not load-bearing** — do not build a plot on it.

**Charles — standing rulings:**

- **He is present in arc two.** Possibly because he is found.
- **He does not die by any unnatural cause.** No drowning, no violence, no dissection, no death in captivity. A natural death — heart, illness — is permissible if it ever serves, and only that.
- **He is never taken by anybody who does not have his best interests at heart.** A near miss is available; a capture is not.
- Corollaries worth holding: he is bonded, so any contact is temporary by nature — he cannot stay ashore and Monty cannot stay in. Anyone who finds him has to be someone who can be trusted with him, or has to fail to find him. And his surfacing is the natural detonator for the whole official story, which means it collides directly with the training-centre fuse; that collision is probably the spine of Part II or the endgame.
- Open: his age (never fixed); what six years in the sea has done to him physically; who finds him, and whether he chooses to be found.

**Pending beats, unplaced:**

- **Reggie says it back.** Not soon. He has understood it and answered it obliquely (*Yes. That. The same.*) at the June 1955 review; the plain sentence, in either language, is owed and should be spent somewhere it costs him — well into arc two or later.

**Still open:**

1. Does the Colonel die inside arc two — and does that end the Institute or transform it?
2. Does John take the door, and when?
3. Whether the "ninth" collision gets repaired by rewording or by retitling installment 2.
