# The Ninth Day

He is doing the water record when the door goes.

It is a beautiful thing, the water record. He does not often think a thing he has made is beautiful, and he is thinking it tonight, at eleven o'clock, with the lamp pulled down and his back sticking to his shirt: eleven days ruled off down the left, colour and volume, and the colour going port wine, port wine, port wine, dark, dark, amber, amber, straw, straw, clear. An animal in acute renal failure comes back. He has the blood work to sit beside it. He has the weights. He has the feed logs in the boy's small mad handwriting to corroborate every ounce of it, because whatever else is wrong with Charles Cholmondeley, that boy writes down everything that happens, including things nobody asked him for, including the *faeces*.

He has been at it for two hours and he could have finished in one, but he keeps going back and ruling the columns straighter.

Then the door, and he does not turn round, because it will be Jean wanting a chat, or John wanting a plate, and he says, "It's late," and hears the rain come in with whoever it is.

"It is," says Reggie Tar.

Well. He puts the pen down and does not turn round.

There is a routine to be got through here. Tar comes to a room, Tar wants something in the room, Tar leaves. The correct response is to be short with him, because Pascual has been furious with him for a fortnight and intends to go on being furious with him for another fortnight at least, having costed the thing out, and he will not have his fury eroded by nine minutes of conversation about the morning's paperwork.

"If it's about the morning, it's ready," he says. "The files are ready. I did them twice. I'm not doing them a third time because you've thought of something."

"That's not it."

"Then what?"

"Pascual."

He turns round.

The man is wet. Not caught-out wet — properly wet, hair down on his forehead, water standing on the shoulders of his shirt, glasses hazed with it, which means he has come the north way past the generator, which is the long way and floods less, and he has been walking in it for some minutes without noticing.

*Ay.* Something is wrong.

"Charles has taken Beta out," Reggie says. "Tonight. He'll be past the reef within the hour, if he isn't already."

---

He does not remember standing up. The stool goes over.

He asks four questions, or five, he is not counting, and gets the same answer twice, and the second time the words come at him in that flat clipped way of his, *I gave him the order and the chart and he's doing it now*, and he wants, with a purity he has not felt in years, to take hold of the front of that shirt and shake him until the smirk comes off.

He says the eleven days. He says the bicarbonate, twice a day, with his own hands.

And Tar says *he's fit*, and gives him the arithmetic — two and a half knots, twenty-five miles — and then quotes his own words back at him from the fifth day, which he did say, which he did say to a mermaid as a joke, standing at the rail with the boy laughing at him.

He stands there with his mouth open and the whole of the rest of it in it, and cannot say a word of it, because it is not true and he knows it is not true.

The animal is fit. The animal is more than fit. The stiffness went on the fifth day and the water was clear on the ninth and he is nine pounds up and he eats like a man at a wedding, and Pascual is the one who wrote every line of that down, ruling the columns straight, two hours at it, for the pleasure of it.

"He is fit," he says. "*Ay.*"

He picks the stool up and puts it square and does not sit on it, because if he sits down he will have conceded something.

"You didn't ask me," he says.

He gets an answer that is not an apology and is not meant to be one, and is, underneath, so exactly true — *if I'd asked you at six you'd have said no, and by seven you'd have said yes, and I hadn't got the hour* — that he has to fold his arms to keep his hands occupied.

"That is not an apology."

"No."

"I did not think it was." He can hear his own voice, low and dragging. It is the voice he uses on a man who has brought him a dog he has beaten. "Why."

---

He gets the name. Spilsbury. He gets what the man will want and in what condition he will want it, delivered in the same voice you would use for a tide table.

"You are guessing."

"I'm not."

"Then how do you know?" — and it is out of him before he can stop it, because it is the wrong question and he knows it is the wrong question the instant the shape of it leaves his mouth. "You have never met a man you could predict in your life, you can't predict *Jean* —"

"I know him."

"How?"

"I am not going to answer that."

Every syllable stands up separately, like something read off a card, and the man's whole face closes as if a hand had gone over it.

Pascual has spent a year across a table from that face at three meals a day. He is an anatomist; faces are his trade and this one is a very poor specimen, hardly anything ever moving in it, but that is precisely why the little that moves can be read. He looks at it now, in a wet shirt, in his own sick bay, at eleven at night, and he thinks: *somebody has done something to you, or you have done something, and either way it was done in a place with a name that you do not say.*

He is not going to get it tonight and he is not sure he wants it tonight.

"All right," he says.

Tar does something with his shoulders. It is very small. Pascual files it in the place where he keeps the small things.

Then he thinks of the boy, and asks about the boy, and gets a pause instead of an answer.

---

It is the pause that does it. He has never once had a pause out of this man.

"He's going with him."

And then Pascual is shouting, and he knows he is shouting, and he cannot get any purchase on it because every single thing that comes back at him is worse than the last. *He has water. And food, and zinc, and lines, and hooks, and a knife.* — *Because I watched him pack it, over the last five days, and I didn't stop him.* — *I knew when I put it in his hand.*

He has hold of the bench behind him. He is aware that he has hold of the bench behind him and that this is the reason he has not done something he would have to live with.

"*Why.*"

"Because I would rather he drowned," Tar says, "than that Spilsbury had him on a table to find out how it works."

And he hears the man arrive at it as he says it. That is the thing. He hears the sentence be new.

Pascual lets go of the bench.

He does not shout now. He goes and stands nearer, which is what he does, and he says the true thing, which is that the animal would have died here and the boy would have died with him, slower, and that this is his professional opinion and Tar may put it in his file. Because it is true, and because he has been eleven days looking at a beautiful record of an animal getting well in a concrete box, and he knows better than anybody on this island what the record was going to say next.

"Thank you," Tar says.

"It is not for you, it is what I think."

And then he looks at him properly for the first time since he came in — the wet shirt, the water still on his glasses, the wrists, the way he is standing like a man on a parade ground because he has not got anything else to do with himself — and says:

"Have you eaten?"

---

He has not, of course. He never has. He is like a bird; he eats what a bird eats and then he talks for two hours.

Pascual gets the bread out, and the tin, and the bottle, and puts them down on the clean end of the bench and moves him off the fixative, and watches him eat with his arms folded and does not say anything at all, because there is nothing to say and because if he says anything the man will stop eating.

*He came here,* he thinks. *He did the thing, and then he came here. He could have gone to Bevan. He could have gone to his own room and read a journal and been perfectly comfortable.*

"The material from Alpha," Tar says with his mouth full. "Jean's blocks. Are they still in the store?"

"Yes. Both boxes. Why?"

"No reason."

Pascual's whole face does what it does. He is aware of it doing it. There is no help for it and never has been.

"No reason," he repeats.

"Not tonight."

He looks at him for a good long moment. He does not let things go; ask anyone. He puts this one down within easy reach, on the understanding that he will pick it up in the morning, and says, "Eat your bread."

---

He decided about Tar in February, on the jetty, in the dark, with a lamp.

There is a bollard out at the seaward end that is where no bollard should be, and he put a hand on the back of the man's neck to steer him round it — the way he would move anyone, the way he moves Jean, the way he moves a horse — and the neck under his hand was warm and thin and the man came where he was put without the slightest resistance, three steps, and said nothing, and Pascual took his hand away and looked at the pumps and thought, with the absolute clarity of a diagnosis: *no.*

Not *no, this is not so.* He is forty-one and has known what he is since he was a boy. *No, you will not do this again, because you could not stop at once, and there is nowhere on this island to put it, and the man is your senior colleague and does not think about anybody as a body, including himself, and it would be a cruelty to hang it on him.*

So he has not touched him since. Not once, in fifteen months. Not the elbow, not the shoulder, not the hand under the arm going down into the boat, and he touches everyone, and he has had to think about it, every single time, which is not how a man's hands are meant to work.

He tells himself it has been a piece of ordinary discipline.

It has been the largest thing in his life for over a year.

---

So when the man puts down the plate and squares it to the edge of the bench and says, "I don't want to go back to my room," Pascual answers about the cot, because that is the answer he has ready and has had ready, he now understands, for approximately fifteen months.

"That isn't what I meant."

And then he stands there in his own sick bay while Reggie Tar, in a wet shirt, takes his glasses off and folds them and puts them down on the bench, and lays out the evidence.

February. The bollard. And then — *and then you haven't touched me since. Not once. You touch everyone. You had your hands in Charles' hair this morning getting a fish hook out of it.*

He has been observed. For eleven months he has been observed, by a man who observes nothing, who cannot tell you the colour of the Colonel's front door, and the thing that was observed was not the hand.

It was the not doing it again.

"Do you know what it would cost," Pascual says. His voice comes out wrong.

He gets the odds quoted at him. He gets *I've known what it would cost since I was fifteen*, and *you may say no, say it in a sentence*, and by then he is already moving, three steps, the same as the jetty.

"You are the most infuriating man I have ever worked with."

"Yes."

"You *know* that. You know it and you don't care —"

"No."

"— and I have wanted to put you against a wall since the second week."

"Well," Tar says, and his voice does something, "there's a wall."

---

And then Tar reaches up and puts his hand flat against the side of his face.

Not the mouth. Not the shirt, not the belt, not any of the several things a man does at the front end of this. The face — the palm along the jaw and the fingers back into the hair over the ear — and he holds it there and does not move, and looks at him with his eyes open and his glasses off and the whole of him gone still, like a man taking a reading.

Something goes out of Pascual's knees.

He hears himself make a noise, the low one he made on the verandah, the one that got away from him. Fifteen months of his hands doing what he told them and here is this man, this dry, exact, exasperating little man who has not looked directly at a human being since he came to this island, holding his face as if it were an instrument he had been waiting for.

He puts his own hand up against Tar's face, because there is nothing else that can be done, and the eyes shut, and something crosses that face that Pascual has never once seen on it in fifteen months of meals.

Then he gets his mouth on him and stops thinking in any organised way for some considerable time.

---

The bolt is Admiralty and it went in in 1943 and it takes him two goes because he will not let go with the other hand.

The room is his own: eight by ten, iron cot, the net up in a knot, the corner of the window that lets in when the wind is south. Nobody comes within thirty yards of it after dark. He puts him against the wall, as advertised, and gets his hand behind that head first, because whatever else happens in this room tonight the man is not going to be hurt in it by accident.

"Don't," Tar says, "don't be gentle with me, for God's sake —"

"*Quieto*," he says into his throat. "I will be exactly as I like."

And that, it turns out, is the thing. He learns it in about four seconds and files it for ever: that this man, who has organised his entire existence so that nothing may be done to him, wants to be held on to and told to be still, and that he will make a noise about it that Pascual is going to be hearing in his sleep at seventy.

He is skinny and he is stronger than he looks and he is filthy-minded in a dry, articulate, appalling way, and he laughs — the man *laughs*, flat on his back with Pascual's hand spread on his chest — and says he is delighted, there is a difference, come here.

The cot is a disgrace. The rain covers everything. It is hot and it is loud and at the end he has Tar's mouth on his shoulder to muffle him, offered there for that purpose, and his own name said in a way he has never heard it said in this language or the other.

Afterwards there is a wet head on his collarbone, on the old winch scar, and a heart going like a bird's, and Pascual lies in the dark of his own room with his arm over Reggie Tar and thinks, quite calmly: *fifteen months. What an idiot I have been.*

And then: *and it is a quarter past midnight and the Americans come at ten.*

---

He is running his thumb up the man's spine before he notices he is doing it, and then he goes on doing it deliberately.

He tries the Alpha question again and gets *in the morning*. So he says the thing out loud, to see the shape of it in the room: *you will ask me to write something that is not true, and put my name under it, and give it to an American at ten o'clock.*

"In the morning," Tar says.

"Ask me now, then, and get it over with."

"No."

"Why not?"

There is a pause of the kind he did not get out of this man for a year and has now had twice in one night.

"Because I'd rather have tonight first," Tar says.

Pascual's thumb stops. Then goes on.

"All right," he says.

Some while later he learns, lying in the dark, that the thing with the hand on the face is a mermaid vow out of Andersen and Wilde, that Hester Bevan recited it in her sitting room with her hands folded, and that Reggie Tar did it to him *first*, deliberately, without telling him, in his own sick bay.

He gets up on his elbow to say what he thinks about this. He looks at the face — legible from the door, for once, both of them lit by nothing at all — and lies down again and hauls the man in against him instead.

"Then it's done," he says to the ceiling. "There. It's done, it's your own fault, and I'm not doing it again in the morning, so don't ask me."

"Ten hundred hours," Tar says.

"Go to sleep."

---

He wakes at ten past four because he always does, and the man beside him is asleep, and Pascual lies in the grey and looks at him.

Asleep, Reggie Tar looks about nineteen and thoroughly annoyed about something.

*Ay, Dios,* he thinks. *Look what you have gone and done.*

---

He goes down to the pen at half past five, in the rain, alone.

He does not know why he goes, since there is nothing to be done there and he has an animal arriving at ten and a declining animal in the next pen who needs him more. He goes anyway, up over the neck of the cay with his collar up, and stands at the rail and looks at the water.

The pen is very quiet. The generator is going, as it always goes, and the rain is coming down onto the tarp Jean rigged, and there is nothing in there at all. The water is grey and empty and slightly greenish at the sides where the algae is. The morning's fish are in the bucket where the boy left them, covered against the birds, weighed and counted, because of course they are.

He had a name. The boy called him Monty, thinking nobody heard.

Pascual stands there for a while with the rain going down inside his collar and does not do anything about his face, since there is nobody to see it.

He is a practical man, so after a while he starts walking the deck, because in about four hours somebody with an American accent and a great deal of curiosity is going to walk it too.

The drag mark comes out of the pen at the shallow end and goes over the lip and away down the concrete and off onto the sand. Eleven days ago there was another one going the other way. The rain has taken most of it and will take the rest of it by eight. There is a scrape of epidermis on the lip where the animal came over — real, fresh, unmistakable, and no more than five hours old — and a bootprint half filled with water, and no tarp anywhere, because the tarp went into the sea with them.

He stands and looks at all of it for a long moment with the rain on the back of his neck.

Then he says, out loud, to nobody, in Spanish, a short sentence about Reggie Tar, and goes to look at Gamma.

---

Tar comes to the sick bay at seven with two cups of tea and his hair combed, which is somehow the most obscene thing he has done in twelve hours.

"Right," he says. "I need Beta dead."

"Good morning to you too."

"He died in the night. Renal failure, sequel to the escape on the ninth. You'd been treating him for eleven days and he went into crisis and you lost him, and we'll want the material to hold up, so —"

"No," Pascual says.

Tar stops.

"You are not putting another animal's kidney under a glass with Beta's name on the label. Alpha died of pneumonia in a pen after four months, his kidneys are the kidneys of an animal who died of pneumonia, and if that man brings a pathologist with him — and if he is the sort of man you say he is, he will bring a pathologist with him, or he will *be* one — then we are both finished before lunch and so is the Institute and so is Hester's arrangement, and so, incidentally, is Gamma. No. Absolutely not. And I did not open him, and I will not write that I opened him, and there is no body, and I would never in my life have thrown a body over."

"Then we have a problem," Tar says, "because I need him dead, and there's no body."

"You have a bigger problem than that." Pascual puts his cup down. "Come out to the pen and look at the deck."

---

They stand at the rail in the rain, the two of them, and he shows him.

"There," he says. "And there. He came over the lip on his belly, and he was dragged, and it goes down onto the sand and into the sea. That is what happened and it is written on the concrete in his own skin. A dead animal in a pen does not do that. You cannot have him die quietly in the water at three in the morning, Reggie, because the deck says he went out. Anybody who was here eleven days ago knows what that mark is. John *drew* the last one."

Tar looks at it for perhaps four seconds.

"Then he got out," he says.

"He got out. He was found out. He was in crisis and he got himself over the lip the same as he did on the ninth, only this time he made the water, and the boy went in after him, and neither came back."

"That's very nearly true."

"It is entirely true," Pascual says, "except for the order of two things, and the ordering is not mine, it is yours, and you may carry it."

He hears himself and finds he does not want it back.

Tar takes his glasses off, finds them wet, puts them back on. "It's the better story," he says. "But it puts an animal in the water alive. Do you understand what that means? They'll want to know how far a sick mermaid gets from this jetty in twelve hours, and then in twenty-four, and Spilsbury will draw a circle on a chart, and the Navy has boats, and it has aircraft, and there is a man on the surface holding on to him with both hands because he cannot swim well enough to let go."

Pascual has been about to say something and does not say it.

"So the file has to say the animal was dying," Tar says. "It has to say it in your hand, with dates. Because that's the only thing that decides whether they draw the circle at five miles or fifty."

The rain comes down on the tarp.

He thinks of the eleven days, ruled off down the left. Port wine, port wine, dark, dark, amber, straw, straw, clear.

*Ay, Dios mío.*

"You did not have to argue that," he says thickly. "I would have got there."

"I know," Tar says. "I was watching you get there."

---

The file is on the bench under the lamp where he left it at eleven o'clock last night, ruled straight, done twice.

He turns the pages back and finds the ninth day.

There is nothing to falsify. That is the thing he keeps arriving at and going away from and arriving at again. He does not have to write one untrue word. Every figure on every sheet was taken by his own hands and is exactly correct, and the record of an animal in acute renal failure over eleven days is a terrible document, and it becomes a lie the moment he stops it.

The last two sheets are the tenth and eleventh days. Weight up nine pounds. Surfacing intervals four and five minutes. Water clear. Feeding without prompting. Muscle tone normal. *No stiffness observed. Animal playing.*

He had ruled those columns three times.

He takes the two sheets out of the file, and folds them in half, and folds them again, and puts them in his shirt pocket.

He had thought — he is aware of having thought, sitting here last night at eleven o'clock with his back sticking to his shirt — that he would put this file in front of the Americans himself. That is the shameful part and he may as well know it. Not for the Institute. He had wanted the American to look at the columns and then look up at him.

"I could burn them," Tar says quietly, "if you'd rather not have them in the room."

"You will not touch them."

"No."

Pascual squares the remaining pages, and takes the pen, and writes the entry for the twentieth of May in the same hand and the same ink as all the rest of it. *Specimen Beta.* Not the other thing. He writes it slowly and it is nine lines long, and every word of it is true, and the whole of it is a lie, and it goes in easily, which is the worst of all of it.

He signs it. *P. Pobil.* The pen does not shake, which he resents.

Then he sits back and puts both hands over his face for a moment.

There is a silence of some length. He can hear Tar not knowing what to do, which is a sound he did not previously know a man could make.

"It's the best result anyone at this institute has ever had," Tar says at last, "and you will never be able to publish a word of it."

"Get out of my sick bay."

"Yes."

And Reggie Tar gets up, and goes to the door, and on his way past he puts his hand on the back of Pascual's neck.

He leaves it there. Warm, thin, absolutely deliberate, for about as long as it takes to walk three steps on a jetty in the dark.

Then he takes it away, and says nothing, and goes out into the rain.

Pascual sits with the file in front of him and both hands flat on the bench and does not move for some time.

---

At half past eight the rain thins and the sun comes through it in the sickly way it does, and Pascual is out on the deck at Gamma's pen with the buckets, getting the second pen scrubbed and the gates checked because in ninety minutes there is going to be a new animal in it and Hester Bevan has ruled that the two of them will go in together, and if that works — if it works — then everything, every part of it, the whole of the last fortnight —

He hears John before he sees him, coming up the path from the huts, whistling, with the portfolio under his arm and the pencil case, because it is Thursday and he draws Beta on a Thursday.

"Morning," John calls. "Filthy day. Is our friend up and about? I want him before the Americans get here, they'll be under my feet all —"

He stops, three yards off, looking past Pascual at the empty pen.

Pascual straightens up with the brush in his hand.

"He got out in the night," he says. "He made the sea. Charles went in after him."

It comes out of him smooth as a spoon, in the right voice, with the right amount of nothing in it, and he watches the whole thing land on John's face and thinks, from somewhere far off: *so that is how it will be, then. That is how easily I can do it.*

Out beyond the reef, faint under the noise of the generator, an engine.
