# Ordinary Time

The first thing the fortnight does is take everything away.

He sleeps eleven hours on the Monday night, which he has not done since he was a student, and wakes on Tuesday at half past seven in broad daylight with the rain going and no idea in the world what he is supposed to do. He lies there for a while working out what is wrong with the morning.

Nothing is wrong with the morning. That is what is wrong with it.

He goes down to the pens and feeds two animals who are in better condition than any mermaid has ever been in a pen anywhere, and weighs one of them, and writes it down, and it takes him forty minutes, and then it is a quarter past eight in the morning and there is nothing else.

Jean sleeps until noon and comes to breakfast at lunchtime looking hollowed out and says, "Is anyone else absolutely furious?"

"Yes," says John, with his mouth full.

"Good. I don't know what about."

"Nor do I," John says.

---

The rains come in properly on the Wednesday, three days late, and after that it rains for eleven days out of fourteen: warm grey vertical rain that starts at four in the afternoon and goes on all night and drums on the iron so that the generator is nearly inaudible under it.

The tarp over Gamma's pen sags and has to be re-rigged twice. Chumley discovers rain and is beside himself about it, going round and round the pen at the surface with his face up, and Gamma tolerates this for approximately four days and then begins cuffing him out of the way, which Pascual records in the day book with more satisfaction than the entry strictly requires.

*Specimen designation:* the calf goes into the day book as *Chumley (Delta)* in John's round hand on the twenty-fourth of May and by the second of June nobody on the station, including the Colonel, calls him anything else.

Gamma is up eleven pounds on the twentieth of May. She has stopped lying on the bottom entirely.

---

The other thing the fortnight does is much worse and takes him about four days to identify.

For five days they were a machine. There was a thing to be done every hour, and the two of them did it together, and where they went and when they went there and how long they stayed was decided by the work and not by either of them, and a man could walk into the sick bay at eleven at night because there was a reason.

Now there is no reason.

Now it is an ordinary Thursday in the rain and Pascual Pobil is standing in his own dispensary at nine in the evening with a clean bench and nothing to do on it, discovering that he does not know how to want a thing without an emergency attached to it, and that neither does the other one, and that they have therefore not spoken to each other alone in three days.

It is ridiculous. He is forty-one. He goes and stands in the doorway and looks at the rain for a while, and does not go up the path.

At twenty past nine Reggie comes down it.

He is very wet and has plainly been standing about somewhere and has a rolled-up sheaf of somebody's arithmetic under his arm as a pretext, and he says, "I've got Jean's salinity figures," in the voice of a man saying something entirely different, and Pascual says, "Then bring them in," and shoots the bolt, and that is how they solve it, and neither of them says one word about it, then or ever.

---

They work.

That is the shape of the fortnight, in the end, and he is surprised by how much he likes it. Two hours at the bench most evenings with the rain going: Gamma's weights, Chumley's weights, surfacing intervals, the food figures, John's dated plates spread over everything with the corners weighed down by specimen jars.

Reggie is a much better statistician than he is and does not once make him feel it, and is wrong twice in one week about the sample size and admits it both times with enormous cheerfulness. Pascual is a better clinician and says so, loudly, and gets an *obviously* by way of reply.

On the second of June they get the food-intake curves for Gamma laid out beside Alpha's and Beta's, all three from the same institute in the same pens, and Reggie goes quiet, and then says, "Look at that. Look at *that*," and Pascual looks and does not see it for about four seconds and then does.

Alpha, alone: down and down and dead. Beta, alone, then not alone: down, then up the day Charles started sitting on the rail. Gamma, alone: down, and down, and then straight up off the bottom of the graph on the twenty-first of May like something released.

"It's the same curve," Reggie says. "It's the same curve three times."

He is standing at the bench with both hands flat on it and his hair in his eyes and he looks about nineteen, and Pascual thinks, with some care, that he had better not say anything at all for a moment.

---

The row happens on the fourth of June and it is about a name.

"I want Cholmondeley on it," Reggie says.

Pascual puts the pen down. "On the paper."

"Fourth author. Tar, Pobil, and the plates, and Cholmondeley. He did the intake work that all of this rests on — those are his figures, in his hand, every ounce of them, and the entire solitude hypothesis came out of his head before it came out of mine. I'll not put my name over the top of a dead man's result."

"No."

"Pascual —"

"*No.*" He is up off the stool. "You will put the name of a man who is officially drowned onto a paper about mermaid solitude, in a journal, where any person in the world may read it, three weeks after Spilsbury got on a boat saying he would think about all of this for ten years —"

"He'd expect it," Reggie says. "That's the point. Not putting him on it is the odd thing. Any reader who knows the station knows he did that work. Leaving him off says we've something to be quiet about."

"It says he is *dead*, which is what we have told the world, and dead men do not need to be advertised!"

"He isn't an advertisement, he's an author —"

"He is a *boy* who is somewhere in the sea," Pascual says, "and you want to put his name in a journal so that the record will be correct."

There is a pause of the wrong kind.

"Yes," Reggie says.

"*Ay, Dios mío.*"

"Yes, I do. Because it is his and because I am not going to be the man who took it." He has gone quite white round the mouth. "Do you know what else is going in that paper? The tables. My tables. Four years in print, everybody in the field citing them as though they described the animal, and the fact is they describe what happens when you put a social species in a concrete box on its own. I am going to have to say that. In writing. Under my own name, in a footnote that every man I trained with will read." He is not shouting. "I'm going to do it. It's the correct thing to do and I'll do it and I do not want any credit for it whatsoever. And I'll be damned if I do that piece of honesty and leave his name off the same page."

And Pascual, standing there furious in his own sick bay, discovers that he is also, simultaneously and inconveniently, enormously proud of him.

"It is still no," he says.

"Then give me a reason that isn't fear."

"Fear is a *reason* —"

"It's the only one you've got," Reggie says, "and you have been carrying it about for a fortnight, and I have watched you do it, and you may as well put it on the bench where I can see it."

Pascual stops.

He stands there for a moment with the rain going on the roof, and then he says, "Sit down."

---

The three pages have been in his shirt since the twentieth of May. He has moved them from shirt to shirt eleven times. Two of them are his own file sheets, soft at the crease and going brown at the fold. The third is a drawing.

He puts it on the bench and unfolds it and turns it round.

"John's," he says. "From the book, on the Friday, when I took the two plates out for Hester's envelope. Undated. Unnumbered. Drawn from memory, from inside the water, from about a yard away."

Reggie looks at it.

He does not say anything for a long time. Long enough that Pascual has time to watch him understand, in order: what the gesture is; that John drew it without being told; that John therefore had the whole thing before anyone told him anything; and — last, and it goes through his face like water going through sand — what it is a picture *of*.

Charles Cholmondeley with his hand flat on a mermaid's cheek and his fingers back into the hair, and the mermaid's hand up against his face in exactly the same way, and neither of them looking at anything else in the world.

"You've had this a fortnight," Reggie says.

"Yes."

"In your *shirt*."

"Yes."

"You told me about the file pages and the plates. You said, *other things*." His voice has gone very flat and very quiet, which is the register Pascual has learned to be frightened of. "You looked at me across this bench and said *other things* and let me think you meant a swab."

"Yes."

"Why."

"I do not know," Pascual says. "I have asked myself for a fortnight and I do not have a clean answer, and I am not going to invent one for you now." He makes himself go on. "When I unfolded it, I understood the whole of what that boy was, in about one second, standing at this bench. And I had made him eat, and made him sleep, and taken his spectacles off his face and cleaned them on my own shirt, for eleven months. And I never once asked him what was happening to him, because nobody told me anything was. You knew. Jean knew. Hester knew. All of you let me look after him without saying what I was looking after."

"Yes," Reggie says. "That's fair and I'll take it."

"That is the small one."

Reggie's head comes up.

"Here is the large one." Pascual puts both hands flat on the bench. "I have not said it since the night you gave him that chart, and I am going to say it now while there is nothing else to do.

"You took that boy off that animal and you put him in the other pen and you would not let him back. Eleven days. And I stood on the verandah and told you what it was doing, in front of the whole station, at a volume they could hear at the jetty, and you did not stop it. The animal went down and the boy went to pieces. And on the eighth night that animal hauled itself out of a pen on its arms and lay on hot concrete for six hours going into kidney failure, looking for him — and if the boy had not woken up it would have died there on the deck. You did that. Not Spilsbury. Not the Navy. *You.*"

The rain on the roof. Nothing else.

"Yes," Reggie says.

"And I have wanted to know for a month why."

"Because I had a result."

Pascual stops with his mouth open.

"That's the whole of it," Reggie says. "There is nothing underneath it. I had an animal that had beaten my own tables by four months and a postgraduate who could not account for it, and I had a hypothesis, and I could not leave it alone. I did not weigh the boy against the finding. I never got as far as weighing anything at all — that is what I want you to have, because it's worse and it's true. I have never in my life been able to leave a thing alone and I did not start that fortnight."

"You told him you regretted it."

"I did, and it's the truest sentence I have ever said out loud, and it isn't worth the paper."

Pascual leans back against the shelf and looks at him for a long moment, and something arrives that he does not enjoy.

"It is the same defect," he says slowly. "Every time. The trial. The notebook — *you would put every one of us in the water for that book*, and you said yes to me, in this room, without one second of hesitation. The boy's name on that paper. It is one thing wearing three coats and it is the only thing that has ever gone wrong with you."

"Yes."

"And you will do it again."

"Almost certainly," Reggie says. "I'd like to tell you otherwise. I've been thirty-four years and it has never once come out differently."

"Then I am going to be standing in front of you every time," Pascual says, "and I will be a great deal louder than I was in May, and you will not enjoy it."

"Good," Reggie says. "That is not an apology, before you tell me so."

"I was not going to bother."

Something happens at the corner of Reggie's mouth that is almost the smirk and is not quite.

Then he looks down at the drawing again, and it goes out of his face entirely.

"He's laughing," he says.

"Yes."

"I never saw him do that. Not once in eleven months. He came off that boat and he was frightened of me inside a fortnight and I never once — " He stops. He takes his glasses off and holds them. "Where does it go?"

"Nowhere," Pascual says. "It stays with me."

"That's not —"

"It is not a document, Reggie. It has no date and no number and it cannot go into any file in the world, and Hester has the book and the book has to be kept and I understand why. But somebody on this station should have one thing of that boy that is not evidence." He folds it, twice, and puts it back in his pocket, and looks at him. "You may say no. You have as much right to it as I have."

"No," Reggie says. "Keep it." And then, after a moment: "Would you show it to me sometimes?"

"*Ay.*" Pascual has to turn round and put the pen back in the jar and take rather a long time about it. "Yes. Yes, of course."

"One thing." Reggie is looking at the pocket. "It's John's."

"I know."

"He drew it, and you took it out of his book without asking him, and you've had it a fortnight." A pause. "I'm not making a point. I'm telling you that you'll have to ask him, because you're the one who can, and because he'll notice sooner or later and I'd rather he heard it from you."

Pascual stands there and discovers that he does not have a single thing to say to that.

"Yes," he says. "All right."

---

He asks John on the sixth of June, at the rail, in the rain, and makes himself do it without any preparation at all, because if he prepares it he will make it worse.

"John. On the Friday, when your book went up to Mrs Bevan, I took a sheet out of it. I have had it in my pocket for a fortnight and I did not tell you and I am not going to explain why."

The pencil stops.

"Which one?"

"You know which one."

John does not turn round. He looks at the pen for a while, at Gamma lying near the surface with the calf going round her.

"The undated one," he says.

"Yes. I should like to keep it, and I am asking you, and if you say no I will give it back to you this afternoon and that is the end of it."

A long pause. The rain does its work on the tarp.

"Keep it," John says. "It was never in the sequence anyway. It's not a record of anything, it's a — " He stops, and starts again. "I did it in one go the night after Jean sent me down to sit with Beta. I didn't see it happen; I saw about a second of it from thirty feet away and then she took him off to the bungalow. So I drew it to find out whether I'd seen what I thought I'd seen." He rubs at something on the paper with his thumb. "And I had."

"Yes."

"Keep it," he says again. "It's no use to me."

"John —"

"Anyway I don't need the sheet." He picks the pencil up. "It's in my head. It'll be in my head when I'm eighty. That's the thing nobody understands about it — I could do it again on Tuesday, and better."

And he goes back to Gamma's shoulder, and Pascual stands at the rail in the rain for a moment with his hands in his pockets, and does not know what to call the feeling and does not much like it.

---

The paper problem is solved on the seventh of June by Jean, in about forty seconds, over breakfast, having been told none of it.

"Put him on the fisheries paper," she says.

They look at her.

"The intake-and-residue methods paper. The dull one, that he actually did — he built the whole method, I watched him do it, he was cataloguing mermaid faeces at eight in the morning when the rest of us wouldn't touch it." She butters her toast. "*Cholmondeley and Leslie*, and I'll do the write-up, and it goes to a fisheries bulletin that four hundred people read and none of them are interesting. His sisters get an offprint with his name on the front of it. And the big paper says *the intake methods are those of Cholmondeley (1954)* in the second paragraph, cited like anybody else, which is exactly what you'd do for a man who'd gone off to another post, and no reader on earth looks twice at a citation."

There is a silence.

"That's better than mine," Reggie says.

"Yes," Jean says. "It usually is."

"And Jean is on the large paper," Pascual says.

"Jean is second author on the large paper and has been since the first of June." Reggie looks up and appears mildly puzzled to find three people looking at him. "She did Alpha's post-mortem, she built every graph in it, and the habitat section is entirely hers. I put her in a week ago."

"Second," says Jean.

"Where else would you be?"

"And where am I?" says Pascual, who would like this cleared up.

"Third."

"Put me fourth. Put me in the acknowledgements. I took blood out of an animal and I wrote down what colour it was; I did not do one line of the arithmetic and I am not going to stand about pretending I did."

"You'll be third," Reggie says, "and you can take it up with me in writing," and goes back to his egg.

There is a pause of a different quality.

"You put me in a week ago," Jean says, "and you did not *mention* it."

"You'd have seen it on the draft."

"Reggie. I have worked for you for four years and I have never once seen a draft."

"Ah," says Reggie.

Jean sets her knife down and looks at the ceiling for a moment, and Pascual watches a young woman decide against throwing a teapot.

"Right," she says. "Then Cholmondeley goes first on the fisheries paper and I go second, and I don't want a word about it. It was his method, and his sisters are going to open that envelope in Shropshire and see his name at the top of the page and not have to hunt for it." She stands and collects her plate. "And I should like the salt ponds as my own project with a line in the budget and my own name on it, and Charles's fish counts to go with them."

"Yes."

"Right," says Jean, and takes her toast away.

Pascual catches her by the door of the dining hut afterwards and does not know how to begin, and she says, "Don't."

"I was going to say —"

"I know what you were going to say and I'd rather you didn't, because if you say it out loud then I have to have known it, and I have spent three weeks very carefully not knowing anything at all." She looks at him. She is twenty-nine and she is going to be running an institute of her own inside fifteen years and Pascual would not care to be the man who got in her way. "He gave me a fish study and a budget line. That's what I wanted out of it. Everybody got something."

"Did they."

"Some of us got rather a lot," Jean says, and goes.

---

He says the other thing that evening, at the bench, because it has been sitting in him for a week and will not go away.

"Reggie. When this is published."

"Mm."

"Every institute in the world will be able to keep them alive."

The pen stops.

"Yes," Reggie says.

"They have been dying in boxes for thirty years, and that is the only reason there are not more of them in boxes. It is expensive and it is a bad business and the animal dies inside six months and you cannot easily get another." Pascual puts both hands on the bench. "And we are about to publish the method. Two to a pen and they live. Do you understand what I am telling you? They will take *more* of them. Not fewer. More. There will be a mermaid in a pen in Florida inside three years and one in Naples the year after."

The rain on the iron. Reggie sets the pen down and looks at the graph in front of him for a while.

"Yes," he says. "I know. I worked it out on the Tuesday and I have not been able to put it down since."

"And?"

"And I'm going to publish it."

"*Ay.*"

"Because Gamma is alive," Reggie says, "and because there are four of them in pens on this earth that I know of today, and every one of those four is going to die of it inside the year unless somebody tells the men holding them what the matter is. Because I am not able to know how to stop a thing and say nothing." He takes his glasses off. "Which is the same defect you put on this bench in front of me on Friday, and I have not got a better one to offer you, and I am not going to pretend the arithmetic is clean. It is not clean. It comes out badly either way and I have picked the way that saves the ones who are alive now."

Pascual thinks about it for a long time, longer than he wants to, with the rain going.

He thinks about a female mermaid squaring her shoulders on the bottom of a pen. He thinks about Alpha, whom he never met, and Beta on the concrete, and about a whole species of animals being put one at a time into boxes for thirty years by men who thought it was pneumonia.

"Do you want me to hold it?" Reggie says. "I will, if you ask me to. A year. Two. I'd not enjoy it and I'd do it."

"No," Pascual says at last. "*Dios* help us both. No."

---

On the eighth of June John brings him a letter.

It has come in with the mail boat and it is from Woods Hole, forwarded, and it is two pages in a fast clear hand and it is perfectly friendly. It says that Mr Clarke's plate sequence is the finest scientific illustration its writer has seen in fifteen years. It says that if Mr Clarke should ever wish for an introduction to certain people, the writer would be very glad to make it, at any time, with no obligation of any kind. It says nothing else at all.

"He's not asking me for anything," John says.

"No."

"That's the bit I don't like."

"No," Pascual says. "Nor do I."

John folds it up. He has changed in three weeks; there is something set about him now that was not there in May, and Pascual is not sure whether he did that to him or Spilsbury did or Charles did by drowning.

"What will you do?"

"Nothing," John says. "Yet."

And Pascual, who has heard that exact construction from that exact source, feels the back of his neck go cold, and says, "Show me anything else that comes."

"Obviously," says John, and goes off to the pen with his box.

---

It rains all that night and they are in the small room with the window and the knot in the mosquito net, and afterwards Reggie is lying on his front with his arms folded under his head and Pascual is doing the thing he does, which is running a thumb slowly up and down the ridge of the man's spine and pretending he has not noticed.

"I want to ask you something," he says, "and it is not about Spilsbury."

"Mm."

"When he came ashore, you put on a — " He does not know the word he wants in either language. "A whole different man. For four days. You did it in one second on that jetty and you were *good* at it, and it fitted you, and I could not find you anywhere inside it."

The muscle under his thumb goes tight and then, deliberately, loose again.

"Yes," Reggie says, into his arms.

"Is he still there?"

"Yes."

"Always?"

"Always." He turns his head so that one eye is visible. "That's the whole of it. He doesn't go anywhere. And I didn't build him, which is the part you want and the part I mind — he was *issued* to me. School at eight, and Cambridge after that, and by the time you are twelve you could not get the thing off with a chisel. There were four hundred of us and every one of us had one. It is the most useful object an Englishman owns and I was extremely good with mine, and I was never once required to think about it, because it is not a trick, it is simply what a room does when you walk into it."

Pascual keeps his hand where it is and says nothing.

"And then somewhere about twenty-six I noticed what I'd been using it for." Reggie's voice does not change at all. "Which was not dinner parties. It is how you are given a room. It's how you are given a budget, and material, and a signature on a form, and how a man twice your age who ought to have stopped you says *of course, Tar, whatever you need*. Every single thing I did at that place, I did because I could walk into an office and be liked for ninety seconds. It was not the science that got me any of it. It was that." A pause. "So I stopped. I stopped all of it, I came four thousand miles, and I stood on a jetty for two years being rude to postgraduates, and I thought that if I never did it again it would go away."

"And it took eleven seconds on the jetty."

"It took eleven seconds and it fitted perfectly, and it will fit perfectly when I'm sixty. That's what I've known since the twentieth of May."

"You did not eat for five days."

"That was the boy," Reggie says. "The coat is why I didn't sleep."

Pascual thinks about that for a while, with his hand flat between the man's shoulder blades.

"Then I would like an arrangement," he says.

"An arrangement."

"When you put him on again — and you will, because sooner or later someone will come and it will be the correct thing to do — you tell me first. One sentence. *I am going to do it now.* And then afterwards you come here and you sit in that chair and you are dull and rude to me for an hour until you are yourself again." He shrugs. "That is all. I am not asking you to be a better man. I am asking to be told."

Reggie is quiet for such a long time that Pascual begins to think he has said the wrong thing.

"Nobody has ever offered me anything like that in my life," Reggie says.

"Then it is about time somebody did."

---

There is one more thing and it happens on a Tuesday, at half past ten at night, and Pascual is not remotely ready for it.

Reggie sits up, and finds his glasses, and reaches for his trousers over the end of the cot, and takes a folded sheet of paper out of the pocket, and hands it over without a word.

It is covered in his small ruled handwriting. It is a column of figures.

Pascual holds it under the lamp and reads it and it takes him some time to understand what it is, because it is, on the face of it, a costing.

The remaining term of the Institute's lease on the cay. Two salaries, one senior and one veterinary, with increments, out to 1961. The rent of a house on the north side of the harbour at the main island, with a note beside it: *two men, one household, unremarkable if one of them is the medical officer and the other is the director.* The cost of a boat. A list of four other institutions in three countries, with a note against each one about accommodation. A note that a bachelor scientific establishment in the tropics is the least-watched place on earth and that this is a resource and should be treated as one.

And at the bottom of the page, underlined twice, a year.

1984.

"You said thirty years," Reggie says. He is sitting up with his arms round his knees and he is not looking at Pascual, he is looking at the window. "On the Sunday night, when you were being practical at me. You said it in a way that let me off, and I heard it, and I have thought about almost nothing else since."

"Reggie —"

"I don't say things —"

"You have said a great deal." Pascual sits up on one elbow. "You told me about that place standing up in my own sick bay for a quarter of an hour and did not stop once. You told me about the coat. You told me on Friday that you had no defence and you did not go looking for one. A month ago I could not get four words out of you at a meal and now I cannot make you leave off, so do not lie there and tell me you do not say things."

"That's not the same thing," Reggie says. "That is all *account*. I can give an account of anything; I'm extremely good at it; I have been trained since I was eight to stand up in a room and give an account, and a confession is only an account with worse material in it. I'm not telling you it was cheap — that one took something out of me I shan't get back, and I knew it would before I opened my mouth. But there is a *form* for it. I know how it's done. What I cannot do is ask for something, because there is no form for that." He has hold of his own wrist. "I have never once in my life asked anybody for a thing I wanted and been told yes. I don't know the procedure. And I am not going to learn it at half past ten at night on an iron cot." His jaw is tight. "So no. I don't say things. I've had thirty-four years to fix it and I have not fixed it. And so I did the only thing I know how to do — I costed it. It's what I'd have done with a research programme and it is the best I have got, and if it's an insult, then I'm sorry, and you may put it on the fire."

The rain on the iron. Somebody's shutter banging, far off.

Pascual looks at the year at the bottom of the page.

He is not able to say anything for a while, and when he can, he does not trust himself with English.

"*Sí. Treinta años,*" he says, into the man's shoulder.

Reggie does not ask what it means. Pascual does not tell him, and lies there noting, with some disgust, that he has just committed himself out loud to thirty years in a language the other man does not have, and that he has still not said the smaller thing in any language at all, and that this is precisely the wrong way round.

Then he makes himself do the sensible thing, because one of them has to and it is not going to be the one holding the pencil.

"Reggie. How long have we been doing this?"

"Nineteen days."

"You have the number."

"I have the number," Reggie says, "and I've had it every day since, and I'd have it if you woke me at four in the morning."

"Nineteen days." Pascual sits up properly and puts the paper on his knee. "Of which five were a fraud, a funeral and a man in the guest hut, and the fortnight since has been the first ordinary time either of us has had. And in nineteen days you have costed me out to nineteen eighty-four."

"Yes."

"That is not sensible."

"No."

"People are wrong about each other in nineteen days as a matter of routine. I have been wrong in nineteen days. So have you — you told me on Saturday that you cannot read a man you worked beside for four years." He taps the paper. "This is a beautiful piece of work and it may also be the sort of thing a man does at two in the morning in the third week."

"Yes," Reggie says.

"Then say something that is not yes."

Reggie is quiet for a moment. Then he says: "I've never in my life been able to do anything by halves. It isn't a virtue and it isn't romantic; it's a defect, and it has cost other people a great deal, and you had it on the bench in front of me on Friday and you were right about every word." He looks up. "The trial was that. Wanting the boy's name on the paper is that. Publishing a method that will fill four new pens is that. And this is that, and I'd rather say so than let you find it out in June. I'm not putting it forward as a recommendation. I'm telling you what you'd be taking on."

"*Ay.*"

"I don't have the setting. I've never once wanted a thing moderately and I do not know how to want you for a year." He turns the paper over on his knee. "So I did the whole of it on paper, because on paper it can be looked at, and if it's mad you can say so and I'll not argue. I'd rather be told I'm mad than pretend I'm not."

"*Ay.*"

"You may say a year."

"I am going to say something better than a year," Pascual says, "because I have been thinking about it as well, and I am older than you and considerably more of a coward." He holds out his hand. "Give me the pencil."

He puts the paper on his knee, and under the underlined year at the bottom, in his blunt hand, he writes: **June 1955 — review.**

"There. In one year, in this room, we sit down, both of us, and we say honestly whether it is still true. Not whether we are still frightened, which we will be. Whether it is *true*. And if either one of us says no then that is the end of it and there is no argument and no scene and nobody is a villain." He hands the pencil back. "That is what I can give you tonight and I am not going to pretend to more."

Reggie takes the paper and looks at it for rather a long time.

"That's an interim analysis," he says.

"It is whatever you like."

"No — no, that is actually correct practice. You do not run a trial to term without a prespecified interim, it's the only honest way to do it, you fix the point *before* you have any data because otherwise you'll simply keep going until it tells you what you —" and he is off, sitting up naked on an iron cot at eleven at night explaining trial design to a veterinary surgeon of nineteen years' standing, with his hair on end and his glasses in one hand.

Pascual lies back and lets him do it and does not stop smiling once.

"— and it is the first sensible thing anybody has said to me about my personal life in thirty-four years," Reggie finishes.

"How many people have said things to you about your personal life?"

"Two. Including you."

"*Ay, Dios.*"

Then Pascual reaches over and takes the paper back and holds it under the lamp.

"You have not crossed the other one out," he says.

There is a pause.

"No," Reggie says.

"Good."

---

"You have put the boat before the house."

"The boat is essential."

"The boat is *not* essential —"

"You've seen that harbour road in October —"

"I have seen that harbour road in October, I was *on* it in October, and I am telling you that a man who cannot cook does not save money by owning a boat."

"I can cook."

"You cannot cook. In thirty years I shall be seventy-one and still doing every meal in the house."

"Yes," Reggie says.

And Pascual stops with his mouth open and looks at him.

"Yes, you will," Reggie says, entirely calmly, "and we've just agreed to review the position in June, so you needn't look at me like that," and takes the paper out of his hand and puts it on the floor and his glasses on top of it.

"You are not a serious man."

"I'm the most serious man you know."

"That is true and it is the whole of the problem —" and then Pascual does not finish the sentence, because he has been taken by the back of the neck, and it turns out that Reggie Tar has been learning things. He does not kiss him. He puts his mouth just under the angle of the jaw, on the place he found on the second night and has been extremely careful with ever since, and stays there, and does not hurry about it at all.

The sound that comes out of Pascual is not one he has any say in. It arrives from somewhere well below the ribs.

"*Ay* — "

"Say the thing you said in Spanish." It goes straight into his throat; he feels it in his back teeth. "The three words. Again."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because you will make me write it down," Pascual says, with his eyes shut and one hand already fisted in the man's hair, "and put a *date* on it," and reaches across him, and puts the lamp out.

---

Later, when the rain has settled into the steady sort that will go on until morning, he lies in the dark with his shirt on the back of the door, three folded pages in the pocket, one of them a picture of a boy laughing in the water with his hand on somebody's face.

*One year,* he thinks. *Bueno. We shall see about the rest of it.*

Down at the pens, in the rain, in the dark, two animals who are not alone move about in the water; and one of them, in about four years, will be big enough to name a paper after.
