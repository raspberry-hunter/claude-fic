# Negative Result

At twenty past five he comes down the path with the torch off, because there is enough light now, and there is a man in Gamma's pen.

For about a second and a half he does not believe it.

The linen jacket is folded on the rail with the shoes squared underneath it and the watch on top, and Dr Spilsbury is in the water at the deep end in his undershorts with one arm hooked through the ladder, perfectly calm, holding absolutely still.

Gamma is on the bottom in the corner. She is not lying down. She is up off the floor of the pen with her tail under her and her shoulders squared and the calf is directly behind her, jammed into the angle of the wall, and she has put herself between him and the ladder.

"*You get out of that water.*"

"Dr Pobil —"

"*Ahora mismo. Fuera. Out.*"

He is at the rail with his hands on it and he does not remember covering the ground. Spilsbury comes up the ladder without any fuss at all, and Pascual has hold of his upper arm before both feet are on the concrete, hauling him back from the edge, and then lets go as if the arm were hot, because he has just discovered what his hands were about to do.

"How long."

"Twenty-five minutes."

"*Twenty-five —*"

"I had my hand on her for about a minute of it," Spilsbury says. He is dripping and entirely composed and reaching for his towel, and he is not in the least ashamed, which is the part Pascual will remember. "She let me. She wasn't happy but she let me. And there was nothing. Not a flicker. Not an image, not a sensation, not a scrap of anything at all." He rubs the towel over his head. "Which of course proves nothing whatever. Cholmondeley was plainly unusual, and one negative result on one man is not a refutation, it's an anecdote. But I had to have the anecdote. You do see."

"I see that you were told no. By me, at four o'clock yesterday, and by Colonel Bevan at half past four."

"Yes."

"And that you waited until dark and came anyway."

"Yes," Spilsbury says, quite simply, and goes on drying his ear.

---

The Colonel comes down at six in his dressing gown, which nobody on this island has ever seen, and stands at the rail and looks at the pen for a while, and then at the folded jacket, and then at Spilsbury.

"Dr Spilsbury. You will not go into my pens again."

"No, sir."

"You will be off this island tomorrow at eleven."

"Yes."

"Good," says the Colonel, and goes back up the path.

And that is the whole of it. Pascual stands there with his heart still going and thinks, not for the first time this week: *I have been living beside these people for a year and I did not know what any of them were.*

Afterwards, when Spilsbury has gone up to dress, he gets down on the deck at the deep end and watches the two of them for twenty minutes, and Gamma stays exactly where she is, squared, with the calf behind her, and does not come off the bottom for fish.

*She put herself in front of him,* he thinks, through the anger, in the cold flat part of his brain that never stops taking notes. *A female with no relation to that calf, nine days off her own death, put her body between him and a strange animal at the ladder.*

Nobody has ever recorded that. Nobody has ever had two of them in a pen to record it in.

He wants very badly to tell somebody, and there is exactly one person on this island he wants to tell, and he cannot go and find him at ten past six in the morning in front of a man who is leaving tomorrow.

---

Breakfast is quiet. Spilsbury eats a great deal and apologises to Hester for the disturbance with perfect grace and she accepts it in a voice you could have chilled a room with.

Then he puts his cup down and says, "There's no notebook in the inventory."

Pascual keeps his hands where they are.

"Sorry?"

"Cholmondeley's effects. I read the inventory last night — no notebook." Spilsbury looks round the table, mildly, at nobody in particular. "A man who weighed faecal residue to the ounce every day for eleven months and wrote *no change* in the behaviour column two hundred times running was keeping a private book. Men like that always are; it's practically diagnostic. There'll be nine months of daily observations in it and it is the most valuable object on this station." He picks up his cup again. "So where is it?"

Nobody moves.

Pascual can feel Jean go still on his left. Down the table Hester is buttering toast with enormous attention. Reggie draws breath, and Pascual knows — the way you know a man is going to be sick before he does — that whatever comes out of Reggie's mouth in the next second is going to be smooth and quick and slightly too quick, and that Spilsbury will hear the speed of it and not the words —

"He kept it at the pen," John says.

Everything stops.

John is not looking up. He is spreading marmalade with the side of his knife in a bored, put-upon sort of way, and his ears have gone red, and his voice is perfectly level.

"He hadn't slept in our hut since February," he says. "He slept on a pad at Beta's pen and he wrote it up down there at night; I could see the lamp from the doorway. It was never in the hut after Lent — I know, because I looked for a pencil in his things in March and got told off about it." He puts the knife down. "And everything loose on that deck went into the sea that night. The tarp went. His pad went. His towel went. If you want it, it's four hundred fathoms down about a mile east of here, along with the rest of it."

There is a silence of perhaps three seconds.

"Ah," says Spilsbury. "That's a pity."

"Yes," says John. "It is, rather. It was mostly about *me*, probably. He hated me."

And he picks up his toast and eats it.

Pascual sits absolutely still with his hands round his cup and thinks: *he did that in one second, from a standing start, with no warning, and he did it better than I could have done it in a week.*

---

He finds John at nine, at the rail, drawing Gamma with the calf's face just visible behind her shoulder.

"John."

"Don't," John says.

Pascual stops.

"I mean it. Don't say anything about it." The pencil goes on moving. "I have thought about it all morning and I have decided that I don't want to know. Do I."

"No," Pascual says. "You don't."

"Right." A pause. The pencil goes on. Then, very fast, without looking up: "Is he alive?"

"I don't know," Pascual says.

It is the truth. He is enormously glad, standing there, that it happens to be the truth.

John nods twice. He works on the shoulder for a while, and rubs at it with his thumb, and makes it worse, and swears under his breath.

"Was he happy?" he says. "At the end. Only — I was fairly foul to him for two years, and I've been lying awake about it, and if you tell me he was miserable the whole time I shall simply have to live with that, but I'd rather know."

Pascual thinks about a man in the water with an animal, laughing, being splashed. He thinks about a man who could not be made to leave the pen long enough to eat a plate of food.

"He was happy," he says. "That one I know. I saw it every day for a week and I have never seen anything like it in my life."

John nods again and does not say anything, and Pascual puts a hand briefly on the back of his shoulder — the boy is twenty-four and nobody has touched him kindly since Charles went — and leaves him to it.

Behind him, after about ten yards, John says: "And don't any of you ever thank me."

---

The samples go into their boxes at three, in the sick bay, with Pascual watching every single item, because nothing leaves this station without his hands on it.

It is all perfectly proper. The pen scrapings, the epidermis off the lip, the swab from the urine stain, duplicates of four blood films. He has written the manifest himself and Spilsbury countersigns it and they are, for half an hour, two professionals doing an ordinary thing, and it is almost restful.

Then Spilsbury sets down the pen.

"Dr Pobil. Tell me one thing, and I give you my word I'll not raise any of it again while I'm on this island."

"That is not a fair way to begin a question."

"No," Spilsbury agrees. "It isn't." He straightens up. "Did the animal die?"

The generator. The rain starting on the roof. Somewhere down the path John shouting something to Jean about the light.

"Yes," Pascual says.

Spilsbury looks at him.

He is a man with an expressive face and he has known it since he was six years old, and he has never once in his life been able to do anything about it, and he stands in his own sick bay in front of a boxed set of true samples and lets it be looked at.

"Thank you," Spilsbury says.

He picks up the pen and finishes the manifest and asks whether the paraffin bath is really 1930 or whether it only looks it, and Pascual answers him, and they discuss the paraffin bath for four minutes.

*He has it,* Pascual thinks, the whole time. *He came in here to look at my face while I said one word and now he has it and he has not got a single piece of paper.*

---

Supper is the last one and it is almost pleasant, which is obscene.

Spilsbury tells a very funny story about a walrus at Woods Hole. The Colonel unbends about a quarter of an inch. Jean gets him talking about a fellow at Plymouth she wants an introduction to and gets the introduction, in writing, on the spot, because she is going to be running an institute herself in fifteen years and she has never once let a thing like that go past.

Gamma has eaten twice and come off the bottom. Pascual reports this to the table, since he is asked, and does not mention what he watched at half past five, because that observation is his and he is not giving it to a man who is leaving in the morning.

Reggie says almost nothing all evening and does not once do the voice.

---

He comes to the sick bay at half past eleven and stands in the middle of the floor with his hands behind his back.

"Nicholson," Pascual says.

"Yes."

"Sit down before you tell me."

Reggie sits on the low chair, which puts him at a disadvantage, and takes it without complaining, which tells Pascual more about the state of him than anything he is going to say.

"Nicholson was a scientific officer at the labs. He was — " He stops. Starts again. "Four years ago he was arrested in a public lavatory in Camberwell. He was fined and he lost his position and his wife left him and I believe he is in Southport now, doing something with paint."

"Ah."

"He was a good man and not a fool. He was simply unlucky once." Reggie is looking at the floor between his shoes. "And that is not why Spilsbury told the story. Or it may be. I don't know."

"Reggie."

"I've known him eleven years." He says it fast. "We worked in adjoining rooms for four of them. He is the most interesting man I have ever met and I have never been able to read him for a single minute of it, and there was — " A pause of about three seconds. "There was an evening, in 'forty-six, that has never been mentioned by either of us since. Nothing happened. Something very nearly happened. I have never known which of us stopped it and I have never known what he understood by it, and I have arranged eleven years of my life around never finding out."

He stops. The rain does its work on the roof.

"So when you ask me what he was doing at that table," Reggie says, "the honest answer is that it might have been a threat, or a warning, or an offer, or a man saying an unlucky thing at dinner because he has never in his life known what lands. And I cannot tell you which. I have sat in rooms with him for eleven years and I cannot tell you which."

Pascual thinks about this for a while.

Then he says: "Do you want to stop?"

Reggie's head comes up.

"You are asking me what he meant," Pascual says, "and that is not the question in this room. The question is whether you want to stop, tonight, for good, because a man told a story about Camberwell over the wine. You may say yes. I would not think less of you and I would not raise it again. I am forty-one and I know exactly what this costs and I knew it on a jetty in February."

"No," Reggie says.

"Say it properly."

"No. I don't want to stop." He has gone rather white. "Which is an appalling piece of arithmetic, and I have done it, and it comes out the same every time I do it."

"*Bueno.*" Pascual pulls the stool round and sits down on it in front of him, close enough that their knees are nearly touching, which is as far as he intends to go tonight with a man in the guest hut awake. "Then here is what we do, and it is not brave and you will not like it. He is off this island at eleven tomorrow. Until then you do not come to this room again, and I do not come to yours."

"It's twenty to twelve."

"Then it is an easy rule and even you will manage it." He does not smile. "And after tomorrow we go on, and we are careful, and we are *dull*, and we behave, in front of every other person on this station, exactly as we have behaved for a year, which as it happens is very badly."

Reggie makes a sound that is almost a laugh.

"And that will do for the next thirty years," Pascual says, "and I have thought about it, and it will."

"He could have finished me eleven years ago."

"That is not a comfort," Pascual says. "That is a man keeping something."

"Yes." Reggie takes his glasses off, and holds them, and does not clean them. "Yes. I know."

---

There is one more thing and Pascual has been saving it and finds he is going to spend it anyway.

"I asked you twice this week what you did at that place."

"Yes."

"I am asking a third time."

Reggie is quiet for a long moment.

"When he's off the island," he says. "I'll tell you the whole of it. I'd rather not have it in a room while he's still on the same piece of land, and that isn't an evasion, and if you want to make me say it now I will."

Pascual looks at him: the wrists, the flat mouth, the glasses held in both hands. A man who will not burn nine months of a dead boy's handwriting to save any of their necks, and who told him so to his face, and who is not sorry.

*You would put every one of us in the water for that book,* he thinks. *And that is not finished, and I am not going to start it tonight either. We are becoming a pair of people who put things in a drawer.*

"All right," he says. "Wednesday. After the mail boat."

"Wednesday."

"Now go to bed, and go the north way past the generator, and do not stand about."

Reggie stands up. At the door he stops, and turns round, and says: "Gamma. This morning. You saw something."

"Yes."

"You didn't say it at supper."

"No."

"Good," Reggie says, and there is something in his voice that Pascual would very much have liked to have got closer to. "Tell me on Wednesday."

Then he goes.

---

He puts the lamp out at midnight and stands in the doorway for a minute in the warm dark, listening to the rain finish.

Down at the end of the line of huts the guest hut is dark.

It has burned until three every night since the man came, and tonight it is out, because he is packed and his boxes are corded and his manifest is signed and there is nothing left on this island that he needs to read.

Pascual looks at it for a while.

Then he goes to bed, and does not sleep for a long time, and thinks about a female mermaid squaring her shoulders on the bottom of a concrete pen and putting her body between a stranger and somebody else's child.
