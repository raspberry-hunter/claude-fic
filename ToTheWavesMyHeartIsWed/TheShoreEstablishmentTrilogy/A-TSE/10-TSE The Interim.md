# The Interim

A year does a great deal to an animal.

Gamma is thirteen months paired and she is enormous. She is the oldest mermaid ever held anywhere by a margin of about nine months, and she is bored, which is a complaint Pascual has never in his life been able to make about a captive merperson and which he makes daily, loudly, to anybody who will hold still. Chumley has put on twenty-two inches and can take a fish out of the air at four feet and has learned to bang the ladder with the flat of his tail when he wants attention, which is always.

The station has three more men than it did. There is money, for the first time since the reclusive millionaire, and money means people, and people mean that Pascual now has a technician, a boy from the village called Nestor with excellent hands, and that he no longer does everything twice because there is nobody else to do it once.

The paper came out in March.

He has read the letters. Everybody has read the letters; Reggie leaves them about the office in a way that is not accidental. Two men wrote to say they had always suspected the tables were an artefact and were glad somebody had had the nerve. One wrote a full page to say that Tar had, with a single footnote, removed the only quantitative foundation the field possessed and put nothing in its place, and Reggie read that one aloud at supper twice, with relish, and has pinned it above his desk.

And there are two other letters, which do not get left about.

---

The first is from a marine station in Florida that would like to buy a pair.

It says so in those words. *We should be glad to purchase a breeding pair, or failing that a single animal with a view to obtaining a second,* and it goes on to explain, courteously, that they have the capital, the frontage and the water, and that they propose to follow the Tar–Leslie–Pobil protocol exactly.

The second is from a ministry, by way of a naval attaché, and it is much shorter, and it proposes that the Martin should train men.

*It would be of considerable value if officers and scientific staff from associated establishments might attend at your Institute for periods of instruction in the husbandry methods described.* And then a figure, which Pascual, who has spent fifteen months watching Hester Bevan make one pound do the work of four, understood the significance of immediately.

Both have been on Reggie's desk for three weeks.

Pascual has not asked about them. This is deliberate and it is costing him something. He said, in that same sick bay, that he would be standing in front of the man every time and that he would be louder than he was in May — and he has decided, on his own, that a letter a man has not answered for three weeks is a man arguing with himself, and that he is not going to interrupt.

He gives it until Wednesday.

---

The other thing about the year is that it has been extremely dull, and that this has turned out to be the best year of his life.

They are careful. They are so careful that it is almost funny. Reggie is short with him in public and Pascual grumbles about Reggie in public and neither of them has to act, because both things are also true; they row about the sample sizes, about the feed budget, about whether Nestor may be trusted with the centrifuge, about the boat, always about the boat. Jean rolls her eyes at the pair of them across the table twice a week. Nobody sees anything, because there is nothing to see that was not there before.

And then at half past ten most nights there is a door with a good Admiralty bolt on it.

In March he found a Spanish grammar under the man's cot when he was looking for a bootlace. It was a cheap one, ordered by post, and the spine was broken in three places and there were pencil marks all through it. He put it back exactly where it had been and said nothing at all, and assumed it was for the fishing crews, and thought about it perhaps once a week for four months.

---

The review is at eight o'clock on the fourteenth of June, and they have both known the date since the ninth of June last year, and neither of them has mentioned it once in twelve months.

Pascual spends the afternoon being unbearable. He redoes the whole of Chumley's growth series, which does not need redoing. He shouts at Nestor about a rack of tubes and then apologises within the minute, which is not like him and which Nestor, bless the boy, has the sense to let go past.

At ten to eight he scrubs the bench down and puts the lamp where the light is good, and then feels ridiculous, and moves it back.

At eight o'clock exactly there is a knock at his own door, which has not happened in a year.

Reggie is shaved. He is wearing a clean shirt. He is carrying a folder.

"*Ay, Dios mío,*" says Pascual.

"Don't."

"You have brought a *folder.*"

"There are three items," Reggie says, and shoots the bolt behind him, and sits down in the low chair with the folder on his knee like a man about to present figures to a board.

---

"Item one," Reggie says.

"Reggie —"

"If you interrupt me at item one I shall lose my nerve entirely and we shall be here until Thursday."

"All right."

Reggie looks at the folder and does not open it.

"A year ago," he says, "you said that in one year, in this room, we would sit down and say honestly whether it was still true. Not whether we were frightened. Whether it was true." He clears his throat. "I have thought about how to answer that for eleven months and I have four drafts, and they are all appalling, so I am going to say it badly.

"It is still true. It is more true. I have been happier in the last twelve months than in the whole of the rest of my life put together, and I include the entire period of my education and every result I have ever had, and I am aware that is a considerable claim and I have checked it. And I do not want to review it again. Ever. I would like to strike the whole procedure and take the other figure at the bottom of the page, and I have wanted that since about the third week of last July, and I have not said so because we had an agreement and I was not going to be the man who broke it in month two."

He stops. His ears have gone red.

"That is item one," he says.

Pascual sits on the stool and looks at him for a moment.

"Yes," he says.

"Yes what?"

"Yes, it is still true. Yes, take the other figure. Yes to all of it. *Sí a todo.*" He puts his hands on his knees. "Was that the whole of item one? Because you did not eat your supper, and you have been shaved since six, and I have had to watch it."

Reggie's head comes up rather fast.

"Item two," Pascual says, "if you please."

---

Item two is the house.

It is entirely practical and takes eleven minutes and Pascual enjoys every second of it. The lease on the north-side house is up in November and the man who holds it is going home to Barbados. Reggie has been to look at it twice, on Institute business, both times with a witness. There is a garage that would take a boat.

"I am not discussing the boat tonight."

"I've costed the boat again —"

"I am *not* discussing the boat tonight."

"Fine." Reggie makes a small mark on the folder. "Then that's item two, and it's carried, subject to the boat."

"It is not subject to the boat."

"Everything is subject to the boat," says Reggie, and turns to item three, and stops.

---

He does not say anything for long enough that Pascual sits up.

"Item three," Reggie says. Then: "I've been learning Spanish."

"I know."

"You —"

"The grammar under your cot. In March. I was looking for a bootlace and it fell out and I put it back and I have said nothing about it for four months, which you may take as evidence of my character."

"Ah," says Reggie.

"You have a shocking accent and Nestor thinks you are wonderful."

"Nestor is being paid." He takes his glasses off. "Pascual. I could have looked it up in about eleven seconds. I've had the dictionary since last August. Three words, and I know two of them now without any dictionary at all, and I have known for months roughly what shape the third one is."

"Then why have you not —"

"Because you said it to me," Reggie says. "You didn't give it to me. You said it into my shoulder in the dark in a language I hadn't got, and then you did not tell me, and I decided — " he turns the glasses over in his hands " — I decided that taking it out of a book would be a kind of theft. So I have not. For a year. And now it is item three, and I am asking you, and you may say no and I will not ask again."

Pascual finds that he has to look at the floor for a moment.

Then he says: "*Sí. Treinta años.*"

There is a pause of about a second and a half while a man with a shocking accent and a broken-spined grammar assembles three words he already knew.

"...Thirty years," Reggie says.

"Yes."

"You said *thirty years*."

"Yes."

"That *night*."

"Yes."

"You accepted thirty years, out loud, in June of nineteen fifty-four, at approximately eleven o'clock —"

"It was later than eleven."

"— and then you *sat up*," Reggie says, and he is on his feet now, and Pascual has to put his hand over his own mouth, "and you looked me in the face, and you told me it was not sensible, and you gave me a lecture about nineteen days, of which five were a fraud and a funeral, and then you sold me a twelve-month trial —"

"I did."

"— *while already having accepted the thirty years!*"

"Yes."

"That is the most outrageous thing anybody has ever done to me."

"It is not," says Pascual, "and you know exactly what is, and it happened in this room and I have forgiven you for it."

"Don't change the — " Reggie stops, and points at him. "Don't. You enjoyed that. You have been enjoying that for a *year*."

"*Every day.*" Pascual takes his hand away from his mouth. He is not managing his face at all and has no intention of starting. "Every day, Reggie. At breakfast. Across the table, while you were being short with me about the centrifuge. It has been the great pleasure of my life."

"You are a horrible man."

"Come here."

"You will not distract me. You let me stand at that rail on the twentieth of March and explain interim analysis to two new postgraduates —"

"*Come here.*"

"— and give a speech about *prespecified endpoints* —"

"You enjoyed the prespecified endpoints enormously," Pascual says. "You lectured me for four minutes, naked, on an iron cot."

"Three."

"Four. I counted, because I could not believe it was happening." He gets up off the stool. "And I let you, and I would let you again, and that is precisely the point, so sit down and let me tell you why I did it, because you have not asked me and you are about to."

Reggie sits down. He does it fast and without dignity, which Pascual will be thinking about kindly for some years.

"Because you had just told me," Pascual says, "that you have never once in your life been able to do a thing by halves, and that it is a defect, and that it has cost other people a very great deal. And you were right, and I had watched it cost a boy and an animal in the same fortnight. And you had put a piece of paper on my knee with a year on it that you will not see until you are sixty-four." He shrugs. "So I said yes to myself in the dark, where it could not hurt anybody, and then I put a brake on it in the daylight. Because one of us had to, and because you had just finished telling me that you have never in your life been the one who does."

The rain has not come yet. It is nearly the season and the air is standing still and hot in the little room and the generator is doing what it always does.

"Yes," Reggie says. "All right. Yes."

"And I have had a year of watching, and you have not once tried to talk me out of a *revisar*, and you did not break the agreement in month two though you say you wanted to, and here you are with a *folder*." Pascual spreads his hands. "So. The brake comes off. That is my answer to item one, and it was my answer a year ago, and I am sorry for the twelve months and I am not sorry at all."

---

Reggie takes the costing sheet out of the folder.

It is soft at the folds and there is a ring on one corner where somebody put a cup down. *June 1955 — review*, in Pascual's blunt hand, under the year underlined twice.

Pascual holds out his hand for the pencil, and Reggie gives it to him, and he draws one flat line through *review* and writes beside it, in the same hand: **taken.**

"You can't strike out a prespecified interim," Reggie says immediately. "The whole point of prespecifying is that you cannot go back and —"

"I can and I have."

"That is *exactly* the practice I was complaining about in the Naples paper —"

"Reggie."

"It's methodologically indefensible —"

"It is," Pascual agrees, "and I have done it, and if you say one more word about Naples I shall put the lamp out with you still talking."

Reggie looks at the paper for a while. Then he folds it and puts it back in the folder and puts the folder on the floor, well away from the wet part of the bench, which is a thing Pascual notices him do and does not comment on.

"Thirty years," he says.

"Twenty-nine now."

"*Twenty-nine —*"

"You have used one," says Pascual, and gets him by the front of the clean shirt.

"That is not — you cannot count the first year as *elapsed*, the agreement was concluded eleven minutes ago, the term runs from execution —"

"Mm."

"— and in any event it would be an inclusive count, which is a schoolboy's —"

He kisses him, which stops it, but only for as long as it takes Reggie to get his mouth free.

"— which is a schoolboy's error," Reggie finishes, in triumph, from three inches away.

"*Ay, Dios mío.*"

"You can't distract me. You've been trying it for a year and it has never once actually —"

So Pascual stops trying to be quick about it.

He gets a hand up into the hair at the back of the man's head and takes his time instead, which is the thing that works and which both of them have known for about eleven months and neither of them has ever said out loud, because it is one of the great pleasures of the whole arrangement and it would be spoilt by being named. The sentence about inclusive counting dies somewhere in the second word. Reggie makes a noise that is not a word in any language and gets both arms round him and holds on.

It goes on for a good while. There is nothing to be quick for. Nobody is arriving in the morning; nothing has to hold; there is no man in the guest hut and no boat at ten hundred hours and no paper that has to be got right before the mail. There is a bolt across the door and twenty-nine years on a piece of paper on the floor, and the whole of it is, for once, simply theirs.

"You shaved," Pascual says, somewhere in the middle of getting the clean shirt off him, "and you put this on."

"It was a formal occasion."

"It was a *review*."

"Yes," Reggie says. "And I've been to a great many meetings in my life and I have never once wanted anything out of one before, so I shaved, and if you laugh at me I shall bring the boat up again."

Pascual does laugh at him. He does it into his shoulder, helplessly, for some seconds, and gets called several things for it, and then puts the lamp out.

---

Afterwards, with the lamp out and the window open on the flat still air, there is one more thing, and he has been carrying it about for a year and six days, and it is time.

"Reggie."

"Mm."

"I said the large thing that night and I have never once said the small one, and I noticed it at the time, and it is the wrong way round, and I have thought about it perhaps four hundred times." He has his hand flat on the man's chest, over the ribs, where it can be felt. "So."

He says it in Spanish, quietly, because he is a coward in exactly one direction and always has been.

And the man beside him — who has a shocking accent, and a broken-spined grammar under the cot, and a year of pencil marks all through it, and who could have looked any of it up in eleven seconds and would not — goes absolutely still, and then turns over, and puts his hand up against the side of Pascual's face.

"Yes," Reggie says, in English. "That. The same."

---

In the morning there are two letters on the desk in the office that have been there for three weeks and one day.

Pascual comes in at seven for the weight book and Reggie is already there with both of them squared in front of him and his chin on his hands.

"Florida wants a pair," Reggie says, without looking up. "The ministry wants to send us men to train."

"I know. I have known for three weeks."

"You haven't said anything."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because you have not answered them for three weeks," Pascual says, "which is the longest you have ever left anything, and I decided that a man arguing with himself should be allowed to finish."

Reggie is quiet for a moment.

"Sit down," he says. "I want to say the whole of it out loud to somebody and I want it to be you, and I want you to be exactly as loud as you promised to be."

So Pascual sits down.
