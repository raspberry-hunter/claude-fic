# A Likeness

The paper went off on the third of July, all forty-one pages of it, with the plates in a separate wrapper and the footnote on page nine that will cost him whatever it costs him.

He read that footnote eleven times before he let it go. *The tables presented in Tar (1950) should be understood as describing not the natural mortality of the species but the outcome of a particular husbandry practice, and the present writer, who is responsible for that practice as much as for the tables, withdraws the interpretation offered there.* Jean said it was too long. Pascual said it was too short. Reggie let it stand exactly as it was, sealed the envelope himself, and has not thought about it since more than about four times a day.

Nothing has come from Spilsbury. Not to Reggie, not to Pascual, not even to John, who has heard nothing at all since the letter in June and mentions it about once a fortnight in a voice that is trying to be careless.

Gamma is up thirty-one pounds on the twentieth of May and is, in Pascual's professional opinion, delivered at supper with a great deal of grumbling and one enormous badly hidden grin, *fat*. Chumley is four inches longer than he was and has learnt to take fish out of the air.

The rains are down to about an hour a night.

And on the nineteenth of July the mail boat comes in with two sacks, and in the second sack there is a letter for R. Tar Esq. in a woman's hand, postmarked Shrewsbury.

---

He takes it up to the office and sits down and does not open it for some minutes.

> Dear Dr Tar,
>
> I have your letter of the 24th of May, and the official one from Mrs Bevan which came with it, and I am obliged to you for both.
>
> I want to thank you for writing plainly. Mrs Bevan's letter is a very kind letter and I have read it twice and I could not have told you afterwards what was in it. Yours told me what happened and where and how deep the water is. My sister thought it unfeeling. I did not. One is not able to begin on a thing one has not been told.

Her name, at the bottom, is Victoria. He has been addressing envelopes to Miss Cholmondeley for two months.

> I should like to ask you two things, and you must say if either is a trouble.
>
> The first is that we have no photograph of him after the age of nineteen. He would never sit for one; he said he came out looking like a startled hake. There is one of him at school with his hat crooked and it is not a bit like him. If any of your people have a snapshot, even a poor one, even in the background of some other subject, we should be very glad of it.
>
> The second is only for my own peace of mind and you must not trouble to soften it.
>
> Was he alone?
>
> I do not mean at the end particularly, though I have thought about that. I mean out there, altogether, for the whole of it. He was a solitary boy and he did not much care for people and I have always been afraid that he went four thousand miles and got exactly what he had always had, which was nobody. He wrote to us every three weeks and his letters were all fish. In the last four or five there was a great deal about a particular animal. He sounded, Dr Tar, extremely happy, and it frightened me, because he had never sounded like that in his life and I could not tell from the letters whether there was one single person out there he could say a word to.
>
> If the answer is that he was alone I would rather have it. I have had two months to get ready for it.
>
> Yours sincerely,
> Victoria Cholmondeley

Reggie sits with the letter flat on the desk in front of him for a long time.

Then he goes and finds John.

---

There is no photograph. He knew there would be no photograph before he asked. He asks anyway, and John says, "God, no — he'd bolt. Jean tried to get a group one at Christmas and he went and stood behind Juan."

"Right."

"Wait." John is already going through the shelf under his cot. "Wait, wait, wait."

He comes up with the working book, the loose one, the one that is not the numbered sequence, and goes through it fast with his thumb, and stops.

"There," he says.

It is a study of the corner of the pen, done for the light and the algae line, three inches by four in the top corner of a sheet otherwise given over to a tarp knot. There is a man sitting on the rim with his feet in the water and his back to the artist and his head slightly turned, and he is about a quarter of an inch high, and it is unmistakably Charles Cholmondeley.

"That's the back of his head," John says. "That's no good to them."

"No."

John looks at it for a while, chewing the side of his thumb.

"I could do it properly," he says. "From that, and from the others, and from — " he taps his temple, irritably, as if it were an untidy drawer — "from having looked at him across a hut for two years. I've got him. It's not the same as having him in front of me and it won't be perfect. But I could give them a likeness."

"How long?"

"Three days. Four."

"Do it," Reggie says, "and send me the bill for the paper."

"Don't be an ass," says John.

---

He does it in two.

He does it on good paper, in the evenings, with the loose book open beside him and a lamp, and he does not let anybody look until it is finished — and then on the twenty-first of July he comes into the office and puts it on the desk without a word and stands there with his hands behind his back like a man waiting for a verdict.

It is a head and shoulders, three-quarter on. The spectacles. The mouth doing that faintly apologetic thing it did. And John has put him in the open collar and the awful old shirt with the sun on the side of his face, and has caught, God knows how, the particular way he used to hold his head when he was listening to something with his whole attention and had entirely forgotten to arrange his face.

Reggie looks at it for rather longer than is comfortable for either of them.

"Is it him?" John says.

"Yes."

"Only I can't — I couldn't get him actually *smiling*, it kept coming out like a chap in an advertisement —"

"John. It's him."

John lets his breath out.

"Right," he says. "Good. Right." And then, at the door, going a bit red: "I've done a second one. Same thing, smaller. If you wanted one."

He is gone before Reggie can answer, which is probably just as well, since it takes Reggie about a minute and a half to be in a fit state to answer anything.

---

He shows Pascual that evening, at the bench, with the rain doing its hour.

Pascual holds it under the lamp with both hands and does not say anything at all for a while.

"He has made him kind," he says at last.

"He was kind."

"Yes. That is what I am saying. Look at what the boy is doing there — he is listening. Nobody draws a man listening." He puts it down very carefully on a clear part of the bench, well away from anything wet. "*Ay.* That is going to Shropshire."

"On Friday's boat."

Pascual nods. He gets his own shirt pocket out, and unfolds the sheet that lives in it, and lays it on the bench beside the portrait: the boy up to the collarbone with his hand on a mermaid's face and the mermaid's hand on his, drawn by the same pencil, out of the same head, from a second and a half seen at thirty feet.

The two of them stand and look at the pair of drawings for a while.

"One of these they may have," Pascual says.

"Yes."

"And the other they may not, ever, and I am sorry about it and I would do it again." He folds it up and puts it away. "He is a very great deal better than any of us deserve, that boy."

"He's twenty-four."

"Yes," Pascual says. "And in fifteen years he will be the man who says what this place was, and neither of us will be able to stop him, and I am not sure that we should."

---

He writes back on the Thursday night, at the end of the verandah table, with the lamp and the moths and the generator, and it takes him three sheets to get right, which is two fewer than the first one did.

He tells her that there is no photograph and never was one and that he understands the startled hake entirely, and that instead he is sending a drawing by the Institute's illustrator, who shared a hut with her brother for two years, and that everyone here who has seen it says it is a very good likeness, and that it is the illustrator's gift and not the Institute's and she should say so if she writes to him.

Then he takes the other question.

He thinks about it for some time — about a young man in the water at four in the afternoon being splashed and laughing; about *tomorrow: ask P. about the bicarb*; about a cocky, furious, hollow-ribbed animal that hauled itself sixty-one feet over hot concrete on its arms rather than be by itself for another night; about a hand flat on a face and a hand coming up to answer it.

Then he writes:

> No. He was not alone.
>
> I know it is the answer you were braced against and I would not give it to you if it were not true. He was not much good with people and I was no good at all with him, and for the first months here I think he was as alone as you feared. That changed. In his last year he was, so far as I could see and I saw him every day, more completely in company than any man I have known.
>
> I cannot tell you a great deal about it in a letter, because a good deal of it is not mine to tell and some of it is the Institute's business. What I can tell you is that he found something out here that nobody else in the world had found, and that he was not alone in it, and that it made him happy, and that he was answered.
>
> He was not alone. Not at the end and not for a long while before it.
>
> Yours very sincerely,
> R. Tar

He reads it through once, and seals it, and sits for a minute with his hand flat on the envelope.

Down at the pens, in the dark, in the rain, there is the small sound of water moving: twice, and then again.
