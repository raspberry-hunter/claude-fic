# Adjoining Rooms

The accommodation question is settled on the second of November at the ordinary Friday meeting, in about ninety seconds, and Reggie will remember them for the rest of his life.

There are four rooms in the new block above the store, built in the spring for the intakes, with a shared bathroom and a covered walk and a view of nothing whatever, and they are perfectly decent and there is no earthly reason for the subject to come up at all.

"Now, Spilsbury," says the Colonel, going down the paper. "Six weeks or thereabouts. He can't have the guest hut; it leaks and he'll say so. Put him in the block."

"Yes, sir."

"Though the block's full in the second week — the February men come early for the pens." The Colonel looks up mildly. "Tar, you've a spare room at the north side, haven't you."

There is a pause of about one and a half seconds during which Reggie is aware of Pascual, four feet to his left, becoming extremely still.

"It's full of the fisheries records," Reggie says.

"Is it?"

"Since October. There were eleven boxes in the office and Mrs Bevan wanted the floor."

"I did," says Hester, without looking up from the minute book.

"Ah," says the Colonel. "Well. Then he's in the block for six weeks and the February men can double up, and they'll survive it. Next: the drains."

---

Afterwards, walking down to the jetty, Pascual says nothing at all for about forty yards.

Then: "Eleven boxes."

"Eleven."

"You moved eleven boxes of fisheries records into that room in October."

"Yes."

"*Why* did you move eleven boxes of fisheries records into that room in October?"

"Because Mrs Bevan wanted the floor," Reggie says, and goes on down the path, and does not look round, and can hear the man not saying anything with tremendous force for the whole of the rest of the way.

---

Spilsbury comes ashore on the fourteenth of November at twenty past four with two cases and a crate of books, and is fifty-one now and greyer and otherwise entirely unchanged, and the first thing he does is walk past the reception line to John Clarke and put out his hand.

"Mr Clarke. Two years and four months, and worth waiting for."

"Sorry about the delay," says John.

"You weren't sorry at all. You were deciding, and it took you two years, and then you did it on your own terms with a ministry adoption in the second paragraph and your card enclosed." Spilsbury is smiling, and it is a real smile, and it reaches all the way up. "It was the best letter I've had in five years and I read it twice on the train. Well done."

John goes scarlet to the ears and says something about the crate, and gets it away up the path, and Reggie watches him go and finds his chest doing something complicated.

Then Spilsbury turns round.

"Pobil."

"Dr Spilsbury."

"You look well. You look about eight years younger than you did in 'fifty-four, which I put down to the animals rather than the company." He shakes hands. "And I gather I've you to thank for being here at all."

"You may gather what you like," Pascual says.

"Splendid," says Spilsbury, delighted, and turns to Reggie, and says: "Right. Where's the record?"

---

They start at seven the next morning and by half past eight Reggie has stopped noticing that anything has changed at all, and that is the whole of the difficulty and it takes him five weeks to see it.

Here is what it is like.

There are two rooms off the main office, which were the wireless room and the wireless store when this was a seaplane station, and they have a door between them that does not shut properly. Spilsbury takes one and Reggie has the other, and the door stands open from seven in the morning until whenever, and the whole of the first fortnight is conducted through it, mostly by shouting, occasionally by throwing.

Spilsbury reads the entire Martin record in nine days. All of it: the day books from 1948, the intake series, the necropsies, Jean's ponds, four years of John's plates in sequence. He reads at a speed Reggie has only ever seen in one other person, which is himself, and he reads with a pencil, and on the ninth day he comes through the door and puts a sheet on the desk with eleven items on it and says, "Those are the eleven places where the record is thin, and six of them are thin for ordinary reasons, and I'd like to talk about the other five."

Two of the five are Charles.

Reggie says, "Two of those are a man who died," and Spilsbury looks at him for perhaps a second and a half and says, "Right," and crosses them off, and never raises either of them again, and Reggie spends about four days waiting for a return that does not come.

---

The paper takes six weeks and it is the best thing Reggie has done in ten years.

That is not a modest assessment and he is not in the habit of making immodest ones and he checks it eleven times over the following year and it does not move.

Spilsbury's contribution is not knowledge, or not chiefly. It is that he is the only person Reggie has ever worked with who is not, at any point, in any degree, slower than he is. Everything lands. Nothing has to be set up. On the fourth day Reggie starts a sentence about the intake lag and Spilsbury says "yes, and therefore your Naples man's margin note is the finding, not the anecdote — put it in the results," and it is *right*, and it reorganises the entire middle of the paper, and it has taken about four seconds.

They cut the whole of Reggie's third section on the ninth day, which was eleven hundred words and had taken him a fortnight.

"It's very good," Spilsbury says. "It's the best-written thing in the folder and it's doing nothing. You wrote it because you enjoy that argument and you've been having it in your head since March. Out."

"It's the justification —"

"It's a *defence*. You're defending yourself against a reader who hasn't objected yet, on a point he wasn't going to raise, and the effect is to tell him he ought to." Spilsbury is already turning the page. "Never furnish the room for a man who hasn't come in. Out."

Reggie takes it out.

It is better without it.

---

And it is *fun*.

That is the part he does not report to anybody, including himself, for about a month.

They work until two in the morning and swear at each other through a door that will not shut and eat standing up and argue about a comma for twenty-five minutes and win one each. Spilsbury has an appalling and inexhaustible fund of stories about people in this field, at least eleven of which are libellous and all of which are true, and he tells them beautifully. He does an imitation of a certain editor in London that reduces Reggie to a condition he has not been in since about 1948.

On the twenty-second of November they are still going at half past one and Reggie realises he has not thought about the training centre, the returns, the broker or the Colonel's health for nineteen hours, and that he feels *magnificent*, and he goes home across the water at two and lets himself in and stands in the dark front room with his shoes in his hand and thinks, without any alarm whatever: *I had forgotten what this was like.*

---

The place where it goes wrong is the recommendation section, and it goes wrong in the last week of November, and Pascual stops it.

They have written four pages. They are extremely fine pages. They set out the composition rule, the intake practice, the shade requirement and the residue discipline, in numbered form, with the reasoning compressed out because the reasoning is in the body, and they are lucid and confident and they read like the law.

Pascual reads them at the office desk on the Thursday afternoon with both hands flat on the table.

"No," he says.

"It's the tightest thing in the paper," says Spilsbury.

"It's a *protocol*."

"It's a set of recommendations."

"It's four pages of numbered instructions with the reasons taken out, and a man in Lisbon'll tear it out and pin it above a tank and follow it exactly, and when his animal starts to go down and the protocol doesn't cover it he won't know what to do, because you've removed every single sentence that would have told him how to think." Pascual has not raised his voice and is not going to; this is the other one. "This is a paper whose whole subject is that men killed animals by following a paper. And you've written the same paper again. Better. Which is worse."

There is a silence in the room.

Reggie sits down slowly on the corner of the desk, because his legs have made the decision without consulting him.

"You're right," he says.

"He's right," Spilsbury agrees, immediately, without a flicker. "Damn. He's entirely right and I'd have printed it." He is already pulling the sheets back across the table. "Pobil, sit down and tell me what a man ought to be able to do at the rail that he can't do out of a numbered list, and I'll write it as we go, and it'll be four times the length and read like a dog's breakfast and be the only useful thing in the paper."

It takes them eleven days. It is nine pages. It reads like a dog's breakfast.

It is the only useful thing in the paper.

---

Here is the thing Reggie does not see for five weeks, and he is going to be angry about the five weeks for a long time.

Pascual is in the paper. He is third author and he ought to be second and Spilsbury says so twice. The husbandry section is his and the nine-page section is very largely his and he attends every working session he can get to and is heard, at length, and taken seriously, and nobody in that room ever once treats him as the veterinary surgeon.

And he is off.

Not in any way Reggie can put a hand on. He is a little quieter. He goes to bed before Reggie four nights out of five, which is ordinary, because Reggie is coming home at two. He argues about the boat exactly as much as usual and about the paraffin exactly as much as usual and laughs at all the right things and does not sulk, because he has never sulked in his life.

Reggie's account of it, for five weeks, is: *he dislikes Spilsbury, which is correct, and he is tired, which is reasonable, and he is being extremely good about a man he has every right to want thrown off the island.*

It is a perfectly coherent account. It fits everything he can see. He is rather pleased with it.

---

What breaks it is a sentence he says himself, on the eleventh of December, at about one in the morning, in the office, with the door open.

They are on the mortality of the trade. The one-in-three figure, which Reggie asked in July not to publish, and which Spilsbury has been circling for three weeks with immense patience and is now, at one in the morning, going to have.

"You want it out because it names the broker's business and you think it makes the Martin an enemy," Spilsbury says. "It does. Publish it. It's the only number in the paper that'll make a treasury official put his cup down."

"It'll be checked."

"It'll be checked and it'll hold; I've done it twice from the Trinidad side and I get one in 2.8." He shrugs. "And if you want it airtight there's eleven years of consignment records in Port of Spain and a man there who'd sell them to me for about forty pounds, and I'd rather like to have them anyway."

"Then get them," Reggie says.

He hears it as he says it. Not afterwards. As it comes out.

*Then get them.* Four words, no cost, no procedure, no hesitation of any kind — and the man across the table does not blink, does not check, does not so much as change the angle of his head, and writes *P. of S. — records — £40?* in the margin and goes straight on to the next thing.

Reggie sits there with his pen in his hand.

He has said a great many things to Alec Spilsbury in ten years, in a room with a door between them that did not shut, and not one of them has ever produced a flinch. Not one. Not in 1947, not in 1949, not in the eleven months when he already knew and went on anyway, and not at one in the morning on the eleventh of December 1956.

He had it, for the whole of those ten years, as being *understood*.

---

He goes home at half past two and does not sleep, and at four he gets up and sits in the repulsive chair in the dark by the shutters, which is not what the chair is for.

Pascual finds him there at six.

"How long have you been in that?"

"Two hours."

"*Ay.*" He goes and puts the kettle on and comes back and stands in the doorway with his arms folded. "Say it."

And Reggie, who has had two hours and has got nowhere, says the wrong one first, because it is the easier one.

"I've worked out why I stayed four years."

"At that place."

"Everybody there was interested and nobody there was ever *shocked*. That's the whole of it. I said something last night at one in the morning that I wouldn't have said in front of you and I didn't know I wasn't going to say it in front of you until it was out of my mouth, and he didn't blink, and he's never blinked, and I've had that for ten years as one man understanding another." He has both hands round his knees, which is ridiculous, in a chair that low. "I didn't stay because of the work. The work was everywhere. I stayed because in that building there was no cost to wanting things, and I've never in my life been anywhere else where that was true, and I couldn't have told you that at any point before about half past one this morning."

Pascual takes rather a long time to answer.

"That's a great deal to have got in one night," he says.

"It's not the thing I sat down about."

"No," says Pascual. "I didn't think it was."

---

And here it is, and Reggie — who has spent five weeks with a coherent account and been rather pleased with it — gets it in eleven words and does not see it coming.

"You think I have been worried because I dislike him."

"...Haven't you?"

"I do dislike him. I shall dislike him at ninety." Pascual is in the doorway with his arms folded and he is not doing it comfortably. "That is not it and it has never been it, and you have had five weeks to notice and you have been extremely busy. I am going to say this once, properly, and then I would like never to say it again."

Reggie does not move.

"You are *faster* with him. I have watched it for five weeks through a door that does not shut. He says four words and the middle of your paper reorganises itself and neither of you ever has to explain anything to the other, not once, not one single time in six weeks, and I have never seen you like that with any living creature including me. You have had him since 1946. You have had me since 1953. He is your kind of animal and I am not, and I have known that since the day I met you, and it did not matter until there was a door open in the next room.

"On Thursday I told you the four pages were a protocol. You said to me: *you are right.* And he said, to you: *he is right.* Not to me. To you." Pascual's chin comes up. "Neither of you heard it. I have heard nothing else since.

"And there is the other thing, which is worse, and which I could not put a name to until about the ninth of December. You are not carrying anything."

Reggie opens his mouth.

"The returns come in and Hester answers them," Pascual says. "The Colonel goes up that path slower every month and you have not said his name to me since November. There is a man in Port of Spain with an order for June and you have not mentioned him since the summer. You go over on the launch at seven and you come back at two and you are *light*. I have never in my life seen you light. In three and a half years I have never once seen you put a thing down, and you have put the whole of it down in five weeks and you did not notice doing it, and you are *happy*." His voice does something and he goes on through it. "You are happier than I have ever seen you. And none of it is mine, and I do not know what to do.

"And every time I open my mouth in that room I am the slowest person in it. I am not being modest. I am being accurate. And I go in anyway and I say *no, it is a protocol*, and I am right, and I am slow, and I do not mind being slow." He unfolds his arms. "What I mind is that I have begun to wonder whether the brake is the whole of what I am for, and whether you have been putting up with it for three and a half years because it was the only thing on offer. Because a man who has that in the next room, at his own speed, with nothing whatever to carry — what does he want with a brake?

"That is all of it. It is not a reasonable thing to be worried about and I am aware of it. You may say so."

---

The kettle starts up in the kitchen. Neither of them goes to it.

"You think I want it," Reggie says.

"I have *seen* you want it."

"You've seen me *like* it." He gets his hands round his knees, which is ridiculous in a chair that low, and does not care. "And I do. Of course I do. I like it the way a man likes a drink at eleven in the morning. Now stand exactly where you are for about an hour while I take it apart, because if you go and sit down I shall lose my nerve."

Pascual stays where he is.

"You've got the observation right and the interpretation wrong, and the observation is better than anything I've done in five weeks," Reggie says. "I'm not carrying anything. That's true and I hadn't noticed and it's the whole of it.

"Because that is what that building *was*. That's the thing I've been trying to describe to you since half past one this morning and getting at sideways. It was not that they were wicked. Nobody there was wicked. It was that nobody there ever had to bother about anything they did not feel like bothering about. Somebody else signed for the material. Somebody else decided what it was for. Somebody else answered the letters and did the estimates and told the widows. I had a question and a room and a technician and four years, and I was not responsible for one single hard thing, and I have never been that light again until the fourteenth of November."

He has stopped looking at Pascual and is looking at the shutters.

"And I want to tell you why I left, because I've never said it out loud and you're going to think worse of me and I'd rather you had it.

"I did not leave because I worked out that it was wrong. I worked that out in about the spring of 'forty-nine and it moved me precisely nothing; I've told you, there are eleven months on the other side of it. What moved me was that on an afternoon in the eleventh month I noticed I was *enjoying myself*. Not the work. The afternoon. I was standing in a room being perfectly happy, doing a thing I already knew the character of, and the happiness and the thing would not sit in the same head, and it frightened me so badly that I was out of the building in nine weeks and out of the country in five months." He rubs the bridge of his nose. "It was not conscience. It was fright. I got out by about a fortnight and I have never been able to work out which fortnight, and I have thought about that every year since, and last Thursday I came home across the water at two in the morning feeling *magnificent* and it took me until four o'clock this morning to recognise the sensation."

---

"Now the other thing," he says, "which is the part you've got wrong.

"I don't put up with the arguing." He is going red about the ears, which he is aware of, and would rather be almost anywhere else. "I like it more than I've ever liked anything, and — this is the part that matters and I want it on the record — I *want* it. Not the way you want a thing you've been offered. The way you want a thing you'd go and get. I measured that garage in March with a rule and I said nothing about it for three months, and I've mentioned it four times since, and you counted, and I *knew* you were counting. I did the paraffin on purpose on Tuesday. I did it after supper because that's when you're at your worst and I wanted twenty minutes of it. That is not a man tolerating a brake. That is the best hour of my week and I've been having it twice a day for three and a half years."

"*Ay,*" says Pascual.

"So. The brake." Reggie gets his elbows onto his knees. "You've got it as a service you perform for me, and I'd rather you had it as a fact about what I want, because those aren't the same and the difference is the whole thing.

"I've now had six weeks of the other arrangement. Everything I put up goes through at speed with nothing taken off it and nothing to carry, and I know exactly what that makes of me, because I've watched it make it once already over four years. I like it and I do not want it. Those are two different words and I've spent ten years failing to tell them apart and I only managed it at about four o'clock this morning." He looks up. "What I *want* is the thing where I have to say what I want out loud to somebody who's going to make me pay for it. Not because it's good for me. Because it's the only arrangement I've ever been in where I could stand being the person doing the wanting."

"And the rest of it," Pascual says. "You said item by item."

"I'll go through it item by item now if you like. It's about forty minutes and some of it's embarrassing."

"Later." Pascual's mouth has gone crooked. "Write it down. I shall ask for it in March, when you are astonished about something else."

---

"Then the last part," Reggie says, "and you'll like it least.

"It didn't occur to me for one second that you might mind, and I want to be accurate about why, because it isn't modesty and it isn't anything nice. There are two halves to it and only one of them is any use to you.

"The first is that what I've got about you isn't a quantity. It isn't something that could be higher on one Thursday and lower on another. It's the shape the whole thing has had since about the second week of June 'fifty-four, and there's no mechanism in it for changing, in the way there's no mechanism for the tide not coming in. So you've spent five weeks frightened of an event that can't occur. I can tell you that. Telling you that is easy and I'll do it as often as you like.

"The second half is the one that's actually wrong with me, and I've only had it since about four o'clock this morning." He is looking at his hands. "I can't do anything by halves. You've known that since about the fourth week and it's the only thing wrong with me that's ever mattered — it's what the labs were and it's what the paper was and it's what you are.

"And because that's what it's like in here, it doesn't *arrive* that it could look like anything else from where you're standing. I know as a proposition that you can't see inside my head. I can't hold it as a fact about the room. So you spend five weeks frightened and I don't notice, because from in here the question you're asking isn't a question. It's like being asked whether I'm certain about the tide."

He rubs the bridge of his nose.

"Which is no use to you at all, and I'll look astonished the next time and the time after that, and I'm sorry about it in advance."

He gets out of the low chair, which takes him a moment, because it is a repulsive chair.

"Which is no use to you at all," he says. "Because it means you're living with a man who cannot make the reassurance *arrive*, and all I can do is tell you it's a fact, and I've told you before, and I'll have to tell you roughly twice a year until one of us dies, and you'll have to take it on my word every single time. That's the deal and it's a bad one and I'm sorry."

---

Pascual crosses the room and takes the kettle off in passing, because he is incapable of not doing that, and puts it down somewhere with a bang, and then Reggie has him.

He is not built for this and never has been. His arms go round the man badly and too hard, the way they always do, one across the shoulders and one somewhere at the small of the back, and he holds on with his eyes shut and his chin on somebody's shoulder and does not say anything at all, because the words have all gone.

Pascual takes hold of a handful of the back of his shirt and says nothing at all, which is not like him.

They stand there for some while in the middle of the front room at ten past six in the morning, with the kettle going off the boil on the wrong side of the stove and the light coming up through the shutters in bars, and Reggie gets a hand into the back of the man's hair and keeps it there.

"I know," Pascual says eventually, into his collarbone.

"Do you."

"*Ay.* You have just talked for eleven minutes about a building in Wiltshire in order to tell me one thing, and I had it in the first four." He lets go and stands back and looks at him, and his face is a ruin and he does not appear to mind. "*Ay, Dios mío,* you are hard work."

"Yes."

"Don't go in till nine."

"I've got the mortality section —"

"Don't go in till nine," says Pascual, "or I shall come across on the launch and be a nuisance in front of him, and I promise you that I will enjoy it."

Reggie does not go in until nine.

---

The paper is finished on the twenty-first of December and it is fifty-three pages and it is called *Mortality and Husbandry in Captive Sirenia: a cautionary review, with recommendations*, and Spilsbury will place it in the spring in a journal that everybody reads, because Spilsbury sits on the committee that decides.

They celebrate on the verandah on the twenty-second with a bottle of rum the Colonel has produced from somewhere, and Hester comes, and John comes and is talked to about plate numbering for twenty minutes by a man who means every word, and Nestor is fetched and made to accept a glass and is embarrassed and pleased, and it is, on any reasonable accounting, one of the best evenings the William Martin Institute has ever had.

At about eleven the others go up the path and there are three men and half a bottle left on the verandah, and Spilsbury puts his glass down and says:

"Right. My item."

---

He does it beautifully, which is the worst part, and he does it in about four minutes, and there is nothing in it that could be called a threat.

"I want to run a proper series on contact," he says. "Twelve to sixteen subjects, over about eight months, on both your animals and on any animal at Whitley or Naples that Chettle and Bassani will let me have. Blind scoring — the man in the water doesn't know what's being measured and the scorer can't see who's in the pen. Standardised approach, standardised duration, no touching in the first four sessions. Every session on Clarke's plate numbering, in sequence, no gaps.

"And I'll tell you what I expect to find, because you'll want it in advance and so do I. I expect fourteen of the sixteen get nothing whatever. I expect one or two get something, and I expect the field to say it's the animal, and I want to know whether it's the animal or the man, and that's the entire question and there's no way on this earth to answer it with one man and one animal and an afternoon."

He picks the glass up again.

"Pobil, you're going to refuse, and you're going to refuse on the animals, and you'll be right that sixteen strangers in a pen is a cost. I'll take conditions — Pobil's conditions, whatever they are, in writing, and he stops it at any point without giving me a reason. I'll take a veto on every individual subject. I'm not in a hurry; I've waited two and a half years and I can wait two more."

The moths go round the lamp.

"Why?" Pascual says.

"Because I've had one negative result for two and a half years and it has been the most irritating fact of my professional life." Spilsbury smiles at him, entirely without malice. "I got into that pen in 'fifty-four before it was light and I put my hand on that animal and I got absolutely nothing, and I said at the time that a negative result on one man is an anecdote and not a refutation, and I meant it, and I've had to live with it.

"And Cholmondeley was getting a great deal."

Nobody says anything at all.

"I'm not asking about Cholmondeley," Spilsbury says. "Cholmondeley's dead and it was two years ago and I've crossed those two lines off my list and I'm not putting them back on. I'm asking about the phenomenon. He was one man and now he's an anecdote too, and I've got two anecdotes pointing in opposite directions, and I'm fifty-one, and I'd very much like, before I finish, to find out which of them was the interesting one."

He finishes the rum.

"Think about it over Christmas," he says. "My passage is the eighth of January and I'd like it before I'm on it, and if it's no I'll take it without a word and we'll have had a very good six weeks. Merry Christmas, both of you. It's been the best six weeks I've had in a decade and I don't say that sort of thing."

And he goes up the path whistling, and does not look back, and does not need to.

---

The Institute shuts from the Sunday to Boxing Day. The paper is finished, the February men are not due until the ninth, and there is nothing on the island but the animals and Nestor and a skeleton watch.

They do not discuss it on the twenty-third, or on Christmas Eve, or on Christmas Day, which they spend at the north-side house with a chicken about which Pascual has views, a bottle sent from Naples and no other human being whatsoever, and which is the best day of the year. They spend Boxing Day on the water in a boat called *Doris*, catching nothing, four miles off the north reef, saying almost nothing for six hours; and Spilsbury spends Boxing Day going through the 1948 day books, because there is nobody on the island to talk to and he cannot help himself, and will produce on the twenty-seventh a list of four errors in the 1948 weights, all of them real.

They discuss it that night, at the kitchen table, at eleven o'clock, because they go back on the twenty-seventh and neither of them is prepared to be in a room with the man again without having said it out loud first.

"He has made it free to refuse," Pascual says. "That is the part I keep going back to. I stop it at any point and I do not have to give a reason. He put that into the offer himself before either of us could ask for it."

"And he'd keep to it."

"He would. He has kept to every single thing he has said to us since the fourteenth of November, including two lines on a list which he crossed off on the ninth day and has never once put back." Pascual turns his glass round on the table. "So. We say no on Tuesday, he sails on the eighth, and there is no cost of any kind to either of us."

"None."

"Then say why we have not said it."

Reggie does not answer.

"I will say it, then, because I have had it since the verandah and it has been getting worse." Pascual puts the glass down. "I want to know.

"Two and a half years. If it is the animal, then Beta is somewhere out there and whatever it was is still going on and Charles was an ordinary boy who happened to be sitting on the right rail, and I can put the whole of it down. And if it is the man — then there was something about that child that nobody on this island ever saw except a mermaid, and we put him in a boat, and I signed the certificate." He is looking at the table. "I do not know which of those I want and I have wanted one of them very badly since the twenty-second of December."

"Yes," Reggie says.

"That is what he has actually put in front of us. Not a study. The one thing neither of us can get anywhere else on this earth, and he did it in four minutes on a verandah with half a bottle of the Colonel's rum, and he does not know that he has done it."

Reggie looks at the window for a moment.

"Doesn't he," he says.
