# Intake

The word for it, in the fisheries return and in the day book and in every one of Hester Bevan's beautiful documents, is *intake*.

It is the word for an animal's arrival weight. It is the word for the fish that go into the pen at seven in the morning, weighed, against the residue that comes out at four in the afternoon, weighed. It has been the most important word on this island for two years and it belongs to a boy who is officially drowned.

And on the twenty-sixth of September it comes to mean six men on a boat.

---

Pascual has been dreading this since June and has told everybody so, at length, daily, and is aware that the telling has become a performance and has continued anyway, because it is the only thing keeping him from having to say the actual objection out loud, which is that he does not know how to do it.

He has never taught anybody anything in his life. He qualified in 1936 and he has worked alone in bad rooms with insufficient equipment ever since, and the entire body of his professional knowledge lives in his hands, and hands are not a curriculum.

He said as much to Reggie in July, once, in the dark, where it did not count.

"You taught me," Reggie said.

"I've never taught you anything."

"You taught me the whole of what I know about dolphins in about March of last year while I was trying to read a journal, and I've used it twice since in print." A pause. "You'll be extremely good at it and you'll hate the first week."

He was right about the first week.

---

The station is not the station any more.

There are two new huts on the rise where the scrub was, put up in August by men from the village under Juan's brother, and a third that is a lecture room with a blackboard and eleven chairs and a roof that leaks in one corner. There is a proper store. There is a microtome that arrived in a crate in July and that Pascual has not entirely got over. There is money, and money has done what money does, which is to make the place look like a place instead of an abandoned American seaplane station with four scientists camped in it.

The Colonel comes down to the jetty at ten to meet the boat, and comes down the path slowly, and Hester walks on his left the whole way at exactly his pace and does not once appear to be doing it.

The six of them come ashore with their kit and stand about in the sun looking at the pens, and Pascual, watching from the rail with his arms folded, thinks: *they are all looking at the water and not one of them has looked at the buckets.*

---

They are:

**Mr Chettle**, who is fifty-one and has run the marine hall of a large aquarium in the north of England for nineteen years and has kept, by his own account, everything except a mermaid.

**Dr Bassani**, from the Stazione at Naples, who is courteous and quick and has read every word the Martin has published including the fisheries bulletin, which nobody has.

**Dr Vance**, American, from the Florida station that wanted to buy a pair, who has a great deal of money behind him and knows it and does not make a noise about it.

**Mr Ames**, who is twenty-three, who came down from Cambridge fifteen months ago, and who is here because the ministry sent him and who has not stopped talking since the launch came round the head.

And two others, Pickersgill and Roque, who are serious and quiet and will give nobody any trouble at all and whom Pascual will find, at the end of eight weeks, that he can barely remember.

---

The first morning he takes them to Gamma's pen at seven and does not let them in.

"You will stand behind that line," he says, "which is where the wet stops, and you will not cross it this week. Any of you. That's the rule and I'm not explaining it."

Chettle says, "For a week?"

"For a week."

"With respect, Dr Pobil, I've been handling large marine animals since before —"

"Then you already know why," Pascual says.

There is a small pause and Chettle, to his credit, shuts his mouth. Down in the water Chumley comes up under the surface at speed, sees six strangers, and goes down again fast and flat, and Gamma lifts her head off the bottom and does not put it back for four minutes.

"There," says Pascual. "That took nothing. That took six men standing still on a piece of concrete and it's cost that animal four minutes of watching a wall instead of eating. By Thursday she won't bother. That's what the week's for and now I've explained it, which I said I wouldn't do, so you may all consider yourselves fortunate."

Ames writes this down. Pascual has not told anybody to write anything down.

---

Then he gives them the buckets.

"Every morning. Fish weighed in, by you, on that balance, and written in the book. Every afternoon, residue netted, drained, weighed, written in the book. All six of you, in rotation, for eight weeks. You'll come to love it."

"The residue," says Chettle.

"The residue is uneaten fish and it's faeces and it's going to be the most useful eight weeks of your life." He is beginning to enjoy himself and knows he should stop and does not. "You will all of you go home and hire a boy to do this, and the boy will be honest and careful and it won't matter, because the point of the bucket isn't the number. The point of the bucket is that the man who does it is at that rail twice a day with his hands in the water, and he sees the animal, and on the eleventh of March he thinks, *she took a fish and put it down again*, and he writes it in the margin. The weight won't tell you that for three weeks. By then you've lost a fortnight you couldn't afford."

"That's in the fisheries paper," says Bassani.

Everybody looks at him.

"*Cholmondeley and Leslie*," Bassani says, "'fifty-four. The intake-and-residue method. Half of it is a costing exercise for a fishery and the other half is —" he moves his hand — "the other half is not about fisheries at all. I could not understand why it had been put there."

Pascual looks at him for a moment and finds that he has to look at the water instead.

"No," he says. "Nor could anybody."

"Is he here? Cholmondeley?"

"He died the year before last."

"Ah." Bassani takes this in. "I am sorry. It is a beautiful piece of work. I have written to the bulletin twice for a second offprint and had no reply."

"You'll have mine," Pascual says.

---

By the fourth day he has stopped noticing that he is teaching.

This is the part he did not expect. What he had imagined was standing at a blackboard being wrong in front of educated men. What actually happens is that six people follow him about while he does his ordinary work and ask him questions, and he answers them, and about half the time the answer turns out to be a thing he did not know he knew until it came out of his mouth.

He is also, he discovers, physically incapable of taking credit for anything.

"That's Miss Leslie's graph and she built it out of nothing," he says, four times in one morning. "That plate's Clarke's and the numbering's his own idea and it's the reason any of this is a record and not a story. Tar did the arithmetic; I don't do arithmetic. The intake method's Cholmondeley's and he was twenty-four and everybody on this station thought he was a joke, including me."

Ames writes all of that down too.

---

The trouble with Chettle comes on the ninth day and it is not about being taught by a horse doctor, which Pascual had been braced for and would have let go past.

They are at the rail after the weighing and Chettle is explaining, quite pleasantly, how his own establishment intends to run it: the pair together in the main tank, and then each animal drawn off into the holding tank for a fortnight in turn, for a proper individual work-up, weights and bloods and an accurate measure of intake without the confounding —

"You're not doing that," says Pascual.

"It's a fortnight."

"It's a fortnight *alone*."

"In a tank thirty feet from the other one."

"You will not do it."

"Dr Pobil, one cannot get a clean intake figure with two animals in a tank, it's simply not possible, and if the whole of your method rests on the intake figure —"

And it comes up out of him like a boiler, exactly as it has been doing since he was nineteen years old, and he does nothing whatever about it.

"*You will not do it.* Alpha, four months, alone, dead. Beta, nine months, alone, and he took himself out of that pen on his arms across sixty-one feet of hot concrete and lay on it for six hours because he'd rather do that than spend one more night by himself, and I've got the epidermis in a jar and I'll fetch it and put it in your hand. Gamma, nine days from dying, alone. Three animals. Three out of three. And you want to take an animal that's *well* and put it in a box for fourteen nights so your arithmetic'll be tidy —"

"Pascual," says Reggie, from about ten feet behind him.

Pascual stops.

He gets hold of it. It takes him a moment and everybody can see it take him a moment and there is nothing to be done about that either.

"Mr Chettle," he says. "I beg your pardon. That wasn't addressed to you personally and I've shouted at better men than you this year." He puts his hands behind his back, which helps. "The clean intake figure. Yes. You're right that you can't get one with two in a tank. That's a real difficulty and I've had it for sixteen months and it's a genuine cost of doing it this way, and the answer's that you don't get a clean figure and you learn to read a dirty one. I'll show you how, this afternoon, and it'll take about an hour, and by December you'll be better at it than I am, and your animals will be alive."

Chettle looks at him.

"Sixty-one feet," he says.

"Sixty-one."

"Right," says Chettle, and takes his hat off and puts it on again, and that is the end of the trouble with Chettle for the whole of the eight weeks.

---

Bassani asks for a private word on the eleventh day and Pascual takes him into the sick bay and shuts the door.

"I'd like to tell you what we were going to do in March," Bassani says.

He has the pen built and the water plumbed and the money spent. He was to have had two animals in November, from a broker, and the broker had two, and they were both adult males, and Naples was going to put them together, because the paper says two.

"And it does say two," Bassani says. He is not making an excuse; he is being exact, the way a good man is exact when he is frightened. "I have it here. Page thirty-one. *Cohabitation of a mature female with a juvenile male resulted in immediate cessation of decline.* And then the discussion says the mortality figures are an artefact of solitary husbandry, and recommends cohabitation, and it does not say — Dr Pobil, it does not anywhere say that it was one pen and one pair and that the pair was chosen because it was the safest pair in the world."

"No," Pascual says. "It doesn't."

"An adult female past breeding, and a calf. Of course. Of *course*." Bassani puts the offprint down on the bench very carefully, as though it might go off. "I have read that paper perhaps forty times. It is a beautiful paper. And I would have put two adult males in a concrete box in November and I would have watched what happened and then I would have written to you to ask what I had done wrong."

The generator does what it does.

"You wouldn't have written," Pascual says. "You'd have had two dead animals and you'd have thought the method was no good, and you'd have said so to a colleague at Genoa, and he'd have said so to a man in Lisbon."

"Yes," Bassani says.

---

He finds Reggie at nine that night in the office with the lamp on and the lecture roster in front of him, and does not sit down.

"Naples was three weeks off putting two adult males in one pen."

Reggie's head comes up.

"They've got the pen built. They've got the money spent. The only reason it didn't happen is that the ministry sent that man here in September and he read the roster and found out he was to be here for eight weeks, so he told his broker to hold." Pascual has both hands on the back of the chair. "*Sample size of one.* I said it to you at that bench in February and in March and again when you were putting the discussion together, and you said the discussion carried the caveat, and I read the discussion and I let it go, and it doesn't carry it, Reggie, it carries a *recommendation*."

"Yes," Reggie says.

"Don't do that."

"I'm not doing anything. You're right. I've known you were right since about the fourth of August, when the letter came from the man at Genoa asking whether it worked with two adults, and I didn't tell you about that letter, and that's the piece you should have and haven't got."

Pascual stands very still.

"There's a letter from Genoa."

"There are three letters. Genoa, a man in Bergen, and a person at Marineland who signs himself *Curator* and doesn't give a name." Reggie takes his glasses off. "All three ask the same question and all three ask it the way a man asks a question he's already decided the answer to. I've replied to all three and I've said no in the plainest English I own, and I've no way on earth of knowing whether any of them believed me, because I'm forty-one pages and a signature to those men and forty-one pages can't put its hand out and stop somebody."

He is holding the glasses in both hands, which is the thing he does.

"I published a case report," he says, "and I wrote a recommendation into the discussion of a case report, and I did it because I wanted it acted on quickly, and I knew when I wrote it that it wasn't what the data would bear. That's the whole of it. I've been standing in it since August. Say the rest."

"I was going to say this is the same defect."

"Yes."

"It's the sixth time."

"I've counted seven," Reggie says. "You're missing one, and I'm not going to tell you which, because you'd be up all night."

Pascual pulls the chair round and sits on it after all.

---

The correction takes them eleven days and it is, in the end, four hundred words.

It cannot go where the paper went. The journal will not print a note on pairing composition; a note is not a result and there is no result, because the Martin has one pen and one pair and nothing whatever to report except a caution. Reggie tries anyway and gets a polite letter in nineteen days.

So it goes to the fisheries bulletin, which will print anything, and which four hundred people read.

*Note on the selection of animals for cohabitation.* Tar, Pobil and Bassani — Reggie puts Bassani on it without asking and Bassani goes very quiet and then says that he will send it to eleven people himself, personally, with a covering letter, and does.

"That's not going to be enough," Pascual says.

"No," says Reggie. "The only thing that's going to be enough is that a man comes here for eight weeks and stands at that rail and is told to his face."

And Pascual, who has been arguing against the training centre in his own head since the fifteenth of June out of pure habit, finds that he has just been handed the argument for it by the least welcome possible route, and does not enjoy it at all.

---

John's lectures begin in the third week and Pascual would not have missed one of them for money.

He has been expecting an embarrassment. What he gets is a young man of twenty-five in the lecture hut with the leaking corner, in his shirtsleeves, standing over six professional men with a pencil in his hand like a sergeant.

"Right. Everyone's got paper. Everybody's going to draw the animal. Yes, you as well, Dr Vance. No, it doesn't matter that you can't; nobody can; that's not what this is."

They draw. It is dreadful.

"Now," says John. "Look at what you've all done. Every single one of you's drawn the head and the shoulders and not one of you's drawn the tail, because you were bored by the time you got there. That's the entire problem with illustration and it's why you can't trust a photograph either. You draw what you're interested in. So the rule is: you draw the whole animal, every time, even the boring end, because in four years the boring end's going to be the bit that matters and you won't know that yet."

Chettle, who has been at this longer than John has been alive, is writing.

"Second rule. Date it. Every sheet, the day you did it, on the sheet, in ink, at the time — not in the evening, because in the evening you'll put the wrong date and you'll never know you did.

"Third rule, and this is the one nobody does and it's the only one that actually matters. *Number it.* Consecutively, from one, for as long as you live, in one sequence, no gaps. Not one sequence per animal. Not one per year. One sequence."

"Why?" says Ames.

"Because then nothing can go missing," John says, "and if something does go missing, everybody can see it has."

He says it perfectly cheerfully, and turns round to the blackboard, and Pascual, at the back of the room by the door, has to look very hard at the leaking corner of the roof for a moment.

---

Vance is the best of the six and everybody knows it by the second week, and Vance also knows exactly what he came for and it is not entirely mermaids.

Pascual watches it happen over about ten days and cannot decide, at any point, whether he is watching something to be stopped.

It starts with the habitat section of the big paper, which is Jean's, entirely, from the first line to the last, and which Vance has read the way Bassani read the fisheries bulletin. He asks her one question about salinity gradients at the third pond and she answers it for forty minutes. By the following Tuesday he has been out to the salt ponds twice in the truck. By the Friday he has seen two years of counts and the plots, and has stopped talking about mermaids altogether.

"She built the whole of it out of an unfunded corner of somebody else's institute," he says to Pascual at the rail, admiringly, in the tone of a man appraising a boat. "In her own time. With a postgraduate's allowance and a borrowed boat."

"Yes."

"You know we've got eleven miles of frontage."

"I've gathered that."

"Six ponds is a chain. We've got a whole system." Vance is quiet for a moment. "I'm going to make her an offer, Dr Pobil. I'd rather you heard it from me than from her, and I'd rather you didn't tell Tar for a week, and if you'd rather not agree to that I'll understand."

"I'm not going to agree to that."

"No," says Vance, unoffended. "Fair enough."

---

He tells Reggie that night. Reggie says, "I know. She told me on Thursday," and goes on writing.

"She told you."

"She came into the office and said, *the American's going to offer me something and I want you to know I'm going to listen to it, and I want you to know I told you before he asked.*" Reggie puts the pen down. "Then she said that if I made it awkward she would take it out of me for the rest of my life."

"And what did you say?"

"I said I'd write her the best letter anybody in this field has ever had, and that I'd rather she had a system than a corner." He rubs the bridge of his nose. "And then I said the wrong thing, which is that I'd been expecting it since March, and she was extremely angry, because it turns out a woman doesn't care to be told her leaving was foreseeable."

"*Ay.*"

"Yes."

Pascual thinks about it.

"She knows everything," he says. "About all of it. The file, the plates, the boy, the two of us. Everything."

"She knows all of it and she's never once said a word of it out loud, including to us, and she isn't going to start in Florida." Reggie picks the pen up again. "That's not why she's going and it won't be a consideration in it, and if you want the truth, I wouldn't stop her if it were. She's thirty. She's had four years of me. I've watched her be right in front of a room and have somebody else's name put on it, and I did the putting."

---

The boat goes out on the seventeenth of October, on a flat morning, for a turtle transect.

It is on the roster because the fisheries work is still the thing that pays the electricity, and because six men who have come to learn about mermaids can perfectly well spend a day being useful, and because Hester's schedule has a line in it about survey method that has to be discharged.

Pascual goes because he has not been on the water in five months.

Ames swims the line with fins and a mask and a tape, which nobody expected of him and which he turns out to be quite good at, calling up the count. Pickersgill writes it down. Bassani is sick over the side twice, with great dignity, and goes on working.

And Reggie handles the boat.

He does it for four and a half hours. He does it in an old shirt with the sleeves gone and no hat, going brown as a nut and filthy to the elbows, keeping the launch a constant sixty feet off the transect line so that the wash does not put the turtles down, in a two-foot swell and a wind that shifts twice, holding a position relative to a moving man in the water with nothing to steer by but the man and the reef.

He is completely silent for four and a half hours. He does not smirk once. Twice he takes his glasses off, wipes them on his forearm, and puts them back without looking at anything except the water.

At about eleven Pascual realises he has been watching him for forty minutes and has not thought about anything at all.

---

They come in at two and the six of them go up the path arguing about turtle density, and Pascual stays at the jetty and helps make the boat fast, and says, without any preparation, because if he prepares it he will make it worse:

"Now I've understood about the boat."

Reggie coils the line.

"You've been arguing with me about a garage for eighteen months," Pascual says, "and I thought it was a joke, and then I thought it was you being stubborn, and this morning I watched you do a thing for four and a half hours with your whole self and not once look up." He sits down on the bollard, which is not where a bollard should be. "And there's no result in it. That's the part I couldn't get. You can't publish a morning like that. There's no paper in it at all."

"No."

"It's the only thing you do that's got nothing in it for you."

"It's the only thing I do," Reggie says, "where I'm not thinking."

He gets the second line and does not look up.

"I've never been able to stop. Not once, not for an hour, not asleep — it goes on the whole time and it isn't a pleasant condition and I haven't got a word for it that doesn't sound like boasting or complaining. And a boat off a transect line in a swell wants the whole of your attention every second and gives you nothing back and can't be got right, only kept right, and while you're doing it the other thing stops." He straightens up. "That's all it is. I found it out in the second month I was here, running the line for Charles, and he asked me at the time why I liked it and I told him it was a job that wanted doing, and that was a lie, and he was too polite to say so."

The water slaps under the jetty.

"Buy the boat," Pascual says.

"You've fought me about that boat for —"

"Buy the boat," says Pascual. "In November, with the house. And I don't want to hear one word about the eighteen feet, and I'm not saying this twice, and if you make me repeat it I shall change my mind out of pure temper."

Reggie stands on the jetty with a coil of wet line in his hand and looks at him, and does not say anything at all, and Pascual has to go and be busy with the fenders.

---

Ames is the other thing, and it takes Pascual until the twentieth of October to see it, and when he sees it he sees it all at once.

The boy is everywhere. He is at the rail at six. He is in the lecture hut copying John's numbering rules onto a card. He has read the fisheries bulletin because Bassani told him to and has then read Labat, in French, which nobody asked of him. He asks Pascual four questions a day and three of them are good. He is not clever in the way John is clever, which is a hand and an eye; he is clever in the other way, the way that takes a thing apart on the table to see how it goes.

And Reggie has begun giving him work.

Not lessons. Work. The bloods from the last quarter, to be plotted against the intake series, unsupervised. The whole of the Bergen correspondence, to summarise. A question about the surfacing intervals that Reggie has been chewing for a year and has now handed over entire, in a sentence, at breakfast, the way one hands somebody a spanner.

Pascual watches Ames come out of the office on the twentieth with a folder under his arm, going up the path at a sort of trot with his ears red, and thinks, quite mildly: *how nice. He is being brought on.*

And then, about four steps later, with the concrete going cold under him in the sun: *that is how it was done to you.*

---

He does not say it that night, or the next.

He says it on the twenty-second, at half past eleven, in the small room with the window, with the rain finally coming, and he says it badly.

"Ames."

"Mm," Reggie says.

"You've given that boy the Bergen letters and the blood series and the surfacing question."

"He'll do the surfacing question. He's already got further with it than I did and he's only had it eight days."

"Reggie." Pascual props himself up. "Who gave *you* the surfacing question?"

The room is very quiet. Out on the iron the rain begins in earnest.

"Halliwell," Reggie says, after a while.

"Was that a name at that place?"

"He ran the section. He was sixty-one and he was one of the four or five most interesting men in England and I would have gone through a wall for him." Reggie is lying on his back with his arms folded under his head, looking at the ceiling, and his voice has not changed at all, which is the thing Pascual has learned to be frightened of. "You want me to tell you that they gave me orders. Nobody at that place ever gave me an order in four years. That is not how it was done and it would not have worked on me for a fortnight. What was done was that a man of sixty-one looked at a boy of twenty-three and said: *Tar, I have a question I have never been able to get anywhere with, and I do not suppose you would care to have a go at it.* And gave me a room, and a technician, and as much material as I asked for, and stood back."

"And you did the work."

"I did the work and I was extremely happy," Reggie says. "That is what you are not going to be able to hold in your head, and I would rather you had it in the plainest form there is. It was the happiest I have ever been at anything until this island. Four years. I got up in the dark because I wanted to be in the room. Nobody made me do one single thing. It was all *appetite*, from the first week to the last, and the reason it went on so long is that it never once felt like anything but the best work anybody was doing anywhere, which incidentally it was."

Pascual lies in the dark and makes himself keep his hand where it is, flat on the man's chest, over the ribs.

"When did you know?"

"There is not a date. I have looked for one." A pause. "There is an afternoon in the spring of 'forty-nine when I was doing something perfectly ordinary and I found I had stopped, and I could not have told you what had stopped me, and I went and washed my hands and went back and did it. And there are eleven months after that. Eleven months in which I knew and went on, and that is the part I mind, and it is the part I would have to say out loud in any room where I said any of the rest of it."

"And Halliwell?"

"Died in 'fifty-two. Perfectly peacefully, at home, and there was a very fine obituary in *Nature* and I read it four times." Reggie turns his head. "He taught two of us. Me, and a man called Reynard, who was a year behind me and better with his hands than I ever was and who is not dead and is working now, and I have read his name in three journals since the war and I know precisely what he is doing from the titles, because I taught him half of it myself."

There it is. Pascual had known there would be one and had not known what shape it would be.

"You taught him."

"I taught him. He was twenty-four and he was eager and he was extremely good, and it did not once cross my mind to do anything but bring him on, because bringing a good man on is the most natural act in the world and there is no version of it that feels like harm while you are doing it." Reggie's voice is entirely level. "And that's the whole of my objection to Ames, and it isn't an objection, because I'm going to go on giving him work in the morning. He is the best young man I have had in ten years and he has come to learn how to keep an animal alive, and if I don't bring him on, somebody else'll bring him on somewhere else and I shan't be in the room."

"So you're going to do it anyway."

"I'm going to do it anyway. Under a schedule Hester wrote, on an island where you're forty feet away at all times, in a place where I've got to say out loud at supper what I've given him and why." He shifts. "That's the difference and it's the only one I've got, and I want you to know that I know it isn't a large one."

Pascual lies there for a long time.

"It's larger than you think," he says at last. "Halliwell stood back. That was the whole of his method — he stood back, and he was sixty-one, and there wasn't anybody in that building whose job it was to be a nuisance to him." He gets his arm round the man properly and hauls him in, because the alternative is to go on talking. "I'm going to be a nuisance to you until I'm seventy-one. It isn't a large difference. It's the only one there's ever been."

---

The rain goes on all night, the steady kind, the kind that runs off the new roofs of the new huts where six strangers are asleep.

In the morning it will be the twenty-third of October. There are five weeks of the first intake left, and then there will be another six men in February, and another six in the spring, and Hester's schedule runs to 1962 on the current instrument.

Pascual lies awake a while and does the sum he has been doing since June.

Two animals in this station, both alive, one of them thirteen pounds heavier than any mermaid ever recorded. Two more in Naples in the spring, in one pen, correctly paired, because a man read a roster and held his broker. Eleven miles of frontage in Florida, and a woman going out to it who has never in her life let a thing go past. A young man of twenty-three with a folder under his arm going up the path at a trot.

And somewhere off the south end of the chain, four hundred miles away, six or seven boats and a broker who is paid by the head.

He does not get it to come out. He has never once got it to come out. He goes to sleep with his hand on somebody's back, and down at the pens, in the rain, in the dark, two animals who are not alone move about in the water.
