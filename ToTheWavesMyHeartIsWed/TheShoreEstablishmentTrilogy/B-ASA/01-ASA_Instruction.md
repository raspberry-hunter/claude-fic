# Instruction

He has been in the office since twenty past four, which is not a hardship, because he has not slept much and did not want to.

The two letters are squared on the desk in front of him with a paperweight on each, and he has read them so many times that reading them has stopped doing anything, and the light is coming up grey through the office window in the flat way it does before a rain that is now nine days late.

Pascual sits down in the chair with the split cane in it.

"Right," Reggie says. "The whole of it. Stop me when you want to."

"I'm not stopping you until you've finished."

"You will."

"I know," says Pascual. "Go on."

---

He starts with Florida, because Florida is the easy one and he wants the easy one out of the room.

They have money and frontage and they have read the paper properly; the man who wrote it quotes the surfacing intervals correctly and has understood the intake curves, which puts him ahead of about half the field. He would like to purchase a breeding pair. Failing that, a single animal with a view to obtaining a second.

"And we haven't got a pair to sell," Pascual says.

"No. That isn't the point."

"Then what's the point?"

"The point," Reggie says, "is that I've spent three weeks finding out where he'd get one, and I have found out, and I didn't enjoy it."

He turns the paperweight round with one finger.

"They come off the south end of the chain. Four hundred miles. There are men who take them and there have been men who take them for about forty years, and it isn't a trade, it's six or seven boats and a broker in Port of Spain who's got a standing arrangement with three institutions and the aquarium business. They're paid by the head, delivered alive. Beta came that way. Gamma came that way. The Navy brought Chumley in a canvas tank because the Navy could, but the Navy bought him off the same broker, and I have the invoice, because Hester keeps everything."

Pascual has gone very still.

"About one in three survives the passage," Reggie says. "That's not a guess and it isn't the broker's figure, which is four in five. It's mine. I built it out of the Martin's own intake records against what he says he shipped in the same years, and it's the only honest number anybody's got, and I have had it for eleven days."

"You've had it for eleven days."

"Yes."

"And you didn't —" Pascual stops himself, with both hands, visibly. "Go on."

"So when a marine station in Florida writes to the William Martin Institute asking to buy a pair," Reggie says, "what he's actually asking is that three animals be taken out of an estuary so that two of them arrive. And if I write back and say we haven't got any, he'll write to the broker on Tuesday, because the paper told him it can be done and the paper has my name on it."

The generator does what it does. Down at the pens somebody drops a bucket and there is a burst of laughing.

"That's the easy one," Pascual says.

"That's the easy one."

---

The other letter is four lines longer and has a figure in it.

*It would be of considerable value if officers and scientific staff from associated establishments might attend at your Institute for periods of instruction in the husbandry methods described.*

It comes from a ministry. It has been forwarded by a naval attaché who is American, which means two governments have discussed the Martin with each other before writing to it, and Reggie has been turning that fact over for three weeks the way one turns over a stone one already knows the underside of.

"The figure is more than three times the whole establishment," he says. "It isn't a grant. It's a standing arrangement, renewable, and it'd put this Institute on a footing it's never once been on since the millionaire died, and the Colonel knows the exact shape of it because I put it in front of him on Monday."

"And what does it buy them?"

"Men. In relays. Eight weeks at a time, six or eight a year, learning how to keep a mermaid alive."

"Learning it from you."

"From me," Reggie says, "and from you, and from John's plates, and from Jean's figures, and from watching you take blood out of an animal that lets you. There's no other way to learn it. That's the whole of the position. There's a paper, and the paper's forty-one pages, and forty-one pages won't teach a man to do this any more than a book will teach him to deliver a foal."

Pascual sits with his forearms on his knees and looks at the floor.

"So they can't do it without us," he says.

"They can do it badly without us. They're going to do it badly without us; four institutions have written to Jean since March asking about pen dimensions, which is precisely the wrong question, and one of them is in Naples and has a pen already and is going to put two adult males in it." Reggie hears his own voice and lowers it. "They'll kill a great many animals learning what we already know, and then they will know it anyway."

"That's an argument for doing nothing."

"It's an argument for either. That's what I've been sitting with."

---

Pascual gets up. He goes to the window and stands with his back to the room and his hands in his pockets, which is not a thing he does.

"Say the part you haven't said," he says.

Reggie considers several ways in and takes none of them.

"If the Martin is the only place that teaches it," he says, "the Martin sets the terms. Nobody learns it from me who hasn't agreed in writing to keep them in pairs, to send returns, to declare where the animal came from. I can put conditions on the instrument and I can make them conditions of the money and not of my goodwill, and Hester can write it in a way that bites. That's the argument for yes, and it's the only one I've got, and I've checked it eleven times, and it holds."

Pascual turns round.

"It's paper," he says.

"Yes."

"Reggie. It's *paper*." He is not shouting. This is the other thing, the quiet one, and Reggie's stomach goes over. "I've spent a year of my life on this island learning exactly what paper's worth. I took two sheets out of my own file with my own hands and folded them into my shirt and a man is officially dead because of it. I watched Hester Bevan put two plates into a fisheries return in a place where they will not be found in a hundred years. I have signed a certificate that is true in every word and a lie in the whole, and it went in *easily*, and I haven't got over that and I don't expect to. And you're standing there telling me you'll make them behave by writing it down."

"Yes."

"And in fifteen years there'll be a man in a ministry who's never met you, holding a piece of paper he didn't sign, in a year when somebody wants something quickly." Pascual's hands have come out of his pockets. "You know this. You know it better than I do, because you *were* that man, in that building, and you've told me what you did there, standing up, in my sick bay, for a quarter of an hour."

Reggie does not say anything.

"Well?"

"I have not got an answer to that," Reggie says. "I have looked for one since the twenty-eighth of May and there is not one. You are right. The conditions will hold for some years and then they will not, and I cannot tell you which year, and I am going to do it anyway. That is the true position and I would rather give it to you than the other thing."

Pascual looks at him for a long moment.

"*Ay,*" he says, and sits back down.

---

Here is the part Reggie has not budgeted for, and he ought to have.

Pascual does not go on. He sits in the split-cane chair with his hands loose between his knees, in a room going slowly light, and he does the arithmetic, and Reggie watches him do it and finds he cannot breathe properly.

Because this is the moment. This is precisely the moment he described on an iron cot a year ago last week — *I am going to be standing in front of you every time, and I will be a great deal louder than I was in May, and you will not enjoy it* — and he has been standing in front of him for twenty minutes and been louder, and Reggie has not got a single thing left to say, and it is now entirely in the hands of a veterinary surgeon of nineteen years' standing whether the William Martin Institute for Marine Studies teaches the world to keep mermaids.

"Four animals," Pascual says at last.

"What?"

"I've known four animals of this species. Alpha I never met; he was dead before I came and Jean opened him and found pneumonia, which wasn't pneumonia. Beta was in a box on his own for nine months and went down and down and it took a boy sitting on a rail with his feet in the water to stop it, and he's in the sea now, if he's anywhere. Gamma was nine days from her death and I watched her get up off the bottom of that pen. And Chumley's four inches longer than he was in March and this morning he hit the ladder with his tail until I came." He looks up. "Three of the four should be dead. Two of them are alive, and the reason's a thing that's written down and can be taught in eight weeks to a man of ordinary competence. That's the whole of my professional life and there's nothing else in it."

"Yes."

"So." Pascual sets his hands on his knees. "If we do nothing, they'll pair them anyway, because the paper tells them to, and that is the only thing the paper tells them. They'll pair whatever two animals they happen to have got. And the ones who pair them wrongly will lose both, and the ones who pair them rightly will still lose them in eighteen months instead of six, because the pairing isn't the whole of it and we have never once written down the rest."

"What's the rest?"

"You know what the rest is. Shade over the water in the afternoon. Somebody who comes when the ladder is hit. Fish weighed in and residue weighed out every single day, by a man who does it himself, because the intake goes down three weeks before the weight does and the weight is the only thing anybody thinks to measure." He is counting on his fingers, which he does when he is angry and being accurate at the same time. "That is not in your forty-one pages. It is in a fisheries bulletin that four hundred people read, under the name of a man who is officially drowned. And if they get eighteen months instead of six, Reggie, that is *worse*, because eighteen months is long enough to make the trade pay and not long enough to make it good."

Reggie sits with that for a moment.

"Yes," he says.

"And if we teach them, they'll take more of them, and most of the ones they take will live properly." Pascual shrugs, angrily, the way he does when the shrug is admitting something. "I've done that sum four times since March and it comes out the same way every time and I hate it, and it's still the right way round."

Reggie sits very still.

"Say it plainly," he says.

"I'm agreeing with you." Pascual's jaw is set. "There. That's me agreeing with you, and I want it noted, because I've said in this room that I'd stand in front of you every time and this is the first time and I'm not standing in front of you, and in ten years you won't be able to tell me I didn't know."

"I wouldn't —"

"You wouldn't do it on purpose. You'd do it kindly, which is worse." He points at the desk. "Write it down. Now, in the day book, in your hand and mine both. *P. Pobil concurs, 15.vi.55, having been told the one-in-three figure.* Every word of it. Because when it goes wrong I'm not going to be a man who was carried along by a colleague, I'm going to be a man who read the arithmetic and said yes, and I'd like that in ink where I can go and look at it."

Reggie gets the day book and unscrews the pen and writes it, and turns it round, and Pascual signs it in his blunt hand and blots it with the side of his fist.

"Now," Pascual says. "Terms."

---

They do the terms in an hour and a half, on the back of two spoiled sheets, with the light coming up properly and the heat arriving with it, and Reggie enjoys it more than he has enjoyed anything since March, and knows he is enjoying it, and does not stop.

*No animal to be held alone.* Not for observation, not for treatment, not for quarantine, not for a fortnight, not for a week. Where an animal must be separated for medical reasons it must be within sight and hearing of another, and the period must be reported.

*No instruction by correspondence.* No protocol to be issued separately from the man. Every establishment sends a man; the man comes here; the man does not go home until he can do the whole of it.

*Not less than eight weeks*, and every week of it hands-on, including the residue and the hosing and the parts nobody will want to do.

*Quarterly returns*: weights, intake, surfacing intervals, mortality, to the Martin, from every establishment, for as long as it holds an animal.

*Source declared.* Every animal, every time, with the name of the man who took it and the date it came out of the water.

*No animal to be taken from the wild for any establishment that has not got a second animal ready to put it with.*

"That last one's the one they'll argue about," Pascual says.

"That's the one I want."

"Then don't put it last. Put it third, and put something ridiculous last, and let them win the ridiculous one."

Reggie looks up.

"Where did you learn that?"

"From watching a woman of sixty-two do it to the United States Navy," Pascual says, "on a verandah, with a teapot," and Reggie puts his head down on the desk and laughs until he has to take his glasses off.

---

The Colonel receives them at eleven, in the bungalow, with the fans going and the shutters half over.

He is seventy-one this year. Nobody says so. He came down the two steps from the verandah into the sitting room this morning and put his hand on the frame of the door as he did it, which he did not do a year ago, and Reggie noticed it and has been carrying the noticing about ever since like something in a pocket.

Reggie reads the terms out. It takes four minutes.

The Colonel listens with his chin down and his hands on his knees and does not interrupt once.

"Mrs Bevan," he says, when it is done.

Hester takes the two spoiled sheets and reads them through twice, and then a third time with a pencil.

"Your conditions are conditions of instruction," she says. "That is no good at all. A condition of instruction is enforceable by withdrawing instruction, which means that in five years' time, when a station in Naples has three animals and has stopped sending returns, your only remedy is to refuse to teach a man they have already got." She makes a mark. "Make them conditions of the *money*. The grant is annual and renewable and the renewal is audited by people who have never seen a mermaid and do not care about mermaids and will not deviate from a schedule by one comma. Put the returns on the schedule. Then it is not you who has to be brave in 1961; it is a clerk doing his job, and clerks are very reliable."

Reggie sits back.

"That's better than mine," he says.

"Yes," says Hester. "It usually is. I shall want the drafting to myself and I shall want three days."

"Will it hold?"

She puts the pencil down.

"It will hold for about seven years," she says. "That is not nothing. I have made things hold for a great deal less, and I have made two of them hold for forty. But you asked me and I will not tell you a lie about a document. After seven years it will hold only for as long as the person reading it wants it to hold, and by then there will be men in the field who have never heard of you, and one of them will read your name on the schedule and think: well, he is dead, or he is old, or he is four thousand miles away."

"Yes," Reggie says.

"So make certain that what you teach them with your hands is better than what you write down," Hester says. "The paper is for the seven years. The men are for the rest of it."

The Colonel makes a low sound of agreement and shifts in his chair.

"Tar," he says. "Money from a ministry is not money. It is a hand on your collar which they do not use for some years. I have taken it four times in my life and it has never once been given back and I would take it again, in your position, this morning, and I want you to understand that I am telling you both things at once."

"Yes, sir."

"They'll want a director's name on the instrument."

The room does something very slight.

"There is a director's name," Reggie says.

"There is *now*," says the Colonel, and looks at him for a moment with an expression that Reggie cannot read at all and will think about for a considerable time, and then reaches for the water jug. "Answer them by Saturday's boat. Mrs Bevan will have the schedule. Pobil — you'll have men in your sick bay eight weeks at a stretch, six or eight a year, from Michaelmas."

"I know it."

"It'll be perfectly bloody."

"It will," Pascual agrees.

"Good," says the Colonel. "Then you have thought about it."

---

Jean gets it out of him at four o'clock at Gamma's pen, in the flat white heat, with the sky standing there refusing to do anything.

She does not ask. She has the pen dimensions correspondence under her arm and she is only there to hand it over, and she looks at his face for about two seconds and says, "Oh, you're taking it."

"Yes."

"Good. Take it." She leans on the rail. Down below, Chumley goes past very fast and upside down for no reason anybody has been able to establish. "You'd be mad not to. Three times the establishment and we'd have a proper microtome by Christmas and I'd have a boat for the ponds that isn't Juan's cousin's."

"Mm."

"Have you got as far as the other thing yet?"

Reggie looks at her.

"Ah," Jean says. "No, then."

"Say it."

"Six or eight strangers a year, eight weeks each, in relays, for ever." She says it quite kindly and does not look at him while she says it. "Reggie, there hasn't been a fortnight on this island in two years when there wasn't something on it that couldn't stand a stranger. This place *runs* on nobody being here. That's how we got Charles's inventory done, and that's how Hester keeps whatever it is she keeps, and it's how you and Pascual manage to have a blazing row about the centrifuge at breakfast every Tuesday and nobody thinks anything of it at all."

The heat comes up off the concrete. Somewhere behind them John says something to Nestor about the light.

"From Michaelmas," Jean says, "there's always going to be somebody here who doesn't know us. Always. For thirty years, if it works. That's what you're buying, and I think you should buy it, and I'd have signed it this morning. But you ought to have got there before four o'clock in the afternoon and I don't think you had."

"No," Reggie says. "I hadn't."

"Mm." She straightens up off the rail. "You never do see the room, you know. It's the single most restful thing about working for you and it's going to get somebody hurt one day."

She goes off up the path with the correspondence under her arm.

Reggie stands at the rail for some time.

He is aware — distantly, in the way one is aware of a tooth — that he has been standing at this rail for three weeks doing an enormous amount of extremely careful arithmetic about mermaids, and that a woman of thirty has just done the sum he did not do, in one sentence, without being told a single thing.

Down in the water Gamma comes up under the calf and moves him bodily out of her way with one shoulder, the way she does.

---

John finds him at half past five, sunburnt, with a pencil behind his ear.

"Is it true they're sending us men?"

"From Michaelmas, probably."

"To teach."

"Yes."

John considers this. He is twenty-five now and has stopped going red about things, mostly, which Reggie regrets more than he expected to.

"Will I have to teach them to draw?"

"You'll have to teach them to draw, and to date it, and to number it," Reggie says. "That's the part that's yours and there's nobody else who's got it. Half the value of everything we know is that you numbered them."

"God," says John, with feeling. "They'll all be frightful at it."

"Yes."

"Some of them will be quite good, though. One or two." He looks out at the water for a moment. "That's a bit better than nothing, isn't it."

"It is," Reggie says.

John nods, and hitches the portfolio up under his arm, and does not mention the letter from Woods Hole that has been in his footlocker for a year and eight days, and goes off to supper.

---

He writes the answer on the Friday night, at the end of the verandah table, with the lamp and the moths, because that is where he writes anything that matters and he has stopped pretending it is a coincidence.

It is not a difficult letter. It says yes. It says that the Institute will undertake instruction from the Michaelmas quarter; that instruction cannot be given except in person and on the station; that the conditions annexed are conditions of the grant and not of the Institute's goodwill; and that the Institute will require, from every establishment holding an animal in consequence of this instruction, a quarterly return in the form set out.

Hester's schedule runs to two pages and is a beautiful piece of work. The ridiculous condition is number nine and concerns the minimum permissible volume of the water changed daily, and it is far too high, and they will win it in the first exchange and be pleased with themselves.

Number three is the one about not taking an animal for a pen that has not got a second animal in it.

He signs it, and puts the pen down, and sits for a minute with his hand flat on the envelope, and thinks about a broker in Port of Spain, and about one in three.

Then he seals it.

---

At half past ten the bolt goes across, and there is a small room eight feet by ten with an iron cot in it and a knot in the mosquito net, and the rain is still not coming, and the window is open on air that has not moved since Tuesday.

Pascual is lying on his back with one arm behind his head and Reggie is more or less across him, which is an arrangement they arrived at some months ago without discussion and have not since seen any reason to revise.

"I want to tell you something," Pascual says, "and you aren't going to like it, and I'm going to say it anyway, because we've become a pair of people who say things."

"Go on."

"When you were laying it out this morning — the money, and the men, and the eight weeks. Somewhere in the middle of it, about when you said *three times the establishment*, I thought: then the lease on the north-side house is nothing. Then it's eleven pounds a month out of a budget that won't notice eleven pounds. Then there's a director's house, and a medical officer who lives in it because the launch is unreliable in October, and nobody on this earth thinks anything about it at all."

He says it to the ceiling, flatly, the way he says a bad result.

"I thought that," he says, "in the middle of a sentence about animals being taken out of an estuary, and then I went on listening to you, and then I told you I had done the sum four times and it came out the same way. Which is true. I did and it does. But I want you to know there was a thing in the room with me while I did it, and that I'd rather tell you than sit in that chair in a year's time knowing I didn't."

Reggie lies still and feels his own heart going.

"Was it in the room in March?" he says.

"What?"

"When you did the sum the first time. In March, when Jean had the letters from the four institutes and you sat at that bench and told me about Naples. Was it in the room then?"

There is a pause.

"No," Pascual says. "No, it wasn't."

"Then it didn't decide anything," Reggie says, "and you'd have got to the same place with your eyes shut, and you know it, and you're going to lie there and be disgusted with yourself for a fortnight regardless, so I'd rather you did it out loud where I can hear you."

"*Ay, Dios.*"

"You do the same thing to me. It's not agreeable."

"It isn't meant to be agreeable," Pascual says, and Reggie can hear that he is smiling and does not have to look, and puts his hand flat on the ribs where it can be felt, and leaves it there.

The generator goes on. There is no rain.

"November," Pascual says, after a while.

"November."

"The garage takes a boat."

"It takes a *boat*," Reggie says, sitting up so fast that the cot makes its noise. "It takes an eighteen-foot boat, I've measured it, I measured it in March with a rule and I haven't mentioned it once in three months —"

"You've mentioned it four times."

"Twice."

"Four. I counted, because I could not believe it was happening." Pascual hauls him back down without any particular effort, which Reggie permits, in the interests of the experiment. "Go to sleep. There'll be six strangers in my sick bay by Michaelmas and I intend to be rested."

---

He does not, particularly.

He lies in the dark with the window open on air that will not move and thinks about the letter in the office and the schedule that will hold for seven years, and about a station with somebody on it who does not know them, always, from September, for as long as any of this works; and about a house on the north side with a garage that takes a boat; and about a hand on a door frame at eleven o'clock this morning that was not there a year ago.

Somewhere about two the air changes, and it comes at last: the first of it on the iron, small and fast, like sand thrown.

Down at the pens, in the dark, two animals who are not alone move about in the water.
