# Custody

Mrs Bevan has kept the minute book of the William Martin Institute for Marine Studies since the fourth of October 1946, in a hand that hasn't altered by a degree in eleven and a half years, and she's never once written down anything that mattered.

That isn't deceit, it's method. A minute records what a meeting decided; if it records why, or who was frightened, or what the Director's face did at the word *Naples*, then it isn't a minute, it's a weapon lying about the place waiting for somebody to pick up. She learned that in 1922 from a man in the Colonial Secretary's office who'd learned it the hard way, and she's kept eleven and a half years of impeccable, useless minutes, and everything of consequence that has ever happened at this Institute lives in her head.

There's rather a lot of it now.

---

The Colonel is taken ill on the fourth of February at about twenty to five in the morning, in his own bed, and doesn't make a sound.

She wakes because he has stopped snoring.

Afterwards — much afterwards, in April — she'll find that this is the detail she can't get past. Not the colour of him, nor the eleven minutes it took Pobil to come up the path with his bag, nor the four days in which nobody would say anything useful. The silence. Forty years of a particular noise in a room, and then no noise, and a woman of sixty-two sitting bolt upright in the dark at twenty to five having understood the whole thing before she was properly awake.

She's done that before. It was December of 1917 and she was three quarters of a mile away with a book in her hand, and there's nobody left alive on this island who knows it.

---

He doesn't die.

Pobil's on the island in nine minutes and in the room in eleven and doesn't leave it for thirty-one hours, and on the second day he tells her the truth in about nine words: that the Colonel has had a coronary thrombosis; that he's survived it; that men who survive one frequently have another; and that he mustn't be allowed to go up the path.

"How long?"

"I've no idea," says Pobil. "Nobody has. It may be eleven years. It may be March."

"Thank you."

"Mrs Bevan —"

"That wasn't a courtesy," she says. "You are the fourth doctor I have had dealings with in my life and the first who has answered the question I asked."

By the tenth of February the Colonel is sitting up and complaining about the drains, and it's generally agreed on the station that he's got off lightly; and Hester Bevan, who has spent six days doing the accounts and the correspondence and the intake schedule for the Whitsun course exactly as usual, has come to a decision.

---

She has said the name Tom aloud twice in forty-one years.

Once in her own sitting room on the twenty-first of May 1954, to a boy of twenty-four with a click and a hum, because Jean Leslie had brought him to her and Jean Leslie had worked the whole thing out from a generator and a hand on a cheek. And once more that same afternoon, when Jean took that boy's hand and put it against Hester's face without asking either of them, and Hester shut her eyes and did not withdraw.

Not answered. Not the vow — only the one hand, the way a calf reaches up to its mother, which is a different thing altogether and everybody who has it knows the difference.

It was the first human contact of that kind she had had since 1917 and it lasted about four seconds and she has thought about it every week since.

The boy is dead in the register and probably alive somewhere south of here. Miss Leslie is in Florida.

So she has told two people in forty-one years and one of them can't be written to and the other one has eleven miles of frontage and a programme, and the Colonel is seventy-two and has had a coronary thrombosis, and if Hester Bevan goes the way the Colonel nearly went on the fourth of February then everything she knows goes into a box at the churchyard on the point.

That's the decision. It takes her nine weeks to work out how.

---

Here's what she has, in the order she generally has it.

She was Hester Carrow and she was twenty-one and it was 1916, and her father was in the customs service on the Essequibo, and there was a war on somewhere that took her two brothers and didn't otherwise reach them.

Below their landing there was a run of mangrove nobody went into because of the mud. She went into it because she was twenty-one and bored past bearing and there was nowhere else on that coast a woman could be alone.

She doesn't remember the first time. That's one of several things about it she has never been able to explain: there's no first time in her memory, there's simply a period during which she went to the mangrove because she liked it and a period afterwards during which she went because she couldn't do otherwise, and no join between them she has ever been able to find.

She called him Tom. He was an adult male of about eleven feet and he was there for a year and eight months, and one afternoon in the shallows she put her hand on the side of his face because she wanted to, and he raised his own and put it against hers, and the circuit shut with a click she felt in her teeth.

For a year and eight months after that there was not one hour, waking or asleep, in which Hester Carrow was by herself.

She was twenty-one. She thought that was how the world was and that she had simply come late to it.

---

On the ninth of December 1917 there were four men in the mangrove with a net and a boat and a tarpaulin, and they were paid by the head.

She isn't going to set down what that was like, here or anywhere, and she never has.

What she can set down is the part that's useful, and it's the reason she has to speak, and it is not the part anybody expects.

The bond does not survive. That's the thing people assume and they have it exactly backwards. It thins. Wherever they took him — and she has never known, and has spent forty-one years not enquiring, because an enquiry is a record — it was far enough and long enough, and over eleven weeks in the spring of 1918 the whole of it went quiet by degrees, the way a lamp goes down as it is carried out of a room, and by the summer there was nothing there at all.

He did not die in the mangrove. That is the part she has never been able to put down, and it is the part that matters for Aguilar, so they are going to have to hear it.

It thinned. It did not stop; it went by degrees, over eleven weeks, getting fainter and further and never once going out, which is what distance does and only what distance does — and so she knows, and has known since 1918, that they carried Tom alive out of the Essequibo and along a coast and on to wherever men paid by the head take them, and that he was in a tank or a tarpaulin or a pen somewhere at the end of it, calling, and that she went on being able to feel him getting further away for two and a half months and then could not.

Whether he was alive in 1919, or in 1930, or last Tuesday, she has no way whatever of knowing and never will, and she is sixty-two, and she has not enquired, because an enquiry is a record and there is nothing at the end of it but a man with a ledger.

What does not thin is the silence.

That's the correction she needs to give them and it's worth the whole afternoon. Grief heals; she's had grief, twice for brothers and once for a daughter, and it is a different animal altogether and it *closes*. This does not close. The channel goes and the place where the channel was stays exactly the size it was, permanently, and a person may live an entire competent life around it — may marry a good man in 1923 and be fond of him for thirty-five years and mean every day of it — and be, at every hour, in a room with something missing from it.

She was ill for two years and nobody ever gave it a name. In 1919 she stopped eating and in 1920 she started again, and there are eleven months in there she has never recovered and doesn't want.

She has been, since about 1922, entirely well and entirely accompanied by an absence. She is sixty-two. She can do it, and she is aware that she is the only person known to have done it for forty-one years, and that this makes her, on the only question that now matters at this Institute, the world's sole authority.

---

The William Martin Institute for Marine Studies exists because Hester Bevan wanted somewhere to put people like herself, and no one has ever suspected it, and it took her thirty-two years.

She didn't build it. She has never had a shilling of her own. What she has had is a husband with an extremely good war record and a very great many acquaintances, and forty years of being the person who writes the letters; and in 1946, a millionaire's executors looking for somewhere respectable to put a bequest, and a Colonel who wanted a project, and an abandoned American seaplane station going for very little.

She found the station. She wrote the memorandum. She wrote the constitution and the Colonel signed it, and it's a perfectly ordinary constitution with one clause in it that she put there on purpose.

Then she waited eleven years for somebody to bring her a mermaid that lived.

Alpha died. Beta went down and down in a concrete box. She read Dr Tar's mortality tables in 1950 and understood from them, with total clarity and no science whatever, that she had built a place where mermaids came to die at eleven months, and she was fifty-four years old.

She engaged Mr Cholmondeley in the autumn of 1952 and Nestor Aguilar in the spring of 1953, over the Colonel's preference in both cases, in the correspondence, where it doesn't show.

In the spring of 1954 she watched a young man come up from the pens grey to the lips and sit in the common room for an hour with his head in his hands, and she made a pot of tea and put a cup down beside him and went away without saying anything at all, because she knew precisely what had happened to him and precisely what he would be telling himself about the sun, and she had been telling herself things for thirty-seven years and knew better than to interrupt a man at it. She had hopes of Miss Leslie and was wrong. She considered Mr Clarke for four months in 1953 and concluded — correctly, she still thinks — that what Mr Clarke has isn't the thing; it's an eye, and a quite exceptional one, and no more mysterious than a good ear.

And she has never in twelve years been down to the pens.

Not properly. Not in the way that counts, which is with a hand in the water, and there are two reasons and only one of them was ever any good.

The bad one is that she is the Colonel's wife and the chief administrator of the Institute and a woman of sixty-two, and chief administrators do not climb about in pens like junior postgraduates, and she has spent twelve years finding that a perfectly sufficient answer whenever it came up.

The good one is the seventh of August 1949.

She went down at six in the morning when there was nobody about, and knelt on the concrete beside Alpha's pen, and put her hand in the water — and what came up her arm was not a hum. It was the white, and the call and no answer, and it went through the top of her head and put her sitting on the concrete with her ears ringing and the pen going round, and it took her about forty minutes to get back up the path, and she was ill for a day and a half and told the Colonel it was the heat.

That was proof, she decided. Proof that whatever this is, it is not for her any more; that she was too old or too long out of it or had imagined the whole of 1916 and got a dose of the sun for her trouble.

It was proof of nothing whatever except that Alpha was dying alone in a box, which she had known perfectly well before she knelt down.

So she built the place, and staffed it, and has sat in a bungalow four hundred yards up the path for twelve years with the whole of it going on below her, and has never once found out whether the thing that happened to her in 1916 was real or whether she made it up in a hot country when she was twenty-one.

---

Which leaves Aguilar, and here she has to be careful, because the thing she wants to be true is not the thing that is true.

He is not bonded. She has been watching him since October with the closest attention she's given anything in a decade and she's as sure of it as anyone can be at ninety feet: there has been no vow, because Chumley has one.

A mermaid makes two in his life if he's lucky. The first he makes as a calf, one hand up to his mother's face and no hand back, and it is not the vow and everybody who has it knows the difference. The second is the one with two hands in it, and Chumley made that some time in the winter of 'fifty-five with an enormous elderly female who has never had a calf of her own, and neither of them told anybody, and Dr Pobil worked it out in about a fortnight from the way she cuffs him and has never once written it in the day book.

And a mermaid does not make a second of those while the first is standing.

What Aguilar has is the other thing. He is sensitive — as sensitive as anybody she has met besides the boy — and there is an animal four hundred yards down the path who has noticed and who answers, in the way that a creature with company to spare will answer somebody standing on the edge of the water.

And so what she actually has is this: a man of forty-four who is sleeping badly; who has begun coming in on his day off; who was at the rail at half past five three mornings running in January when the calf was out of sorts; and a calf who, on the twenty-ninth of March, when Aguilar's youngest had a fever and he was kept at home two days, went to the ladder rather more than usual and got no answer and was perfectly well by Wednesday.

That is not a marriage. It is nothing like one and she would not dress it up.

It is a friendship between a lonely competent man and a young animal who has a mother and is in no distress whatsoever, and it is the most dangerous arrangement on this island, and nobody has seen it but her.

Because Gamma is old.

Because when Gamma dies — and Dr Pobil has been doing that arithmetic every morning for two years and she's been doing it since 1955 — it will not thin. Thinning is what distance does. Death does the other thing, and it does it in the time it takes an animal to stop, and there is no preparing for it and no getting used to it on the way down.

There will be in that pen, on that morning, a solitary adult male of about eleven feet, in the silence, with nothing standing.

He will be Beta.

And Nestor Aguilar will be standing four feet away on the concrete, sensitive, and fond of him, and perfectly free to put his hand on the side of that animal's face.

---

She goes across on the launch on the nineteenth of April, a Saturday, in the afternoon, having sent word that she'd like an hour.

She's been in the north-side house four times and has never once remarked on anything about it, which she considers to be the whole of what she owes those two men and is aware is less than they deserve.

They give her tea. There's a repulsive chair by the shutters which she notices and doesn't enquire about. Dr Tar has the look of a man braced for an administrative reorganisation; Dr Pobil has the look of a man who's worked out that it isn't that, which is a point to Pobil and always has been.

"I told Dr Tar in November that he might have the rest of it in due course," she says. "This is the rest of it. It will take about twenty minutes, and I would be obliged if neither of you spoke until I had finished. I have said it twice in forty-one years and both of those people are four thousand miles from this room."

She takes twenty-six minutes.

She gives them the Essequibo and Tom and the year and eight months and the hand and the answering hand. She gives them the ninth of December 1917 in one sentence and does not elaborate and neither of them asks her to. She gives them the spring of 1918 and the light going out over an hour, and the two years, and the eleven months she has never recovered, and 1922, and the difference between grief, which closes, and the other thing, which does not.

She does not trouble them with Andersen and Wilde. She recited those three lines to Dr Tar in her own sitting room in May of 1954 and he had them in front of Dr Pobil inside a fortnight, and both of them can do the two sentences as well as she can.

What she gives them instead is the sum, which she has been carrying since November and which she does not believe either of them has done.

The only part of this whole business that is not secret in any degree is the vow itself. It has been sitting in two of the most famous stories in the English language for seventy years, in plain words, where any child may read it. Everybody in the field has read them and not one working naturalist in forty years has taken the two sentences seriously — with a single exception, and the exception took a copy of Andersen out of a dead boy's crate on the twenty-second of May 1954 and read the passage aloud in a hut with two members of this staff standing in front of him, and put it back, and has not mentioned it once in four years.

"He has had one half of it since 'fifty-four," she says, "and he has now got the other half on eleven hundred feet of his own film. He does not need Mr Cholmondeley's notebook and he does not need me, and I should be very surprised if he has not already done what I have just done, some while ago, sitting on a train.

She gives them the twenty-first of May 1954 and Miss Leslie's hand round a boy's wrist.

She gives them the constitution, and the seaplane station, and two engagements over the Colonel's preference, and Miss Leslie, and the four months in 1953 about Mr Clarke.

And she gives them Aguilar, and the calf, and what will be standing in that pen on the morning after Gamma dies.

Then she stops and drinks her tea, which has gone cold, and waits.

---

Dr Pobil puts his face in his hands for a moment and takes it out again and says: "*Ay, Dios mío.*"

Dr Tar hasn't moved at all.

"May I ask about the clause?" he says.

She'd wondered which of them would go for it first, and she'd thought Pobil, and she is pleased to have been wrong, which is a sensation she doesn't often get.

"Clause eleven," she says. "*The Institute shall maintain in its care not fewer than two living specimens of Nereopithecus aestuarius at all times, save where no specimen is obtainable.* You've read it perhaps forty times. Everyone has taken it for a statement of scientific purpose and no auditor has questioned it in twelve years."

"It isn't a statement of scientific purpose."

"It is a provision," says Hester. "It is the only thing I have ever built that will outlive me, and it has sat in a safe in Bridgetown since 1946 doing nothing whatever, because for the first eight years this Institute could not keep an animal alive for eleven months, and a provision is no use at all if the thing provided for keeps dying."

She sets the cup down.

"You have made it work. Both of you, and Cholmondeley, and Clarke, and Miss Leslie, and I have watched you do it and said nothing. I should like you to understand that from where I have been sitting the last four years have not been a scientific advance. They are the completion of something I began in 1926 and had entirely given up on."

---

Then she tells them what she wants, because she is not a woman who visits people in order to be understood.

"I want clause eleven made real, and I want it made real before the Colonel dies.

"At present it is a line in a constitution with no money behind it, no trustee off this island, and nothing whatever to stop a Director in 1971 — and it will not be you, Dr Tar — from applying to have it struck out as an obstacle to the research programme. Which it is, and always was going to be.

"I want an endowment, separately held, with its own trustees, that pays for the keeping of two animals in perpetuity whether or not one paper is ever written about them. I want it drafted so that the trustees are obliged and the Director is not consulted. And I want it written into the terms of employment of this Institute that any member of the staff at any grade, including a technician, who is found to have formed an attachment of this kind, has a post here for life at the Institute's discretion and the animals' company at his own, and that this is not a favour and cannot be withdrawn for cause.

"I have drafted the whole of it. It runs to nine pages. Dr Tar will not like about four of them and Dr Pobil will not like one, and it is the one that matters, so we shall do that one first."

---

She'd expected an hour of argument, had budgeted for it and rather looked forward to it, and she doesn't get it.

Dr Tar reads the nine pages twice, straight through, without saying anything, which takes eleven minutes.

Then: "Where does the money come from?"

"That is the question I hoped you would ask and I have not got an answer to it. I have about four hundred pounds of my own."

"You'll have the standing body."

"I beg your pardon?"

"There's going to be an international body for captive sirenians in about three years, eleven governments, a budget line, an inspectorate. It'll want a reference station that's manifestly not a commercial holding and not a national laboratory, and it'll want it very badly, because every one of those eleven governments will refuse to let it sit in any of the other ten." He's taken his glasses off. "A station under a perpetual obligation to keep two animals alive, held by trustees, with a Director who can't touch the endowment, isn't an obstacle. It's the single most attractive constitutional feature such a body could possibly find, and if I put your nine pages in front of the right man in the right month he'll think he thought of it."

"You are going to be offered that body."

"I've been offered it. In November. I said ask me in 'fifty-nine."

"And what will you say?"

"I've no idea," says Dr Tar. "But I know what I'm going to want in exchange, as of about four minutes ago, and it's nine pages long."

---

Dr Pobil's objection is the one she ranked first, and it takes him longer to make than she expected, and she understands why while he's making it.

"Clause eleven. *Not fewer than two living specimens at all times.*"

"Yes."

"Where do they come from?"

"Yes," says Hester.

"You're asking me to sign a document that says this Institute must always have two mermaids in it. That isn't a provision, Mrs Bevan, that's a standing order to a man in Port of Spain. If Gamma dies in 1961 — and she will — then the day after she dies that clause obliges us to buy one." His hands are flat on the table. "And about one in three survives the passage. I've had that figure since June of 'fifty-five and it hasn't got easier."

"I know."

"Then you know what you're asking."

"I am asking you to sign a document that will, over thirty years, cause the deaths of a number of animals which I cannot estimate and neither can you," Hester says, "in order that there should exist somewhere in the world one place where a person to whom this has happened is not obliged to survive it alone. Yes. That is exactly what I am asking, and I have had nine weeks to find a way of putting it that is less ugly, and there is not one."

Dr Pobil looks at the table for a long time.

"There is one," he says.

"I beg your pardon?"

"There's a way of putting it that's less ugly, and it's in Florida, and it's thirty years long." He gets up and goes to the shutters, which he apparently does when he's being accurate. "Miss Leslie isn't in Florida to keep mermaids. She's finding out what an estuary has to be like before a mermaid will breed in it, and she's given herself thirty years, and everybody including me has been treating that as a very fine thing a clever young woman is doing in the corner.

"It isn't in the corner. It's the only exit from the sum you've just put in front of me. Your clause obliges us to hold two animals for ever; the trade can't supply that for ever without killing three for every two — and it won't have to, if there's anywhere on this earth by 1975 where they're being *born*." He turns round. "So I'll sign your nine pages. And I want a tenth, and the tenth says a fixed proportion of that endowment goes to Miss Leslie and to whoever has her work after her, in perpetuity, whether or not she is ever able to show one single result, for as long as this Institute holds an animal that came out of a boat."

Hester Bevan sits with that.

"That is better than mine," she says.

"It usually is," says Dr Pobil, without a flicker, and Dr Tar makes a noise into his tea that she elects not to notice.

---

At the door, with the papers under her arm and the launch waiting, Dr Pobil says:

"Mrs Bevan. One more thing and then I'll let you go, and it isn't administration."

"Yes?"

"Gamma is twenty-six months paired, she is fat, she is bored, she has a calf who supervises her, and she is the calmest animal of her kind that anyone has ever recorded." He says it in the voice he uses for a prognosis, which she has now heard twice. "You've never been down to the pens. In twelve years."

The harbour goes on doing what it does.

"No," Hester says.

"Because Alpha drove you back, and Gamma drove you back in the first year, and nobody would have let you near Beta."

"Yes."

"That was five years ago." Dr Pobil has his hands behind his back. "There's nothing down there now that could drive anybody back. There hasn't been since about August of 'fifty-four, and it didn't once occur to me, or to him, or to anybody on that station, that there was a woman four hundred yards up the path who'd been kept out by a noise that stopped four years ago."

Hester finds that she has to put her hand on the door frame.

"I've never asked."

"You've never asked for anything in your life except on somebody else's behalf, and I've been watching you do it since 1953." He steps back. "Come down on Tuesday. Early, before the intake. There'll be nobody there but Aguilar and me, and we'll both be at the far end doing the weights with our backs turned — and if you'd rather we weren't there at all, say so now and we won't be."

---

She goes back on the six o'clock launch with nine pages and a tenth in Pobil's blunt hand on the back of a feed sheet, and does not look at either of them once.

The spray comes over. The harbour does what it does in April.

She has been forty-one years getting to this. She has given away the whole of it in twenty-six minutes to two men who took it without blinking and improved it inside the hour, and she finds she has nothing left that nobody knows, and turns that over for about a minute the way she turns over an unbalanced column, and puts it down, because it isn't accurate.

There is one thing more and she has not given it away and she is not going to. It is on page two hundred and six of a notebook in a place where she has kept something for forty-one years, in a dead boy's handwriting, and it came nearer in one sentence than she has managed in four decades, and she has carried it about in her head since May of 1954 the way one carries a stone in a pocket:

*I do not think he is company. I think he is the place where I am not alone, which is a different thing, and I do not know what I shall be if it ever shuts.*

---

On Tuesday the twenty-second she comes down the path at twenty past six in the morning, in her ordinary clothes, with her hat on, because a woman of sixty-two does not go down to a pen without her hat.

Aguilar and Pobil are at the far end with the balance and the buckets and their backs turned, exactly as promised, and neither of them looks round once in the next forty minutes, and she will be grateful for that for the rest of her life.

She stands at the rail.

Nothing. Of course nothing. Dry concrete and a rail and a great deal of noise from the generator, and Hester Bevan standing on it in a hat, sixty-two years old, feeling exactly what she has felt every time she has stood on this concrete since the seventh of August 1949, which is that she is a foolish woman who imagined something in a hot country a very long time ago and has built an institution on top of it.

She could go back up the path now. She has done it before.

There is the edge of the pen, eight feet in front of her, where a boy used to sit with his feet in the water.

---

Getting down off the rail and across to it takes her some while and is not dignified and she does not care.

There is a step cut into the wall just above the waterline, where a towel used to be left. She sits down on the edge above it and arranges her skirt, because one does, and looks at the water for about a minute.

Then she puts her hand in.

---

It is a hum.

That is all it is. It is very slight and it is perfectly ordinary and it comes up the arm and settles somewhere behind the breastbone, and it is the same hum it was in the mangrove below her father's landing in the spring of 1916, before there was ever a name or a hand or a vow, when it was only a girl of twenty with her fingers in brown water finding out that the world had something in it she had not been told about.

Forty-one years. She has not had it once. She has not had it because she has not put a hand in the sea since 1918 — not in the Atlantic on the way to England, not in the Bristol Channel, not in this harbour in twelve years — and she has told herself for four decades that this was because there was nothing to hear any more, and it was not.

Her hat is going to come off. She takes it off and puts it on the step.

---

Gamma comes up the length of the pen without hurrying.

She is very large and extremely fat and she is twenty-six months paired and she has not been frightened of anything since the summer of 1954, and she comes to the wall below the step and puts her head out of the water and stops there, about four feet away, and waits.

There is nothing coming off her. That is what Hester cannot get over, sitting on the edge with her hat beside her: no wall, no white, no call and no answer, none of the thing that came up her arm out of Alpha's pen on the seventh of August 1949 and put her sitting on the concrete. Just an enormous animal in warm water, well fed, mildly interested, with her calf asleep at the shallow end, waiting to see what the woman is going to do.

Hester Bevan has not decided anything.

She finds her hand is on the side of Gamma's face.

---

Nothing comes back. Not a hand; not the click; not the thing that shut in the mangrove with a noise she felt in her teeth. Gamma is Chumley's and has been since the winter of 'fifty-five and will be until one of them dies, and Hester knew that before she came down the ladder and would not have it otherwise.

What she gets is one hand and no answer and about four seconds, which is the same as she got in her own sitting room on the twenty-first of May 1954, and it is nothing whatever like it.

Because there is no pain in this one.

There is warm water and a full stomach and a shape at the shallow end that is *hers* and is asleep and is fine, and a very faint idea about fish, and somewhere behind all of it a great slow steady unbothered *thereness* that has not the smallest thing to say to Hester Bevan and is simply going on, the way a fire goes on in a room where a person is sitting.

Four seconds of a room with something in it.

She takes her hand back.

---

Gamma stays where she is for a moment, and then rolls over and goes off down the pen after a fish, because she is an animal and it is twenty to seven and there is a bucket at the far end.

Hester sits on the step for a while with her wet hand in her lap.

*It was real.*

She has been sixty-two years old for four months and she has run an institution for twelve of them and she wrote the constitution of it herself in 1946 with one clause in it that nobody has ever questioned, and she has not, until twenty to seven this morning, been in possession of one single piece of evidence that any of it happened.

Then she puts her hat on, and gets up, which takes her some while, and goes back across to the rail.

She straightens her cuffs and looks at the far end of the pen, where two men are doing the weights with their backs turned and have been for forty minutes.

"Dr Pobil," she says.

He does not turn round.

"Mrs Bevan."

"The Whitsun intake is six," she says. "I have put them in the block and doubled up the second week and I shall need the roster from you by Friday."

"You'll have it Thursday."

"Thursday will do."

And Hester Bevan goes up the path.
