# Returns

The house on the north side has eleven steps up from the road, a verandah that faces the wrong way for the evening, four rooms, and a garage that will take an eighteen-foot boat.

They move in on the ninth of November with two Institute trucks and Nestor and Nestor's cousin and a great deal of unnecessary shouting.

By four in the afternoon it is done, and the trucks are gone, and Reggie Tar is standing in the middle of a room he has been costing since June of 1954 with a crate of books at his feet and absolutely no idea what to do next.

He has never in his life arrived somewhere he was trying to get to. There have always been the next four things. He puts his hands in his pockets and takes them out again and looks at the wall, and behind him in the kitchen there is the sound of a man unpacking a saucepan and swearing at it, and it occurs to Reggie, with something close to alarm, that this is now simply where he lives.

"There is no shelf in here," Pascual says, from the kitchen.

"I know."

"Why is there no shelf?"

"Because the previous tenant took the shelf."

"He took a *shelf*."

"He took two shelves, a light fitting and the bath plug," Reggie says, "and I've got the inventory, and I signed it, and I didn't read it."

There is a silence of some quality.

"You didn't read it," Pascual says.

"No."

"You. Who read a footnote eleven times."

"I was thinking about something else."

"*Ay, Dios mío,*" says Pascual, with enormous satisfaction, and goes on unpacking, and Reggie stands in his own front room and finds that his chest has gone tight and that he is smiling at a wall with no shelf on it.

---

The first week is not comfortable and he had not expected it to be.

Eighteen months of a bolt on an inner door has taught them both a set of habits that make no sense here and will not switch off. He catches himself listening for the generator, which is four miles across the water and cannot be heard. He wakes at ten past four out of pure discipline and lies there working out what he is supposed to be being careful about.

There are neighbours: a retired customs officer on the harbour side, a Portuguese family with six children below the road, and a Mrs Hanley who keeps the shop at the corner and knows everything about everybody within four hours of it happening.

What Reggie had not properly understood — and he is aware that Hester understood it, and that this is why she made no comment at all when he told her — is that a household is *more* visible than a room and considerably safer. A bolted door at eleven at night invites one question. Two bachelors sharing a house because the Institute launch is unreliable in October and one of them is the medical officer and the other is the Director invites none, because it has an answer, and the answer is dull, and dullness is the best cover there is.

Mrs Hanley, within a fortnight, has decided that Dr Pobil does the cooking because Dr Tar is helpless, which is true, and that Dr Tar is a bit sharp, which is also true, and having settled it she loses interest.

"She has decided about us," Pascual reports, on the third Sunday, coming up the eleven steps with the bread.

"What has she decided?"

"That you're difficult and I'm long-suffering."

"That is the correct finding."

"It is," Pascual agrees, "and it's taken her two weeks, and it took this island fifteen months, and I've never in my life been so glad to be badly thought of."

---

The boat is bought on the second of December and she is eighteen feet and she is called *Doris*.

"You'll change it," Pascual says.

"I won't."

"Reggie. It's a boat called *Doris*."

"It's unlucky to rename a boat."

There is a pause on the quay of a length that Reggie will be enjoying for years.

"It is *what*?"

"Unlucky." He is coiling the bow line and does not look up. "You don't do it. Everybody knows you don't do it."

"You," says Pascual, "withdrew four years of your own published tables in a footnote on page nine and read it eleven times to be sure it was honest, and you won't paint over the word *Doris* because some fisherman in 1810 —"

"It's a well-attested principle."

"It's a *superstition*."

"It's a well-attested principle," Reggie says, "and I've no intention of defending it, and if you say another word about it you can row."

Pascual sits down on a bollard and puts both hands over his face and stays like that for some time, and the men working the next boat along look over with interest, and Reggie coils the line and is aware of being, at that moment, entirely happy.

---

The Michaelmas six go home in the first week of December.

Chettle shakes his hand and says, "You've got a good man in Pobil," in the tone of somebody conceding a hand of cards. Bassani says nothing at all for about four seconds and then embraces him, in front of everybody, which the Colonel affects not to see. Vance says he will write, and does, monthly, and always answers.

Ames goes last and does not want to go, and stands about on the jetty with his kit until it is embarrassing.

"The surfacing question," he says.

"You've done four months' work on it in eight weeks. Finish it."

"It's yours."

"It was mine when nobody could get anywhere with it. It's yours now, and you'll put your name on it, and I'll be second, and I'd be glad to be second." Reggie sees the boy's face do something and looks away from it, because there is no honest way to receive that. "Ames. You'll be told to do things at that ministry. Some of them will be interesting."

"Sir?"

"Send me the returns yourself," Reggie says. "Every quarter. Not through the office — under your own hand, with a covering note, and put in it anything that struck you as odd, including things you can't support. Especially those."

Ames says he will.

He does, for eleven years.

---

Jean sails on the seventeenth of February.

It has been coming since October and it has been arranged with enormous competence by everybody concerned and Reggie has been dreading it in a way he has not admitted to a single person, including himself, until about the tenth of the month, at which point he began sleeping badly and told Pascual it was the heat.

The letter he writes for her is four pages and it is the best thing he has written in two years and he does not show it to her. He shows it to Hester, who reads it twice and says, "Yes," and hands it back.

Her last night there is a dinner on the verandah with three speeches, two of which are bad, and John gets extremely drunk and presents her with a portrait of herself at the salt ponds in a hat, which is the funniest object anybody on the station has ever seen and which she will hang, for the next forty years, in every office she ever has.

Afterwards Reggie walks her down to the jetty because her kit goes aboard at six in the morning and she wants to see where it is stowed, which is a lie and they both know it is a lie.

They stand at the seaward end near the bollard that is where no bollard should be.

"Right," Jean says. "One thing and then I'm going to bed."

"Yes."

"I drew the map."

The water does what it does under the planks.

"In the tin," she says. "The chart you gave him. *Reef opens here*, *Fresh water*, *Keep clear — ordnance*. That's my hand, and I did it on the Sunday, and I gave it to Hester and Hester gave it to you, and you've never once asked me about it and I've never said a word about it to any living soul."

"I know it's your hand," Reggie says. "I knew when it was in my pocket."

"Yes, well. I've been carrying it about for twenty-one months and I find I don't want to take it to Florida." She has her arms folded. "I'm not confessing. I'd do it again tomorrow and twice on Sunday. I want one person alive to know I did it, and it can't be Hester, because Hester knew before I did, and it can't be Pascual, because he'd want to *discuss* it."

"He would."

"So it's you, and you're welcome, and don't say thank you."

"No," Reggie says.

They stand there for a while. Out past the head there is nothing but water and the loom of the light on the point.

"He'd be thirty this year," Jean says.

"Thirty-one."

"Thirty-one." She lets out a breath. "Right."

Then she says, in an entirely different voice, brisk, the voice he has heard her use to bully a boy into eating his lunch: "Now. The broker."

"Jean —"

"You told me the one-in-three figure in November and then you looked at me as though you'd handed me a live shell, and I've been thinking about it ever since, because that is what one does with a live shell." She turns round. "Eleven miles of frontage. Six ponds is a chain; a system is a system. And every mermaid in captivity anywhere on this earth was pulled out of an estuary by a man who is paid by the head, and the entire field, including you, has decided that this is simply the weather."

"It's a supply problem —"

"It's a *breeding* problem, and nobody's looked at it, because nobody's ever had two of them in one place who weren't dying." She jabs a finger at the dark in the direction of the pens. "You've had a pair alive and well for twenty-one months and the only reason you haven't got a calf out of them is that Gamma's past it and Chumley's a baby. That's an accident of who you happened to be given."

Reggie opens his mouth and finds nothing in it.

"I'm not going to Florida to keep mermaids," Jean says. "Anyone can keep them now; you saw to that. I'm going to Florida to find out what an estuary has to be like before a mermaid will breed in it, and then I am going to spend about twenty years being extremely tiresome about estuaries, and in thirty years I intend nobody to have to buy one from a man in a boat."

"That'll take —"

"Thirty years. Yes." She smiles at him, crookedly, in the dark. "I'm thirty. I've got them."

---

He goes up the path afterwards and does not go to bed, and sits on the office step until half past one working out, with some difficulty, why he feels as though somebody has hit him.

It is not that she is leaving. He costed her leaving in about March of last year.

It is that in four minutes on a jetty a woman of thirty has laid out a thirty-year programme that is better than anything he has thought of since he came to this island, and that she has been assembling it in her own head, unfunded, unsupervised and unmentioned, while doing everybody's graphs.

He has been her supervisor for five years and he has never once asked her what she wanted to work on.

---

The Colonel does not come down to the jetty in the morning.

He has been up since five and dressed and he intends to, and Hester puts a hand on his sleeve in the doorway of the bungalow and says something too quietly for anybody to hear, and the Colonel says, "Yes. Quite right," and sits down again.

Reggie sees it happen from twenty yards off. So does Pascual. Neither of them mentions it, that morning or afterwards, and Reggie notices that he has begun keeping a private record of small things of this kind, the way he once kept a record of a man not touching him, and that he has about seven entries in it now and that he has never written any of them down.

The Colonel signs the instrument of appointment on the twenty-ninth of February, in the office, with Hester and a clerk from the Colonial Secretary's office as witnesses, and it takes ninety seconds, and afterwards he shakes Reggie's hand and says, "Tar. Don't let it become a school."

"Sir?"

"A school teaches the syllabus," the Colonel says. "Get on."

---

The first quarterly returns are due under the schedule on the thirty-first of March.

Hester has built the form. It is a single foolscap sheet with the columns already ruled and the headings already printed, and it goes out with the covering letter and comes back by post, and it is a masterpiece, because a man who will not write a report will fill in a form, and a man who will not fill in a form will be chased by an auditor in a ministry who has never seen a mermaid.

Four come in during the last week of March. The fifth comes on the fourth of April.

**Bassani, Naples.** Pair received 19 December. Both alive. Weights up. The intake column is filled in daily in two hands, Bassani's and somebody else's, and in the margin against the second week of January somebody has written, in Italian, *she took a fish and put it down again*, and in the next column the intake goes down for four days and comes back up, and there is a note about shade.

Reggie reads the margin note three times.

**Chettle, Whitley.** Immaculate. It is the best-kept record of the five and it is better kept than the Martin's own was in 1954. Pair received 11 January, together, in one tank, with a second tank standing empty which Chettle notes he has now had converted into a filtration plant so that the temptation is removed. A separate page, unrequested, of observations on play behaviour, in a small careful hand.

**Vance, Florida.** No animals. Construction. A page and a half on frontage, salinity and pond chains that Reggie recognises within four lines as having been dictated by somebody else entirely.

**Roque.** Thin, late, correct, nothing to say.

And **Pickersgill**.

---

He opens it at the office desk at half past seven in the morning on the fourth of April with a cup of tea going cold beside him.

It is a beautiful return.

That is the first thing and it will not stop being the first thing. Every column is filled. Every date is there. The weights are weekly and the intake is daily and the surfacing intervals are recorded three times a week and there are no gaps anywhere in ninety-one days, and the hand is small and neat and does not vary from the first line to the last.

Pair received 4 January. Female, adult, estimated eleven years. Male, juvenile.

Correctly paired. Exactly as taught.

Reggie reads the covering letter first, because it is on top.

> I am sorry to have to report that the female was found dead on the morning of the 26th of March. I attach the full return as required and I have not omitted or amended anything. I have gone over the whole of it a number of times and I cannot find where I went wrong. We kept them together throughout and did not separate them at any point for any purpose. I should be grateful for any observations you may have, however severe.
>
> The male is presently alone. I am aware of what that means. I have written to the same source for a second animal and expect delivery in June.

Then Reggie turns to the sheet and runs his finger down the intake column, and finds it in about eleven seconds.

---

He is still sitting there at nine when Pascual comes in for the weight book.

"You look —"

"Pickersgill's return." He pushes it across. "Read the intake."

Pascual reads it standing up. It takes him perhaps a minute and a half, and Reggie watches his face the whole way down the column, because he cannot help it and because that face has never once failed to tell him anything.

"*Ay,*" Pascual says.

"Say it."

"The twenty-fourth of February." He puts his thumb on the sheet. "Here. She takes eleven pounds instead of fourteen. Then eleven, then twelve, then ten. Then a week of ten and eleven. Then nine." He goes on down the column with the thumb. "And on the fifteenth of March, three. And she's dead on the twenty-sixth."

"Now the weights."

Pascual moves to the next column and stops.

"She is up two pounds on the eleventh of March," he says.

"She is up two pounds on the eleventh of March," Reggie says. "There it is. That is the whole of it. The weight of an animal that size lags a month behind and it lies to you all the way down, and this man weighed her every Sunday like a good man and every Sunday it told him she was well, and he had the true answer under his own hand, in his own column, in ink, for four and a half weeks, and nobody had ever told him to look at it."

He has heard his own voice go flat and level and does not do anything about it.

"He filled in the form," Pascual says.

"He filled in the form perfectly. He complied with every condition on Hester's schedule and he did not deviate by one line and the form did what the form was written to do, which is to produce numbers on the thirty-first of March. And here they are." Reggie puts his hand flat on the sheet. "They arrived nine days after the animal died, in an envelope, and I read them in eleven seconds, and I could have written to him on the twenty-fifth of February and told him what was going to happen."

"You could not have. You did not have the sheet."

"I know what I did not have. I am telling you what was on it."

---

They take it apart over the next two days and it does not get better.

There is nothing to find. There is no cruelty in it and no negligence and nothing anybody could put in a schedule. The pen had shade. The pair was right. The man was there twice a day and did the bucket himself, which they establish from the hand, because a hired boy does not write *slight residue, one fish only* in the margin at the end of a long column.

Bassani saw the same four days in January and wrote it down and did something about it and his animal is alive.

Pickersgill saw the same four days in February, wrote them down accurately, and did not know that four days was a sentence.

"It's in the fisheries bulletin," Pascual says on the second evening. "Two paragraphs. *Intake declines in advance of measurable weight change by a period of some weeks.* It's on page four of a journal four hundred people read, under the name of a boy who's officially drowned."

"And he had it. That's the part I keep going back to. He had the offprint; you gave him the offprint yourself in October; you gave them all one." Reggie takes his glasses off. "He had the sentence and he had the column and he did not put the two together, because putting the two together is not a fact, it is a *practice*, and a practice is not in any paper anywhere and cannot be."

"It is in your hands."

"Yes."

"And in mine, and in Bassani's now, and in Chettle's, and in the hands of four men in that room in October." Pascual sits down on the corner of the desk, which he does when he is about to be a nuisance. "That's your training centre, Reggie. That's exactly the thing you argued me into in June and it's the only thing on this earth that would have saved that animal, and it's going to save Naples and Whitley and it didn't save this man, because eight weeks is eight weeks and he didn't happen to be standing at the rail on a day when a fish went down."

Reggie does not say anything for a moment.

"That's the most generous construction anybody could put on it."

"It isn't generous. It's what happened." Pascual folds his arms. "Now say the thing you haven't been saying since Wednesday morning, because you've been walking about this island like a man carrying a bucket."

---

"He is going to buy another animal," Reggie says.

"Yes."

"He has one mermaid and she is alone, and he knows what that means, because we taught him what it means, and he has done the only decent thing available to him, which is to write to the broker in Port of Spain for a second. It is in his letter. He says so plainly and he says it as though he expects to be congratulated on it, and he is *right*, and it is the correct action, and I would give him the same advice this afternoon."

He is aware that he has stopped contracting and cannot get hold of it.

"So an animal died in a tank in the north of England," he says, "and the consequence of that death, under the conditions I wrote in June, under a schedule I asked Hester Bevan to make bite, is that a man in a boat four hundred miles south of here will go out in May and take one more mermaid out of an estuary. And about one in three of them survives the passage. So there is a reasonable probability that two others go in the water on the way, in a tank, on a deck, and nobody will ever record it, because there is no column for it on any form in the world."

The generator changes note.

"That is the arithmetic," Reggie says. "It has been the arithmetic since the fifteenth of June and I did it then and I told you I had done it, and I find I had not done it in this direction. I had it as a total. I had not had it as one man, doing the right thing, on the fourth of April."

"No," Pascual says.

"And there is no condition I can write that stops it. I have tried eleven versions in two days. Every single one of them ends with the survivor alone in a tank, which is worse, and I will not write that, and I am not able to write anything else."

---

They walk down to the pens in the dark afterwards, which they have not done together in months.

Gamma is enormous and asleep at the shallow end with her tail curled under her, and Chumley is not asleep, because Chumley is never asleep, and comes up the wall and hits the ladder twice with the flat of his tail and gets nothing and goes off round the pen in a huff.

"Twenty-two months," Pascual says.

"Twenty-three next week."

"She's the oldest animal of her kind ever held anywhere and she's bored and fat and the worst-tempered creature I've ever nursed, and I wouldn't give her up for a department." He puts his forearms on the rail. "And in three years the same'll be true in Naples and in Whitley and in Florida, and none of those animals would be alive without the two of us, and you're standing there doing sums about a boat off Trinidad."

"Both are true."

"Both are true. I've been carrying both since June and it hasn't got lighter and it isn't going to." Pascual looks down into the water. "So. What do you want, that you haven't got?"

Reggie thinks about it for a while, which he has been doing for two days, and finds that setting it out is the easiest part.

"Three things," he says. "One: somebody who reads the returns as they come in and writes back the same week. Not me — I've four establishments now and I'll have eleven by 1959, and a quarterly return read in April about a death in March is a post-mortem. That's staff and money and Hester can build it.

"Two: the practice has to get to the men who will never come here. Whitley and Naples are safe; there's four institutions I've never met holding animals tonight, and there'll be twenty by 1960, and the eight-week course doesn't reach one of them.

"Three." He stops.

"Go on."

"Three's that the thing which would reach them is a proper cautionary paper in a journal everybody reads, with the composition data and the intake practice and the whole of it, and I can't get one printed. I've tried twice. There is no result in it — there is one pen and one pair and a table of somebody else's deaths — and no editor in this field's going to give eight pages to a man saying *be careful*, and they're quite right, because it isn't science, and I'd have refused it myself in 1950." He puts his hands in his pockets. "It wants somebody with standing who doesn't need to be liked. Somebody who sits on the committees and can put a thing in front of the people who decide what gets printed and who gets an animal, and who won't be argued out of it, and that nobody can afford to ignore."

The water moves.

"I've been through the entire field in my head for two days," Reggie says. "There's perhaps six men alive who could do it. Four of them would want the Martin's material and I wouldn't give it to them. One's seventy-eight."

Pascual is quiet for rather a long time.

Then he says: "Say the sixth."

"No."

"Reggie."

"No. I've thought about it and put it down and thought about it and put it down, and it isn't going to happen, because of what he is and because of what he's got on us, and because you told me in that sick bay that you wouldn't forgive him if you lived to be ninety, and you were right about him and you were right about me and I haven't forgotten one word of it."

"I said that in May of 1954," Pascual says, "and I haven't changed my mind about him by so much as a hair, and I'm not going to."

He straightens up off the rail.

"And there's four institutions holding animals tonight that neither of us has ever seen," he says, "and a man in the north of England writing to a broker in May."

Down in the pen, Chumley comes back round and hits the ladder again, twice, hard, the way he does when he has decided that this time somebody is going to come.

"Say the name," Pascual says.

Reggie looks at the water for a long moment.

"Spilsbury," he says.
