# Correspondence

The room in Massachusetts is eleven feet by fourteen and has a window that does not open and four hundred and six index cards in a shoe box, and Alec Spilsbury has been sitting in it since half past five this morning with the shutters of the projector clicking.

He's fifty-two. He's been doing this since 1928. He has published a hundred and nine papers of which about eleven are any good and two will outlive him, and he's never in thirty years been able to explain to anybody what the actual work consists of, which is sitting in a small room being certain about things one's no business being certain about and then spending four years finding out whether one was right.

He's certain about this. He's been certain since the eleventh of August, on a train between Boston and New Haven, with a folder on his knee and nothing to look at out of the window.

---

Here's what he's got, and it's a great deal, and none of it came out of anybody's private papers.

He's got the vow. He's had that since the twenty-second of May 1954, out of a crate in a hut, in a copy of Andersen belonging to a boy who was three days dead, and he read the passage out loud in front of Tar and the illustrator to see what would happen, and what happened was that Tar wrote *Andersen* on an inventory in his ordinary hand and didn't look up, and the illustrator stood absolutely still by the crate. Two men, no reaction whatever, taking about four seconds longer to have no reaction than men who had never heard of it would have taken.

He put the book back. He hasn't mentioned it in four and a half years.

He's got the contact series. Eighteen subjects, two animals, eleven hundred and six reels, blind-scored by two people in this building who have never been within three thousand miles of a mermaid. Fourteen get nothing. Aguilar gets it on both animals; Bassani gets a fraction of it on one after four days in the country. It is the man.

And he's got the third thing, which took him four years to see because it was printed in a journal with his own subscription label on it, and which is why he doesn't need one page of Cholmondeley's notebook and never did.

*Tar, Leslie and Pobil, 1954.* The big paper. Page nineteen. The arrested decline of Specimen Beta on the introduction of a single human attendant — weights, intake, surfacing intervals, the lot, in a table, published by the man himself.

Set that against Aguilar and a hose fitting.

Cholmondeley got a *great deal* off an adult male five months into a decline in a concrete box. Aguilar gets a calf who fetches things off an animal that is fat and mated and has never wanted for anything in his life. The difference between the two isn't the men — Aguilar's at least as sensitive as the boy was, he'd stake the shoe box on it — and it isn't the species.

The signal goes with the state of the animal. The worse they are, the louder it is.

He wrote that on a card on the eleventh of August in a railway carriage and has looked at it about two hundred times since and it hasn't moved.

---

Which brings him to the experiment, and he isn't going to do it, and he wants the reasons written down properly because he doesn't trust anybody's account of their own motives, including his own.

The experiment's obvious and it's four lines long. Take a sensitive subject and a healthy paired animal; establish a baseline; separate the animal from its mate for a defined period; score the same subject at intervals across the separation and the reunion. Eleven weeks. It'd settle the question completely and there's no other design that would.

Reason one. Pobil would refuse, and Pobil has a written stop on the whole series that Spilsbury drafted himself in January of 'fifty-seven and improved, and he's never yet been fool enough to build a lock and then complain about it.

Reason two, and this is the one that actually kills it. The design won't answer the question.

You can't blind a subject to whether the animal is alone. He's spent four days trying to construct a version in which you can and there isn't one: the man's standing at the rail, there are either two animals in that pen or one, and he can *see*. So any change in his scores across the separation is confounded, permanently and irrecoverably, with the fact that he knows quite well what has been done and to whom, and that he's a man with a stake in it, and that he'll be in a state himself by the fourth week whether or not one single thing is coming off the animal.

He'd get a beautiful curve and it would mean nothing. Two years, an animal's health and the whole of his standing at that station, in exchange for a result Bergen could take apart in a paragraph.

He notes in passing that if the confound could be got rid of he doesn't know what he'd do, and that he isn't going to find out, and that this is a piece of luck and not a virtue.

Reason three. If it were done and it worked and it were published, then inside four years there'd be a man in Naples or Lisbon or Florida with an animal in a separate tank on purpose, and the eleven governments would have their headline, and the whole subject would be finished — not disgraced, *finished*, the way whaling science is finished, with nobody able to get a licence or a shilling for forty years. He wants forty years. He's said so in writing to Tar and meant every word.

So: no.

He writes *NO — not to be attempted, A.S., 12.ix.58* on the card, and puts it in the box under M, where he keeps the things he has decided against, and there's eleven cards in there and he's never taken one out.

---

He is going to publish the prediction.

That isn't the same thing and he wants to be clear with himself that he knows it isn't the same thing.

It goes in the discussion of the contact paper, four sentences, properly hedged: that the magnitude of response appears to vary with the condition of the animal; that the 1954 arrested-decline data and the present series are consistent with an inverse relation between the animal's welfare and the strength of the effect; that this is a single comparison across two studies with different designs and no weight should be placed on it; that a controlled test is possible and the authors do not recommend it.

Four sentences. It's honest. Every word of it's defensible and he'd defend it in front of anybody.

He also knows exactly what happens next, and he knows it because he's watched it happen a hundred times in thirty years and twice to his own work. Somebody reads the four sentences in a place with money and no scruple and no Pobil, and does it, and it takes about eighteen months, and there's nothing whatever anybody can do about it, and the man who does it will cite him.

The alternative's not to say it, and he's genuinely considered that for three weeks, which he wouldn't have done at forty.

But a hypothesis this size, sitting in one man's shoe box, isn't a kindness to anybody. If it's true then every establishment on earth is at this moment running a welfare experiment by accident, and half the men in the field are standing next to animals in various conditions with no idea the thing they feel is data. Somebody'll get there. Bergen isn't stupid. Vance isn't stupid, and Vance has Leslie now, and Leslie's a great deal cleverer than Vance.

And if he says nothing, and somebody works it out in 1963 with no hedge and no *the authors do not recommend it*, then it'll arrive in the world naked, and that's worse.

He puts it that way to himself twice, and finds it holds twice, and is aware — distantly, the way one is aware of weather in another country — that this is precisely the reasoning R. Tar used in the discussion of a case report in 1954, and that Tar's been standing in the consequences of it ever since, and has said so to his face on a verandah.

He notes that. He does not do anything about it.

---

He sails on the fourth of November and is on the island by the twenty-first, and the William Martin Institute is unrecognisable and he says so.

There's eleven buildings. There's a second lecture hut, a proper store, a slipway, and a laboratory with a microtome in it that made him briefly and genuinely envious. The Michaelmas intake's been and gone and there's two men left over doing an extra fortnight because Pobil wouldn't sign them off, which he approves of so much that he says nothing about it at all.

Bevan is out of it. Not dead — sitting on his own verandah in the mornings with a rug and a great deal of temper, being read the drains file by his wife.

And the wife has taken the whole of it up and is running it, and has been since February, and nobody on the station appears to have noticed that this has happened.

He watches Mrs Bevan for four days.

She's the most competent administrator he's met in thirty years in institutions, which he'd worked out in 1954, so that isn't the interesting part. The interesting part's a small one and he can't make it go away. On the second morning he comes round the corner of the store at twenty past six and she is at the pen rail, in a hat, entirely alone, doing nothing whatever, and when she sees him she says good morning and remarks that the Whitsun roster will want doing by January and goes up the path.

At twenty past six.

He puts it on a card that evening — *H.B. — pens, 6.20 a.m., no apparent business there* — and turns it over for about ten minutes, and gets nowhere, and files it under the ones he hasn't solved, of which there's rather more than people suppose.

---

He asks Tar about the standing body on the twenty-fourth, because he said he'd ask in 'fifty-eight and there's five weeks of it left.

They are in the office with the door open and the generator going.

"Rome," says Tar.

"That's not an answer."

"It's most of one. The answer's yes, in 'sixty-one or 'sixty-two, on terms, and the terms are nine pages long and drafted by Mrs Bevan and you're going to be shown them on Monday and you will like about six of the pages." Tar takes his glasses off. "I'm not going to pretend to think about it for another eleven months. You were right, and I've had a year to find the hole in it and there isn't one, and I'd rather say so now than perform a hesitation."

Spilsbury sits with that for a moment and finds, with some interest, that what he feels is not satisfaction.

He's spent two years arranging this. He's had four men to dinner about it. He's spoken to a person at the Foreign Office twice and to a person at the Smithsonian rather more than twice, and he's done all of it on the assumption that when it came off he'd feel the thing he felt in 1938 when the Bergen chair came through, and he doesn't, and the reason's perfectly obvious and takes him about four seconds to locate.

If Tar is in Rome running an inspectorate, Tar will not be here.

It isn't an unreasonable thing to notice. The reference station'll be here and Spilsbury will come to it twice a year for the rest of his working life and the correspondence'll go on exactly as before, and better, because a man in an international secretariat has a great deal more to write letters about than a man administering drains on a cay.

He notices, and puts it down, and asks the next question, which is about the endowment.

---

He tells Tar what he is going to publish on the twenty-eighth of November, at the end of the verandah table, with the lamp and the moths, which is where Tar writes things and which Spilsbury's begun to find restful in a way he doesn't intend to examine.

He gives him the four sentences.

He gives him the reasoning: that the comparison is real, that it is in Tar's own 1954 table and Tar published it, that a hypothesis of this size in one man's box is a hazard and not a kindness, that Bergen will get there and Leslie will certainly get there, and that a naked version arriving in 1963 with no hedge is a worse world than a dressed one arriving in 'fifty-nine.

He gives him the card in the box under M, and what is written on it, and the date.

And then he waits, because Tar's face has done something he hasn't seen it do in twelve years.

"Say it," says Spilsbury.

"You've just given me my own discussion section," Tar says.

"Yes."

"Word for word. *It is defensible. Every sentence of it is defensible. Somebody will get there anyway. A hedge is better than no hedge. I want it acted on and I know what the data will bear and I am going to write the other thing.*" He is not raising his voice. "I wrote that in July of 1954. It has killed animals in four countries that I know about and I have the returns in a drawer eleven feet from where you're sitting, and a man in Whitley lost an adult female in March of 'fifty-six because I recommended cohabitation on a sample of one, and I have not had one night since where I did not know it."

"I know all that. I helped you write the correction."

"You did. It was nine pages and it read like a dog's breakfast and it was the best thing either of us has done." Tar puts his glasses down on the table. "And now you're going to do it again, in four sentences, hedged, and you're right that it's defensible, and you're right that Bergen will get there, and I have not got one single thing to say against it that I did not say to myself in 1954 and overrule."

"Then say the thing you've got."

There is a pause of some length.

"Nothing," says Tar. "That's the whole of it. I have got nothing, and I want you to understand that I'm not being generous. I haven't the standing to object. I am the man who did this exact thing four years ago on worse evidence with less hedging for a better reason, and I would do it again, and the only difference between us is that you did the arithmetic first and I did it afterwards."

---

Spilsbury sits on that verandah for some while after Tar has gone in.

He isn't troubled. He's done what he came to do; he's been told his reasoning's sound by the one man alive whose opinion on the point is worth having, and he's been told it in a form that cost the man a good deal, and he's aware of the cost and doesn't know what to do with the awareness, so he leaves it where it is.

One thing does strike him, and he thinks well of it.

Pobil wasn't in that conversation. Not in the room, not sent for, and — this is the part — his name didn't come into it once in forty minutes, from either of them.

Two years ago that would have been unthinkable. In January of 'fifty-seven Tar couldn't agree the length of a session without turning round and putting it to the veterinary surgeon, and Spilsbury sat through eleven weeks of it and found it an admirable arrangement in a young institution and a slightly wearing one in a man of thirty-eight.

Tonight he took the whole of it himself, at a table, in the dark, and didn't reach for anybody.

Spilsbury has watched a great many men come into their own and he knows exactly what it looks like, and he goes up the path pleased about it.

There is one thing.

He's had, since the twenty-third of May 1954, a fact about R. Tar. He established it that week without much trouble and without any particular intention, and he's never used it, and has never referred to it since the evening he put it on a tablecloth to establish that he had it, which is a thing one does.

He was asked once, by Pobil, on a jetty in 'fifty-four, what he wanted. He said nothing, which was true.

Sitting here in December of 1958 with the moths going round the lamp, he finds he can construct, quite easily, four separate occasions in the last two years on which producing that fact would've got him something he wanted, and that on all four it didn't occur to him to do it, and that this isn't like him at all.

He turns it over the way he turns over anything.

The answer he arrives at is that the fact's worth more unused than used, which is true of most facts and is the whole of his method, and he writes that down on a card and looks at it and finds it isn't the answer.

There's one other card of that kind and he takes it out and looks at it, which he hasn't managed since August. It says *AGUILAR — dep. var.?* — he wrote it in a railway carriage in a perfectly good temper as an obvious next step, and he hasn't been able to look at it since, and he's no account of that either.

Two cards. No account of either. He's fifty-two years old and has spent thirty of them being able to say why he does things.

He doesn't get any further. It's late, and he's a great deal to do before the eighth.

He does notice, going up the path in the dark, that he hasn't the smallest desire to be on that boat.

He files that too.

---

There is one other thing and he has no idea what to make of it.

Tar has not gone home since Sunday.

He is in the block, in the end room, with the light on at two in the morning on Monday and on Tuesday and on Wednesday, and at breakfast he is exactly as he always is, and there has been no word of any kind about it and Spilsbury has not asked and is not going to.

He turns it over and produces four constructions, of which the likeliest is a row about the endowment, and he rather hopes it is, because Bevan's nine pages are the best thing to come out of that station in two years and would be worth rowing about.

He makes a card. He gets as far as *R.T. — block, 4 nights —* and stops, because he finds he does not want to write the rest of the sentence down, and files it blank, which is a thing he has not done since 1934.

---

On the fourth of December he goes down to the pens at six because he can't sleep, and finds Aguilar already there, weighing fish in the half-light, and stands at the rail with him for about twenty minutes without either of them saying anything.

The calf comes up and hits the ladder twice and gets an answer, which is Aguilar saying something to him in Spanish that Spilsbury does not follow, and goes away satisfied.

"Dr Spilsbury," Aguilar says, after a while.

"Mm."

"In your study. The men who got nothing." He is not looking up from the balance. "Fourteen of eighteen. Was it fifteen?"

"Fourteen."

"And you?"

"Nothing," says Spilsbury. "Twice, in 'fifty-seven, blinded like everybody else, and once in 1954 in that pen at half past five in the morning with my hand on an animal that was dying of it. Nothing at all. Not a thing." He puts his cup on the rail. "It's the most thoroughly established negative result in the literature, I established it myself, and I've read the score sheets about forty times looking for a way round it, and there isn't one."

Aguilar nods slowly and writes a figure in the book.

"I'm sorry," he says.

Spilsbury opens his mouth to say there's nothing whatever to be sorry about; that it's a datum and a good one; that a man who minds his own negative results has no business in the work.

He finds he doesn't say any of it.

He picks his cup up again and they stand at the rail in the half-light while a calf goes round and round the pen, and somewhere behind them the generator changes note, and in eleven weeks the contact paper will go to the printer with four hedged sentences in the discussion, and in three years and two months a man in a place with money and no conditions will read them.
