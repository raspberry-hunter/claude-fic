# Preferment

The calf round begins on the twelfth of August and it takes nine weeks and it settles the question, and Reggie is going to spend the rest of his life wishing it had not.

Chumley is four years old and six feet two inches and possessed of a curiosity that has cost the Institute two thermometers and a hose. He is, as a subject, everything Gamma is not: he comes to look at anything, he approaches everybody, and for the first fortnight the reels are useless because a juvenile male mermaid approaching a man at four feet is not a finding, it is Tuesday.

Spilsbury sees the problem in about a day and a half and fixes it by adding two items to the scoring schedule that Reggie would not have thought of in a year — orientation on departure, and whether the animal returns after leaving — and the second one turns out to be the whole thing.

Fourteen men. The calf comes to all of them, looks at all of them, leaves all of them, and does not come back.

Nestor goes in on the twenty-sixth of September at twenty to seven.

The calf comes at nine seconds, which is the shortest latency in eleven hundred feet of film. He goes away at two minutes. He comes back at three minutes forty. He goes away again. He comes back. He does this six times in eleven minutes, and on the fourth return he brings the hose fitting he has been carrying about since July and puts it down in the water in front of the man and backs off and waits.

Two people in Massachusetts who have never been to this island, working from a picture with nothing in it but water and an animal, score that reel outside the band on nine items out of eleven.

---

So it is not the animal.

Reggie writes it out on the ninth of October in eleven words and looks at it for some time.

*Two animals, eighteen men, one man in both. It is the man.*

He is a scientist of twenty years' standing and he knows precisely what he has: a single individual, replicated across two animals, in a design tight enough that the replication cannot be got rid of, plus one supporting case in Bassani who was in the water for four days and never came back. It is not a result. It is a *direction*, and it is the strongest direction anybody in this subject has ever had, and by 1960 there will be men in nine countries putting themselves in tanks to see whether they have got it.

There is also the thing he writes underneath it, which is smaller and worse.

*N.B. Both animals are well.*

Because whatever this is, they are measuring it on a pair that has been together four years and is fat and bored and in no distress of any kind, and what they get for it is a calf who fetches a hose fitting. In 1954 it was a solitary male five months into a decline, and what came off that animal went through a boy of twenty-four like weather and put him on a rail with his feet in the water for four hours at a stretch. Charles wrote it himself in November of 'fifty-three and Reggie has had the sentence in his head for three years: *I think it is both of us. I do not think he would do it to just anyone and I do not think just any of them would do it to me.*

So there is a quiet end of this and a loud end, and eleven hundred feet of film is the quiet end.

And the only reason nobody in the world knows there is a loud end is that the entire published husbandry says keep them in pairs, and the only record in existence of a man standing beside a mermaid in despair is four hundred pages in a dead boy's hand, in a place where Hester Bevan has kept something for forty-one years.

Reggie writes the note on the ninth of October and looks at it for a while and does not take it any further that day.

---

The third man may not be dead.

Reggie is careful with that sentence even inside his own head, because it is the only honest form of it. He is dead in the register of the Colonial Secretary, and on a certificate signed *P. Pobil*, and in a letter in a woman's hand in Shrewsbury. He went into the water on the twentieth of May 1954 with an arrangement, and the arrangement was a good one, and Reggie would put it at rather better than even that it held.

Rather better than even, and not one degree more than that. A man of twenty-four, alone, in a trade where boats are lost every season, with a name that was not his and papers worth nothing, and three and a half years in which no word has come — because no word could ever come, because the entire design of the thing was that no word should ever come.

So the honest position is that he is probably alive somewhere south of here, and may perfectly well have drowned in 1955, and nobody is ever going to know which. That is the exact and deliberate extent of it.

Neither of them has ever looked. Not once, not obliquely, not through Juan's brother, not by asking a harbourmaster an idle question in a bar in Bridgetown. Reggie has wanted to about eleven times a year since 1954 and Pascual, he suspects, rather more often than that, and they settled it between them on the second night and have not reopened it since, because the arithmetic is not difficult.

A dead man is safe precisely and only for as long as nobody makes an enquiry about him. An enquiry is a record. One letter, one payment, one question asked twice in the same port, and there is a thread running from a closed file with a death certificate in it to a living man — and the certificate, which is the whole of his protection, becomes instead the single most dangerous document in the Caribbean, with a medical officer's name at the bottom of it.

They do not look for him. The not looking is the protection. It is the only thing either of them has ever done for Charles Cholmondeley that cost nothing at the time and has gone on costing ever since.

---

The Nestor letter goes through the Colonel on the fourteenth of October and takes eleven minutes.

Nestor Aguilar, technician, on the establishment from the first of November, at a salary that Hester revises upward twice without being asked and once without telling anybody. Named in the acknowledgement of the eight-week paper and named as an author on whatever comes after. Told everything, in Spanish, by Pascual, over about two hours, with the door open and no notes taken; told that he may withdraw tomorrow; told what withdrawal would cost, which is nothing at all, in writing, signed by the Director.

He does not withdraw. He asks two questions. The first is whether it will hurt the calf. The second is whether he can have a copy of the film.

"Give him the film," says the Colonel.

"It's Spilsbury's film."

"Then buy him a copy out of my discretion and don't tell me what it cost."

---

Hester comes to the office on the sixteenth and shuts the door, which she has done four times in five years.

"I should like everything about Aguilar," she says. "The reels, the score sheets, the schedule, Dr Pobil's notes and whatever you have written down for yourself. Not a summary."

"You'll have it this afternoon."

"Thank you." She sits. She does not take out the minute book, which is the thing Reggie notices and puts away and will take out again later. "Now. Some questions, and I would rather you did not ask me why I want the answers."

"Go on."

"Does he sleep badly." She does not say it as a question. "Has he ever been unable to go near that pen — not unwilling; unable. Does he know when the calf is put out before he comes round the wall and can see him. When he came out of the water on the twenty-sixth of September and said something in Spanish about the cold, was he cold." She stops. "And I should like Dr Pobil to ask him, in Spanish, alone, with nothing written down, whether he has ever dreamed about the water in a way he did not care for."

Reggie sits looking at her.

He has spent eleven months inside the most carefully designed study he has ever been near, built by the best man in the field, scored blind in Massachusetts on a schedule of eleven items. Not one of the eleven items is on that list. Every single one of the things Hester Bevan has just asked for in about ninety seconds is a better question than anything on it.

"Where has that come from?" he says.

"I engaged Nestor Aguilar in the spring of 1953," Hester says, "and I engaged Mr Cholmondeley in the autumn of 1952, and neither of them was the strongest applicant, and in both cases I over-ruled the Colonel, who does not remember it because I did it in the correspondence."

The generator does what it does.

"On what basis?"

"I have been reading references and letters of application for forty years," she says, "and there is a particular kind of awkwardness that comes into them, and I have never been able to define it and I know it when I see it and I am right about two thirds of the time. That is all I am going to say about the method and it is all there is." She smooths her skirt. "I had hopes of Miss Leslie for about eighteen months and I was wrong. Miss Leslie's awkwardness was that she was a woman who intended to do science, which is a different thing and rather more curable."

"Mrs Bevan —"

"You may have the rest of it in due course," Hester says, "and not this week, and not because I am being mysterious, but because I have carried it for a very long time and I find I cannot do it to order." She stands. "Everything about Aguilar. This afternoon. And Dr Tar — the salary I put on that letter is not generous. I should like you to look at it again in April and again the April after that, and I should like it to become extremely difficult, over about four years, for that man to be offered anything by anybody that he would want more than what he has got."

She goes out and closes the door behind her.

Reggie sits for a while and then takes the minute book out of the drawer and looks at it and does not open it.

---

Reggie is pleased with the letter for about four days.

On the fifth day he is at the office window at half past six with the light coming up, and it arrives on him the way these things do, whole and unwelcome and about eleven months late: that he has protected a man by putting his name in a document.

He has heard the argument against it in his own kitchen. *It is paper, Reggie. It is paper.* He has heard Hester Bevan say to his face that a thing she drafts will hold for about seven years and after that only for as long as the person reading it cares to let it. He has watched a schedule with teeth produce a perfect return about a dead animal nine days too late.

What he has actually built for Nestor Aguilar is a salary, a name on a masthead, a signature from a Director who is thirty-eight and in good health, and the personal regard of the head of an institute who is seventy-two.

It is better than what Charles had, which was nothing at all.

It is not, he thinks, standing at the window, of a different kind from what Charles had.

---

He was asked once, by Pascual, in October, why this is not the same emergency, and he gave two answers at the time and has since found a third that he does not much care for.

The first is time. In May of 1954 it was one evening. There was a man on the island who had seen the thing happen with his own eyes and a boat off the jetty at eleven the next morning, and whatever was going to be done had to be done before breakfast. Nothing about Nestor Aguilar is going to happen before breakfast. What endangers him is a published finding that does not exist yet and will not for two years, and when it comes it comes by post, through committees, by way of men who write first. There is lead time. There was none at all in 1954, and the entire difference in the noise they are making is the difference between a jetty and a mailbag.

The second is what a man can be offered. Spilsbury takes people by offering them something — it is the only method he has ever needed and Reggie has never seen him need another — and Charles Cholmondeley at twenty-four was the most takeable human being Reggie has ever met. A postgraduate's allowance, no post, no publications, nothing behind him, a room in a hut with John Clarke, and a discovery he did not know was a discovery. He would have gone. He would have gone gratefully and written home about it and been in that building inside a year. Nestor Aguilar is forty-four and has a wife and four children and two brothers and a house at the top of the village, and there is nothing Alec Spilsbury has got that he wants. That is the actual protection, it is not paper, and Hester Bevan is quietly making it thicker every April.

Which raises the question Reggie has been carefully not asking himself since the sixteenth, and asked himself anyway on the seventeenth, and has now had eleven days of.

Could they have outbid him?

A post. A proper salary. A name on the fisheries paper, which was going to happen anyway. Hester over-ruling the Colonel in the correspondence the way she had already done twice without telling anybody. Somebody in a sick bay who would have sat down with him. Any of it, all of it, and a boy who wanted nothing on earth except to be allowed to go on doing what he was doing might have stayed and been safe and been *paid*.

And the honest answer is that Reggie Tar on the nineteenth of May 1954 could not have seen it, because on the nineteenth of May 1954 he did not know that Hester Bevan would back him — he did not know she had ever backed anybody — and he did not know what Pascual Pobil was to him, because the answer to that arrived somewhere in the following eleven hours and he had had no notice of it whatever. He had a boy in danger, a man leaving at eleven, and nobody he was sure of. So he did the only thing a man with nobody does, which is the thing he can do alone.

It is not a good answer. It is the true one. He has been standing in it for eleven days.

And the third answer is not new at all. It is the one he finally remembered on the ninth, having somehow contrived to mislay it for three and a half years, which is its own piece of information about him.

The boy had packed a knapsack.

Not on the twentieth. It had been in the corner of that hut for the better part of a week, and John saw it and thought nothing of it, and Reggie saw it on the Tuesday and thought nothing of it either, and it was three days before he understood what he had been looking at. Charles Cholmondeley was going to go. With permission or without it, in a boat or off the end of the cay in the dark, and probably within the fortnight, because he could not be in a room four hundred yards from that animal and he was not going to be talked out of it by anybody alive.

So Reggie did not get him away. That is the sentence he has had to take apart. What Reggie actually did, on one evening, with nobody behind him, was find out about a departure that was going to happen regardless and make it survivable — a name, a plan, an arrangement, money, and a fighting chance instead of a boy swimming out into the dark on his own with a rucksack.

He would still put it at rather better than even.

None of which is any use whatever for Nestor Aguilar, who is not going anywhere, and does not want to, and cannot be sent.

---

Spilsbury asks for an hour on the eighth of November, which is a Friday, and asks for it in the ordinary way, in the office doorway, at eleven in the morning.

"Not now. This evening, and not in here, and I'd rather Pobil weren't in the room, and I'd like you to know in advance that that's my preference and not a condition and you may bring him if you want him."

"Why wouldn't I want him?"

"Because one of the two things I'm going to say is about you," says Spilsbury, "and you'll want somewhere to put your face."

---

They sit on the verandah at six with the lamp not yet lit and the whole harbour going orange, and Spilsbury does the first thing in about four minutes, and it is the best offer Reggie Tar has ever had in his life.

"There's going to be a standing body," he says. "Not a committee — a body, with a secretariat and a budget line and about eleven governments in it, and it'll do for captive sirenians roughly what the whaling convention was supposed to do and didn't. Licensing of holdings. Compulsory returns with an inspectorate behind them. And — this is the part you'll care about — the power to say who may take an animal out of the water and how many and from where, with the figures published annually.

"It's four years off. It exists at the moment as an idea in the heads of about six people, four of whom I've had to dinner. It will happen, because the alternative is that the trade regulates itself and everyone with any sense now knows what that looks like, and because your paper made it thinkable and my name on it made it respectable.

"They'll want a director. It won't be me — I've got a laboratory and I'd be no good at it and I'd be dead in three years. It'll be based in London or Rome; you may have a preference and it'll be listened to. The salary's roughly four times what this place pays you and that isn't the point and I know it isn't."

He turns his glass round on the arm of the chair.

"You said to me in December of 'fifty-five — no. In your letter. July of 'fifty-six. *The practice has to get to the men who will never come here.* You wrote that down and sent it four thousand miles and asked me for eight pages in a journal, and I got you the eight pages, and the eight pages are not going to be enough and you knew that when you wrote it." He looks up. "This reaches every one of them. All of them. For thirty years, with an inspector and a licence, and a man in Port of Spain who has to write down what he took and where he took it from.

"I want you to have it. I want it more than I've wanted anything professionally in about a decade, and I'm telling you four years early because you'll need three of them to stop being frightened of it. That's all. That's the first thing."

---

Reggie sits and looks at the harbour and finds that he has gone somewhere else entirely, which has not happened to him in a long time.

Nineteen forty-six. November. Two in the morning, and a corridor with brown linoleum and a smell of hot dust off the pipes, and two doors eleven feet apart with a light under each.

He has told Pascual about this. Once, on a low chair in the sick bay in May of 1954, with the rain going and a man in the guest hut awake: that there was an evening; that he had been going to do something and had been stopped, in three seconds, quite pleasantly; that it had never been mentioned since and had never once been awkward; and that he had never known what Alec Spilsbury understood by it, and had arranged eleven years of his life around never finding out.

Every word of that was true when he said it, and he has now got the missing piece, and it has taken eleven years and a verandah.

He is twenty-seven. He has been in that building fourteen months and has not slept properly in six weeks and is the happiest he has ever been. He comes out of his room with a plate in his hand, going to the other door as he has gone to it two hundred times, and Spilsbury comes out of his at the same moment, and they stop, about three feet apart, in a corridor with nobody in it.

He has been looking at the man for fourteen months and in about another second and a half he is going to put his hand out.

And Spilsbury looks at him.

Perhaps three seconds. Not startled. Not angry. And not — this is the piece, this is the thing he has been eleven years getting to — not *surprised*. The way a man looks at a column of figures he added up some while ago and is merely confirming.

Then he said, quite pleasantly: "No, I don't think so."

And then: "Bring the plate through, would you, I want the eleven o'clock one."

And Reggie brought the plate through.

They worked until five. They worked together for three more years, in adjoining rooms, with a door between them that did not shut. Neither of them ever referred to it again — not obliquely, not once, not in eleven years, not on a jetty in 1954 and not at a kitchen table in 1956 — and it has never at any point been awkward, and Reggie has understood since about 1951 that this is because it was not, for Alec Spilsbury, an event.

He was looked at, and added up, and filed. The filing took three seconds and cost the man nothing whatever.

And then the other thing goes off, sitting quite still on a verandah with the best offer of his life in front of him, and it goes off so hard that he has to put his glass down.

*Do you remember Nicholson?*

A dinner table on the twenty-third of May 1954, seven people, a bottle better than usual, and a man saying comfortably to the table at large that Nicholson got himself into a great deal of trouble about four years ago in a public lavatory in Camberwell, and isn't at the labs any more. Reggie has spent three and a half years being unable to decide whether that was a threat, a warning, an offer, or an unlucky remark from a man who has never known what lands.

It was none of them. It was a receipt.

He had had it since November of 1946. He had had it, filed, for eight years, and never once used it or referred to it or altered by so much as a degree the way he behaved in a room — and then he sat at a table on an island four thousand miles from that corridor, having watched Reggie Tar for four days, and put it on the cloth where one man could see it and nobody else could, and went on to the next thing.

Not to make Reggie do anything. He wanted nothing that week and got nothing.

Simply so that it should be established between them that he had it. Because a man who knows a thing has an option, and Alec Spilsbury has never in his life let an option go by unrecorded.

---

He has had eleven years of thinking he could not read that man, and it turns out that he could not read him because he was doing the wrong sum. He kept trying to work out what Spilsbury felt. There is nothing there to work out. There is only what Spilsbury has *noticed*, which is everything, and what he has filed, which is all of it, and what he intends to do with any of it, which is usually nothing at all, right up until the afternoon when it isn't.

And there is one more piece, and it is the one he has to sit very still for.

He has told himself for a decade that he stayed in that building three more years for the work.

Sitting here at thirty-eight, he finds he has known better since about half past one in the morning on the eleventh of December last year, and has simply not put the two things side by side, because putting them side by side means understanding that for three years he was doing the thing he would fail a first-year student for, which is running the experiment again to see whether he gets a different result.

Nobody in that building was ever shocked.

Nothing he ever did landed.

And four miles across the water there is a man who has never once in nearly five years failed to be affected by anything Reggie Tar has said or done, who shouted at him in a canvas hut until the guy ropes rang, who put a hand flat on the side of his face at ten past six in the morning, and who counted the boat.

---

"Thank you," Reggie says. "I mean that and I'm not going to say it twice. Ask me in 'fifty-nine."

"I shall ask you in 'fifty-eight."

"Ask me whenever you like." He puts his glass down. "What's the second thing?"

Spilsbury does not answer at once, which is unusual enough that Reggie turns his head.

"On the ninth day of December 1956," Spilsbury says, "I gave you a list of eleven thin places in your record. You told me two of them were a man who died. I crossed them off and I haven't raised them since, and I want you to confirm that I haven't, because it matters to what I'm about to say."

"You haven't. Not once. Not obliquely."

"Right." He sets the glass on the boards. "I'm asking to put them back on."

---

"I've got a phenomenon," Spilsbury says, "and I've got two men who have it, and one of them is a technician with four years of exposure and the other is a station director from Naples with four days, which means exposure isn't it, which means I have got no idea what it is, and the thing I need more than anything else in the world is a *third case with a history*.

"And there was a boy on this island in 1954 who had it. I got nothing off that animal and he was getting a great deal, and I said so to Pobil at the time in terms I'm not proud of, and I've had it in my head for three and a half years.

"I don't want his body and there isn't one. I want what he did. How often, how long, at what hour, from what position, in what order, and what the animal did back — and it's in that day book somewhere, in the gaps, and it's in the plate sequence, and I'd lay money it's in a notebook of his that somebody on this station has still got in a drawer."

The harbour has gone from orange to grey.

"That's the ask," Spilsbury says. "You may refuse it and I shall not raise it again and I shall not go round you, and you have my word, and you know exactly what my word is worth because you've had it for eleven years and I have never once broken it. And I'd like you to understand that the two things I've said this evening are not connected in any way whatever, and that if you think for one second that they are, I shall be more insulted than I have been in about twenty years."

"I don't think they're connected."

"Good."

Reggie sits still.

---

"No," he says.

"Give me the reason."

"The record is incomplete. He was twenty-four and he was doing it in his own time and he did not know he was doing anything, and there are no dates on most of it and the plate sequence has a hole in it, and what you would end up with is a reconstruction. And I am not going to put a reconstruction of a dead boy into a paper in a field that is about to start looking for men of that kind, under his own name, where he cannot answer it and cannot be asked a single question about any of it, ever, by anybody."

He hears his own voice go flat and level and does not do anything about it.

"That is the reason and it is the whole of the reason and I am not going to be moved on it, and I would rather you did not ask me a second time, because I have found that I am able to refuse you and I would prefer not to find out how many times."

Spilsbury looks at him for a while in the grey.

"Right," he says, and reaches for his glass.

Then, entirely without emphasis, in the tone of a man remarking on the tide:

"In eleven years you've never once refused me anything without handing me something I could argue with."

He finishes the rum and stands up.

"That wasn't a reason, Tar. That was a man standing in front of something. Not a criticism — an observation, and I'd have been failing you not to make it. Good night, Tar. Ask Pobil whether he'll let me have the calf twice more before the eighteenth."

And he goes up the path.

---

Reggie takes the launch across at half past eight and does not turn the light on when he comes into the house, and Pascual, at the table with the feed book, says, "*Ay*. What."

He tells him the whole of it. It takes about forty minutes and he does not leave out one word, including the corridor, which he has told him before and has never once told him properly.

"And I want you to have the reason I gave him," he says, "and then the reason I actually had, because they aren't the same and that's the whole of why he noticed.

"What I gave him was the name. A reconstruction of a dead boy, published, in a field about to go looking for men of that kind, where he can't answer it and can't be asked anything ever. All true. I'd say it again tomorrow." He has both hands round the glass. "What I had was the other one, and I've had it since the ninth of October, and I sat on that verandah and did not say one word of it.

"The notebook is the only account on this earth of the loud end. Four hundred pages of a man beside an animal five months into a decline, on his own, writing it down every night. Nobody else has ever had that — you'd need a solitary mermaid and somebody sensitive in the same place at the same time and the wit to keep a record — and after our paper nobody ever will, because everybody keeps them in pairs now.

"So the field will spend twenty years measuring this on contented animals and getting a calf who fetches a hose fitting, and will conclude it's a small effect and rather charming. And the one document in the world that says it is not a small effect — and says precisely what you would have to do to an animal to make it enormous — is in Hester Bevan's keeping, and I have just declined to hand it to the best man alive at working out what to do next."

Pascual sits very still.

"*Ay, Dios mío.*"

"Yes."

"And you could not tell him the reason."

"I could not tell him the reason," Reggie says, "because the reason *is* the information. If I say *no, because that notebook would tell you to isolate an animal*, then I have told him to isolate an animal, and he'd have it inside four seconds and he would never need to see a page of it." He puts the glass down. "So he got a true reason that wasn't the one, and he heard the difference in about a second and a half, and he told me so, and there was nothing whatever I could do about it."

---

---

Then Reggie says the other part, which is the standing body and the four years and London or Rome, and expects — he does not know what he expects. Alarm, possibly. Argument, certainly. He is braced for argument.

What he gets is that Pascual puts the feed book down and says, quietly:

"I know."

"You know what?"

"I don't know about the body. I know he wants you." Pascual is looking at the table. "He said it to me at the rail on the twenty-first of June and I have had it since and I didn't tell you, and I'm going to tell you now and you aren't going to like any of it."

He says it straight through, all four sentences, more or less word for word, and then the rest: that there was nothing in it; that every word of it was true; that he stood at that rail for twenty minutes working out how there was nothing in it and there was nothing in it; and that he could not think of any way to carry it up the path that did not make him a man who had been counting.

"You said in December it was an event that couldn't occur," he says. "Twice. And you were astonished both times that I could think otherwise. So I decided that I was not going to make you prove it again in June because a colleague put a teacup on a rail." His jaw is set. "And I have had it in a drawer for five months, which is the exact thing I told you in 1954 I had finished doing, and I have known that the whole time."

---

Reggie sits down opposite him.

He discovers, with some interest, that what he feels is not what he would have predicted, and takes about ninety seconds to work out what it is, and it is fright.

Not about Spilsbury. About the five months.

"Right," he says. "Two things, and the first one's easy and I'll do it quickly. He's wrong. Not lying — wrong, which is different and rarer and he'd hate it.

"He thinks I'll want out of here in about four years, and his evidence is that I've never stayed anywhere longer, which is true. And his explanation is that I get bored. I don't get bored. I left that building in nine weeks because I was *frightened* — of myself, on one afternoon, and I've told you which afternoon — and everything else I've walked out of, I walked out of because there was nothing in it to hold on to and no reason to stay past the interesting part.

"I've been here five and a half years. It's the longest I've ever been anywhere by eighteen months and it hasn't got easier once. He's extrapolating from a man who stopped existing in about 1953, and he'd take the correction if I gave it to him, and he would never in his life understand it."

"And the second thing."

"The second thing is that you had it for five months and I'd rather have had a bad night in June." Reggie puts both hands flat on the table. "I'm not angry. Look at me. I'm not angry and I'm not going to be, and I want you to hear the actual objection, because it isn't about trust.

"You decided to spare me, on the basis of what it'd cost me to be told. And you got that from what I said in December, and what I said in December was that I can't make the reassurance *arrive*." He is going carefully, because he has one shot at this. "I never said I couldn't stand being asked. Those are miles apart. You can put that question to me every fortnight for thirty years and I'll answer it every single time and it costs me nothing whatever — it's the one thing in the whole business I'm actually good at, because a question has a form and I've never once been beaten by a thing with a form.

"What costs me is you standing on a rail for twenty minutes on your own deciding you'd sound jealous. That's the expensive one." He stops for a second. "And I've just given you the whole of that corridor. Not the four sentences you got in 'fifty-four — the whole of it, including the half I've been assembling on my own for eleven years and have never said to anybody, including myself, most nights. You didn't ask for it and I wasn't braced and I said it anyway, and I'd like that entered as evidence about what I can stand."

Pascual sits with that.

"*Ay*," he says.

"Yes."

"So the rule is that I say it in June, and you look astonished, and we go to bed."

"That's the rule." Reggie turns his glass round. "And I will look astonished, and I'd like to be clear about what the astonishment actually is, because I've had a year to work it out and I think it's one thing and not several.

"It's the same thing it was in December and it's the only thing there is. I can't do anything by halves, and because I can't, it doesn't arrive that anybody could be in doubt about me. Not as a proposition — I know perfectly well you can't see inside my head. As a fact about the room. From in here it isn't a question that can be asked, so when you ask it I get it fresh, every time, like a result I hadn't predicted.

"It won't drill out. I've tried. But I'd rather look a fool twice a year for thirty years than have you carry one more thing about on your own to spare me."

---

Later, in the dark, Pascual says:

"He isn't wrong about all of it. You won't do this for thirty years."

"No."

"And when it stops, there will have to be somewhere."

"Yes."

"Then in 'fifty-eight, when he asks you again, you'll tell me first and we'll do it at this table with the feed book and a bottle, and we'll decide it *together*, and whatever it is, it'll be a thing with two people in it." Pascual shifts. "And if it's Rome, I've a great deal of Latin and no Italian and I shall be extremely bad-tempered for about a year."

Reggie lies quite still in the dark for rather a long moment.

"You'd come."

"*Ay, Dios mío,*" says Pascual, into the pillow. "There it is. Twice in one evening. Go to sleep."
