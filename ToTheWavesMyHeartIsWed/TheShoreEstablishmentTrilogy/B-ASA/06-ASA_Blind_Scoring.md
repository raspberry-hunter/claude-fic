# Blind Scoring

He says yes on the twenty-seventh of December at nine in the morning, standing up, before anybody has sat down or had any tea, because he has learned that the only way he can do a thing like this is to do it in the first four seconds of the day.

"Yes. With conditions. The conditions will take me ten days and I am not going to be hurried, and if that means you sail on the eighth without them, you sail on the eighth without them."

"Splendid," says Spilsbury. "I'll change my passage."

"You're not changing your passage on my account."

"I'm not changing it on your account, I'm changing it because a study designed in ten days by a man in a temper is worth about four times one designed in three by two men being polite." He is already pulling a chair round and sitting on it backwards, which is a thing men of fifty-one should not do. "Right. Tell me what kills it."

---

The ten days become sixteen, and they are, Pascual will admit under no circumstances whatever, sixteen of the most interesting days of his professional life.

Here is what he had rehearsed.

He had rehearsed a fight. He had eleven conditions written out on the back of a feed sheet in the small hours of Boxing Day, in order of how hard he expected to have to hold them, and he had gone over the hard ones in the launch on the twenty-seventh the way a man goes over an argument he is going to lose.

What happens instead is that Spilsbury accepts all eleven inside forty minutes without negotiating a single one of them, and then improves four.

"Your stopping rule's no good," he says on the second day.

"It's a very good stopping rule."

"It's an excellent stopping rule and it's no good, because you've written *the session shall be discontinued if the animal shows signs of distress* and you have not said who says so. Which means it's you. Which means at some point you're not on the rail, or you are on the rail and you hesitate for four seconds because you're aware that it's the eleventh session and Spilsbury has come four thousand miles." Spilsbury taps the sheet. "Put it in behavioural terms. Write out the signs — all of them, in a list, on a board at the rail. And then say that *any person present* may call it, and the session ends at once, and no reason is given and no reason is ever asked for, and that includes Nestor and it includes the boy who does the buckets."

Pascual sits with that for a moment.

"You've just given a technician a veto over your own study."

"I've given a technician a veto over four seconds of my own study, and in exchange I get a stopping rule that will not fail in the eleventh month when everybody's tired, which is the only month it matters." He shrugs. "You had the right rule and you'd made it depend on a person. Never make anything depend on a person. Persons get tired."

---

The intake rule is Pascual's and Spilsbury does not touch it.

Daily intake and residue on both animals throughout, weighed by hand, in the book, by a man and not a boy. A sustained fall — four consecutive days below the running fortnight's mean, by the margin set out in the schedule — and that animal comes out of the series for three weeks, automatically, with no discussion and no appeal, and it applies equally at Whitley and at Naples.

"That's Cholmondeley's," Spilsbury says, reading it.

"It is."

"You've made a dead boy the umpire of my study."

"I've made the intake column the umpire of your study," Pascual says.

Spilsbury looks at him for a second longer than is comfortable, and then writes *agreed* in the margin and initials it and goes on, and Pascual sits there with his heart going and cannot tell, then or afterwards, whether anything happened at all.

---

The fight, when it comes, is about Chumley, and Pascual loses it, and he loses it because he is wrong.

"The calf's out," he says. "He's three years old and he's in the middle of a growth I've got no comparison for, and I'm not putting sixteen strangers in a pen with him."

"Then you haven't got a study."

"I've got a study with one animal in it."

"You've got a study with one animal in it, and the whole question — the entire question, the thing I've waited two and a half years and come four thousand miles for — is whether the effect is in the man or in the animal, and you cannot answer that with one animal, ever, by any method, at any expense." Spilsbury is not raising his voice either. "One animal and sixteen men tells you which men Gamma likes. That's a fact about Gamma. It's a perfectly nice fact and it isn't worth anybody's life."

Pascual stands at the window of the office with his hands in his pockets for rather a long time.

"Full round on Gamma first," he says. "Every subject. Complete. Then the calf, and half-length sessions, and not one man in that pen who has not already been in the other one, and I stop it on the first thing I do not like and I will not tell you what the thing was."

"Done," says Spilsbury, and writes it down, and the entire matter has taken eleven minutes.

---

The subjects are the difficulty and the solution is the one Pascual likes least, which is that they are already coming.

Six men a quarter, four quarters a year, eight weeks each, standing at that rail every morning at seven because the William Martin Institute for Marine Studies undertook in June of 1955 to teach them. Vetted, housed, insured, present. Sixteen subjects in four intakes and not a penny of travel.

"It rides on the training centre," Pascual says, and hears his own voice go flat, and does not much like the sound of it.

"It rides on the training centre." Spilsbury does not look up. "You may take that up with the Director. I only build them."

Reggie, at the other end of the table, says nothing at all, which is not like him.

---

The rest of the design is eleven weeks of correspondence and four hundred pounds of Spilsbury's money and it comes out like this.

A subject enters at the marked point on the north side, in the water to the chest, and stays eleven minutes — Spilsbury wanted fifteen; Pascual said eight; they settled it in four minutes without either of them enjoying it — and does nothing in particular, and is not told what is being looked at. No touching in the first four sessions. Nobody in the water alone: Nestor or Pascual on the rail throughout, with the board of signs at their elbow.

And the scoring is done off film.

That is the piece Pascual would not have thought of in a hundred years and it is the piece that makes the whole thing work. A 16mm camera on a scaffold above the north wall, running for the whole eleven minutes, framed on the *water only*: the man is below the edge of the frame and out of it, and there is nothing in shot but a mermaid and eleven minutes of pen.

The reels go home with Spilsbury. They are scored in Massachusetts by two people who have never been to this island, on a schedule of eleven items — approach latency, closest approach, orientation, surfacing pattern, tail carriage, the rest — and who do not know, and cannot know, which of sixteen men is standing four feet outside the bottom of the picture.

John takes one look at the scoring schedule and says, "You'll want them numbered in one sequence from the first reel, not by subject and not by session," and Spilsbury says "Yes I shall, thank you," and it is done that way, and the sequence will run to eleven hundred and six.

---

The comparison group is where Nestor comes in and nobody makes anything of it at the time.

Spilsbury wants long-exposure subjects — people who have been about these animals for years — set against men who have been on the island eight weeks. It is obviously right. It costs nothing. There are four of them: Pobil, Clarke, the technician Nestor, and, after an argument Pascual does not witness and would have given a great deal to see, the Director.

"You're in it," Pascual says that evening, at home.

"I'm in it. Blinded like everybody else, eleven minutes like everybody else, and if I'm the one who gets something I shall be the most compromised man in the history of the subject and he'll have to run the whole thing again with somebody honest." Reggie is looking for his glasses, which are on his head. "He said that himself, incidentally. Before I did. That was the argument — I said I couldn't be in it because I'm the Director, and he said that's precisely why I had to be, because the first thing any reader asks is whether the man who wrote the paper kept himself out of the water."

"And you said?"

"I said several things I'm not going to repeat, and he waited through all of them, and then observed that if I refuse I've got a study with a hole in it exactly the shape of me." Reggie finds the glasses. "Forty minutes. He never raised his voice once, I lost every single exchange, and about halfway through I noticed I was enjoying it."

---

Spilsbury sails on the twenty-second of January and comes back on the fourth of May with a camera, eleven hundred feet of film, a scaffold in sections and a young woman called Miss Aldridge who does the loading and will not be scored and is not to be spoken to about mermaids, on which point she is extremely clear.

The first session is on the ninth of May at half past six in the morning.

It is Chettle's successor, a man called Warne, from the Whitsun intake, and he goes in at the marked point and stands in the water to the chest for eleven minutes and nothing whatever happens, and Gamma stays at the deep end with her back to him and eats a fish.

Warne comes out and towels his head and says, "Was that right?"

"That was perfect," says Pascual.

---

They run eleven weeks.

The thing nobody tells you about a well-designed study is how *boring* it is, and Pascual, who has spent nineteen years being called out at two in the morning, discovers a species of tedium he did not know existed. Six-thirty. The board. The reel. Eleven minutes. Out. Weigh the fish. Weigh the residue. Write it in the book. Do it again tomorrow.

And in the eleven weeks he finds himself, twice, standing at the rail at seven in the morning in a state that he can only describe as *delight*, and both times he catches it happening and both times he does nothing about it at all.

Because it is a beautiful piece of work. That is the thing. It is the most carefully built thing he has ever stood inside, and every single piece of the care in it is either his or was put there by a man he despises specifically in order to protect his animals, and at some point in the sixth week Pascual is standing on the concrete with the board at his elbow and understands, all at once and with his whole chest, exactly what happened to Reggie between the fourteenth of November and the eleventh of December.

He does not say this to Reggie.

He does not say it because he can see how it would land — as absolution, as *there, you see, I have done it too, we are quits* — and it is not that. It is worse than that. It is that he can no longer be certain, about anything he decides in the next eight months, that he is deciding it as the brake.

---

Of the sixteen, fourteen get nothing.

That is exactly what Spilsbury said in December on the verandah with the Colonel's rum, and Pascual has been quietly waiting for him to be wrong, and he is not wrong, and it is somehow the most unnerving thing that happens all summer.

Fourteen men stand in the water for eleven minutes, three times each, and a mermaid ignores them. Not fearfully. Not with interest. The way one ignores furniture.

Two get something.

One is Bassani, who is not a subject at all — he comes in June to see the plates and is put in the water on the fourth day because he is here and it is free data — and who gets an approach to within about eight feet, twice, which is above every one of the fourteen and outside the band by a distance that survives being scored by two people in Massachusetts who have never heard of him.

And the other is Nestor.

---

Pascual is the only person on the island who is not blind, and it is his own arrangement, and he did not think about what it would cost him.

He is on the rail for every session. He knows who is in the water. He has been watching Gamma for four years and can read her at ninety feet.

So he sees it on the eleventh of June at twenty to seven, live, before any reel goes anywhere, and he has to stand there for the remaining nine minutes of it with the board at his elbow and his face doing nothing.

She comes up the pen at forty seconds.

She does not approach, exactly. What she does is come to about fifteen feet and turn side-on and stay there — the whole eleven minutes, drifting, with her head up and one arm out along the water — which is what she does at the sick-bay end when Chumley is being handled and she has decided to supervise. Pascual has seen her do it perhaps four hundred times and has never once seen her do it for a man in the water.

Nestor comes out at eleven minutes and takes the towel and says something in Spanish about the cold that Pascual does not entirely catch, and goes off to weigh the fish, because he is the technician and the fish need weighing.

He has been on this island since 1953. He has fed those animals every day for four years. He has never in his life been a subject of anything.

---

Pascual takes it apart for a week before he says a word to anybody, because he has learned what a single case is worth.

Familiarity is the obvious answer and it does not work, and this is the part that keeps him awake.

Nestor has fed her for four years. So has Pascual. Pascual has ten times the contact hours — hands on the animal, in the water with her, four hundred nights — and Pascual gets, in his own three sessions, scored by two strangers who did not know it was him, precisely what Warne got.

Nothing.

Not a thing. She surfaces, she eats, she goes to the deep end. He has nursed that animal off the bottom of a pen and she does not care whether he is standing in the water or not.

John gets nothing. Reggie gets nothing, twice, and is unbearable about it for a day and a half in a manner Pascual eventually recognises as relief.

So it is not exposure and it is not affection and it is not the animal, because Chumley has not begun yet and Bassani got his eight feet in his first week on the island having never been in a pen in his life.

It is something about the man. Two men out of eighteen, and one of them has been within four hundred yards of that pen every working day for four years and nobody, including Pascual, has ever thought to ask him a single question about it.

---

He tells Reggie on the nineteenth of June, at the kitchen table, and Reggie sits very still for a long time and then says: "Bassani's the safe one. Nestor is not."

"No."

"Bassani's a station director. If the field decides it's *some men*, they'll look at Bassani and they'll say: Italian, quick, likes animals, good hands, and they'll go home and try it on themselves." Reggie has taken his glasses off. "And then somebody will look at the other one, and the other one's a local man on eleven pounds a month who was never asked, and that's a different sort of question, and it's going to occur to somebody in about four minutes that there was a third."

Neither of them says the third one's name.

"Nestor gets told," Pascual says. "Everything. What it means, what happens next, and that he can walk out of the whole thing tomorrow and nothing is written down."

"Yes."

"And I want him paid for it. Not a bonus. On the staff, on the establishment, with his name on the paper if there's one."

"Write it and I'll sign it," Reggie says.

---

The other thing happens on the twenty-first of June at the rail, at about six in the evening, and it takes eleven seconds, and Pascual will still be turning it over in the November after next.

Spilsbury is leaning on the top bar with a cup of tea, watching Chumley do the thing he does with the ladder, and he is in a good temper, because the reels have come back clean and the scorers have not fallen out and he has got the finest eight weeks of data of his life.

"He was twenty-seven when I met him, you know," he says.

"Who?"

"Tar. Nineteen forty-six." Spilsbury smiles at the water, and it is a perfectly ordinary smile, and there is nothing whatever in it. "Rooms next to each other for four years and a door between that didn't shut. He'd come through it at one in the morning with a sheet of paper and stand there until I looked up." A sip of tea. "You'd have liked him. Frightful manners. Best mind I've ever been near, and I've been near most of them."

"I don't doubt it."

"Mm." Spilsbury puts the cup down on the rail. "He can't do this for thirty years, of course. Nobody could. Six men a quarter and a form to fill in — he'll be extraordinary at it for another four years and then it'll start to eat him and he won't notice for about eighteen months." He says it kindly. He is not needling; Pascual would stake a great deal on that. "You've been the making of him, Pobil, and I don't say that sort of thing either. I've watched him for six months and he's the best he has ever been, and it isn't the animals and it isn't the ministry."

"Thank you," Pascual says.

"Not at all." Spilsbury picks the cup up again. "I'd only say — and it's not my business and I shan't say it twice — that when it does start eating him, he'll need somewhere to go, and he won't ask, because he's never asked anybody for anything in his life." He is watching the calf. "Somebody had better be ready to have it already arranged."

And he goes up the path to supper.

---

There is nothing in it.

Pascual stands at the rail for about twenty minutes working out how there is nothing in it, and there is nothing in it, and that is precisely the trouble.

Every sentence is true. Reggie will not last thirty years at this and Pascual has thought so himself since March. Reggie does not ask. Somebody had better have it arranged. A colleague of ten years' standing, in a good temper, at the end of a good season, saying an obvious and generous thing to the man who lives with him — and there is not one word in it that Pascual could carry up the path and put in front of Reggie without sounding like a fool, or worse, like a jealous man.

*He'll need somewhere to go.*

And Alec Spilsbury has a laboratory, and a committee, and four thousand miles of ocean, and the whole of 1946 to 1950, and no earthly need to be liked.

---

He tells Reggie about Nestor that night, at length, twice, and they sit at the kitchen table until half past one and draft the letter putting a technician on the establishment.

He does not tell him the other thing.

He is entirely clear with himself about why, which is not the same as being comfortable: that there is nothing to tell; that saying it out loud would put a shape into the room that is not in the room yet; and that the man across the table has told him, twice this year in plain words, that it is an event which cannot occur — and has looked, both times, like a man being handed a result he did not predict.

That is the thing about him, and Pascual has had three years to learn it. The man cannot do anything by halves, and because he cannot, it does not occur to him that anybody could doubt him. He is not being modest and he is not being careless. From in there it is not a question that can be asked.

*I shall have to be told twice a year until one of us dies.*

Well. He has been told. And he believes it, and he is not going to make a man who cannot say *te quiero* prove it again in June because a colleague put a teacup down on a rail.

He goes to bed and lies in the dark with a hand on somebody's back and does not sleep for some time, and finds that he is doing the thing he swore in 1954 he had finished with, which is holding something and putting it in a drawer.
