# Nothing Asked

The trouble starts on the ninth of April and Pascual, who ought to know better by now, finds it funny for the first fortnight.

"Have you written it?"

"Not yet."

"It's a letter."

"I'm aware of what it is."

"It's one letter to one man," Pascual says, "and you write eleven letters a day, and last Thursday you wrote four pages to a person in Bergen you've never met, telling him he was wrong about salinity, and you did it between the soup and the fish."

"That was easy."

"Why was it easy?"

"Because I wasn't asking him for anything," Reggie says, and takes his plate through to the kitchen, and that is the end of the conversation on the ninth of April.

---

By the second week of May there are drafts.

Pascual finds them because they are not hidden: a folder in the second drawer of the desk in the front room, and on the front of it, in that small ruled hand, *SPILSBURY — DRAFTS*, which is the most Reggie Tar object in the house and possibly in the world.

He does not open it for four days. On the fifth day he opens it.

There are nine.

He reads all nine, standing up, in the middle of the afternoon with the shutters half over and the harbour going about its business below the road, and by about the fourth one he has stopped finding any of this funny at all.

They are magnificent. Every one of them is fluent and easy and exactly the right length. There is a joke in the second paragraph of six of them, and it is a good joke in five. There are two sentences of genuine warmth in the fourth, placed where a man's eye goes, and a paragraph in the seventh about the crossing in 1954 that is so charming Pascual can hear the man's voice coming off the page, the one from the jetty, the one that fitted perfectly in eleven seconds.

Not one of them asks for anything.

He goes through them again to be certain. Draft three describes the Pickersgill return and stops. Draft five sets out the whole of the problem in four hundred words, better than Pascual could do it in a fortnight, and closes with *I thought it might interest you.* Draft eight goes as far as *it has occurred to me that you might have a view.*

Nine letters. Not one request. Not one sentence in which Reggie Tar says: *I want you to do something and I cannot do it without you.*

Pascual squares them up and puts them back in the folder and puts the folder back in the second drawer, and goes and sits on the verandah that faces the wrong way for the evening, and does not know what to do.

---

He does the wrong thing that night, which is to say nothing, and lies awake about it.

He does the right thing on the Sunday, which is to be direct, because there has never been anything else available to him.

"I have read the drafts."

Reggie, at the sink, does not turn round.

"All nine?"

"All nine."

"Then you know."

"I know they're the best letters I've ever read in my life," Pascual says, "and that a man could get anything he wanted with the fourth one, and that not one of them asks him to come."

"No."

"Reggie."

"I know exactly what's wrong with them." He puts the pan down. "I've known since about draft three. Each one's a very good piece of work at a slightly different angle to the thing I actually want, and I don't know how to write the thing I actually want, and I've now spent five weeks producing furniture instead. It isn't that I haven't tried. I sat down on Tuesday and wrote *I need you to come here* at the top of a clean sheet, and I looked at it for about four minutes, and then I found I'd put the kettle on and was reading the Naples return."

He is holding the edge of the sink with both hands.

"I told you on a cot in June of 'fifty-four that there was no form for it," he says. "I thought that was self-pity. I've been finding out it's a description."

---

The low chair comes over from the sick bay on the twenty-second of May, in the launch, on its side, roped, with Nestor holding it and looking amused.

It is the ugly one Pascual has kept for fourteen years for people who have fainted. It goes in the corner of the front room by the shutters, where the light is bad, and it is the single worst piece of furniture in the house.

"There's a perfectly good chair in this room already," Reggie says.

"There is."

"That one's repulsive."

"It is," Pascual agrees, and puts a cushion on it, which does not help. "It's also the chair, and we're four miles from the sick bay now, and I'm not having an arrangement that only works on an island I don't sleep on any more."

Reggie looks at it for a while.

"Right," he says.

---

The other thing that happens in the spring is John.

The second intake comes in February and the third in May, and by the end of the third week of the third intake Pascual understands that he is no longer the most feared man in the lecture hut.

John does not raise his voice. What John does is stand behind a professional man of forty and look at what he is drawing and say, "You've gone and left the tail off again," in a tone of infinite patience, and the man goes red to the ears and starts over.

He has produced a card. It is printed, at his own expense, in Bridgetown, and it is the size of a postcard and it says:

> **1. The whole animal, every time.**
> **2. Date it, on the sheet, in ink, that day.**
> **3. Number it. One sequence. From one. No gaps. For ever.**
> *J. R. Clarke, William Martin Institute*

Every man who leaves the Martin leaves with one. Bassani has his pinned over the bench at Naples. Chettle has had his framed, which John has heard about and pretends to find embarrassing and mentions roughly twice a week.

And in June the ministry writes — not to the Director; to *Mr J. R. Clarke, Illustrator* — to say that the visual-record convention in use at the Martin is to be adopted as standard at all establishments in receipt of instruction, and would Mr Clarke be so good as to prepare a note of not more than four pages setting it out.

John brings the letter down to Gamma's pen and reads it aloud to Pascual twice and then goes away and reads it to Nestor.

"He's going to be insufferable for a month," Pascual says at supper.

"He's going to be insufferable for a year," says Reggie, "and he has earned it, and I intend to sit here and take it."

---

Reggie tells John on the twenty-sixth of June, in the office, with the door shut.

Pascual is not there. He is at the pens and he has been told what is going to happen and he has said that he thinks it is right and he has meant it, and he spends the whole forty minutes doing a job that does not need doing and knowing that he is doing it.

John comes to the sick bay at nine in the evening.

He has done this precisely once before, in May of 1954, at nine in the evening, with his hands in his pockets and his chin up, and Pascual is aware of the repetition from the moment the doorway darkens and is quite sure that John is not.

"Sit down," Pascual says.

"No, thanks."

So that is different.

"He's writing to Spilsbury," John says.

"Yes."

"To ask him to come here and take a hand in the cautionary paper, and to put it in front of the people who print things, because Dr Tar can't get eight pages out of anybody and Spilsbury can."

"That is the whole of it."

"And he sat me down and told me himself, before it went, which he didn't have to do, because he could've got the address off any masthead in the world and I'd never have known till the man was standing on the jetty." John is looking at the shelf above the bench and not at Pascual. "He told me that in May of 'fifty-four he sat on Charles's bed and told me my Beta theory would finish my career, and that the whole of that conversation was him managing me, and that he's known it for two years and has never said so."

Pascual puts the cloth down.

"Did he say anything else?"

"He said he was sorry."

The generator does what it does.

"He's never in his life said that to anybody," Pascual says.

"I know. I could tell. It came out like a man reading a foreign word." John rubs the back of his neck. "It was awful, actually. I'd rather he'd been beastly."

---

Then he says the thing he has come to say, and he says it standing up in the doorway with his hands in his pockets, and Pascual finds that at some point in the last two years he has stopped being able to predict this boy at all.

"I'm going to answer my letter."

"John —"

"The Woods Hole one. June 'fifty-four. It's in my footlocker and it's two years and one month old and I've never replied to it." John's chin is still up. "*Introductions offered, nothing asked.* That's what it says. Nothing asked. I've read it about four hundred times and it's never once asked me for a single thing, and that's the whole reason I couldn't answer it, because you can't refuse a man who hasn't asked you for anything."

"You don't have to do this."

"I know I don't have to. I'm telling you I'm going to."

"Reggie won't let you."

"Reggie has already not let me," John says, "for about twenty minutes this afternoon, at some volume, and he was extremely clear, and I heard all of it, and I'm doing it anyway." He takes his hands out of his pockets. "Because think about it for one second, Pascual. Reggie Tar writes to Alec Spilsbury out of a clear sky after two years and asks him for help. What does that look like from Woods Hole?"

Pascual, who has been thinking about very little else since April, does not answer.

"It looks like a man in trouble," John says. "It's a letter from somebody who needs something, and Spilsbury'll read it in about four seconds and he'll know exactly how badly, and Reggie's spent two years teaching me what that costs. Whereas a letter from Clarke, the illustrator, two years late, apologising for the delay and saying he has been rather busy and by the way the ministry has adopted his numbering and by the way there is an interesting difficulty about pairing composition that Dr Tar has been trying to publish —"

"*Ay,*" says Pascual.

"— is an entirely ordinary letter from a young man with a career, and it makes Reggie's letter the second one, and a second letter is a different animal altogether." John shrugs, and goes slightly pink, and the shrug and the pink are the only parts of him that have not changed. "So mine goes first. On the same boat. And he can be as angry as he likes."

---

Pascual sits with that for a moment.

"You're being managed again," he says. "You understand that. Not by him. By the shape of the thing."

"Yes."

"He'll write to you afterwards. Not to Reggie. To you, because you're the one who answered, and it'll be perfectly friendly and there'll be nothing in it he wants, and that's how it's done."

"I know," John says. "He did it to me in 1954 and I've had two years to look at it." He picks at the paint on the door frame. "Here's the part I can't get anybody to understand. I've thought about that letter every week for two years, and about half the time what I feel about it is not fear. Half the time I take it out and read it and think: somebody who is very good indeed looked at my plates and said they were the finest he had seen in fifteen years, and meant it, because he had no reason to lie and he wasn't asking me for anything."

"He meant it," Pascual says. "That's why it works."

"Yes. Well." John straightens up. "I'm twenty-six and I've taught eighteen men and the ministry wants my rules on a card, and I'd like to find out what happens when I answer him with something in my hand instead of nothing. That's not bravery, before you say it's bravery. It's just that I'd rather be the one holding the pen."

He goes.

Pascual stands in the sick bay for some time and then goes out and walks up the path to the office, where the lamp is still burning at half past nine, and says, from the doorway:

"He's going to do it and he's right and you're going to let him."

"I know," Reggie says, without looking up. "I worked it out about four minutes after he left."

---

The letter is written on the third of July and it takes him two hours and it is one page.

Pascual is made to read it at the kitchen table with the man standing over him, which is not a comfortable arrangement for either of them.

> Dear Spilsbury,
>
> In May 1954 you told me on a jetty that you would think about all of this for ten years and come back. Two of the ten have gone. I am writing to ask you to come back now, at my request, for a reason of mine, and I am aware of what I am handing you by doing it.
>
> The position is as follows. Since the paper there are five establishments holding animals under instruction from this Institute and there will be more than twenty by 1960, and the eight-week course reaches only the men who come. In March an establishment in the north of England lost an adult female. The record was faultless. The decline is in the intake column in the man's own hand for four and a half weeks and he did not know that it was a decline, because the thing that would have told him is a practice and not a fact, and there is nowhere in the literature that a practice can be put.
>
> The consequence of that death is that a man in Port of Spain has an order for one more animal, and about one in three survives the passage. I attach the figure and its derivation. It is mine and it has not been published and I would rather it were not, yet.
>
> What is needed is a cautionary paper in a journal that everybody reads: composition of pairs, intake practice, and the mortality of the trade. I cannot get it printed. I have tried twice and both refusals were correct — there is no result in it — and I have no standing with the people who decide, and I have spent six years arranging my life so that I should not have to ask any of them for anything.
>
> You have the standing. You sit on the committees. You do not need to be liked and never have, and you are the only man alive who could put this in front of the field and not be argued out of it.
>
> So: I am asking you to come to the Martin and do it with me. I have nothing to offer you in exchange that you cannot take anyway, and we both know it, and I am not going to insult either of us by pretending otherwise.
>
> Yours,
> R. Tar

Pascual reads it twice.

Then he puts it down flat on the table and looks at it for a while longer.

"There's no joke in it," he says.

"No."

"In nine drafts there was a joke in six."

"I took it out." Reggie's hands are behind his back. "It's the only one I've written that I'd be able to look at in ten years. Say the rest."

"The rest is that it's the finest thing you've ever written and it's cost you a great deal, and that I'm not going to say either of those things at length because you'd leave the room." Pascual pushes his chair back and stands up. "Go and sit in the chair."

"I haven't put anything on."

"You've taken something off, which is worse, and you've been doing it since April, and I've watched you do it, and the arrangement doesn't specify direction." He points. "One hour. Be dull. Be rude to me. Start now."

Reggie goes and sits in the repulsive chair by the shutters and is dull and rude to him for fifty minutes about the boat, and about a man in Bergen, and about the price of paraffin, and about the fact that Pascual's arithmetic on the feed budget has been wrong in the same direction for eleven months.

At the end of it Pascual gets up and puts his hand flat on the side of the man's face, and Reggie shuts his eyes and puts his own hand up to answer it, and neither of them says anything at all.

---

Both letters go on the ninth of July.

Then there is the summer, and the summer is very long.

Chumley is five feet nine inches and has begun, this year, to be difficult in a manner Pascual finds professionally thrilling and personally exhausting: he has worked out that the ladder can be hit, that the gate makes a noise, and that Nestor will always come. Gamma is twenty-six months paired. She cuffs him about four times a day and sleeps against him every night.

The August returns come in. Naples is well. Whitley is immaculate and Chettle has sent, unrequested, a further page on play behaviour and a very stiff paragraph suggesting the form ought to have a column for *observations not reducible to figures*, which Hester reads twice and adds to the schedule in November.

And Pickersgill's second animal arrived in June, and the pairing has taken, and both are gaining, and in the margin against the fourth week of July, in a small neat hand, is written: *M. took a fish and put it down. Watched. Shade to that end by afternoon. Intake back Thursday.*

Pascual reads that margin note about six times and then takes it up to the office and puts it in front of Reggie without a word, and stands there while he reads it, and neither of them makes any remark, and it is one of the better afternoons of the year.

---

The reply comes on the twentieth of September, in the second sack, and it is addressed in a fast clear hand, and Reggie brings it out to the pens rather than open it in the office.

They read it standing at the rail.

> Dear Tar,
>
> Yes. I will come, and I can be with you by the middle of November if you can find me somewhere to sleep that is not the guest hut, which I remember with no affection at all.
>
> Do not thank me and do not be grateful, because I am not doing it for you and you would find out inside a week and be disappointed in yourself for having believed otherwise.
>
> Here is why, and you may check every word of it against what you know of me.
>
> Your paper made a field. Before it there were eleven of us and it was mine; now there are men in it with money and no judgement whatever, and they are going to hold two hundred animals badly and kill most of them and produce nothing at all, and in fifteen years the whole subject will be a discredited backwater that nobody will fund, and I shall be sixty-one and it will have been taken away from me by people who cannot read an intake column. I want forty years of this. They want a capability by Christmas. That is the entire quarrel and it has nothing to do with the animals, and I am not going to pretend that it does, because you would not believe me and I would enjoy it less.
>
> Two remarks on your letter, since you were honest with me and I would rather return it.
>
> First. You told me nothing I did not have. I have had the *Bulletin* note since the spring — Tar, Pobil and Bassani, four hundred words, in a fisheries journal, which was a piece of desperation and read like one — and I have had the composition problem since Bergen wrote to me in February asking whether it worked with two adults, and I told him no, and I have no idea whether he believed me either. I know Whitley's return will be immaculate. I did not know which of the others had lost one and I would have said the Cheshire man or the quiet one. So: you have not given anything away by writing to me, and you have been sitting on that fear since April for nothing, and I should like you to stop.
>
> Second. Your one-in-three figure is the most valuable thing in the envelope and you have buried it in the fourth paragraph and asked me not to publish it. We shall discuss that.
>
> As to terms, since you have raised the subject in your last paragraph and done it in a way I did not much care for. I want the run of the record — everything, including the parts I was not shown in 1954, and you may take as long as you like deciding how much that means. I want my name on the paper, third. And I want one line of work of my own choosing while I am there, which I shall put to you when I arrive, and which I would rather not set out in a letter.
>
> Until November.
>
> Yours,
> A. Spilsbury
>
> P.S. My regards to Pobil, who I imagine was the one who said my name.

---

Neither of them says anything for a while.

Down in the pen Chumley hits the ladder twice, hard, and gets nothing, and hits it again.

"He isn't wrong about the *Bulletin* note," Pascual says at last. "It was desperation and it did read like one."

"No. He isn't wrong about any of it. That's the whole difficulty with him and it always has been." Reggie is holding the letter in both hands and has not moved. "Read the last paragraph again."

"I've read it four times."

"*One line of work of my own choosing, which I shall put to you when I arrive, and which I would rather not set out in a letter.*"

"Yes."

"He's put every single other thing in writing," Reggie says. "The reason. The quarrel. The terms. My figure. He's itemised what he wants off us in a document that could hang him, because he doesn't care and never has. And there's one item he won't write down."

Pascual looks at the water.

He is thinking about a man in a linen jacket in an unlit pen at half past five in the morning with one arm hooked through the ladder and his hand on a frightened animal for a minute, getting nothing, and being entirely unashamed, and saying: *one negative result on one man is not a refutation, it is an anecdote. But I had to have the anecdote. You do see.*

"I know what it is," he says.

"So do I," says Reggie.

Neither of them says it out loud, at the rail, in September, with eight weeks to go.

"And the postscript," Pascual says.

"Yes."

"He's never met me above six times." He folds his arms, because otherwise he'll do something with his hands. "He was on this island four days, two years ago, and I shouted at him in front of six people and lied to his face twice, and he's read one letter from you."

"*Ay, Dios mío.*"

"That's him," Reggie says. "That's what he's like, and I haven't been able to convey it in two years of trying, and you may now consider yourself conveyed to."

---

They walk back up the path and Reggie goes to the office, and Pascual takes the letter with him to the sick bay to read it a fifth time, which is how he finds the mistake.

*My regards to Pobil, who I imagine was the one who said my name.*

He did not say it.

He stood at that rail on the fourth of April with his forearms on the top bar and he had the name in his mouth for two days and the whole of the argument for it assembled and ready, and he did not say one syllable of it. He said *say the sixth*, and got a no. He said *say the name*, and waited. And then he stood there in the dark and made a man he loves put it into the air himself, out of his own mouth, about a person who nearly finished him twice.

Pascual sits down on the stool with the sheet in his hand.

Everything else is right. That is what takes a moment to get past — every other thing in that postscript is correct to the inch. That it came from Pascual and not from Reggie. That Reggie would never have arrived at it alone. That there is somebody standing next to the man now who can move him.

And then the last four words, and they are wrong, and they are wrong in a particular direction.

Because a man who says another man's name for him is doing him a kindness: taking the weight, saying the hard thing so the other one does not have to. That is what Spilsbury has assumed. It is a perfectly sensible assumption. It is what Pascual wanted to do, at that rail, more than he has wanted anything all year.

He did the other thing instead, and it cost him, and it cost Reggie more, and it was right, and there is no possible way to get to it from outside.

*He has everything about us that can be found out,* Pascual thinks, folding the sheet along its crease. *He has read one letter and he has the whole of it. And he does not have that.*

---

He tells him at supper, because they are people who say things.

"He is wrong about one thing in that letter."

Reggie looks up. "Where?"

"The postscript. I did not say his name. You did." Pascual goes on eating. "I made you."

There is a pause of about four seconds.

"So you did," Reggie says. "Does it matter?"

"I do not know yet."

But he does.
