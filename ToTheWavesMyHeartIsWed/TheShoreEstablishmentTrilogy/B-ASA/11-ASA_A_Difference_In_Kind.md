# A Difference in Kind

The end room in the block is eleven feet by nine and has a bed, a chair, a table, and a window that looks at the back of the store.

He gets there at twenty past eleven on the Sunday night and sits on the bed in his coat for about forty minutes and then puts the light on and works until four, because the alternative is available and he is not going to take it.

---

Here is what he does not do, in four nights, and he would like it on the record.

He does not decide that Pascual was wrong. He goes through the whole of it twice on the Monday, in order, with a pencil, the way he would go through anybody's argument, and it holds at every point, and the only line he can fault is *it does not matter what I do* — which is not true, because it did matter, and Pascual has spent five years making it matter, and the man knew that when he said it and said it anyway because he was in a state.

He does not decide that he is being punished unfairly. He has been punished exactly fairly.

And he does not, at any point in four nights, go anywhere near the thing that is actually keeping him up, which is a single clause in the middle of a sentence, and which he can quote, and has been quoting to himself since about two o'clock on Monday morning:

*You never stopped running the experiment.*

---

The work goes on because the work does not stop for anything.

The Whitsun roster. The proof of the eight-week reprint. Two letters to Bergen and one to a man in Lisbon who wants to know about pen dimensions, which is still the wrong question after four years. Chumley's quarterly weights, which he does on the Tuesday and gets wrong, by a pound, in the second column, and does not notice.

Spilsbury does not ask.

That is the remarkable thing and Reggie will be grateful for it for the rest of his life, in a complicated way: four mornings at the same breakfast table, two men who have each known for a decade precisely how the other takes information, and Alec Spilsbury asks him nothing at all.

He does mention, on the Wednesday, entirely in passing, that the light in the end room appears to be on a great deal, and that if it is the endowment then Bevan's nine pages are the best thing to come out of that station in two years and worth a fortnight of anybody's sleep.

Reggie says, "It's the endowment."

"Mm," says Spilsbury, and passes him the marmalade, and that is the whole of it.

---

He goes home on the Thursday afternoon, and he has worked out four things to say on the launch, and he says none of them, because Pascual is at the table with the feed book and looks up, and Reggie sees his face.

He has known that face for five years and nine months. He has seen it frightened twice, furious about eleven times, and once — in a canvas hut in 1954 with the rain going — genuinely dangerous.

He has never seen it like this, and it takes him until about the following March to find the word, and the word is *ashamed*.

So he says, "The calf's up four pounds and I've got him down as three," which is true and is a lie of the largest available kind, and Pascual says, "Then correct it," and they eat.

He moves his things into the second bedroom that evening. It takes about eleven minutes and neither of them mentions it, and he is grateful, and he lies in a room with eleven boxes of fisheries records in it and the wardrobe, and does not sleep.

---

The paragraphs arrive on the Saturday night on the back of a feed sheet, and they are not signed with anything except an initial, and they are the best four paragraphs anybody has written about this subject in two years.

He reads them standing up in the kitchen at half past eleven with the man asleep eleven feet away through a wall.

He takes them across on the Sunday six o'clock.

---

"Reason two," he says, in the office, with the door shut. "You said it to me on the twenty-eighth as a reason for yourself. Print it."

And he lays it out — the design cannot be blinded; the man at the rail can see whether the pen holds one animal or two; his knowledge of the state of the animal is inseparable from his response to it; no separation study can distinguish the two effects and any result from one is uninterpretable. He does it from memory, in his own words, in four minutes, and it is the only piece of work he has ever done that he is going to be able to look at without qualification for the rest of his life, and not one word of it is his.

Spilsbury goes very still about halfway through and stays still until the end.

"Damn," he says. "Yes. Obviously." And then, already writing: "It's uninterpretable in *both* directions, incidentally — you can't blind me either, and I've scored my own reels. That goes in as well."

He writes for about ninety seconds.

Then he puts the pen down and says: "Whose is it?"

---

Reggie had known that was coming from the moment he stepped off the launch, and had four answers ready, and finds that only one of them is available.

"Pobil's."

"Of course it is." Spilsbury is looking at the page, not at him. "It's the second time he has done this to me and I want it on the record that I do not enjoy it."

"You enjoy it enormously."

"I enjoy it enormously, which is worse." He turns the sheet round with one finger. "He's a better scientist than either of us, you know. Not cleverer — he isn't, and he'd tell you so himself at some length. But he is the only man I have ever met in thirty years who reliably asks what a thing is *for*, and everybody else in this field including me asks what it would show. That's the whole difference and it is not a small one and it is entirely unteachable."

"I know."

"Yes, I rather thought you did." Spilsbury caps the pen. "Then I'd be obliged if you'd tell him so, because I shan't, and it would come better from you in any case."

---

And then he says the other thing, and he says it kindly, and he is not fishing, and that is the whole trouble with him.

"He hasn't been across the water in a year, has he."

Reggie does not answer.

"It isn't a criticism. He's got a practice on the main island and a man doesn't have to like everybody." Spilsbury is squaring the papers. "I'd only say — and I'm going on the eighth so it's now or not at all — that whatever he thinks I am, he might have a look at what I actually do, because I have been at this thirty years and the thing I have seen finish more good men than drink is a fellow who is right about somebody and decides that being right is the same as being finished with them."

He puts the papers in the folder.

"He was right about me in 1954," he says. "I got into that pen before it was light and I did it on purpose and I would do it again with better conditions. He shouted at me in front of six people and lied to my face twice and he was correct on every count, and I have thought well of him ever since and I imagine he'd be sorry to hear it."

---

Reggie stands in his own office with his hands behind his back and discovers that he has stopped being able to breathe properly, which has not happened to him since 1954.

Because he can hear it.

Not what the man is saying. Underneath it — the thing that has been underneath every single conversation he has had with Alec Spilsbury for twelve years and that he has spent twelve years calling by other names, and which Pascual Pobil named on Sunday night in his own kitchen in eleven words with the rain starting.

*You never stopped running the experiment.*

And here it is, at eleven in the morning on the seventh of December, in a room with a generator going: the man is being *generous to him*. Unprompted. At no cost to himself and no advantage. He has given him Pobil's superiority as a fact and asked him to pass on the compliment, and then, kindly, at no cost to himself, he has advised Reggie Tar to go and mend things with his own —

And there the sentence stops, because there is no word.

Not in this office and not in 1958 and not in any room Reggie has ever been in. *Colleague* is a lie. *Friend* is a worse one. There is one word that arrives and it arrives out of a fairy tale about a vow, from a book in a dead boy's crate that this same man read aloud in a hut four years ago, and it is not available to him, and he puts it away with the speed of long practice and does not look at it.

And he is standing there taking the rest of it in like water.

That is the reaction. That is the thing he has been going back into that corridor for since November of 1946. Not approval. Not even notice. A man of fifty-two with nothing whatever to gain, in a bad-tempered good mood on a Sunday morning, being *warm* at him for about ninety seconds.

He would have paid four sentences for it. He did pay four sentences for it.

"Tar."

"Yes."

"You've gone an extraordinary colour."

"It's the heat," says Reggie, who has lived at eleven degrees north for six years.

"Mm," says Spilsbury, and looks at him for perhaps two seconds — not startled, not curious, not, and this is the part, *surprised* — and then goes back to the folder and says something about the Bergen offprints.

---

He gets as far as the store before he has to stop and put his hand on the wall.

---

Spilsbury sails on the eighth.

On the ninth, in the kitchen, standing up, Pascual says: *I should not have said that and it was not true and I am sorry.*

And Reggie says: *I know, and thank you, and it was about two thirds true.*

Both of them mean it. Both of them are relieved for about four hours. And then it does not work, and this is the first thing in five years and nine months that has not been fixed by saying the true thing out loud, and neither of them has a second procedure and neither of them says so.

Reggie has the third of it in his hand and does not produce it.

He is going to be about nine weeks producing it, and he is aware, on the ninth of December, that he is not producing it, and he has a perfectly good reason which he examines twice and finds to be perfectly good and does not believe.

The reason is that it is the worst thing he has ever had to say to anybody, and that it takes away from Pascual Pobil the only thing Reggie Tar has ever been able to give him.

---

The other rooms are the size of it.

He had not thought it would be, and he is not a man who has ever set much store by the physical side of anything — he has a body, it is adequate, it does what he tells it, and for forty years the whole of his interest in the subject could have been written on a postcard.

That was before 1954.

It turns out that the arrangement he has been living in since May of that year involves being touched by another human being perhaps nine times between waking and sleeping, most of it in passing and none of it planned — a hand on the back of the neck at the sink, a shoulder in a doorway too narrow for two men, a foot against his under a table, and about eleven distinct varieties of the thing Pascual does to the side of his face when he has said something that costs him.

None of it is happening.

He notices on the second day — it is not subtle; it is the largest change in his physical circumstances since 1954 — and then he cannot stop noticing, and by the middle of December he has worked out that he has been going about for four and a half years in a condition which he had assumed was ordinary and which turns out to have been the most sustained physical comfort of his entire life, and that he had no idea, and that this is precisely the same defect as the other one, which is that he does not know what a thing is worth until the arithmetic is in front of him.

Twice in January it is not possible to go on.

He would rather not set down the mechanism and he sets it down anyway, because he is a man who writes things in columns. No light. No words, either of them, at any point. The minimum contact that will do. A duration he could put a figure on. And afterwards one of them getting up in the dark and the sound of the other door.

The word for it is *efficient*, and that is the word he cannot get past, because it is the word he uses for good work.

The second time, Reggie lies in the dark for about an hour afterwards and finds he has arrived somewhere he did not expect, which is that the four nights in the block were not the worst thing that has happened to them. This is. The block was an absence and a man can survive an absence. This is the only thing the two of them have ever done together that was not, at any point, in any degree, funny.

And they are going to do it again, and he knows they are, and he does it again.

---

Gamma goes off her feed on the nineteenth of January.

Intake down three pounds on the Monday, four on the Tuesday, six on the Wednesday, and a residue of eleven pounds in a bucket at four in the afternoon with Nestor Aguilar standing over it not saying anything.

Pascual is across on the six o'clock with his bag. Reggie has the day book and the whole of the intake series since 1954 out on the bench inside twenty minutes and a boy running to the store for the old scales.

And for about thirty-one hours the two of them are exactly what they were.

There is no awkwardness of any kind and there is no time for any. Pascual is in the water twice and on the rail the rest of it and does not sleep; Reggie does the arithmetic and gets a comparison out of eleven years of records that nobody else on earth could have got out of them; they row twice, properly, at volume, once about the bicarbonate and once about whether to weigh her, and Reggie loses the first and wins the second; John does forty plates and numbers them; Hester sends down a hamper at midnight with no message in it.

It is an impaction. It is not the thing either of them was afraid of. She passes it on the Wednesday night with a great deal of indignity and a certain amount of assistance, and on Thursday morning at six she takes four fish in a row off Aguilar's hand and cuffs the calf for being in the way, and Pascual sits down on the concrete with his back against the wall and laughs until he has to stop.

Reggie sits down next to him.

They lean against the wall of that pen at twenty past six in the morning with their shoulders touching for the first time in seven weeks and neither of them moves, and it is the best five minutes of the winter and both of them know it.

---

It lasts until about four in the afternoon.

That is when Reggie comes into the sick bay with the corrected intake table and finds Pascual writing up the notes, and says something perfectly ordinary about the bicarbonate, and watches the man's shoulders come up about a quarter of an inch before he answers.

And that is it. That is the whole of it.

They are not repaired. They have been in a crisis, which they are extraordinarily good at and always have been, and the crisis has now stopped and the thing underneath it is exactly where it was on the thirtieth of November, and the shoulders are the proof.

Reggie stands there with a table in his hand.

*So it does not come from the outside,* he thinks. *There is no event. Nothing is going to happen to us that will do this, and I have been waiting for one since the ninth of December and I did not know I was waiting, and I have just had the best one there is going to be and it lasted thirty-one hours.*

---

He starts the letter that night and it takes him until the second of February.

Not because it is long. It is four hundred words and he has had the whole of it since the seventh of December in a room with a generator going.

It is because every version he writes is furniture.

He has nine drafts by the end of January and he recognises the folder for exactly what it is on about the second of February, standing in the front room at eleven at night with the thing in his hand, and he says, out loud, to nobody, "Oh, for God's sake," and puts the folder in the stove.

Then he goes and knocks on a door in his own house, which he has not done in his life.

---

"It's twenty to twelve," says Pascual.

"I know what time it is."

"Then —"

"I've written you nine letters and burnt them, and I'm not going to do that again, and I'm going to say it standing here at twenty to twelve because if I sit down at that table I'll make it beautiful." He has his hands behind his back. "May I come in?"

There is a pause of about two seconds and the sound of a man sitting up.

"Yes."

---

"On the eleventh of December 1956," Reggie says, "in this front room, at about six in the morning, I told you that what I had about you wasn't a quantity. That there was no mechanism in it for changing, in the way there's no mechanism for the tide not coming in, and that you were frightened of an event that couldn't occur, and that the reason it never crossed my mind you might be frightened is that I can't do anything by halves and from in here the question isn't a question.

"I have said some version of that to you five times in five years. Twice in 'fifty-seven. Once at that table in November. And I meant it every time and I would have gone into a witness box about it.

"It's not true."

---

The room is very quiet.

"It is not a lie either, and I want that said before anything else, because you are going to spend the next hour deciding which it was and it was neither." His voice has gone flat and level and he lets it. "What I told you was that it is a *fact about the world*. A physical property. Something I am not responsible for, like the tide. That is the word I used and I have been sick about that word since the seventh of December.

"And what I found out on the seventh of December, standing in my own office at eleven in the morning, is that I have had a torch for Alec Spilsbury since November of 1946, and that I have been carrying it, unexamined, in a coat pocket, through twelve years and two countries and this house — and that when he was warm at me for ninety seconds about somebody else's paragraph, I stood there and took it in like a man who had not had a drink in a decade, and I had to go out and put my hand on the wall of the store.

"That is not a thing I feel. That is a thing I *do*. I have been doing it since I was twenty-seven. I did it on the twenty-eighth of November on that verandah and it cost four sentences that were not mine to spend. You named it on the thirtieth in eleven words and I have been walking round it for nine weeks.

"So the thing I have been handing you for five years is worth nothing. It was a description, it was not accurate, and you have been right to be frightened — and I have spent five years being astonished at you for it and telling you it could not occur, and it has been occurring the whole time in a pocket."

---

Pascual does not say anything at all.

Reggie makes himself keep his hands behind his back.

"I cannot give it back to you," he says. "That is the position and I am not going to soften it. I have not got a fact to give you any more and I am not going to manufacture one, and you are entitled to know that the reassurance you have had off me twice a year since 1954 was made of something that has turned out not to hold.

"So I've got the other thing instead, and it's smaller, and it's all there is.

"I'm going to choose you. Every time, for thirty years, and it will not always be the easy one, and on the days when it isn't I'm going to tell you it isn't, out loud, on the day, which is the part that's new and the part I'll be worst at. And you can ask me at any hour of any day and I'll answer it and it won't cost me anything, because a question has a form. And if I'm ever in a room with that man and he's warm at me again — and he will be, he's coming in May — I'm going to come home and tell you, on the day, and I'm going to hate it.

"That's what I've got. It's a promise and not a property, and I know perfectly well it's worth less than what I've been giving you."

---

"*Ay, Dios mío,*" says Pascual, from the dark. "It's worth *more*."

"It isn't —"

"Don't tell me what a thing's worth. I'm the one who does the arithmetic in this house and you've been doing it wrong for five years." He is upright and his voice has gone somewhere Reggie has not heard it go. "Reggie. A tide doesn't *choose*. I've had five years of a man telling me he's constitutionally unable to leave, and being astonished that I didn't find it comforting — and I could never say why it wasn't comforting because I couldn't get at it, and it's this. It's *exactly* this. You were offering me a fact about your machinery.

"I didn't want a fact about your machinery. *Ay.* I've wanted this since about the second week and I hadn't got the words for it until you said them standing in a doorway at twenty to twelve at night."

---

There is a silence of about four seconds.

"Come here," says Pascual.

"I'm not going to —"

"*Come here,* you impossible man."

---

He is not built for this and never has been. His arms go round badly and too hard, the way they always do, one across the shoulders and one at the small of the back, and he has his eyes shut and his chin on somebody's shoulder and there is a hand in the back of his hair.

"Now," says Pascual, muffled. "There's a thing I said I'd ask for when you weren't braced, and you're exactly as braced as a man can be, so this isn't it and I'm not asking."

"Ask."

"I'm not going to —"

"I'm not saying it because you asked," Reggie says, into his shoulder. "I'm going to say it because it took me three and a half years to work out that it isn't a measurement. I kept trying to find the true one and there isn't a true one, that's not what it's for." He gets his breath. "It's the same thing I've just said to you on the landing. It's the other way of saying it and it's shorter."

"Then say it."

"*Te quiero.*"

---

It is a perfectly ordinary Monday night in February and the generator is four miles away and cannot be heard and the rain has not come.

"That was appalling," says Pascual, after a while. "Your accent's appalling. It's the worst thing I've ever heard in my life."

"I've been practising it for three and a half years."

"*Ay, Dios mío.* Practising."

"In the boat, mostly."

"In the *boat* —"

"Where nobody could hear me," says Reggie, "including me."

And Pascual makes a noise into his collarbone that is not laughing, and stops, and puts a hand on the side of Reggie's face.

---

He does it slowly.

That is the whole of it and it goes through Reggie like something coming off a wire. There are eleven distinct varieties of that particular gesture and he catalogued all of them years ago and has never told anybody he did; this is not one of the eleven; this one takes about four seconds and the hand does not come away afterwards.

He has counted to none since the fourth of December. He gets hold of the door frame with his free hand because the alternative is to be a man who cannot stand up.

"I want to say —"

"No," says Pascual, and stops him, which he has done perhaps four hundred times in five years by the simplest available method and has not done once since the twenty-eighth of November, and which Reggie discovers, at twenty to twelve at night, he has been waiting for since the twenty-eighth of November.

---

They are slow about it and neither of them is steady.

Twice Reggie has to stop because his hands are not doing what he tells them, and both times Pascual says his name — just the name, twice over, the way a man says a thing he is confirming is still where he left it — and waits, and does not say anything else, and Reggie is extremely glad of that for the rest of his life.

"Don't be careful with me," he says. "I've had eight weeks of it."

"*Ay.*"

"I mean it. Not tonight."

Pascual makes a sound at that which is not laughing either, and is not careful with him.

And it is slow. That is the thing that is different and it is the thing they both need and neither of them says so. Slow all the way through, and both of them unsteady all the way through, and at some point Pascual's hand comes up into the back of his hair and stays there, and Reggie hears himself say *stay* and then say it again, which is not a word he has ever used in his life to anybody about anything.

"I am here," says Pascual, into his shoulder. "*Ay.* I am here. Where would I go."

---

It is not efficient.

That is the only thing Reggie is able to think for some while afterwards, lying in the dark in his own room with a man half across him and the rain not come, and it arrives as a kind of enormous relief and then, about four minutes later, as the other thing, because he has had to notice that it is efficient's opposite in order to notice it at all.

Neither of them is asleep. Neither of them says anything for about a quarter of an hour.

It is not what it was before. There is nothing funny in it anywhere and both of them know it and neither of them is going to say so tonight.

What it is, is the two of them finding out that they are still able to, which turns out to have been a live question in this house since the fourth of December and which neither of them had been willing to put into words.

Pascual says, eventually, "We are not finished."

"No."

"I do not mean tonight."

"I know what you mean," says Reggie, and gets an arm round him, and they lie there.

---

They do not fix it that night, and neither of them pretends they have.

Reggie goes back into the second bedroom on the twelfth of February because there are things Pascual has not said yet and both of them know it and neither of them is going to hurry it, and he moves out again on the second of March, and neither of them marks the date and both of them know it.

There are two things left in that kitchen from the thirtieth of November and they both know what they are. Reggie's is not being able to hear *I am finished* without something going over in his chest, and he expects to have that for about four years. Pascual's is worse and is his own and will be his to bring up when he is ready, and Reggie is not going to ask for it, because he has learned exactly one thing in fourteen weeks and it is that you cannot make a man produce an account before he has got it.

The arguing comes back in March, badly at first, and then all at once.

It comes back on the fourteenth, over the paraffin, at half past eight in the evening, and runs to twenty-five minutes, and Pascual wins it on a technicality about the account book, and Reggie goes out on to the verandah afterwards and stands in the dark for a minute with his hand flat on the rail, which is not a thing he does.
