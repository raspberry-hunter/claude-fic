# Acknowledgements

The offprints come on the twenty-first of March in a parcel tied with string, and there's forty of them, and Pascual takes one to the kitchen table and doesn't open it for about eleven minutes.

He hasn't asked. Since the sixth of December he hasn't asked one single question about those four paragraphs — not whether they went in, not whether they were cut to two, not whether Spilsbury took them and rewrote them past recognition, which is what men do with other men's paragraphs — and he hasn't asked because asking would have meant putting it in the room, and in December there was no room.

He opens it at page thirty-one.

It is all there. Every line of it, in order, better than he wrote it, with a fifth sentence added that is not his and is the best one.

And underneath the discussion, in the acknowledgements, in six-point type where nobody but a fool would look:

*The passage at pp. 31–32 on the non-blindability of separation designs is in substance the work of P. Pobil, who declined to be named for it and is named for it here.*

---

He reads that about nine times.

Then he puts the offprint down and stands at the shutters with his hands on the sill, and finds that what he wants to do is find Reggie and shout at him — and that this isn't a bad sign but an extremely good one, because it's the first time in four months he's wanted to shout at the man about anything that wasn't a figure in a book.

He does it that evening.

"*Declined to be named for it.*"

"Mm."

"I didn't decline. I wasn't asked. I wasn't in the room and I wasn't speaking to you, and you told him a thing about me that wasn't true."

"I told him you'd declined." Reggie is not looking up from the roster. "He wrote the rest himself and I didn't see it till the proof, and by then it was set. If you want it corrected in the errata I'll write to him tonight and he'll do it and he'll enjoy it."

"That isn't —"

"You wrote four paragraphs on the back of a feed sheet in a house where nobody was speaking to you, at midnight, for nothing, and left them on a table and went to bed," Reggie says. "I was not going to walk into that office and let a man think they were mine. That's the whole of it and I'd do it again, and if the alternative was a lie about you declining then I'd rather tell it than the other one."

Pascual stands in his own kitchen with an offprint in his hand.

"*Ay, Dios mío,*" he says.

"Yes."

"You are extremely annoying."

"I know," says Reggie, and turns a page of the roster, and there is something in the set of his shoulders that has not been there since November.

---

Spilsbury comes on the fourth of May for six weeks, with Miss Aldridge and a camera and eleven hundred feet of film, and the whole station knows a week in advance because Hester has put it in the minute book.

Pascual's across on the launch on the fourth of May at six in the morning and is standing at Chumley's pen when the boat comes in, which he's thought about carefully and decided on, and which he'll be glad of for years.

"Pobil."

"Dr Spilsbury."

"Page thirty-one." Spilsbury does not put his case down. "It's the best thing in the paper and it isn't close, and I have been telling people so at dinner tables since February in a manner that has become tedious to everybody including me."

"Thank you."

"Not at all." He picks the case up again. "I'd have got there in about four years. You got there in four days in the middle of what I take to have been a very bad fortnight, and you did it by asking what the paragraph was *for*, which is the thing I do not do and cannot teach myself to do, and I have thought about that a good deal on a boat."

And he goes up the path, and Pascual stands at the rail with his ears going.

---

Here's the thing about the six weeks that Pascual wouldn't have believed in December.

They're perfectly all right.

He's across three days a week because the contact series is running and he's got eleven conditions in force and a stop he can call without giving a reason, and he calls it once, on the twenty-second of May, over a subject from the Whitsun intake who's standing wrong, and nobody argues and nobody asks.

He and Spilsbury work together for six weeks and it's fine. It's more than fine on about four occasions. The man's exactly what he was — quick past bearing, entirely unembarrassed, extremely funny about a professor at Uppsala — and Pascual finds he still doesn't like him and no longer minds being in a room with him, and that these are two different facts and he's spent five years running them together.

He works out why on about the second of June, at the rail, in the middle of a perfectly ordinary argument about session length.

It's because Spilsbury has stopped being a question.

For four and a half years the man was a thing that might happen. Now he's happened — the whole of it, the worst version, the four sentences and the verandah and a kitchen on the thirtieth of November — and what's left is a colleague of fifty-three with a camera and a card index who's going home on the fifteenth of June.

---

And on the twenty-ninth of May, Reggie keeps the promise.

He comes across on the half past six in the evening and comes up the eleven steps and puts his hat down and says, from the doorway, in the voice of a man going into cold water:

"He was warm at me this afternoon and I've been in a state about it for four hours."

Pascual puts the pan down.

"Sit down," he says. "*Ay.* Sit down, don't stand there like that."

"I'd rather stand."

"Then stand. Go on."

"It was about the calf's approach latencies." Reggie has his hands behind his back and is looking somewhere over Pascual's left shoulder. "I'd built a thing out of the 'fifty-seven series that nobody's done before and it's good, and he looked at it for about ninety seconds and said *that's rather beautiful, Tar*, and went back to what he was doing."

"And?"

"And that was the whole of it and I've had four hours of it since and I couldn't tell you what I did between three and five o'clock." He is going red about the ears. "I want to be clear that nothing occurred. He said six words about a graph. I'm reporting six words about a graph and I'm aware of how this sounds and I said I'd do it on the day, so I'm doing it on the day, and it is the single most humiliating thing I have ever done in my life."

---

Pascual sits down at his own kitchen table and puts his hand over his mouth for a moment.

"Is that laughing," says Reggie, dangerously.

"No."

"It is."

"It isn't laughing." Pascual takes his hand away. "It's —"

And he can't get it out for a second, because it isn't laughing; it's the other thing; it's a man of forty standing in a doorway with his ears scarlet, reporting six words about a graph on the day, as promised, at a cost he's quite plainly been paying since three o'clock.

"Come here," he says.

"I'm not going to be —"

"*Come here.*"

---

Afterwards, at the table, with the pan burnt:

"You will have to do this again," Pascual says. "He is here until the fifteenth."

"I know."

"Possibly twice more."

"Probably four times. He's in a good temper and the reels are clean." Reggie rubs his face. "Does it help? I want to know whether it actually helps or whether I've just handed you something horrible twice a month for thirty years."

Pascual considers the question seriously, because it deserves it.

"It helps," he says. "Not the way you think. It isn't that I need to know — I'd have known by supper, you've got the worst face in the northern hemisphere. It's that you *came up the steps*." He turns his glass round. "For five years you've handed me a fact and told me it couldn't occur. Tonight you walked into this house and handed me the occurrence, in the doorway, at six in the evening, going red. That's a man doing something. The other was a man describing something."

"That's what you said in February."

"Yes. And in February you'd said it and I believed it, and this is the first time you've *done* it, and there's a difference." He drinks. "Also it was very funny and I intend to have it for years."

"I know you do."

"*Rather beautiful, Tar.*"

"Don't."

"It's going on a card," says Pascual, "in my own shoe box, under R," and Reggie gets up and takes the pan off the stove and does not answer, and the back of his neck stays red for about eleven minutes.

---

The other thing happens on the eighth of July, four weeks after Spilsbury sails, at the kitchen table, at about ten at night, and Pascual's been working up to it since the second of February.

He does it badly. He's decided in advance that he's going to do it badly, because doing it well would be a performance, and the whole content of the thing is that he's capable of performing.

"There is one more piece of the thirtieth of November," he says, "and it is mine, and I have had it since the sixth of December and I have not given it to you, and I am going to now, and it is the worst thing I have ever had to say to anybody and I want that understood before I start."

Reggie puts his glass down.

"Go on."

"On the ninth of December I told you I should not have said it and that it was not true and that I was sorry. All of that was true and I meant it. It was also not the thing." Pascual has both hands flat on the table. "I have let you think, for seven months, that I lost my temper. That something in me gave, after five years, because I was hurt, and that I said the biggest thing in the room because it came out.

"That is not what happened and I know exactly what happened because I watched myself do it.

"You had just handed me something accurate about myself. About the launch, and about not having been across the water in a year, and about never having said out loud that I would not be in a room with that man. Every word of it was true and you were not using it as a weapon and you have never once used a true thing as a weapon in your life.

"And I could not stand it. And I looked round that room for the largest object in it, and I found it, and I knew when I found it that it was the one thing I had that could not be replaced, and I picked it up and I hit you with it. Because it would hurt the most."

He makes himself say the rest.

"It took me about two seconds. It was not temper. It was a *choice*, and it was a good one, and it worked in four, and I would like you to know that about me because you have been living in a house with it since 1954 and you did not know it was there."

---

Reggie does not say anything for a while.

He doesn't go flat and he doesn't go level and he doesn't agree with it inside four seconds, which Pascual had been braced for and had dreaded more than anything.

What he does is take his glasses off and turn them over twice.

"Thank you," he says.

"Do not —"

"I'm not being gracious. Give me a minute, I'm going to say something and it's going to come out badly."

He turns the glasses over again.

"For five years," he says, "I've been the dangerous one in this house. That's not modesty and it isn't drama; it's the arithmetic. I'm the one with the labs and the appetite and the discussion section, and you're the one who stands in front of it, and the whole arrangement since May of 'fifty-four has been built on the understanding that the thing that needs watching is *me*.

"I've been perfectly all right with that. I built half of it. But there is a cost to it that I've never been able to describe and have been carrying for about four years, and the cost is that I have never once in five years been in a room with you as an equal risk."

Pascual sits very still.

"You could not hurt me," Reggie says. "That was the position. Everybody in this house has known for five years that I could ruin you in an afternoon — by publishing something, by wanting something, by saying yes to the wrong man on a verandah — and that you could not do anything at all to me except be right, which is not the same, and I have never in five years been able to work out how to say that without it sounding like a complaint about being loved.

"And on the thirtieth of November you reached across the table and did it. Deliberately, in two seconds, accurately, at the maximum available cost. And I have had seven months of not being able to hear *I am finished* without something going over in my chest, and I expect to have about four more years of it."

He puts the glasses on the table.

"I'd rather have it," he says. "I'd rather live in a house with a man who *could* than a house with a man who couldn't. It's the first time since 1954 that we've both been holding something."

---

And there it is, and Pascual sits in his own kitchen and takes delivery of the thing he is going to have for the rest of his life, which is not the one he came to the table with.

He had thought the worst of it was the telling. He had had seven months of dreading the telling and it has taken about four minutes and Reggie has taken it the way Reggie takes things, which is straight.

The worst of it is what the telling has just established. It is now a fact in this house, said out loud by both of them, on the record, that Pascual Pobil can pick that object up and that it works in four seconds.

Which means that for the next thirty years — every argument, every one, about the paraffin and the roster and the boat and the next four sentences some man prints in a discussion section — it is going to be in the room. He is going to be able to feel the weight of it, the way one feels a scalpel on a tray without looking at it, and he is going to have to decide, every single time, not to reach for it.

Not once. Every time.

Reggie's leftover is a wound and wounds close. Pascual's is a standing choice and it does not close at all, and it is going to be the quiet arithmetic of the rest of his life, and he would not now give it back even if there were anybody to give it to.

---

"That's monstrous," says Pascual.

"Yes."

"That's the most monstrous thing anybody has ever said to me, and it's also — " He has to stop. "*Ay, Dios mío.* It's also the first time in seven months I haven't felt like a man who's being forgiven."

"Good. I'm not forgiving you. I'm not in a position to and I don't think you want it." Reggie picks the glasses up again. "I'm telling you what it cost and what it bought, and both columns, because you're the one who taught me to do both and you've been extremely rude about my arithmetic for five years."

---

The rains come on the eleventh of July, four days late.

On the seventeenth Reggie moves the eleven boxes of fisheries records out of the second bedroom and into the office at the station, on the launch, in two trips, with Nestor, and nobody says anything about it, and the room stands empty for the rest of the year and isn't mentioned again.

---

Jean's letter comes in August and it is six pages and there is a photograph in it.

The photograph is of nothing. It's a stretch of mangrove about forty feet across, taken from a flat-bottomed boat in flat grey light, and there isn't an animal in it anywhere, and on the back in her hand it says: *Nothing here. Yet. 14.vii.59. J.L.*

The letter says that the four thousand dollars came through in April from an endowment in Bridgetown she had never heard of, and that she has been to the Colonial Secretary's office twice and got nowhere and has therefore stopped asking and spent it; that she has bought a second boat and hired Willis outright and has a boy; that she has surveyed one hundred and four miles of channel out of eleven hundred; that she has counts of nothing; and that in June a crab crew out of Everglade found a torn net eleven miles up a run that has no name on any chart, and that the tear was four feet across and did not look like a propeller, and that she has been out to it three times and seen nothing at all.

*Do not get excited*, Jean writes. *A torn net is a torn net and there are alligators. I am putting it in the letter because I am putting everything in the letter, because in 1970 when I am forty-five and have found nothing I would like there to be a complete record of how a person spends eleven years being wrong.*

*Tell Pascual the endowment came through and that I know precisely whose tenth page it was, because it is drafted in a style I have been reading since 1954 and there is a clause in it about not being required to show a result which no lawyer on this earth would have written unprompted.*

*The instrument does not call it a grant, by the way. It calls it a standing arrangement — renewable, for as long as the Martin holds an animal that came out of a boat.*

*And tell him that when I read it I sat down on the dock at Everglade for about a quarter of an hour.*

---

Pascual reads that bit twice and puts the letter down and doesn't say anything for a moment.

*Not a grant. A standing arrangement, renewable.*

He has heard that phrase once in his life, at seven in the morning on the fifteenth of June 1955, out of the mouth of a man who had been up since twenty past four with two letters squared on a desk in front of him and a paperweight on each, and who was about to spend four years finding out what it cost.

He had not thought, at the time, that it was a description of anything except money.

"She's put the photograph in on purpose," says Reggie, from the other chair.

"Of course she has."

"Plate one. Nothing here, yet, dated, numbered." He is turning it over in his fingers. "She's got a sequence going and there's one thing in it and it's a picture of some mud, and in 1970 there'll be eleven hundred of them and about four will have an animal in them, and every one of the others is going to be evidence that she looked."

"John taught her that."

"John taught everybody that," says Reggie, "and the ministry made it standard in four establishments, and nobody has ever once thought to tell him that it's the reason any of this is a record and not a story."

"You have told him."

"Once, in 1955, in a lecture hut, and he went scarlet and changed the subject." Reggie puts the photograph on the table. "I'll tell him again this week."

---

In September the first one comes.

It's a letter from a station in Portugal Pascual has never heard of, addressed to the Director, perfectly courteous, four paragraphs long, asking a question that takes three paragraphs to get to and is, when it arrives, whether the Institute considers that the observations at pp. 31–32 preclude *a study of graduated rather than complete separation*.

Graduated rather than complete. Which is to say: not taking the animal away, only moving it further off, and further, and further, and watching what a man at the rail feels.

Reggie reads it aloud at the kitchen table and neither of them says anything at all for about eleven seconds.

"He's read the paragraph," Pascual says.

"He's read it, he's understood it perfectly, and he's spent a fortnight looking for the hole in it. That's what those three paragraphs are." Reggie puts it down. "And there isn't one, and he knows there isn't one, or he wouldn't be asking me."

"So he's asking for permission."

"He's asking for a sentence he can put in his methods section." He takes his glasses off. "And if I give him one he'll do it in eighteen months, and if I refuse him he'll do it in three years with somebody else's sentence, and if I say nothing at all he'll take that as the third thing."

Pascual sits down opposite him.

"Then write to him tonight," he says, "and write the whole of it, and put every figure in it, and be as long and as dull and as accurate as you know how. And when you've written it, bring it here and read it to me out loud before it goes, and I'll be a great deal louder than I was in May of 1954, and you won't enjoy it."

Reggie looks at him for a second and a half.

"I'll get the paper," he says.

---

Gamma is seven years old this year, or eight, or nine; nobody knows and Pascual has four different estimates in four different notebooks and the honest one says *not less than eight*.

She's fifty-one months paired. She's got a calf of six who's bigger than she is and still brings her things. She's the oldest animal of her kind ever recorded anywhere, she's been the oldest for three years, and every additional week is a number nobody's ever had.

On the fourth of October, at twenty past six in the morning, a woman of sixty-three comes down the path in a hat and crosses eight feet of concrete and sits on the edge with her skirt arranged and puts her hand in the water, which she's done eleven times since April of last year and which nobody discusses.

Pascual and Aguilar are at the far end with the balance and their backs turned, as always.

And at the far end, weighing fish, Aguilar says, without looking up: "She's slower this year."

"Who is?"

"The old one."

Pascual writes a figure in the book and doesn't answer for a moment.

"Yes," he says. "I know."
