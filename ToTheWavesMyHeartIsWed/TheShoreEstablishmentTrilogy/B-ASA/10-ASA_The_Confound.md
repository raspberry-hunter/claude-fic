# The Confound

Reggie comes across on the half past nine launch on the thirtieth of November and tells him the whole of it standing up in the kitchen with his hat still in his hand, which is how Pascual knows before a single fact's arrived that something has gone wrong.

It takes about eleven minutes.

The four sentences. The 1954 table on page nineteen. The card in the box under M and what is written on it. Bergen, and Vance, and the naked version arriving in 1963. And then the part where Reggie sat at the end of that verandah with the moths going and heard his own discussion section of July 1954 read back to him at dictation speed, and had nothing.

"Nothing," he says. "That's the whole of it. I've got nothing and I told him so. I haven't the standing to object and I'm not going to perform an objection I haven't got."

Pascual has not moved from the sink.

"When," he says.

"Friday. The twenty-eighth." A pause. "Two nights ago."

"You were on that verandah at eight in the evening and there's a launch that runs till ten."

Reggie looks at him.

"Yes," he says.

---

The awful part — the part Pascual will still be turning over in February — is that he doesn't lose his temper.

He's lost his temper perhaps eleven times in five years in this house and every one of them was about an animal, and this isn't about an animal and it's the worst thing that has happened to either of them since 1954, and what he feels isn't heat at all. It's a kind of terrible clarity, as though somebody had wiped a lens.

He puts the cloth down.

"Sit down."

"Pascual —"

"*Siéntate.*"

Reggie sits down.

---

"I want to go through it," Pascual says, "and I want to do it in order, and I'm not going to shout at you, and by the end of it you'll wish I had."

"All right."

"Four sentences. Say them."

Reggie says them. He's got them by heart, which Pascual notes and puts aside, because a man doesn't have four sentences by heart two days later unless he's been saying them to himself in the dark.

"Now. What does the fourth one buy?"

"I don't follow."

"*The authors do not recommend it.* What does it buy? Not what does it say — what does it *buy*, in animals, on the twelfth of March 1962, when a man in a place with money reads it." Pascual sits down opposite him. "Because in July of 1954 you wrote a discussion section that wasn't what the data would bear, and you haven't had one clean night since, and I've watched you not have them. So tell me what your bad sentence bought."

There is a long pause.

"Beta's decline arrested," Reggie says. "The pairing. Naples, Whitley, Florida, the two in Lisbon —"

"Say the number."

"I've got it as somewhere between forty and sixty animals alive in 1958 that would be dead."

"Forty and sixty animals," Pascual says. "Alive. That's what your bad sentence bought, and you've never once let yourself have it, and it's on the credit side of the page and it's been there four years, and I'm not arguing about it tonight because it isn't the point.

"The point's the other column. Your sentence bought forty animals and it cost an animal in Whitley in March of 'fifty-six and however many since that we don't know about, and that's a real cost and it's yours and you carry it — and the whole of the ugly arithmetic you and I have been doing since June of 1955 is that it *was an exchange*. Known animals against unknown ones. You bought something."

He puts both hands flat on the table.

"Now do his."

---

Reggie does not answer.

"Four sentences," Pascual says. "In the discussion of a contact paper. What do they buy? Which animal, in which pen, is alive in 1965 because Alec Spilsbury printed the hypothesis that a mermaid screams louder when you take its wife away?"

"It's not —"

"*Which animal.*"

The generator does what it does, four miles off, and can't be heard, and Pascual notices for the first time in three years that he misses it.

"None," Reggie says.

"None. Not one. There isn't a single animal anywhere on this earth better off in any year for those four sentences, and that's not my opinion and it isn't a debating point, it's *arithmetic*, and it's the only arithmetic either of us has ever been any good at." He isn't raising his voice and he isn't going to. "There's no credit column. There's a hypothesis, a hedge and a set of instructions; the hedge does nothing, the instructions work, and they'll be followed by somebody in Lisbon or Bergen or Florida inside four years, and every animal that comes out of it is a debit with nothing on the other side.

"It isn't the same act. It isn't a *kind* of the same act. You published a bad sentence to buy something and he's publishing one to buy nothing at all, and the two of you sat on that verandah for forty minutes and agreed they were the same thing, and one of you knew better."

---

"I did know better," Reggie says.

"Then why."

And here's where it goes wrong, because Reggie tells him, flat and level and in complete sentences, and every word of it's true.

"Because he was charging me," he says. "That's the whole of it and I worked it out at about two in the morning on the launch and I have not been able to put it down since.

"I've been waiting four years for somebody who knows enough to send me the bill for July 1954. Not you — you sent it in about eleven minutes and you've gone on sending it and it doesn't count, because you love me and I know what you're for. Somebody who doesn't care. Somebody with no stake in whether I get up in the morning. And on Tuesday night the best man in the field sat down opposite me and put my own discussion section in my hand, accurately, with the dates right, and I've never in my life been so —"

He stops.

"Say it."

"Relieved," says Reggie. "I was relieved. It's the most enormous relief I've ever felt and it lasted about three quarters of an hour, and I'd have agreed to anything on this earth while it was going on — and what I did agree to was that his paper and my paper are the same paper. They aren't. I knew they weren't at the time. I let it go because being charged was worth more to me than the argument was."

He puts his hands on his knees.

"So I didn't lose that argument," he says. "I paid with it. And it wasn't mine to pay with."

---

Pascual sits in his own kitchen and finds he's stopped being able to see very well.

And then he says it.

---

"You did not want to be charged."

Reggie looks up.

"That is what you have just told me and it is not what happened, and I have been listening to you for eleven minutes and I have got it now." Pascual hears his own voice and it is not the loud one and it is not the quiet one; it is a third one and he has never used it before. "You could have had a bill from me in 1954 and you did have one, in about eleven minutes, at that table, and you told me it does not count. You could have had one from Hester Bevan any week for four years and she would have made it beautifully and dated it. You have had one from Chettle. You have had one from a man in Whitley in a covering letter that you keep in a drawer eleven feet from your own desk and read when you cannot sleep.

"None of them did it. Not one. Four years of bills and not one of them was the one, and then a man sat down opposite you in the dark and it was *the* one, immediately, in three quarters of an hour, and you have come home and told me it was because he does not care about you."

"He doesn't —"

"He is the only person who ever looked at you and did not react." Pascual is on his feet and does not remember getting there. "That is the whole of it. You told me on a verandah in November of 'fifty-seven that you spent three years in that building running the experiment again to see whether you would get a different result, and you said it as though it were finished, and it is not finished, it has never been finished, and on Tuesday night Alec Spilsbury finally gave you a *reaction*. He looked at you for forty minutes and had an opinion about you and said it out loud with the dates right.

"And you paid him for it. You paid him with the only argument that would have saved a single animal, and it was not yours, and you would do it again tomorrow, and you know you would, and that is why you have had those four sentences by heart for two days."

---

The rain has started on the iron somewhere in the last minute and neither of them has noticed it start.

Reggie does not go white and does not stand up and does not raise his voice.

"Yes," he says.

"Do not —"

"That's accurate," Reggie says. "All of it. I've been round it since two o'clock on Wednesday morning and I got as far as the relief and stopped, and you've gone the rest of the way in eleven minutes." He is sitting perfectly still with his hands on his knees. "You're right about 1946 and you're right about what I paid with and you're right that I'd do it again. I don't know how to be a person who wouldn't. Put it on the list."

And that — the flatness of it, the *acceptance* of it, the man taking it in full at Pascual's figure without checking a single line — is what turns it.

Because it works. It's worked exactly the way it worked on the verandah. Pascual's just done it, on purpose, with better information and worse intent, and it's taken four minutes.

He should stop here.

---

"Do not do that," he says.

"Do what?"

"Sit there and agree with me. You have not argued with me once in twenty minutes."

"There isn't anything to argue with."

"There is *always* something to argue with, you have argued with me about paraffin —" He has to stop and get hold of it. "You do not fight me any more. You have not fought me about anything that mattered since about July."

"That isn't true."

"Then name one."

Reggie looks at him for about three seconds.

"You haven't set foot on that station in a year," he says.

The rain goes on.

"That's not a retort, before you take it as one. It's the thing you asked me for." His voice has not changed at all. "You built a stop into that study in January of 'fifty-seven and you wrote eleven conditions on the back of a feed sheet at two in the morning, and then you stopped coming to watch it work. Eleven months. You have not been across since last December except to be at Hester's table in April, and you've told me it's the practice on the main island, and there are two doctors on the main island now and you know their names.

"So I'm not going to sit here and be told about a launch that runs till ten." He puts his hands flat on his knees. "There's one that comes the other way as well."

---

There's a silence of a quality Pascual will be able to bring back at will for the rest of his life.

"That is not the same thing," he says.

"No," says Reggie. "It's worse, because you knew he was here for a fortnight and I had four minutes' notice."

"I am the one who —"

"You're the one who won't be in a room with him," Reggie says, "and you have never once said so out loud, and I have known it since about March and said nothing because I thought you'd get to it."

---

Here's the part Pascual doesn't forgive himself for, and he's going to have it in February and in June and at the end of every year of his life.

He's standing in his own kitchen with the rain going. He's been handed something true about himself by a man who's never in five years used a true thing as a weapon and isn't using it as one now — and the correct move, the only move, the one he'd make on any other night in any other year, is to sit down and say *yes, that's true, and I have not looked at it.*

What he does instead is reach for the biggest thing in the room.

"Then find somebody else to do it," he says.

"To do what?"

"To hand you your bills. To stand in front of you and be loud. I am finished." He is not shouting. That is the thing he cannot get past afterwards: he is not even shouting. "Five years. I have done it since a canvas hut in May of 1954 and it has not once got easier and I have not once been anywhere else in the room, and on Tuesday night it turned out that it does not matter what I do, because when the right man says it in the right voice you will hand him *anything*. So do it yourself. I am not going to be the thing you check yourself against any more. Do your own arithmetic."

And Reggie says: "All right."

---

Flat. Level. Immediately. Four seconds, no argument, in complete sentences.

"All right. That's fair and I've been an unreasonable amount of work for five years and I'd have said so myself if I'd got there first." He stands up and picks his hat up off the table where it has been the whole time. "I'll take the eleven o'clock. There's a bed in the block."

"Reggie —"

"I'm going because if I stay I'll say something true to you," Reggie says. "I've got about four and they're all accurate and not one of them is worth what it'd cost, and I've got another hour in me at most before one of them comes out."

He gets as far as the door.

"For the avoidance of doubt," he says, without turning round. "I'm not going anywhere and neither are you, and we've got thirty years, and I'll be back on Thursday. I'd like that in the room before I go out of it, because I'm not going to be able to say it on Thursday."

He goes.

---

The rain goes on for four days.

He doesn't go to the station and Reggie doesn't come across, and on Thursday the fourth Reggie comes across, and it's worse than the four days, because they're both extremely polite.

They eat. They talk about the drains, and Chettle's return, and whether the second lecture hut wants a new roof before the rains proper, and every exchange is courteous and accurate and takes about ninety seconds and then stops dead, and there's nothing to put in the gap, because for five years the thing that went in the gap was an argument about a boat.

On Friday Pascual catches himself starting to say something about the feed budget and stopping, because he can't tell any more whether he's objecting to something or handing the man a bill, and that's the first moment he understands the size of what he's done.

He hasn't withdrawn a service. He's taken the language away.

---

He works it out on the Saturday, alone, at the kitchen table, at about four in the afternoon, with the man on the other side of the island and the rain off.

It takes him three hours, he writes none of it down, and there's nothing clever in it at all.

He has been telling himself for eighteen months that the brake is a small thing and that anybody could do it. Hester could do it; she does it to eleven governments by post. Jean did it for four years with a graph. John does it now, to six men a quarter, with a pencil.

That's true and it isn't the point, and it's taken him until a Saturday afternoon in December of 1958 to see why.

Anybody can say no to Reggie Tar. Nobody's ever made it *stick* before the consequences were already on the concrete — and Pascual knows that better than anybody, because he tried for eleven days in May of 1954 and got nowhere at all, and what finally did it was hitting the man where it hurt most while an animal lay dying on sixty-one feet of hot concrete four hundred yards away. That is what it has taken, every time. That is the price of the thing working.

Because saying no to Reggie Tar has never once been what stopped him. What stops him is that he minds what it costs the person saying it, and there's exactly one man on this earth whose cost he's ever been able to feel. Halliwell couldn't do it. Spilsbury has never in his life tried. The Colonel could give him an order and did, twice, and it held for a fortnight.

So it isn't a service and it isn't a piece of institutional furniture and it couldn't be supplied by a competent stranger, and the reason isn't that Pascual Pobil is clever. It's that the man loves him and can't do anything by halves, which is the entire trouble with him and always has been, and is also the only handle it's ever had.

That's what he's for. It isn't the small half. It's the whole of it and it's enormous.

And on Tuesday night, at about ten o'clock, in his own kitchen, with the rain starting, he reached across the table and took it away.

---

He'd like to be able to say he did it because he was hurt, or because it was true, or because he'd been carrying it five years and something gave.

It was none of those, and he knows exactly what it was, because he watched himself do it.

He'd just been handed something accurate about himself, by a man who has never in five years used a true thing as a weapon and wasn't using it as one then, and he couldn't stand it — so he looked round the room for the largest object in it, and the largest object in it was the one thing he's got that can't be replaced. He picked that up and hit him with it. On purpose. Because it would hurt most.

And it did. It worked in four seconds. He's a veterinary surgeon of twenty-two years' standing, he's put down a great many animals, and he's never in his life done anything as deliberate as that.

Then there's the other part, which arrives about twenty minutes later and is worse.

He's going to fix the paper. He's known since Thursday that there's something to be got out of reason two and he's been turning it over between the courtesies, and he'll do it this evening, on a feed sheet, because there's work and the work doesn't care.

Which means the withdrawal had no substance in it of any kind. He didn't stop being what he is. He couldn't if he tried. He's going to go on doing it tonight, for nothing, in writing, in a house where nobody's speaking to him.

So it wasn't a resignation. It was only the wound.

Pascual sits at the table for a while with his hands over his face.

Then he gets a feed sheet and turns it over, because they can't speak, and there's work.

---

*Reggie —*

*Reason two. He said it to you on the verandah as a reason for himself not to do the experiment. He has never printed it, because a man like that does not think of his own reasons as warnings.*

*Print it. In his name, in the discussion, in the same paragraph. Four lines: the design cannot be blinded; the subject sees whether the pen holds one animal or two; his knowledge of the animal's state is inseparable from his response to it; no separation study can distinguish the two effects and any result from one is uninterpretable.*

*Take out "possible". Put that in.*

*Then a man in Lisbon in 1962 does not read "a controlled test is possible". He reads "worthless". And when he does it anyway — he will — he cannot publish it, because Spilsbury will have told the field it means nothing, and Bergen is on that board.*

*It does not save the first animal and I am not going to pretend that it does. It makes the second one pointless, and the third, and it takes the prize off the table, and a thing no journal will print stops being done in about five years.*

*Take it across. He sails Monday.*

*P.*

He leaves it on the table facing the chair and goes to bed.

---

In the morning it's gone.

He looks for another sheet on the table, and on the dresser, and — because he is apparently a man who does this now — in the bin. There isn't one.

Reggie goes across on the six o'clock.

He comes back at seven in the evening and says: "He's taking it."

"All of it?"

"All of it, and he's added a line I hadn't thought of." A pause of about two seconds. "It's a materially better paper."

"Good."

"Yes."

And that is the whole of the conversation, and Pascual does not ask whether Spilsbury was told where the four paragraphs came from, and Reggie does not say, and they eat.

Spilsbury sails on the eighth. Pascual does not go across to see him off and is not asked to.

What he gets, at second hand, in the kitchen, is: "He sends you his regards, and says to tell you he hasn't worked out what Mrs Bevan is doing at those pens at twenty past six in the morning, and that he intends to."

"Good," says Pascual, and means several things by it, and does not explain any of them.

Reggie does not ask.

---

It takes them until February.

Not because either of them's sulking. Neither of them's capable of it, and they've both said the necessary things — Pascual said *I should not have said that and it was not true and I am sorry* on the ninth of December, in the kitchen, standing up, the day after Spilsbury sailed, and Reggie said *I know, and thank you, and it was about two thirds true* on the same evening, and both of them meant every word.

It doesn't help much, and here's the thing about the two of them that neither has ever had to learn before: saying the true thing out loud has fixed everything they've ever put it to, for five years, and this time it doesn't work, and there's no second procedure.

So they're careful with one another.

That's what it's like. They're courteous and considerate and they check before they say things, and Pascual watches a man who once measured a garage in March and said nothing about it for three months in order to produce it at breakfast now decline to mention the paraffin at all. The house is quiet in a way it's never been quiet. The arguing doesn't come back, because neither of them can find the edge of it any more; every objection's got a bill in it somewhere and both of them can feel the bill, so neither of them starts.

And there are two bedrooms in the house on the north side, because there have to be, and in three years the second one has held a wardrobe, eleven boxes of fisheries records and a bed that was made up once in 1956 when Bassani stayed.

Reggie moves into it on the fourth of December without either of them discussing it, which is the correct thing and the only thing, and which Pascual would have proposed himself by the twelfth.

They don't touch. Not by accident in the kitchen, not on the stairs, not at the table. Pascual hadn't understood before quite how much of a day is made of that; he's apparently been going about for five years being touched some nine times between waking and sleeping and never once counted it, and he counts now, out of a kind of grim professional interest, and gets to none.

Twice in January it's unbearable, and on both occasions one of them goes into the other's room in the dark without a word, and it's quick and silent and not unkind and there isn't one minute of pleasure anywhere in it for either of them, and afterwards whichever of them it is gets up and goes back.

Neither of them mentions it in the morning. Neither of them mentions it at all.

That's worse than the not touching, and Pascual knows it at the time, and does it again on the second occasion anyway.

It isn't a rupture. It's an absence — and Pascual, who is the only man in the Caribbean with forty-one years of professional opinion on that subject sitting four miles away in a bungalow, recognises exactly what it is and doesn't say so, because saying so would be enormous and unfair and he's done enough.

---

On the ninth of January he goes across on the launch for no reason at all and stands at Chumley's pen for an hour, and Aguilar gives him a cup of tea, and neither of them says anything about it.

He does it again on the sixteenth. And on the thirtieth.

On the sixth of February he is at the rail at seven in the morning when Reggie comes down the path with the roster in his hand, and stops, and looks at him for about a second and a half.

"You're here," Reggie says.

"I am here."

"Mm."

"The calf's up four pounds on the quarter and you've got him down as three."

There is a pause of about four seconds.

"I have not," says Reggie.

"You have. It's on the sheet in your hand, it's wrong, and it's been wrong since Tuesday."

Reggie looks at the sheet.

"So it is," he says, and gets a pencil out, and corrects it, and does not look up, and there is something in the set of his shoulders that Pascual has not seen since the autumn.

---

He goes across again on the thirteenth, and on the twentieth, and on the twenty-seventh, and by the end of the month Aguilar has stopped putting the kettle on specially and simply pours him one.

Nothing is fixed. Pascual is extremely clear with himself about that, because it's the sort of month in which a man tells himself a story.

The paper's at the printer with a hypothesis in it and four paragraphs he will not know the fate of until March. Somebody will still try. There's a broker in Port of Spain with an order every time an animal dies, and Gamma's old, and the calf's five, and there's nine pages in a safe in Bridgetown that oblige this Institute to buy a mermaid on the morning after she stops. The Colonel's seventy-three and reads the drains file from a chair. Nestor Aguilar's on the establishment for life and it's a piece of paper.

And there are two rooms in a house on the north side, and a thing said across a kitchen table on the thirtieth of November that Pascual doesn't expect to be able to put down before about 1963.

But the calf was up four pounds on the quarter, and it was wrong on the sheet, and he said so.
