# Not Fewer Than One

## 14 June 1985

The thirty-first review is on the fourteenth of June, at the kitchen table, at eight o'clock in the evening, because that was the date in 1955.

Reggie brings the folder. Of course he does.

The costing sheet is in it, soft at the folds as a chart, with the cup-ring on the corner and *1984* struck through with one flat line, and *2014* beside it in Pascual's blunt hand, underlined twice, from a Thursday twelve months ago which Reggie has been over about four hundred times since and has not improved.

"Item one," says Reggie.

"There's only one item."

"There's only ever one item. It's still item one."

---

What happened on the fourth of April was that Pascual came up the eleven steps at a quarter to seven in the evening, and stopped on the ninth, which he does, and then did not come up the last two, which he does not.

Reggie got him to the top and into the chair and did as he was told, which took about ninety seconds and consisted of: the brown bottle, the second drawer, and *sit down where I can see you, ay, sit down.*

It was eleven minutes. Then it was over, and Pascual sat in the chair with his hand flat on his own chest in a professional sort of way and said, "Well."

"Say it."

"I'm a veterinary surgeon."

"You're a doctor of medicine and have been since 1938 and you've got your own case history in your hand," Reggie said, "and I've watched you do this to four people in this house. Say it."

And Pascual said it, in the voice he uses to owners, which is how Reggie knew what it had cost him:

"It may be eleven years. It may be March."

Which is what he said in 1958, in this kitchen, about the Colonel; and the Colonel had eleven years and died in his chair with the drains file on his knee; and neither of them mentions that, then or afterwards, because it is the only comfortable thing in the room and they are neither of them the kind of men who will take it.

---

So the folder has a second sheet in it this year, and Reggie puts it on the table face down and does not turn it over.

"The observation," he says.

"Go on."

"It's been thirty-one years. Not all of them were good and I've never once claimed they were. It isn't the arrangement's fault that Hester died, or the Colonel, or that a company in Florida drained a hundred and seventy square miles of cypress, or that a man in Massachusetts is dead and I can't stop reaching for him. Those aren't entries.

"Sixty-four days are an entry and I've never taken them off the sheet and I'm not going to now." He has both hands flat on the folder. "And the entry on the other side is that I haven't been in a room by myself since the nineteenth of May 1954, and that I wanted it to go on on every single day of it, including the sixty-four, and that of all the figures on this sheet it's the only one I've ever been entirely certain of.

"That's the observation. It's the same one as last year."

"It's better than last year."

"It's *identical* to last year."

"It is better," says Pascual, "and I'm not arguing with you about it. Now do the proposal."

---

Reggie turns the second sheet over.

It is a single line in his own hand, small and upright and fast.

*Proposed: terminal date to be brought back from 2014 to 1986, and the arrangement reviewed annually thereafter.*

Pascual reads it upside down, which he has been able to do since 1954.

Then he picks up the pencil and draws one flat line through the whole of it, from one end to the other, and hands the pencil back.

"No."

"Pascual —"

"*No.* You can't bring in a terminal date on the day of a review. You said that to me in this kitchen on the fourteenth of June 1984 at about half past nine at night and you were *shouting* —"

"I said you couldn't *extend* one —"

"You said prespecification," says Pascual, "at length, for eleven minutes, and I had to put the lamp out with you still talking, and I have waited a year for this." He is enjoying himself. He is, Reggie can see perfectly well, having the best evening he has had since April. "It's 2014. It was 2014 in June and it's 2014 now and it'll be 2014 in March, whatever March does. I'm not revising a sheet because of an *outcome*. You taught me that."

Reggie sits at his own kitchen table and looks at a line drawn through his own handwriting.

"I taught you that," he says.

"You did. In 1956, about a fish count, and you were insufferable about it, and I've had thirty years of waiting to use it." Pascual reaches over and takes the folder and shuts it and puts it on the counter by the stove, well away from the bottle and the wet ring the glasses have left on the table, which is Reggie's own habit and has been for thirty-one years and which Pascual has never once commented on until now. "There. The sheet stands. Go and get the other bottle, the arithmetic is finished, and I want to hear about the notebook."

---

## July 1985

Pascual gets four things done in July and doesn't tell Reggie about any of them, and is perfectly clear with himself that this is not the same as the drawer in 1958, and checks it twice to be sure.

It isn't the same. In 1958 he had something that was Reggie's and kept it. These are his own and there's a difference, and if he's wrong about that he'll find out in about the first week of August, because he's going to tell him then, all four, at the table, in order.

The first is the practice, which goes to the young Trinidadian woman who's been doing three days a week since the spring and is better at it than he was at forty. That takes an afternoon and a great deal of paper.

The second is Inés, who he sits with on the step of their house at the top of the village for about an hour, in Spanish, and at the end of which she says the thing he came for, which is that she'll be there, and that Nestor will be there, and that neither of them needs telling.

The third is John.

That one takes eleven minutes in the lecture hut with the leak and is the worst of the four, because John Clarke is fifty-six years old and has been Director of this Institute for twenty-three years and is still, in some corner of himself, the boy who drew a fish with blue lines on its face, and he goes white before Pascual has finished the second sentence.

"Don't," John says.

"I'm not asking you for anything —"

"You're going to, and I'd rather you got on with it."

So Pascual gets on with it.

"When he says he wants something he oughtn't to have," he says, "he'll say it out loud. He has done it for twenty-six years and he'll go on doing it, because it's the only way he knows how to hold on to the wheel. And it does not matter who is in the room."

"All right."

"So when he does it, you ask him what it's for."

John stands there with a pencil in his hand.

"That's it?"

"That's it. You don't have to be clever. You'll want to be clever, and you are not to be; if you argue with him he wins, because he is cleverer than you and than me and than anybody who ever came through that door. You ask what it's for, and then you shut your mouth and you *wait*, and you let it take as long as it takes." Pascual looks at him. "It takes about four minutes. It always takes about four minutes. And then he gets there himself, and he gets there hating it, and that's the whole of the method, and it isn't a method. It's a question."

---

And then, because he's not going to leave a man with a job he can't do, he tells him the rest, which he worked out on his own in this kitchen at four in the afternoon on a Saturday in December 1958 and has never said out loud to anybody including Reggie.

"It won't work."

John's head comes up.

"Not the way it works for me. Anybody can say no to that man; it was never once what stopped him." Pascual puts both hands on the back of the chair. "What stops him is that he minds what it costs the one who says it. That's the whole mechanism and the only handle it ever had, and it has nothing to do with being right. Hester couldn't do it. The Colonel gave him an order twice and it held a fortnight. Spilsbury never tried in thirty-one years and would not have known how."

"Then why am I —"

"Because he minds what it costs *you*," says Pascual. "He's minded since 1954. He watched you lie to a man's face at a breakfast table when you were twenty-four and he has never got over it, and he has been careful of you for thirty-one years in a way he thinks nobody noticed." He straightens up. "So it'll work. Not as well. It takes longer and you'll get it wrong sometimes and he'll get past you sometimes, and I'd rather that than nothing, and so would he."

John Clarke sits down on the edge of his own desk and takes his glasses off and doesn't say anything for about half a minute.

"Does he know you're doing this?"

"He'll know in August."

"Pascual."

"*Ay.*"

"He'll hate it."

"He'll hate it very much," Pascual agrees, "and he will thank you for it in about 1990, and by then you will have stopped minding whether he does." He picks his hat up. "Numbers on everything, John. That's yours. Don't let them talk you out of it."

---

The fourth thing takes him four evenings and he does it at the kitchen table when Reggie's across at the office, and it isn't a letter, because he's never in his life been able to write a letter that said anything.

It's feed sheets. Eleven of them, on the back, in the blunt hand, one subject to a sheet, the way he's done it since 1958: the boat, and what's wrong with the engine and who to have look at it. The account book and where the argument about the paraffin actually stands, in case anybody should ever claim otherwise. Inés. Chumley, who is thirty-two and slow this year, and what that's going to want. The steps, and the fact that somebody is going to have to put a rail on them and that it will not be Reggie Tar who decides when.

And one that isn't about any of that, which he writes last, on the fourth evening, and reads twice, and doesn't strike anything out, and puts at the bottom of the pile where it won't be found first.

Then he ties the eleven of them with string and puts them in the second drawer of the dresser under the tin.

And he goes out on to the verandah, because the launch is coming round, and stands at the rail in the dark, and watches the light come up the harbour road and stop at the bottom of the eleven steps, and listens to a small precise man of sixty-six come up all of them at his ordinary pace, talking before he's got to the fourth one.

---

## August 1985

Reggie says it on the ninth of August, standing up, in the kitchen, at about half past nine at night, going red.

"I want something and I'm going to tell you what it is."

Pascual puts the feed book down.

"Go on."

"I want Charles to come out."

---

The generator is four miles away and cannot be heard.

"I've had it since April," Reggie says, "and I've had it every single day since the fourth of April, and I haven't said it because I've been telling myself it wasn't a want, it was an idea. And that's a lie, and here's the truth about it: it's the worst want I've had since 1962 and there isn't one piece of it I'm ashamed of, which is how I know exactly how bad it is.

"He's fifty-five. He's eleven hours away. There's a boy at Cedros with a canoe who does this four times a year and a man at Icacos who talks too much and has never once talked. Jean could have him at the mouth of the channel inside a week and nobody in the world would be the wiser, and he could be in this room, and you could *see him*, and he could see you, and it'd take a day and a half, and the two of you haven't been in a room together since the twentieth of May 1954."

He stops.

"That's the want. I want you to have him in a room. I'm aware I've just said *I want you to have*, and that it's the oldest trick in my repertoire and I've been doing it since I was twenty-four, and I'm saying it anyway, because underneath it the thing is true."

---

Pascual sits at the table.

"*Ay*," he says. "And what's it for?"

"I've told you what it's for."

"You've told me what you want. That is not the same and you know it, and you've known it since a graveyard in 1962." Pascual doesn't raise his voice. "Sit down. Not because I'm cross with you. Because it'll take you four minutes and you can't do it standing up."

Reggie sits down.

---

It takes about four minutes.

"It is so that I do not have to be the one who tells him," Reggie says.

And then he puts his hand over his eyes, because there it is, and it took four minutes, and it always takes four minutes.

"In February or in 1996 or whenever it is," he says, "somebody's going to have to put a tin in a canoe with your name in it. And I'm going to write it, and Chago's going to take it up a channel, and Charles Cholmondeley's going to open it on his own, in the water, four thousand miles from anybody who ever knew either of you, and there will be nothing whatever anybody can do for him, ever, because there is no address and no telephone and no possible reply that is not another six months in a tin.

"So what I actually want is for him to have been here first. So that it isn't the first he hears. So that when it comes he can at least — " Reggie stops and starts again. "I want to spend a man's thirty-one years of protection to make a piece of news easier to receive. That is the arithmetic. I have just done it out loud for the first time and it is appalling."

"It is," Pascual agrees.

"Yes."

"And it's the kindest appalling thing anybody's ever wanted about me, and I'd like that said before we get to the rest of it, because you'll take the rest of it hard and you won't hear the first part afterwards." He reaches over and takes hold of Reggie's wrist and keeps it. "Now. Here's the rest of it.

"I don't need to see him. I saw him. I had him for nineteen months and I shouted at him and made him eat and told him where his glasses were, and in 1984 he sent four thousand miles to tell me his feet were fine, and that's not nothing, Reggie, that's the *whole thing*, and it's on the dresser." His grip does not slacken. "And what would he be coming out *for*? To be looked at by me for a day and a half. And then he goes back in — as a man who came out. And every man at Icacos who's kept his mouth shut for thirty-one years about a story has now kept it shut about a *fact*, which is a different job, and one of them will be drunk in 1991."

"Yes."

"Write to him instead."

Reggie lifts his head.

"Write to him," says Pascual. "Now, in August, while I am here to say what goes in it. Not when it is news. Tell him yourself, in your own hand, in advance, and put in the thing you'd have wanted him to see if he came, and send it down the channel the ordinary way." He lets go of the wrist and picks up the feed book again, which is what he does when he has finished and does not intend to discuss it. "And then he'll have had it from you, sitting in a mangrove, with six months to get used to it, and when the second one comes it'll be a date and not a blow. *Ay.* That's obtainable. The other one isn't."

---

They write it on the eleventh, at the table, over about three hours, and it runs to a page and a half, and Pascual says what goes in it and Reggie writes it down and does not improve one word of it, which is the hardest piece of work he does all year.

The tin goes down to Nestor on the thirteenth and to Cedros on the Sunday.

---

## The night of the tenth of September 1985

He wakes up at ten past two because the bed has moved.

Pascual is sitting on the edge of it with his back to him and both hands on his knees, and he says, in an entirely ordinary voice, slightly irritable, the voice of a man who has been kept from something:

"There's a rail wanted on those steps and you're not to be the one who decides it."

And then he goes.

---

Reggie is not a doctor.

He does the wrong things for about four minutes. He knows they are the wrong things while he is doing them; he has watched this man do the right ones, in this house, to four people, and he has the whole sequence in his head in order and he cannot make his hands perform any of it. He gets the brown bottle from the second drawer of the dresser and it is the wrong bottle. He says the man's name a number of times which he will not afterwards be able to establish.

At about twenty past two he stops.

He is sixty-six years old and he has spent forty years being the cleverest person in every room he has entered, and he sits down on the floor of his own bedroom with his back against the bed, and there is nothing to do, and no part of it can be taken apart, and the arithmetic is not difficult and is finished.

---

He does not telephone anybody for an hour and a quarter, and he wants it on the record that this was not shock and was not decency.

It was that there was a thing he had to do first and no one alive to ask permission of.

---

At about half past three in the morning he gets up off the floor and turns the lamp on and stands in the middle of the room, and says, out loud, in the flattest voice he has:

"I have four things. I have had all four of them for some years and I have not said any of them, and I am going to say them all now, because you are not here to interrupt me, and that is the only way I have ever got through a list in this house.

"One. In February of 1959 I told you that I could not hear *I am finished* without something going over in my chest, and that I expected about four more years of it. It was twenty-six. It never once stopped. Every single time, for twenty-six years, including when you said it about a roof, and about the paraffin, and about a fish. I did not tell you, because you would have stopped saying it, and you would have stopped saying it in an ordinary voice about ordinary things, and I would rather have had it. That is the trade I made and I made it on my own and I would make it again and you are entitled to have been told.

"Two. I have known since 1962 that you gave up a practice you had built in twenty years, and an island, and a language, and went to a city where you could not buy bread, because I was offered a desk. You were bad-tempered for a year exactly as advertised and never once about the right thing. You have never said what it cost and I have never asked, because I did not want the answer in the room. I want it entered that I knew.

"Three. It is not true that I minded the sheet standing at 2014. I said I minded because you were enjoying yourself and it was the ninth of June and you had not enjoyed yourself since April. I wanted it to stand. I have wanted it to stand since the day you wrote it.

"Four." He stops.

"Four. *Te quiero.*"

He says it in the appalling accent, which he has never once improved, because improving it would have been to make it a thing he had prepared; and he says it a second time to an empty room in a house on the north side of a harbour at twenty-five to four in the morning; and there is nobody there to tell him it is the worst thing they have ever heard in their life.

Then he goes down the eleven steps and along the harbour road to the office and telephones John Clarke.

---

## September and October 1985

John is there in nineteen minutes and does not knock. Nestor and Inés are there before six. Jean is on an aeroplane by Thursday and is unforgivable at the funeral and would be again.

There is the medical officer from the main island, who is thirty-four and was taught by him, and who does the certificate at the kitchen table and signs it and then has to go out on to the verandah for a minute.

There is a church, and Inés arranges it, and Reggie sits in the second row because Pascual's cousins have come from Santander and the first row is theirs and this is the correct arrangement and he has no standing whatever and does not raise it and would not have raised it in 1954 or in 1985 or in any year in between, and John Clarke comes and sits down beside him without being asked and stays there for the whole of it.

And afterwards, at the Institute, on the concrete, with about eleven people and the tea going round, Nestor Aguilar comes and stands next to him at the rail and does not say one word for about twenty minutes, and then puts his hand on the back of Reggie's neck, briefly, and takes it away, and goes to do the weights.

That is the only time anybody touches him that week.

---

Here is the thing about the reaching, and he sets it down in October because he is a man who writes things in columns, and because he has done this once before and has the earlier column to compare it with.

With Massachusetts it was a line. That is exactly what it was and he has never found a better word: a line going out from the middle of him to a place he could not see, with a man at the other end of it who knew where his head went; and on a Tuesday morning in November of 1977 he reached along it and it went out and did not come back off anything. *Call. No answer.*

This is not that.

There is no line, because there was never anywhere for a line to go. He does not reach out. He *turns round*.

He turns round in the kitchen at twenty past six with a figure in his hand. He turns round on the launch. He turns round at the rail at Chumley's pen on the third of October, having seen something, with the whole of it already in his mouth. He turns round in the second week about nine times a day and by December about four, and every time he has got as far as the turn before he knows, and the knowing arrives after the turn and not before it, every single time, for months.

A line that goes out and gets no answer is a grief. He knows, because he has had one, and he has it still, and it is a small one and it is his.

This is not a grief with a shape. This is the discovery that a very large proportion of what he had taken to be *himself* — the noticing, the minding, the appetite, the whole apparatus, everything he has ever thought of as the inside of Reggie Tar — was in fact a thing constructed between two people and requiring both of them, and that it does not run on one.

---

He finds the sentence for it on the nineteenth of October, in the office, and it is not his.

*I do not think he is company. I think he is the place where I am not alone, which is a different thing, and I do not know what I shall be if it ever shuts.*

Page two hundred and six. A boy of twenty-four wrote it in a hut about an animal in a concrete box, and had it right at twenty-four, and Reggie Tar read it across a kitchen table on the fourteenth of June 1984 and said *that is you, that is what you are, you are the place*, and was pleased with himself.

He knows now what he shall be. He gets it in the second week of October and it takes about a second and a half and it is the plainest thing he has ever known.

He shall be a man who is alone.

Not lonely; he has been lonely, in a building in England, with the door off its hinges, and it is a different and much smaller condition. Alone. In a room with other people in it. For twenty years or so, at the ordinary actuarial rate, in a house with eleven steps and a rail on them that somebody else is going to decide about.

---

## November 1985

He opens the second drawer of the dresser on the ninth of November, because the tin is on top of it and the tin has to be moved to get at the drawer, and he has been not getting at the drawer since September.

There are eleven feed sheets tied with string.

He reads them over four nights, in order, from the top: the boat and the engine and Cyril's boy who is to look at it. The account book, and the exact and undiminished position on the paraffin, set out in nine lines, closing with the observation that the account book is *available for inspection*. Inés. Chumley, who is thirty-two and slow this year and will want a decision in about eighteen months, with the four options and what each of them costs, in the blunt hand, with no recommendation attached, because there never is one.

The steps.

And at the bottom of the pile, the eleventh, which is not about any of that.

*Reggie —*

*Four things, in order, because you like them in order.*

*1. It was thirty-one years and I would not take one day of it back, including the sixty-four, and I have never once said so out loud because you would have made it into a figure. It is not a figure. Put it wherever you keep the things that are not figures.*

*2. You will want to do something. You always want to do something and it is the best thing about you and it is also the thing that has cost the most. So: do the notebook. All two hundred and nine pages, under his name, with John's plates, and fight the journal about the plates, and take two years over it, and be insufferable to everybody. That is a real want and it is for somebody else and you may have the whole of it with my blessing.*

*3. You are going to want one other thing and I know what it is and I am not going to write it down, because writing it down would be telling you that you had it, and you have not got it, and I would rather you found that out from John than from me. Ask him. He knows what he is to do. He will be very bad at it for about a year. Let him.*

*4. Ay, Dios mío, Reggie. Say it in the boat. Nobody can hear you, including you.*

*P.*

---

He sits at the kitchen table with that in front of him for some while.

Then he takes it across on the launch in the morning, and goes into the lecture hut with the leak, and puts it on John Clarke's desk, and says: "He's left me an instruction about you and I'm not going to follow it, because if I follow it I'll have handled you, and I've been doing that to you since you were twenty-four and I'm too old for it and you're the Director of this Institute. You may read the whole of it and then tell me what he asked you."

John reads it standing up.

Then he goes rather red, and says: "He asked me to ask you what it's for. When you say you want something. That's the whole of it. He said it'd take four minutes and I wasn't to be clever."

"Mm."

"He also said," John says, "that I'd be worse at it than he was. He was very clear about that. He seemed to think I'd want that in writing."

And Reggie Tar stands in a hut with a leak in it and finds that he has had to put his hand on the corner of the desk.

Because it is not the brake. The brake he could have got from anybody and has been got from nobody, and the truth is that he would get past John Clarke about one time in three and both of them know it, and Pascual knew it in July in this room and said so.

It is that he is going to go on being *asked*.

Every time. For as long as there is anybody. A question, with a form, which he has never once in his life been beaten by; and it is the only thing on this earth anyone ever gave him that he could not have got by being clever, and the man arranged for it to go on arriving after he was dead, and did not tell him, and left it where he would find it in November when it was worst.

"Thank you," Reggie says, to a desk.

"Dr Tar —"

"Give me a minute, John," says Reggie. "I'll be perfectly all right in a minute. I'd be obliged if you'd go on standing there while I have it."

And John Clarke, who is fifty-six and has been Director for twenty-three years and still works in the hut with the leak, goes on standing there.

---

## February 1986

The tin comes on the twenty-seventh of February, a Thursday, the way everything comes to that house that does not come in the post: on the launch, in Nestor's hand, in oilskin, from Cedros, from Tito's grandson, from the man at Icacos who talks too much, from a canoe.

Nestor does not come up the eleven steps. He gives it to Reggie at the bottom, on the harbour road, and says "*Doctor*," and goes away without waiting, because it is not his.

Reggie carries it up inside his shirt, on the left, because that is what is done, and stops on the ninth step, and there is nobody at the top to say *four this week* and nobody to swear about it in Spanish, and he stands there for a moment and then goes up the last two.

---

He opens it at the kitchen table.

His hands do it perfectly well. He unties the line and unwraps the oilskin and unscrews the cap and takes out the chart, soft as a handkerchief, gone to rags along one edge, and unrolls it, and there is the reef in Jean's hand and *Fresh water* and *Keep clear — ordnance*, and on the back, in twenty-two years of pencil and ink: *It worked* and *Yes. All of it* and *Tell P. my feet are fine.*

And under all of it, in the small cramped hand, fresh, in pencil:

*Reggie,*

*I had your August letter in October and I have had four months and you were right to send it and he was right to make you.*

*I am sorry. There is nothing I can put on a chart.*

*M. says to tell you a thing and I have argued with him about it for three days and he says you will want it and that I am not the one who decides. So: it does not go quiet. That is the part nobody on your side knows and he says it is the only part that matters. The other one stops, and the place where he was does not stop. It stays exactly where it was and it stays the same size. He says the word you would use is that it does not thin.*

*He says he does not know whether that is a good thing to be told. I said you would say it was data.*

*You asked me nothing. I want that written down: in thirty-two years, not one question, and I know what it cost you, and it is the whole reason I am still here.*

*C.*

*Tell the one who counts the reef has moved again. She will be pleased.*

---

He reads it four times.

Then he goes out on to the verandah in the dark and stands at the rail with the chart in his hand and finds that he is doing the thing he has done twice a day since September, which is to turn round.

He turns round.

And the kitchen is behind him with the lamp on and the table and the two chairs, and there is nobody in it; and the place where the man was is exactly where it was, and exactly the size it was, and has not thinned by one degree in five months and is not going to.

---

## 14 June 1986

The thirty-second review is on the fourteenth of June, at the kitchen table, at eight o'clock in the evening, because that was the date in 1955.

He brings the folder. Of course he does.

He puts the costing sheet on the table, soft at the folds, with the cup-ring on the corner and *1984* struck through and *2014* beside it, underlined twice, in a hand that is not his.

"Item one," he says.

There is no answer, and there is not going to be one, and he knew that before he sat down, and he says it anyway, out loud, in an empty kitchen, because he has been saying things out loud in this house since 1954 and he is not going to stop doing it on account of an outcome.

"There is only one item," he says. "There's only ever one item. It's still item one."

---

He does the observation.

It takes about eleven minutes and he does the whole of it properly, in order, out loud, and there are two entries on the debit side this year and he reads them both out and does not soften either.

Then he takes the pencil out of his pocket.

He looks at *2014* for some while.

The clause says *not fewer than two*, and he has read it perhaps four hundred times in thirty years and has always read the first half, and the first half has never in his life been true of him until the tenth of September, and it is true now and will be true for twenty years and there is no honest way round it.

And the second half of the clause is *save where no specimen is obtainable*, and a woman of sixty spent eleven years in a boat making that sentence untrue, and the whole of the Institute's law turns out to be a document about what you do when you have not got what the rule requires; which is not stop.

He puts the pencil down without writing anything.

The sheet stands at 2014.

He will be ninety-five. He will be insufferable. He has been told so, at this table, at about half past nine at night, by a man who was enjoying himself.

Then Reggie Tar, sixty-seven years old, at a kitchen table on the north side of a harbour, with his glasses in his hand, puts the folder back on the counter by the stove, well away from the bottle and the wet ring the glass has left on the table; and puts his own hand up to the side of his own face, which is not the same thing and is not anything at all and is what there is; and sits there with it while the lamp burns down.
