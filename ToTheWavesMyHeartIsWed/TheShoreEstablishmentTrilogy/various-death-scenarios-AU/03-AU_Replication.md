# Replication

## October 1990

He is seventy-one, and the notebook came out in 1988, two years late, under Charles Cholmondeley's name, with fifty-one of John's plates and a hundred and eleven pages of apparatus at the back; and Reggie was insufferable to everybody for the whole of 1986 and 1987, exactly as instructed.

The correspondence is the thing now. There are about ninety people, in fourteen countries, and he answers every letter, in his own hand, on the day; and eleven of them are young, and four of those eleven are going to be good, and he has told none of them so and all of them know.

---

The other thing is the rail.

It is not a secret. He would say, if anybody put it to him, that he goes down to the middle pen in the late afternoon about once a fortnight because it is the best place on the establishment to think about a design and because the light is good, and he would say it in a tone that closed the subject, and everybody would let him.

What happens is that he sits on the step with the day book on his knee and his hand over the rail in the water, which he has done since 1957 and which does absolutely nothing for him. He cannot feel them. He has never felt anything, not once in thirty-three years, and has long ago stopped minding and has never stopped being interested.

And after about four minutes there is a shoulder under his hand.

It is usually Leslie's granddaughter, who is eighteen and enormous, and she comes in on the surface without being called and puts the flat of her shoulder up under his palm and stays there; and she does not do this for Nestor or for John or for anybody else, and she does it every single time.

He does not know what she is doing and she is not going to be able to tell him. What he knows is that at some point in the first quarter of an hour his right shoulder comes down about an inch, and that he has never once been able to make it do that on purpose.

And then the calf brings him things.

The calf is three. He brings, over the course of an afternoon: a whelk shell, a length of rubber hose which is not his, a dead and rather old parrotfish, and — in September — a spanner, which caused a certain amount of argument in the workshop. He puts them on the concrete by Reggie's foot, one at a time, and goes away, and comes back; and Reggie says "Thank you" every time in exactly the tone he would use to a junior, and writes down what it was and the time.

---

He found out in the June of this year, reading the returns, which he has read every month for thirty-eight years.

It is in John's column — the one for observations not reducible to figures, which John has kept since 1962 and which Reggie made him put in — and it is in John's hand, and it says:

*Dr Tar at the middle pen, 41 min. B's shoulder under his hand within 3 min, unprompted. Calf presented 4 objects. Third occasion this month; cf. entries 14.iii, 2.iv, and see note below.*

And the note below says:

*Correlates with the anniversaries and with the bad weeks. r. not calculable on 33 yr of my own eyeball, but I have been keeping this column since 1962 and I would put money on it. It also plainly does him good, which is the part I would want a successor to know: he goes down there looking like the worst of it and he comes up the path afterwards talking, and there is no other intervention available on this island that does that. He does not know about either half of this. I have never raised it and do not intend to. P. told me in July 1985 that the thing to do was to ask what it was for and then wait, and I have asked him nothing whatever about this, because it is the one thing on this island that is not for anything at all. — J.C., numbered 62,911.*

---

Reggie Tar sits at a desk in the office on the north side with the third-quarter returns in front of him and his glasses in his hand for some while.

Then he goes down to the middle pen at about half past four, because he has a design to think about and the light is good, and sits on the step, and puts his hand over the rail.

Four minutes. Then the shoulder.

"Yes," he says. "All right."

And after a bit the calf comes up with something that appears to be a boot, and puts it on the concrete, and goes away, and comes back, and stops, and holds where he is, with his head just out of the water, about eleven inches off; and Reggie looks at him.

"Thank you, Pascual," he says.

---

## The Thursday

He goes across and says it to John Clarke's face, in the hut with the leak, standing up, at about eleven in the morning.

"I read your note on the middle pen in the June returns and I've been four months getting to this, and I'm not going to discuss the substance of it, because I've thought about the substance of it for four months and I'm not able to." He has his hat in his hands. "What I came to say is that you wrote a true thing about me in a permanent record, with a number on it, knowing I read every return, and you did it anyway. Nobody's done that to me since the fourth of September 1985. I've been told a great many kind things in five years and not one true one. Thank you."

John goes scarlet and says, "Right," and then, "Well," and then, after about four seconds, "He'd have written it in about 1963 and put it in the margin where you'd find it in the wrong order."

"He would," says Reggie. "He'd have spelled *unprompted* wrong."

And John Clarke, who is sixty-one and has been Director for twenty-eight years, laughs out loud in his own office; and Reggie goes down the path with his hat on, and gets about forty yards, and stops.

---

He stops because he has heard himself.

He has just stood in a hut and said a difficult thing out loud, cold, to a man's face, unprompted, at eleven in the morning, without being asked, without rehearsing it, in a register he did not have at thirty-eight and did not have at fifty and cannot now remember acquiring; and it cost him nothing worth entering. Perhaps eleven seconds of difficulty on the way in at the door.

And the arithmetic arrives on the path in about a second and a half, complete, with the dates right, the way the important ones always do.

*He could not have done that.*

---

He goes down to the harbour road and sits on the wall by the chandler's with his hat on his knee and does it properly, because he is not going to have a thing of this size on the strength of a feeling.

The case for the other way round. If it had gone the other way round, Pascual Pobil would have had, at seventy-nine, in that house: Inés, and Nestor, and the village, and the church, and forty people who loved him, and somebody in that kitchen every day of the autumn, and food arriving until the ninth of the following January. He would have been fed and touched and shouted at and taken care of. He would never once have been physically alone, and he would never once have gone eleven days without a hand on him; and Reggie will concede — has conceded, out loud, in the kitchen, on more than one bad night — that on that axis the difference is not close, and is entirely to Pascual, and that he himself has had five years of getting the whole of his touch off an animal that cannot be told anything.

And against it: that the man could not have said one word of it to anybody.

He is fairly sure of that and he puts down what he is sure on. The drawer in 1958, and how long it stayed there, and that not one other living person was ever told. December of 1956, and a man sitting down in a kitchen at four in the afternoon and working out something enormous on his own in about two hours and getting it right and never once saying it out loud, to anybody, ever, including Reggie, including on the last night. Thirty-one years. Forty people. Not one hard thing put into any of their hands.

He takes about three minutes over that, and it is the best-marshalled case he has assembled in five years, and he is rather pleased with it.

Then he looks at it again.

---

And it will not close, and the reason it will not close is that every one of those observations was made in a house that already had a sufficient recipient standing in it.

He has thirty-one years of a man not doing a thing the man had no occasion to do, and he has read it as a property of the man.

Reggie Tar sits on a wall on the harbour road at seventy-one years of age and says, out loud, "*Oh*, for God's sake," because he has done this before. He did it in 1950, over four hundred and eleven pages, and he described a husbandry practice and called it a species, and it took him until 1962 to withdraw it and it killed animals in nine countries in the meantime. He would fail a student for it. He has failed a student for it.

The honest position is that he does not know. He thinks it more likely than not that the man would have gone to John Clarke — four days working up to it and about forty seconds to do it, and it would have cost him something appalling and he would have done it — because that is what Pascual Pobil did with every difficult thing that had nobody else to go to, and the whole of the evidence Reggie has is from a period in which there was always somebody else to go to.

So the case is withdrawn. It is withdrawn unqualified, on a wall, at about a quarter past twelve on a Thursday, and the footnote is that the author's interest in the result should be noted.

---

What is left after he has withdrawn it is this, and it takes him the fourth minute.

It would have been terrible for him. Whatever he could or could not have said to John Clarke, he would have been seventy-nine, with a knee, in a house with eleven steps, holding an estate he could not read, on the ninth of every month, for however many years he was given; and the practice would have been gone, and the question would have gone with the man it was addressed to, and there would have been nothing left that required him daily and would have noticed if he stopped.

It has been terrible for Reggie. Five years of turning round. The apparatus that turned out to be two people's and does not run on one.

The arithmetic does not come out. He has spent four minutes on it and it does not come out, and it is not going to come out, and there is no version of it in which anybody is ahead.

And he would still rather it had been him, because he cannot bear the thought of Pascual having to go through this.

That is the whole of it, and it is not a finding. He goes over it twice to be certain and it is not a finding; there is nothing in it to establish; it is not the conclusion of the case he has just withdrawn and it does not stand or fall with it. It is a preference. He has held it since about 1957 and he has never had occasion to say so, and he is not sure he could have said so; and the reason it took him four minutes and a withdrawn case to get at it this morning is that he cannot pick a thing up unless somebody has built it a form, and nobody was ever going to build this one.

He puts his hat on.

There is an interest in it. There is an enormous interest in it; he is alive and sitting on a wall in the sun and the other man is not. He notes the interest, in the way one notes it at the foot of a page, and finds that it does not touch the sentence at all, because the sentence was never a claim about anything.

---

## The Saturday

He takes the boat out.

*Doris* is thirty-seven and the engine is the engine, and he goes out past the head to where the water changes, and cuts it, and sits on the thwart with his hands on his knees.

"I have one thing," he says, out loud, at seventy-one, to nobody, in the only place on this earth where he has ever been able to start one. "It is not four things and it is not an observation and I am not opening a folder.

"I built a case on Thursday morning that you could not have done this, and I built it out of the drawer and out of 1956, and it is a sample taken entirely from thirty-one years in which you had me to say it to, and I withdrew it before lunch. You would probably have gone to John. It would have cost you four days and you would have done it. I am glad to be wrong and I want that said first, because I would rather have you able to do a thing than have my case stand up.

"And it would have been terrible for you. That part holds. It has been terrible for me and I am not going to sit in a boat and do the arithmetic on which of those is worse, because there is no such arithmetic, and I have tried it, and it will not close.

"Here is the thing I came out to say. I would rather it was me. That it is terrible for for me and not for you. I have wanted that since about 1957 and I never once said it to you in thirty-one years, because it was not a want I could put a form round, and you never asked me for it, and I do not believe it occurred to either of us that it would come up." He is looking at the water. "I have got it. I am extremely glad I have got it and not you. That is not a conclusion and there is nothing underneath it and I am not going to be argued out of it by myself on a wall again."

He sits there for a while.

"*Te quiero*," he says, in the appalling accent, which he has never once improved.

Then he starts the engine and goes in, and is late, and Inés is furious with him.

## November 1990

Jean telephones on the Sunday, which she has done every Sunday since September 1985, and which neither of them has ever once referred to.

He gives her the roof for about four minutes, because some things do not change, and then he says:

"I want to put something to you, and I'd rather not be interrupted while I do it, and I'm aware that saying that to you has never worked."

"Go on, then."

So he says it. All of it, out of order, to a woman of sixty-seven on a bad line from Florida: John's note, and the path, and the wall by the chandler's, and the withdrawn case, and the preference, and the fact that he is glad, and the conclusion he has drawn about what being glad of it makes him.

It takes about six minutes. She does not interrupt him once, which is unprecedented, and he finds towards the end that he can hear her not interrupting, and has to stop twice.

"Right," says Jean. "Now shut up.

"That is the best thing you have ever thought about yourself and you have got it filed under the worst, and I'm going to tell you why, and you're going to let me, because I've been waiting five years to be allowed to say something to you that isn't about the roof.

"You sat on a wall for four minutes and you established that it would have been terrible for him and terrible for you and that you'd rather it was you it was terrible for. That's the whole content. And then — because you're the most ingenious man I have ever met and you use all of it on yourself — you found the one line in it with you in it and you wrote the finding off.

"You've done that for as long as I've known you. In 1954 you gave that boy a chart and a name and two hundred pounds and put him in the sea, and the first thing you did afterwards was tell me it was a design fault. In 1962 you published nine pages withdrawing your own interpretation and then told Hester it was the least you could do. You cannot take receipt of a decent thing. You can *do* them. God knows you can do them. You simply cannot sign for one."

"There's an interest in it —"

"Of *course* there's an interest in it. You'd rather it went the way round where the one who could take it better is the one who has to, and if you weren't glad about that I'd want you looked at." Jean's voice is doing something on the line. "And don't you dare put that down as me being kind to you. I have not been kind to you since about 1957 and I'm not starting on a Sunday. I'm telling you a true thing because it's true and because you can't see it from in there, which is the only reason anybody ever tells anybody anything. Kind would be agreeing with you to get you off the telephone."

He does not say anything.

"He'd have said it in about four words," she says.

"He would."

"Do you want to know what they'd have been?"

"No," says Reggie. "I know what they would have been."

---

There is a silence on the line of about eleven seconds, which is a long time at that price.

"I'm not going to agree with you," he says. "I want that on the record, because you'll go away thinking you've persuaded me, and you haven't, and if you had I shouldn't trust the result — a man doesn't revise a finding about himself on a Sunday because somebody has been truthful at him for six minutes."

"No. I'd expect nothing else from you and I don't much care." A pause. "Did it help?"

"Yes," he says. "A great deal."

"Well, then."

"I want to be exact about it, because it isn't the same thing as agreeing and I'd not have you think it was." He has his glasses in his hand and is looking at the wall. "It helped, and I don't know what the mechanism is, and I'm not going to pretend to. You may have that, and you may have it in writing if you want it, and I shan't be revising the other thing."

"Reggie."

"Mm."

"You're infuriating," says Jean Leslie.

And Reggie Tar stands in the hall of a house on the north side at seventy-one years of age with the receiver in his hand and finds that he has had to put his other hand flat on the wall, because that is the second person in his life who has ever said that sentence to him in that particular tone of voice, and he had not expected to hear it again, and he had not known until this moment that he had been waiting five years for somebody to.

"Yes," he says. "I have been told."

---

## December 1990

He writes to him at the end of the month, and the reason he can is that it takes six months and cannot be answered.

That is the whole of it, and he knows exactly what he is doing. There is nobody on this island he could put it to. He has put it to Jean, and she was truthful at him for six minutes and told him it was the best thing about him, and he sat still for it and felt better and did not move an inch, and he could hear himself not moving. What he wants is to say the thing into a place where nothing can come back at him for half a year, and there is precisely one such place on the earth, and it is up a channel below the Mariusa.

So he writes two pages and puts in the wall, and the four minutes, and the withdrawn case, and the preference, and the fact that he cannot make the preference into anything and does not intend to try again.

He does not ask a question. Thirty-six years, not one; he is not going to start at seventy-one.

The tin goes down to Nestor on the ninth and to Cedros on the Sunday.

---

## 4 July 1991

It comes back on the launch, in Nestor's hand, in oilskin.

He carries it up inside his shirt, on the left, and stops on the ninth step, and then goes up the last two, and opens it at the kitchen table. The chart is soft as a handkerchief now and gone to rags along two edges. *It worked.* *Yes. All of it.* *Tell P. my feet are fine.*

And under it, fresh, in the small cramped hand:

---

*Reggie,*

*I had it in March. I have had four months and I have written this four times.*

*I am not going to tell you what it is the best or the worst thing about you. You would do something to that inside a day and we would both have wasted six months. I am going to tell you two things I know and you may do the arithmetic yourself, since you are better at it.*

*The first is that I have it too.*

*I have had it since about 1961, which is when I stopped being able to pretend I did not know what the arrangement was. I would rather it was me. I have not once wavered on it in thirty years and I do not experience it as a virtue, or as a sacrifice, or as anything at all except the plain shape of the thing; it is simply where the preference sits and I could no more turn it round than change the colour of my eyes. It is not noble. It does not feel like anything. It is about as morally interesting as being right-handed.*

*I tell you this not to comfort you. I tell you because you have spent your whole life declining to believe a result until somebody else has got it independently, and you have now got it twice, in two men who could not be less alike, one of whom has been in the water for thirty-six years.*

*The second is about him.*

*He would have said the same thing and he would have said it faster than either of us. I do not put that forward as an opinion. I was nineteen months in his care and I was twenty-four and I could not do anything for myself, and I know precisely what it is like to be on the receiving end of a man who has decided he would rather it fell on him. You can feel it. It is not a thing that can be hidden, and he did not try to hide it, and he did it to everybody within reach for the whole of his life, and he did it to you for thirty-one years; and if you think you were the only person on that island who could read a face, you are wrong about that too.*

*He would have been horrified to learn you had it and had never said it. Not because he wanted it said. Because he would have worked out how long you had been carrying it on your own, and he would have minded about the years.*

*Now the part I would rather not write.*

*It will not fall that way for me. I am going first — I have been in the wrong water thirty-six years and we have known the figure since about 1971 — and so I get the half that is easier, which is the half I do not want, and he gets the other one, in open water, with nobody on this earth who knows he existed. I have had thirty years to make my peace with that. I have not managed it and I do not expect to.*

*So do not think I am writing to you from any height. You have got the thing I wanted. Not the grief; the arrangement. You were given the half you would have chosen and you are standing in it, and I am not going to be, and that is the whole of the difference between us. I am glad it went that way for you. I find I can be glad about it for you in a way I cannot be for myself, and I have stopped trying to work out why.*

*One more and then I will stop.*

*It has a name. You have been calling it an unexplained result since 1957 and it is not unexplained and it is not a result; you want it to be you because you love him. That is all it is. You have not been carrying an anomaly about for thirty-four years. You have been carrying the ordinary thing, which is that when you love somebody you would rather the bad half fell on you. Pascual had it about you. I have it about Monty. You have had it about me since 1954, which you have also never said, and which I have known since the twentieth of May.*

*C.*

*M. says your people use one word for eleven things and he does not know how you manage. I said we manage badly. He says among his own it is assumed on both sides from the beginning and so nobody has ever had occasion to name it, and that he does not know whether that helps. I said you would say it was data.*

---

The first thing he does is get the notebook.

He is not proud of it and he is not going to pretend otherwise. He reads the letter twice at the table and then gets up and fetches the notebook off the shelf and has actually written *C.C. — pref. held under reversed order; outcome-independent?* before he understands what he is doing, and he puts the pen down and sits back and looks at what he has written for about four seconds.

Then the objection arrives, at the window, out loud, in under four minutes: "That is not the same thing. He has a bond. The cases are not comparable."

And it is technically sound and he will believe it on Tuesday and at Christmas and in 1994, and it does no work whatever, and he knows within a second that it does no work.

Because the objection would matter if the question were what the preference *is*. The question is whether it answers to the outcome. And he held it blind for twenty-eight years, when it had not fallen either way and might have fallen either way; and Charles Cholmondeley has held it for thirty years knowing precisely which way it falls and that it falls the wrong way; and in neither man does it move. It is not responsive to the facts. It never was. A thing that does not answer to the outcome is not a strategy and is not an interest and cannot be written off at the foot of a page as one, and Reggie Tar has spent five years writing it off at the foot of a page.

And that leaves the name, and he has no objection to the name and never had one. He has had thirty-four years of not looking at it, which is a different thing, and which is worse.

He says, out loud, to the window: "Yes. All right."

And then, because he is who he is: "Thirty-four years."

---

The last of it arrives about twenty minutes later and it is the part that gets him off his feet.

Not the preference and not the word. The figure. Thirty-six years in the wrong water, known since 1971 — which means it has been on that chart in the tin every time it came back, and he has read every one of them, and he has never once counted the years, and he told himself for thirty-six years that this was a discipline.

It stopped being a discipline at about 1970.

He sits down on the kitchen step at seventy-one years of age and puts his hand over his mouth.

He does not do that arithmetic this evening. He knows it is there; he can feel the whole shape of it waiting at the edge of the table — the water, and who put him in it, and what a pen does to an animal, and what thirty-six years does to a man — and he will get to it in about eleven days and it will take him the best part of two years and it will be the last great reckoning of his life.

Tonight he puts the kettle on that he does not want, and stands at the window, and does not take back a single one of his objections.

---

He writes back that evening. It takes him the whole of it and he gets seven lines.

*C. —*

*You have taken my objection off me in one paragraph and I have not got another one. I have held it blind since 1957 and you have held it knowing since 1971, and it does not answer to the outcome in either of us, and I have no account of it except the one you gave me. I am not arguing with the word. I have never had an argument with the word. I have had thirty-four years of not looking at it.*

*I have read the figure. I have been reading that chart since 1962 and I have never once counted the years on it, and I told myself for thirty-six years that this was a discipline. It stopped being a discipline at about 1970. I am not going to say the rest of that in this tin. I am going to take some time over it and then I am going to send it, and you are not to answer it.*

*As to the arrangement: I have got it and you have not, and there is nothing I can do with that except be in it, which is what you have told me to do, and which I am going to find very difficult and am going to do.*

*You were right that I have never said it about you. Since the twentieth of May 1954, and I would rather it had been me, and I have had thirty-six years of knowing it was not going to be.*

*Tell M. that we do use the one word for eleven things and that we manage badly, and that it is data, and that I do not know what the mechanism is either.*

*Tell him there will be somebody who knows he existed. I will see to it, and I will put it somewhere with a number on it, and it will outlast the lot of us.*

*R.T.*
