# The Remainder

## November 1946

Two in the morning, and a corridor with brown linoleum and a smell of hot dust off the pipes, and two doors eleven feet apart with a light under each.

He is twenty-seven. He has been in this building fourteen months and has not slept properly in six weeks and is the happiest he has ever been.

He comes out of his room with a plate in his hand, going to the other door as he has gone to it two hundred times, and Spilsbury comes out of his at the same moment, and they stop, about three feet apart, in a corridor with nobody in it.

He has been looking at the man for fourteen months and in about another second and a half he is going to put his hand out.

And Spilsbury looks at him.

Perhaps three seconds. Not startled. Not angry. And not — this is the part Reggie will have thirty-one years to consider and will not get to the bottom of, because he will never once be given a reason to try — not *surprised*. The way a man looks at a column of figures he added up some while ago and is merely confirming.

Then he says, quite pleasantly: "Yes, all right."

And then: "Bring the plate through, would you, I want the eleven o'clock one."

And Reggie brings the plate through.

---

They work until five.

That is the thing he will tell nobody, because there is nobody, and because it is not tellable: that between the sentence and the room there are three hours of ordinary work, done well, at a bench, with the door open, on a plate that turns out to be the best of the series; and that Alec Spilsbury does not refer to it once in those three hours, and is not distracted, and is neither warmer nor cooler than he was at eleven o'clock the previous evening; and that this is not indifference, because at ten past five he puts the pen down and says, "Right," and looks up, and it is perfectly clear that the thing is still on the table and has been on the table the whole time and was never in any danger of falling off it.

He simply had the eleven o'clock plate to do first.

Reggie, at twenty-seven, is so entirely undone by this that he has to put both hands flat on the bench.

He will spend some years understanding it as steadiness. He is not wrong, exactly. It is steadiness. What it is not, and what there is nothing in his life at this date to tell him, is that a man can be perfectly steady about a thing because the thing has already been costed.

---

## 1947

Here is what he had expected, on the eleven feet of linoleum, in the second and a half before he put his hand out.

He had expected to be a nuisance. He had thought of himself as a complication being introduced into a very good working arrangement, and had assumed — in so far as he had permitted himself to assume anything — that the price of it would be a sort of permanent, grateful carefulness on his own part, and that he would pay that price gladly for as long as he was allowed to.

None of that happens.

What happens is that at the end of the first fortnight Spilsbury says, over the balance, in exactly the voice he uses about equipment, "You're wasting eleven minutes a night walking round the corridor. Have the connecting door taken off its hinges and we'll put a curtain up."

So the door comes off. The workmen do it on a Thursday morning and are told it is for the ventilation and believe it, because it is 1947 and the building is full of men who believe what they are told about doors.

And Reggie Tar goes about for three months in a condition he has never been in before and has no word for, and does the best work of his life to that date, and is asked twice by the deputy director whether he has been unwell, because he has lost most of a stone.

---

The other thing takes about a fortnight as well.

He had not known what to expect there either, and had been braced — he can admit this — for something apologetic, or clumsy, or grimly got through; because his private and entirely unexamined theory of Alec Spilsbury was that the man did not have a body so much as own one, the way he owned a microtome, and would consent to be dragged into a room for Reggie's sake and be decent about it afterwards.

That is not what happens either.

What happens is that Spilsbury approaches it the way he approaches a new technique, which is to say he finds out how it works in about a fortnight, and is by the end of that fortnight extremely good at it, and is thereafter neither more nor less interested in it than he is in anything else he has mastered. He is unhurried. He is attentive in a way that goes through Reggie like current. He notices everything — every response, every place, every one of the eleven or so things that Reggie himself did not know were true about him — and he notices them once and does not need to notice them again, and thereafter goes to them directly, without ceremony, the way a man goes to the third drawer down because that is where the thing is.

He says, once, in January, lying on his back in the dark with his hands behind his head: "I like this a great deal. I hadn't expected to and I do. I thought you'd want to know."

Reggie lies there beside him at twenty-seven years of age and is so happy that his eyes sting.

He will take that sentence out and look at it perhaps four hundred times over the next thirty years. He will get nothing further out of it, because there is nothing further in it. It is a complete and accurate statement, made once, by a man who does not repeat himself and has never in his life said a thing he did not mean; and it will be, when the accounts are finally drawn up in a room in Testaccio that in this world does not exist, the single warmest thing anybody ever says to him.

---

What Reggie does, over the same twelve months, is give him everything.

Not as a decision. He does not decide anything. He is not a man who does things by halves, and he has never in his life been given anywhere to put the whole of it, and so the whole of it goes — his hours, his evenings, his Sundays, his address, his Christmases, the entire contents of his head at four in the morning, the two subjects he had been keeping back for himself, the thing about his mother he had never told anybody, the ninety seconds of the war he does not talk about.

He hands it over in pieces across a year, at benches and tables and in the dark, and each piece is received exactly as it comes: attentively, without flinching, with a good question afterwards.

And filed.

That is the part he cannot see from inside it, and there is no one standing anywhere in his life to point at it. Everything he gives is taken and used and kept and never once mislaid. Nothing is refused. Nothing is asked for, either. And at no point in the year — at no point in the thirty-one years — does Alec Spilsbury look at what he is being handed and think: *there is more of this somewhere.*

Why would he. He has the man. He has him in the next room with the door off its hinges, and he has him at five in the morning, and he has all his hours and the best of his thinking, and the arrangement is stable and productive and gives every satisfaction.

There is no remainder. Where would it be kept.

---

## February 1950

There is an afternoon.

It is the third Thursday in February, and it runs from about half past one until about ten past six, and there are four people in the room and a schedule on the board, and what Reggie Tar does in it is not going to be set down here, and he is not going to set it down anywhere.

At ten past six he washes his hands for a long time.

At about seven he is sitting on the edge of a bench in the anteroom with his glasses in his hand, and he cannot get his breathing to do the ordinary thing, and he understands — with a completeness that will not be available to him again for twenty-seven years — that he has just found out something about himself which he is going to have to act on.

That he was not carried away. That he was not obeying anybody. That he had enjoyed it, steadily, from about two o'clock, in the way he enjoys a good number.

He gets up. He goes through the doorway where the door used to be.

---

"I want to say something and I would rather say it standing up."

Spilsbury looks up from the bench. "Go on."

And Reggie says it.

He does it in the flattest voice he has, with his hands behind his back and his eyes on the dark square of the window, and it takes about a quarter of an hour: what the afternoon was, and what he did in it, and what he was doing in the twenty months before it, and the three occasions he is able to identify on which a decent man would have stopped and he did not, and the count, which he gives, because a thing has a number and the number is the only part of it he can pronounce. He does not soften any of it and he does not explain any of it, because there is no explanation. There is only the fact that he was curious, and that being curious was enough.

"I am not frightened of the work," he says. "I want to be exact about that, because it would be a great deal more comfortable if it were the work. I am frightened of the man who does it, and I have been in a room with him since a quarter past one this afternoon, and I do not know how to get out of the room.

"I am going to resign. I have not written it and I am not going to write it tonight. I am telling you before I write it, because you are the only person I have got to tell, and because if I write it first I will not be able to hear what you say about it."

He turns round.

Spilsbury is sitting on the stool with the pen still in his hand and his head slightly on one side, and he has not moved at all, and he is not appalled, and he is not moved, and he is — Reggie can see this and does not know what to do with it — *interested*.

"Sit down," he says.

Reggie sits down.

---

"Right. Three things, and I'll do them in order, and you may have them all before you say anything.

"One. Nothing you've told me is news. I've had all of it since about the spring of 'forty-seven, in rather more detail than you've just given me, because I read the returns and you don't. You're better at it than anybody in this building and you have been for three years, and if you want the honest description of what you are I'd say you're a first-class instrument that has not got a stop on it, and I've known that since I read your Cambridge work in 1945. It's why I had you brought here. It's on a card."

"That is not —"

"I said you might have all three. Two." He puts the pen down. "You've come in here at seven in the evening and told me the most damaging thing about yourself that exists, out loud, standing up, without one word of excuse, on a day when you could perfectly well have gone home and had a bath. Do you know how many men in thirty years have done that in front of me? None. Not one. It is the single most useful thing I know about you and I want you to understand that I mean *useful* and not *admirable*, because I shan't insult you by pretending I'm the other sort of man.

"Three, and then I'm finished." He looks at him. "What exactly would resigning do?"

Reggie sits with his hands on his knees.

"I don't follow."

"You're a curious man who is very good at this and cannot stop, and you propose to remove yourself from a building in which the schedule is written down, reviewed, costed and signed for, and which is full of people who will notice if you take an interest in something." Spilsbury turns the pen round once on the bench. "And go where? Into a university department with a store cupboard and no oversight and eleven hundred pounds a year? Into industry, where nobody counts anything and the returns don't go anywhere? You'd do it inside a year and there'd be no board and no schedule and nobody who'd know what he was looking at.

"The building isn't what's wrong with you. The building is the only thing you've ever been in that has *edges*. If you want a stop, stay where there's a schedule and let somebody else sign it."

---

Reggie sits there for some while.

There is a flaw in it. He can feel the flaw the way one feels a tooth. It is somewhere in the region of the word *stop*, and it will be nine weeks before he can get a finger on it, and by then he will be past the point at which a finger on it would be of any use.

What he says, eventually, is: "That is the most useful thing anybody has ever said to me."

"Mm," says Spilsbury, and picks the pen up again. "Then don't write the letter, and go to bed, and be here at seven. I want your eye on the November series before the board sees it."

And Reggie goes to bed. And is there at seven.

---

The flaw, for the record, is this.

The schedule is not a stop. The schedule is a *shape*. It says what may be done and to how many and by when, and it is signed by men who have never been in the room, and what it does is take an appetite and give it a form — and Reggie Tar has never once in his life been beaten by a thing with a form.

The other flaw, which he will not get to for twenty-seven years, is smaller and worse. He walked through that doorway at seven o'clock in the evening and said out loud, to the one person he had, that he wanted something he ought not to have and was frightened of himself for wanting it.

And the man listened to all of it, and did not flinch, and gave him three numbered answers, and every one of the three was about what it would *do*.

Not one of them was about what it was for.

There was nobody in the room to ask him that. There is not going to be anybody, on this earth, in this life, at any point in the next thirty-one years.

---

## 1950 to 1953

He builds the tables in 1950. Tar (1950): *Mortality and morbidity in captive Nereopithecus aestuarius, with recommendations on husbandry.*

They are very good. They are the best thing anybody has done in the subject and they will be the standard reference for thirty years, and the recommendation in section four — that the species is solitary and territorial by nature, that co-housing produces the elevated mortality observed, and that single occupancy is therefore indicated — is read, adopted and implemented in every establishment in nine countries that holds one of these animals, because Tar's numbers are the numbers and nobody argues with them.

He does not know that it is the wrong way round. Nobody knows. The data are honest and the analysis is clean, and the fault is a thing that cannot be seen from inside a table of deaths: that the deaths he was counting were caused by the practice he was describing, and that the practice had already been general for twenty years before anybody thought to count.

There is no one in this world who is ever going to put two of them in a pen and watch what happens, and then go on watching for four months, and then sit down and write nine pages retracting the interpretation with a footnote on page nine.

That man exists. He is thirty-one years old in 1950 and he has his eye on the November series.

---

The other years go by in the way that good years go by, which is fast and without much to hang on.

He is thirty-four in 1953 and has been happy for six years. He would say so. He does say so, once, to a cousin at a funeral, and is embarrassed about it afterwards for a month.

Set down as figures, because he keeps figures:

Papers, eleven, of which four with Spilsbury and two that will outlive them both. Hours worked, uncounted, and the uncounting is itself the point. Nights spent elsewhere, none. People to whom either of them has explained anything, none; the building's view is that Dr Spilsbury has his young man in the next room and gets four years of work a year out of him, and the building thinks well of the arrangement in the way an institution thinks well of anything that produces returns, and nobody says anything, because it is 1953 and nobody says anything.

Occasions on which Reggie has asked for something: he goes through it at about this period, out of idle curiosity, and gets four. Once a microscope. Once a fortnight in Norway. Once, in 1948, that Spilsbury should come with him to his mother's funeral, and he did, and stood at the back in a good coat and was faultless and left at three because of the train.

And once, at about one in the morning in the summer of 1951, lying with his face in the side of the man's neck, when he said, "Say something about me," and Spilsbury said, without any hesitation at all, "You're the best pair of hands I've ever had in a room and the only man I've met in twenty years who does not need to be told a thing twice," and meant every word of it, and went to sleep.

Reggie lay awake for about an hour after that with a feeling in his chest he could not get a name to and eventually classified, for filing purposes, as gratitude.

---

There is one other item and it does not go under a heading.

He has begun, at some point in these years, to count things. Not results; he has always counted results. Stairs. The days between. The number of minutes a conversation runs. The eleven feet between the two doors, which he paces out sometimes, at night, for no reason, when the other room is dark because the other man is in Boston.

He notices himself doing it in about 1952 and thinks: *that is an odd habit to have got.*

There is nobody to say it out loud to. The only person he says things out loud to is at the other end of the eleven feet, and he cannot say this one there, and he does not know why, and the not knowing why is so uninteresting compared with everything else in his life that he lets it go.

---

## 20 to 24 May 1954

They go out to the Caribbean in May because the Navy has an animal and wants a physiologist and Spilsbury wants a look at the reference station, and Reggie goes because Reggie goes.

The William Martin Institute for Marine Studies is a cay off the north coast with eleven buildings and a generator and three concrete pens, and a verandah where the whole staff eats, and it is, Reggie thinks, coming up the path behind the Navy party at ten in the morning on the twentieth of May, about the best-run small station he has seen.

The old man in the chair at the top is Colonel Bevan, who is a political shield with a moustache. The administrator is his wife, who shakes hands and says three sentences, none of them wasted, and who has a way of looking at a man's face during the second sentence that Reggie will remember for twenty-three years without ever knowing why. There is a veterinary surgeon — Pobil, Spanish, about forty, built like a docker, filthy to the elbow, who is not on the jetty because there is an animal off its feed. There is a girl doing fish counts who is plainly the cleverest person on the establishment and is being paid like a typist. There is a boy of about twenty-two with pencil all over his face who draws.

And there is a boy of twenty-four at the middle pen who has been sitting at the rail with his feet in the water since a quarter to six.

---

The animal is Specimen Beta: adult male, nine feet eleven, scar on the tail, taken in the Pedernales channels in August 1953 and bought by this Institute for two hundred and eleven pounds.

He is five months into a decline. He should be dead by September. He is not dying, and his weights have come up four pounds in three weeks, and the entire reason is sitting on the rail with his trousers rolled up and no idea whatever that he is a reason.

Spilsbury sees it in about forty minutes. Reggie sees it in an hour and a half, which is the only occasion in nine years that Spilsbury has beaten him to something by that margin, and he is not pleased about it and says so at lunch and gets laughed at.

What they have, and what they both know they have by four o'clock on the first afternoon, is the only case in the literature of an arrested decline; a single attendant; twenty-six weeks of one boy's private notes in a hand like an ant fallen in the ink; and a station of eight people, none of whom have understood what is in front of them, because none of them have read enough to know it is impossible.

"Well," says Spilsbury, at the rail, at half past four. "There it is."

"Yes."

"Do you want him, or shall I have him?"

"Have him," Reggie says. "I'll want the animal."

---

He gets four days.

Four days of the best material of his life and he is, he will say afterwards and mean it, as happy in those four days as he has ever been anywhere. He is out at the pens at half past five. He goes over twenty-six weeks of notes with the boy at a table on the verandah for six hours on the Friday, taking him through it question by question, and the boy — Cholmondeley; he will be able to spell it correctly from the Friday onwards — goes from terrified to incandescent inside forty minutes and starts finishing Reggie's sentences by the afternoon, and Reggie has the rare and specific pleasure of watching a first-class mind find out in public that it is one.

At about four hours in, the boy puts his hand over the page and says, "I'm sorry. I know it isn't — I've written it all down wrong, it's not in any order —"

"It's in an order. It is the order you noticed things in, which is the only order there was and the only one worth having." Reggie takes the hand off the page and turns it back. "Now. The eleventh of February. You've put *quiet today*, and then four figures, and the four figures are not quiet at all. What did you mean by it?"

Charles looks at the page for a long moment.

"He wasn't looking at me," he says.

"Write that down. Exactly like that, in the margin, and initial it." Reggie hands him the pencil. "That's the observation. *Quiet* is not an observation, it's how you felt about him. You'll do that about eleven hundred more times before you're thirty and the whole job is catching yourself at it."

Nobody has ever quoted this boy's numbers back to him before. Reggie quotes them at supper, twice, in front of the Colonel, without particularly noticing that he is doing it, and the boy goes scarlet to the ears and cannot eat.

On the Saturday morning Reggie writes out the design.

It takes him about two hours and it is, he thinks, quite the best thing he has ever put on paper. Baseline on the present animal with the present attendant. A second animal, solitary, for contrast. Exposure at intervals. Attendant scored blind on eleven items — no, that will not run, the man can see the pen, and there is no way to blind him, and so one writes the confound into the discussion honestly and one proceeds anyway, because a confounded result is not nothing and the alternative is no result at all.

He takes it through at noon.

Spilsbury reads it standing up, all four pages, and does not say anything for about ninety seconds, which is a long time for him.

"You've done my six weeks in two hours," he says. "Whose idea was the separation arm?"

"Mine."

"Yes. I hadn't got there." He puts the pages down on the table. "That's the paper. That's the next ten years. You know that."

"I know. And there is only one boy."

"No," says Spilsbury. "There isn't. Right — then I'll get the boy, and you get the animal, and we shall want the Navy for the transport."

---

The row happens at half past six on the Sunday evening, at the middle pen, in front of six people.

Reggie has been at the rail since four, with the day book, taking the animal's surfacing intervals in the boy's absence, which requires the boy's absence, which he arranged with a perfectly ordinary instruction at half past three on the Saturday afternoon. Twenty-seven hours. It is the first control anybody has ever run on this and it is not going well; the animal has gone to the bottom and stayed there and has not fed since morning.

Pobil comes round the wall of the store at twenty past six and looks into the pen for about four seconds.

Then he says: "What have you done?"

"I beg your pardon?"

"*What have you done to my animal?*"

He is very loud. He is, Reggie notes with a certain detachment, the loudest thing on this island by some margin, and within about ninety seconds there are six people on the concrete, including Mrs Bevan and Spilsbury, who comes round the store with his hands in his pockets and does not say one word from beginning to end, and the whole of it is audible from the water.

Reggie explains the control. He explains it calmly and slowly, twice, because the man is a veterinary surgeon and cannot be expected to hold the design in his head.

What comes back is a torrent in two languages of which perhaps a third is comprehensible, and the comprehensible third contains the following: that the animal has not fed for eleven hours; that he was fourteen pounds up on the month and will be four down by Tuesday; that a man does not run a deprivation on a patient in a decline without his medical officer's signature; that Dr Pobil has not been asked for a signature, has not been asked for an opinion, and has not been asked so much as the time of day; and that if that boy is not back on the rail before dark he will be fetched, by Pobil, personally, out of his bed.

"He's back on the rail at eight," Reggie says. "I've got what I need."

"You've got —" Pobil stops. He puts both hands on the rail. His face is the most legible thing Reggie has ever seen on a human head; it is doing everything, all of it, at once, with no attempt whatever at concealment, and what it settles on is a kind of flat astonishment. "You've *got what you need*."

"Yes."

"From an animal that stopped eating. He hasn't had a fish since six this morning. He was fourteen pounds up on the month. And the one man he will feed for has been sitting up at the huts since half past three yesterday, because you sent him there. Is that the arithmetic? Is that what we are calling it?"

"That's what the control is *for*," Reggie says, and hears himself say it, and is aware that it is entirely correct, and is aware of something else as well, for about a second and a half, and does not get hold of it.

Pobil looks at him across the rail for perhaps four seconds. Then he turns round and goes off up the path to fetch the boy, shouting for him from thirty yards, and the six people on the concrete disperse in the way people do when something has happened that they would all prefer not to have been present at.

Mrs Bevan does not disperse. She stands at the corner of the store with her hands folded and looks at Reggie Tar for rather longer than is comfortable, and then she says, "Good evening, Dr Tar," and goes up the path after him.

---

They get the boy back on the rail at eight and the animal takes four fish at twenty past.

And at about half past eight, in the dark, with the lamp on the rail and Reggie forty feet off at the corner of the store making a note of the time, Pobil comes down and stands beside the boy and says something to him too low to carry, and the boy shakes his head, and says something back, and Pobil puts his hand flat on the side of the boy's face and leaves it there for about four seconds.

Reggie watches it from forty feet.

And something arrives in him for which he has, at thirty-five years of age, no category, no use, and nowhere whatever to put it — it is not envy, he checks, he does check, and it is not disapproval — and it sits in his chest for perhaps eight seconds and then it goes, because he has the surfacing intervals to write up, and because the only other person on this island he ever tells anything to is up at the guest hut with four pages of a design on the table in front of him, and this is not the sort of thing one carries up a path.

He writes up the surfacing intervals.

He will not think about that hand again for twenty-three years.

---

Spilsbury gets the boy on the Monday morning, at breakfast, in about eleven minutes, in front of everybody, which is not carelessness; it is done in front of everybody so that it cannot afterwards be described as anything other than what it was.

A post. A proper salary, which is eleven times a postgraduate's allowance. His name on the paper, second, after Spilsbury's and before Tar's, which is more than he would get anywhere else on earth and is also exactly one place lower than he has earned. Passage in September. The animal to follow under Navy transport, because the animal is the boy's animal and Dr Spilsbury has no intention of separating a subject from the only man it will feed for.

Charles Cholmondeley says yes before the end of the sentence.

He says it going crimson, and then he says thank you four times, and then he goes out of the room and is seen, by John Clarke, sitting on the step of the lecture hut with his hands over his face, and Clarke reports later that he was laughing.

Mrs Bevan asks for a word with Spilsbury at ten. It takes twenty minutes and the door is shut and nobody hears any of it, and afterwards Spilsbury comes down the path and says to Reggie, with real appreciation: "That is the most formidable woman I have met in twenty years and she has got absolutely nothing. He's of age, he's on an allowance, and there's no post here to keep him in. She knew it before she sat down. She sat down anyway."

"What did she want?"

"She wanted my word about something and wouldn't say what, which is the cleverest thing anyone's tried on me in a decade, because I couldn't give it and now I shall think about it for a year." He is delighted. "And then she asked me whether I had read Andersen."

"Andersen."

"Hans Andersen." Spilsbury looks up the path at the bungalow for a moment. "No, I don't know either. Get the manifest done, we sail at eleven."

---

## 24 May 1954

Pascual doesn't sleep on the Sunday night, on account of the animal, and comes down at half past five on the Monday to find him up in the top half of the water and hungry, which is the only thing on this island Pascual currently cares about.

He feeds him himself. He talks to him the whole time in the voice he uses for frightened animals and for Charles, and gets fourteen pounds into him in about twenty minutes, which is nine more than he'd hoped for, and sits back on his heels on the concrete and says, "*Bueno*. All right. All right, then."

He goes up at seven and finds out at breakfast that they've taken the boy.

That takes the rest of the morning. That, and standing in the sick bay afterwards with his hands flat on the bench for about four minutes, and then going and finding Charles at the lecture hut and shaking his hand and telling him it's the best thing that's happened to him and that he's to write, and meaning about half of it, and then going down to the jetty at eleven with everybody else because you don't not go.

The Navy party goes out on the launch at five past.

Pascual stands at the back with his arms folded and watches it go round the head and thinks that he'll never in his life be able to explain to anybody, including Mrs Bevan, what it is he's afraid of, because there isn't a single fact in it. A man offered a boy a job. It's a good job. The boy wanted it.

Jean comes and stands next to him.

"Well," she says.

"*Sí*."

"He'll be all right. He's clever, Spilsbury is the best in the world, he'll have a laboratory." Jean is looking at the water and her arms are folded the same way. "That is what I have been saying all morning. I've said it eleven times and it gets worse every time."

"*Ay.* Write to him," Pascual says. "You. And John, and Mrs Bevan, and me. Every month, all four of us, so that somebody in that building has to carry four letters down a corridor and put them in his hand."

"I will."

"Who was the little one?" she says. "With the glasses. The one who stopped Beta's feed."

Pascual thinks about it.

He can see the man perfectly well: five foot six, neat, glasses, a smirk that looked past everybody, and a mind that had gone over twenty-six weeks of a boy's notes in six hours on Friday and found four things in them that Pascual had missed. Not stupid. Not cruel, either, which is the part he can't put down: the man had explained it twice, patiently, to a country vet, and had been entirely willing to be reasonable about the time, and had looked at Pascual across the rail while an animal lay on the bottom of a pen and had honestly not understood what the question was.

"Tar," he says. "Something Tar."

"That's it."

"He'll be all right," says Pascual. "The boy. He's clever and he'll have a laboratory."

"Yes," says Jean.

They stand there for another minute. Then Pascual goes back up to the pens, because there's an animal there who took four fish last night and has nobody in this world left to sit on his rail; and Pascual Pobil has four months of his company before a crate comes for him in September, and doesn't know that yet either, and does the only thing there is, which is to roll his trousers and sit down on the step and put his hand in the water.

He never thinks about the little one again.

---

## 1955 to 1957

The separation series runs from the fourteenth of March 1955 to the ninth of November 1956, and it is the most important work either of them will ever do, and it is cited eleven hundred times before 1980.

Reggie does the design and the arithmetic. Spilsbury does the man. Cholmondeley does what Cholmondeley does, which is sit at the edge of a tank for as many hours a day as he is permitted and, by the end, a good many that he is not.

The findings, in order:

That the effect is real, and large, and is not an artefact of the attendant's expectation, which they establish in the second year by a method Reggie is proud of and which is not described here.

That the magnitude of the effect varies inversely, and steeply, with the welfare of the animal.

That the practical consequence — this is the sentence in the discussion, and Reggie writes it, and it is the best-turned sentence in the paper — is that the response is most readily elicited under conditions of sustained solitary confinement.

Which the whole field is already using, because of Tar (1950).

---

He keeps the day book himself for the last six months, because the boy's hand has become difficult to read.

Weights are down. Sleep is bad; Cholmondeley is up at four most mornings and there is no rule against it. He stops eating in company in about June of 1956 and takes his meals in the tank house, and when Reggie raises it — and he does raise it; he raises it twice, once in July and once in September, and the second time he stands in a doorway and says out loud, "I want this stopped," which is, although he will not be able to put it that way for twenty-one years, the only time in his life he has ever done such a thing —

"Say the rest of it," says Spilsbury.

"There is no rest of it. He is nine pounds down since Easter, he does not eat where anybody can see him, and he is on that gantry at four in the morning. I want it stopped."

"On what grounds?"

"I haven't got grounds. That's why I'm standing in a doorway instead of writing it down."

Spilsbury looks up and says: "So do I. It'll be stopped in eleven weeks when the series ends, and stopping it now costs us the whole of the recovery arm, and the recovery arm's the half that will keep animals alive. What do you propose we do with the eleven weeks?"

And Reggie, who has never once been beaten by a thing with a form, has nothing to say to that, because there is nothing to say to it; and he goes back and does the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is correct, and the recovery arm does keep animals alive, some hundreds of them, over the following twenty years.

Nobody asks him what he wanted it stopped *for*.

He did not know. That is the honest position and he has had twenty-one years to look at it. He wanted it stopped the way one wants a window shut. If anybody on this earth had said *what is it for, Reggie* he would have had to stand in that doorway and find out, and it would have taken him about four minutes, and he would have got there.

There was no such person in the building. There was not, at that date, any such person in his life, and there never had been, and the vacancy had been in place since November of 1946 and he had not noticed it, because the whole point of that kind of vacancy is that nothing whatever appears to be missing.

---

Charles Cholmondeley is taken out of the tank at a quarter past four in the morning on the eighteenth of August 1956 by a night porter who has been told to look in every hour, and does not speak for nine days, and is sent home to his sisters in Shropshire in October.

The animal dies in February 1957 of nothing that can be put on a certificate.

Reggie writes to Shropshire twice. The first letter is answered by a Miss Victoria Cholmondeley in four lines, courteously, thanking him for his interest and stating that her brother is not at present receiving correspondence. The second is not answered at all.

He does not write a third. He tells himself at the time that it would be an intrusion, and he is thirty-seven and it is a perfectly good reason, and he is aware at about the level of the pit of the stomach that it is not the reason.

Spilsbury's view, given once, at a table, without heat: "He had eighteen good months and better material than anybody his age in the world, and then he went past what he could take and I did not see it in time and I ought to have. I've put that in writing to the board. It's the third time in thirty years I've missed a man going, and I mind it a good deal, and there's nothing whatever to be done about it now except not miss the fourth."

That is the whole of it and every word of it is true, and Reggie sits there and thinks — for about two seconds, and then puts away, with the speed of long practice, and does not look at again — *you have said three sentences about that boy, and one of them was about the board, and not one of them has his name in it.*

---

## March 1959

He is offered Bergen in the March of 1959. A chair, a laboratory of his own, four people and a budget line, and it is precisely the thing a man of forty does next, and there is not a man in the field who would not have taken it.

He does not tell Spilsbury for nine days, which is the longest he has ever gone about anything.

When he does tell him it is at breakfast, badly, in about four words, and Spilsbury reads the letter twice with the toast in his hand and puts it down and says: "No. I'd rather you didn't."

And that is the whole of it.

No argument. No reasons — Spilsbury does not produce reasons for things he wants; he produces them for things he has decided against. He does not say that it would be a waste, or that Bergen is a bad department, or that Reggie would be unhappy there. He does not say *stay*. He does not put a hand out. At eight o'clock he goes through to the bench and gets on.

Reggie writes to Bergen that afternoon declining with thanks and is so happy that he cannot sit down.

He will take those five words out and look at them for the next eighteen years. It is the nearest thing to a claim Alec Spilsbury has ever made on him; he has it in writing nowhere and does not need it in writing, and it is, he thinks at various points over the following eighteen years, the most valuable thing he owns.

What it is, is a man declining to release an option.

Spilsbury has never in his life let an option go by unrecorded and has never in his life exercised one a day earlier than he had to, and a first-class physiologist in the next room with the door off its hinges is an option held cheaply and permanently, and there is no reason whatever to give it up in March of 1959, and so he does not. He is not jealous; he has never been jealous of anything. He is not afraid of losing him, because it has not occurred to him that he could. He would rather things went on as they are, and he says so, once, accurately, in five words, and then has his toast.

They take the house at Cheam in the autumn.

---

## June 1965

It is a Thursday in June, and they are in the garden of the house at Cheam, which they have had since 1959 and which is in Spilsbury's name because there was no sensible way to do it otherwise and neither of them has ever mentioned it.

Reggie is forty-six. He has been at this nineteen years. He is Deputy Director and will not be Director, which does not trouble him, because the Directorship is committees; he has a laboratory and a schedule and eleven people and the best hands in England, and last month he had four days of the purest pleasure he can remember over a set of figures that nobody else on earth would have looked at twice.

Spilsbury is fifty-nine and has just come back from Massachusetts and is going through the post with a paperknife.

"There's a thing I've been meaning to say to you for about a year," he says, "and I keep not saying it because it sounds like an epitaph."

"Say it."

"You've never once done anything I didn't expect."

Reggie looks up.

"Nineteen years," Spilsbury says. He is still opening letters. "I've watched a great many men and I've been wrong about most of them at some point — I was wrong about Halliwell twice and wrong about myself in 'thirty-eight. I have never once been wrong about you. Not in the corridor, not in 'fifty, not over the boy. I knew what you were the week you came and you have been exactly that for nineteen years, and there's nothing else in my life I could say that about." He puts a letter down and picks up the next one. "It's the highest thing I've got to say about anybody and I thought you should have it."

---

Reggie sits in a garden chair in Cheam at half past six in the evening in June with a glass in his hand.

It goes into him a long way down.

He does not know why. That is the thing he would like recorded, if there were anywhere to record it: that a man he has loved for nineteen years has just paid him the largest compliment in his possession, sincerely, unprompted, at no advantage to himself, and it has gone in like something coming off a wire, and it is *wrong*, and he cannot find one single reason why it should be.

He tries. He sits there and he does the thing he is best at, which is to take an unsatisfactory result apart in his own head, and he gets nowhere at all. Every line of it holds. It is true. It is meant. It is kind, or as near as that man comes, and it is nearer than he comes for anybody else alive.

The nearest he can get, after about four minutes, and it is not near enough to be any use, is: *I have been in this house for six years and he has never once had to wonder where I was.*

And that is a good thing. That is what he has spent nineteen years being.

"Thank you," he says. "That's — thank you. I don't think anybody's said anything like that to me."

"Nobody's been in a position to."

"No. I don't suppose they have."

"Mm," says Spilsbury, and slits the next envelope. "Bergen wants us in September and I've said no. You don't mind."

It is not a question, and Reggie does not mind, and says so.

---

That night, before the kitchen, there is the other thing, because on a Thursday there usually is; Spilsbury is fifty-nine and in the habit, and the habit is a good one.

It is exactly what it was in 1947.

That is not a complaint and Reggie would knock a man down for suggesting it was. It is unhurried, and it is attentive, and it goes to the third drawer down without ceremony; and there is a moment near the end where Spilsbury has a hand flat at the back of his neck and says his name, once, in a perfectly level voice, the way a man reads a figure back to confirm it — and Reggie, at forty-six, with his face in the side of the man's throat, is as entirely undone by it as he was at twenty-seven.

Eighteen years, and nothing has come loose in it, and nothing has been added to it. It is a technique, mastered early, practised since, and it has never once gone wrong and it has never once gone anywhere.

Afterwards Spilsbury is asleep in about four minutes, which he has done every night since 1947.

---

He does not sleep that night.

He gets up at about two and goes down and stands in the kitchen in the dark and finds himself counting the tiles, which is a thing he apparently still does at forty-six.

There is a sentence somewhere in the middle of him and it will not come up. He can feel the shape of it. It has the word *more* in it. He stands in a cold kitchen for about twenty minutes trying to get it to surface, and it does not surface, and he is at the bench at seven.

It would have taken four minutes and somebody asking.

---

## November 1977

The telegram comes at twenty past nine on Tuesday the fifteenth.

*DR SPILSBURY DIED EARLY MONDAY AT HIS DESK. HEART. ALDRIDGE.*

He reads it twice.

Then he folds it and puts it in the drawer, and picks up the third-quarter returns, and reads them, all of them, carefully, to the end, with a pencil.

He is fifty-eight. He goes back to the house at Cheam at the usual time. There is nobody in it and there has been nobody in it since March, when the Massachusetts year began, and so it is not, at first, any different; and he eats what there is, and washes it up, and sits down in the chair with the Bergen returns, and at about half past nine he discovers that he has read the same page four times.

He telephones nobody, because there is nobody to telephone.

That is not self-pity and he will not have it classed as such. It is an inventory, and he does it properly, standing in his own hall at twenty to ten with the receiver in his hand: his mother is dead in 1948. His cousin writes at Christmas and has four children and would come, and he cannot conceive of the sentence with which one would ask her. There are eleven people in the department, of whom one, Fairbrother, would sit up all night with him and be extremely kind, and would then know, and would be extremely kind about that too, for the rest of their working lives. There is Aldridge in Massachusetts, who is the only other person on this earth who knows, and who has known since 1951, and to whom he has spoken perhaps eleven hundred times and never once about anything.

He puts the receiver down.

---

Aldridge comes on the twenty-first.

She is sixty, or nearly. She comes to the house at Cheam with a leather case and two shoe boxes tied with string, and will not take her coat off, and will not sit down, and puts the boxes on the table.

"He didn't leave word about these," she says. "He left word about the papers and the Institute and his sister. Nothing about the boxes. I've brought them because I couldn't think what else to do with them, and because I thought — " She stops. "I thought you'd want them."

"Thank you."

"There was nothing in his wallet," she says, "except the licence and a photograph of his mother. I looked, because they made me empty it at the hospital, and I am telling you because I would rather you heard it from me than went on wondering about it for twenty years."

Reggie stands in his own hall and looks at her.

"That was kind of you," he says. "I'd have wondered about it. You have saved me twenty years of wondering."

"It was not kind. It was the only useful thing I have got." Aldridge picks up her case. "He was the best man I ever worked for and I do not know one single thing about him, and I have been at the next desk for twenty-six years, and neither do you. I have thought that for a long time and I am sixty and I am going to say it once." Her mouth is not steady. "Good night, Dr Tar."

---

He opens the boxes that night.

There are one thousand one hundred and six cards. He counts them, because he counts everything.

He reads them over nine nights, in order, from a card dated 1934 about a man at Cambridge who falsified a set of sodium figures to a card dated the eleventh of November 1977 about a calf at Naples. They are filed by letter. There are ninety-one under B and nineteen under M, *things decided against*.

And he knows the system, because it was explained to him at a bench in 1947 by a man with a shoe box and a pencil: that a card with a full stop on it is finished, and a card with a dash on the end of it is not, and that the dash means he has not got to the bottom of it and intends to.

There are eighty-one cards in the two boxes with a dash on the end.

*BEVAN, Mrs — wanted my word and would not say what about. Asked whether I had read Andersen. —* That is dated May 1954, and it has been gone back to, by the ink, four separate times, the last of them in 1971.

And, three cards further on: *POBIL, vet — asked T. at the rail what the control was for. T. gave him the correct answer. —*

Reggie sits with that one for a while and cannot for the life of him place the name for about four seconds, and then does: a large man in a temper at a rail on a Sunday evening in 1954, on an island he has not thought about in ten years.

And then, arriving behind it, unasked, whole, after twenty-three years — the same man in the dark an hour later, standing beside a boy at the same rail with his hand flat on the side of the boy's face, and leaving it there.

Reggie sits at a table in Cheam at half past one in the morning, with a card in his hand about a veterinary surgeon he met once, and cannot get his breath, and has not the faintest idea why.

---

Under R there are four.

*R.T. — 27 — cannot leave a result alone. Useful.* November 1946.

*R.T. — will give the whole of it if you take any of it. Cheap at the price.* December 1946.

*R.T. — told me the February business himself, standing up, unprompted. Keep.* February 1950.

*R.T. — nineteen years, no surprises. Best appointment I ever made.* June 1965.

Four cards in thirty-one years.

Not one of them has a dash on the end.

---

He sits at the table in Cheam at about two in the morning with the four cards in front of him in a row.

They are all true. That is what he will find, going over them, that night and on other nights: there is not a word in any of them that is wrong, or unkind, or stinted. They are, if anything, generous. The man wrote down what Reggie Tar was in 1946, correctly, in eleven words, and was never once obliged to revise it.

And on the twelfth of March 1962 he wrote nine cards about a technician at Naples whom he had met twice, and went back to them in 1966, and again in 1974.

And on the fourth of December 1958 he made a card that says *AGUILAR — why does a cost decide it? —* about a man whose face he saw once at a rail, and he had still not got to the bottom of it in November of 1977, and had gone back to it, by the ink, three separate times.

---

*He never wondered about me.*

That is the sentence. It comes up at about ten past two in the morning on the fifth night, thirty-one years late, in a kitchen in Cheam, and it comes up whole.

He had it in a garden chair in June of 1965 and could not get it to the surface. He had it in an anteroom in 1950 with his glasses in his hand. He had it, if he is honest, at ten past five on a morning in November of 1946, standing with both hands flat on a bench while a man capped his pen and said *right*.

He was seen once, in a corridor, accurately, in three seconds, and he was never looked at again. Not from cruelty; there was no cruelty in it anywhere. From *sufficiency*. The man had a complete and correct account of Reginald Tar by December of 1946, and it never once failed, and so he never once had occasion to go back to it — and everything Reggie handed over for thirty-one years afterwards went into a column that had already been added up.

There was more of him. There was a great deal more of him. It was there the whole time, in the next room, eleven feet away, with the door off its hinges.

Nobody ever came looking for it, and so it was never spent, and a thing that is never spent does not keep. It just goes on sitting there, at the bottom, unused, for thirty-one years, until a man finds it at two o'clock in the morning at fifty-eight years of age and discovers that he cannot now give it to anybody, because he has never once in his life practised.

---

He goes to the desk on the Tuesday.

The third-quarter returns for the Caribbean holdings have come in. He reads them, the way he has read every return that has come to this building for twenty-seven years, and on the sheet from a station off the north coast — eleven buildings, three pens, a generator, a woman running the whole thing in her husband's name — there is a line about a female, solitary, off her feed for two days after a storm and then coming back on.

And Reggie reads it, and puts a small pencil mark beside it, and thinks, *he will have seen that*, and reaches —

He reaches. That is the only word for it. Without deciding to, the way a man reaches for a rail on a stair in the dark, some part of him goes out towards Massachusetts, to the one other person on the earth who would have read that line the week it came in and caught the same thing and had an opinion about it that Reggie would have minded.

And there is nothing there.

He sits with the pencil against the paper.

*Call*, he thinks. *No answer.*

---

There is a second thing, and it arrives about four minutes later, and it is the one he is going to have for the rest of his life.

It is that there is nobody to tell about the first thing.

He has just discovered, at fifty-eight, sitting at a desk, the precise shape of the hole in the middle of his own life, in about ninety seconds, complete, with the dates right. It is the most important result he has ever had. He could put it on paper in four hundred words.

And he goes down the list — Fairbrother, the cousin, Aldridge, the eleven people in the department, the Director, the man at the Ministry he has lunched with for twenty years — and there is not one name on it that he could say it to.

He sits at the desk for a long time.

Then he does the only thing available, which is what he has done every morning since he was twenty-six years old, and picks up the next return, and reads it, all of it, carefully, to the end, with a pencil.

---

## December 1977

He does one more thing and then he does not do anything else.

On the eleventh of December he takes a card out of the second box, from the front, under B, and a pencil, and sits down at the table in the house at Cheam.

He writes: *A.S. — thirty-one years — *

And stops.

He looks at it for some while. He is a man who has written perhaps a hundred thousand lines in thirty-five years and never once been unable to finish one.

Then he puts the pencil down, and leaves the dash where it is, and puts the card back in the box, at the front, under B, where he will be able to find it; and he is aware, putting it back, that he has just done the only thing either of them ever did in thirty-one years that the other one would not have been able to account for.

There is nobody to show it to.

He turns the lamp off and goes up.

---

## November 1982

He is sixty-three and he is Director, which is committees. He does the committees on Tuesdays and Thursdays and writes off the rest.

What he has instead is the tables.

He began it in the February of 1978 for no better reason than that the evenings had to be filled with something, and he began it at the only place a man begins who cannot leave a result alone, which is the thing he has always privately known was the weakest join in his own best work: that the deaths in section four were counted in establishments which were already housing the animals singly, and that he had therefore described a practice and called it a species. He wrote that sentence out on the fourth of February 1978 and looked at it and understood that he had known it, at some depth, since about 1952.

It takes four and a half years, because there is no experiment available to a man in England with no animals; there is only correspondence, and the correspondence is with forty-one establishments in nine countries, and a great deal of it is with people who are using his own recommendation as an authority and do not wish to be told. He writes about two thousand letters. He learns, at sixty-one, to be charming in a letter, which he does badly, and to ask for a favour, which he does worse; and one of the better consequences of the whole business is that by 1981 he has eleven correspondents he would describe as friends if anybody put the question to him, which nobody does.

The paper goes to the journal in the September of 1982. It is nine pages. The interpretation in section four of Tar (1950) is withdrawn in the second paragraph and the withdrawal is unqualified, and the footnote on page nine says that the author regrets that thirty-two years of husbandry practice in nine countries has rested on it.

He reads the proof through twice at the kitchen table at Cheam and there is nobody to show it to, and he has stopped expecting there to be, which is not the same as being reconciled to it and is a great deal better than 1978.

---

Three other things have changed and he is aware of all three.

He talks out loud in the house. He began it by accident in about the spring of 1978 and has kept it, and he does it deliberately now, at the bench and in the kitchen and in the car, because he found out at fifty-eight that the saying was never for the stopping, and has decided at sixty-three to stop pretending he requires an audience.

He has taken to asking Fairbrother what things are for. He does it clumsily and about four times a year, and Fairbrother, who is forty-four and adores him and has no idea whatever what he is being handed, answers the question as though it were an ordinary one. Twice it has worked. Once it worked in about four minutes.

And on the ninth of March 1979 he wrote a third letter to Shropshire.

There was no reason to think it would be answered and it was not answered for eleven weeks, and then it was, in a different hand from the first two — a Miss Lettice Cholmondeley, who is the younger sister, and who wrote four pages, and who said among a great many other things that her brother had kept every offprint of the 1955 and 1957 papers in a drawer in his room and had taken them out, and that if Dr Tar were ever in the county he might write first.

He has been twice. It is not a success. Charles Cholmondeley is fifty-two and does not say very much and will not sit with his back to a window, and the second visit was worse than the first, and Reggie has arranged to go again in the spring.

He does not tell himself it is restitution, because it is not; the arithmetic on that will not come out and he has done it. He goes because a man is alive in a house in Shropshire who once finished his sentences on a verandah in 1954, and because nobody else who was in that room is going to go, and because on a Tuesday morning in November of 1977 he found out exactly what it costs to be a thing nobody ever came looking for.
