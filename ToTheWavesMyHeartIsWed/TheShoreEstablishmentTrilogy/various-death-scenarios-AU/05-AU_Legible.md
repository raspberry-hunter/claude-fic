# Legible

## February 1986

He sees it on a Tuesday at breakfast and he doesn't say anything for eleven days.

That is the worst eleven days of his life to that date and he will revise the ranking in September, and what he does with them is what a man of seventy-three with a bad knee and a medical degree from 1938 does, which is to watch, and count, and write nothing down, and be perfectly ordinary about the marmalade.

The colour. The way he sits down now, which is in two movements. Nine pounds since Christmas, and Pascual knows it is nine and not eight because he has been standing at that stove for six years watching the man not finish things and has a number in his head for every week of it.

He doesn't put a name to it in this house. He hasn't put a name to it out loud anywhere, then or since, and he isn't going to; he has spent forty-eight years telling owners the name of the thing and he knows exactly what saying it does to a room.

On the twelfth day he says, over the feed book, without looking up:

"You're going to Port of Spain on Thursday and I'm coming with you."

"I've got the Bergen proofs —"

"*Ay.* Thursday."

Reggie looks at him across the table for about four seconds.

Then he says, "Yes, all right," in a tone Pascual has heard him use perhaps twice in thirty-two years, and puts the proofs down, and doesn't argue, and it's the not arguing that tells Pascual that the man has known since January and has been letting him get there on his own.

---

Colón in Port of Spain on the Thursday, and a surgeon on the Monday, and then the other one at Mount Hope on the eleventh of March who is the best in the islands and takes twenty minutes to say what the first two said in an hour.

Six months. Possibly nine. Nothing to be done that's worth what doing it would cost, and the man says so plainly, to both of them, in the same room, because Reggie asked for it that way at the start and Pascual hadn't thought to.

They come back on the Wednesday launch. Nestor is at the bottom of the steps with the mail and knows before either of them has said a word, because Pascual's face has never in its life kept anything for longer than four seconds, and Nestor Aguilar has been reading it since 1954.

"*Doctor*," he says, and takes the case, and goes up all eleven steps with it, which he has not done since 1979.

---

## March to August 1986

Here's what Reggie Tar does with six months.

He doesn't stop working, because there was never the smallest chance of that and Pascual would have had him certified if he had tried. He finishes the Bergen proofs on the twenty-first of March. He writes about four hundred letters, in his own hand, on the day, the way he has since 1962.

And then, from about the middle of April, he starts a folder.

It isn't the review folder. It's a new one, and then a second one, and by June it is four boxes on the end of the kitchen table where the lamp goes, and it has an index, and the index has an index, and Pascual comes in from the pens at half past five one afternoon and finds a man of sixty-seven who can't get up a flight of stairs in one movement sitting at that table numbering pages.

"What is that?"

"It's for you."

"*Ay, Dios mío.*"

"Don't," says Reggie, without heat. "It's four hours a day and it is the only four hours in the day when I am entirely all right, and if you take it off me I shall simply do it at two in the morning and lie about it."

---

So Pascual lets him, for eleven weeks, and hates every hour of it, and the hating gets worse in July when the four hours become six.

The row is on the ninth of August at about nine at night, and it's the last real one they have, and Pascual starts it on purpose because he has been holding it since June and has worked out that there isn't going to be a better time.

"Two hundred and eleven pages."

"Two hundred and nineteen."

"*Two hundred and nineteen pages*, and I've read the index, Reggie, I read it this morning while you were asleep in that chair, and do you know what isn't in it?" He has both hands on the back of the chair. "There's a page on the Copenhagen man. There's a page on the endowment and a page on the Bergen returns and eleven pages on the notebook royalties, and a page — a *page* — on which bank in Port of Spain and why, and what the man there's called, and what he's like, and that I'm to ask for him by name and not take the other one.

"And there isn't one line in the whole of it that's written to me."

Reggie sits at the table with the pen in his hand.

"No," he says.

"Then say something to me. Not paper. Say it out loud, in this kitchen, like a man, the way I would —"

"I know how you would."

"Then *do it*."

---

And Reggie Tar puts the pen down and looks up, and his face does the thing it has been doing since April, which isn'thing at all, and he says:

"I can't."

"You can. You've been doing it since 1959. Twenty-seven years, in this kitchen, standing up, the worst things about yourself, and you've never once —"

"I can say *I want it*," Reggie says. "I've practised that for twenty-seven years, and you asked me for it, and I've never once failed to do it, and I could do it now.

"I have never once started the other thing. Not in thirty-two years. You started it every single time and I answered, every single time, and I was glad to, and you have never noticed the asymmetry, because it has never cost you anything to start. It's the only thing in this house I've never been able to do, and I have had thirty-two years to learn it and I did not learn it, and there is no time now, and I am not going to stand in a kitchen in August and be worse at it than I would have been in 1960."

He puts his hand flat on the top box.

"So I did what I can do," he says. "You won't think it is the same thing. It is not the same thing. It's the largest one I have got."

---

Pascual goes out on to the verandah and stands at the rail for about twenty minutes.

Then he comes back in and stands behind the chair and puts both hands on the man's shoulders and says, "*Ay.* All right. All right. Finish your boxes."

And Reggie says, "Thank you," and picks up the pen; and three hours later, at about half past midnight, with the lamp down to nothing, he says, apparently to the page:

"It was thirty-two years and I would not take one day of it back, including the sixty-four."

Which is the only sentence Pascual Pobil is ever going to get, and he doesn't answer it, because answering it would have made it a conversation, and he understood within about a second and a half that the man couldn't have survived it being a conversation.

He sat there instead, in the other chair, for another hour, and said nothing, and let him work.

---

## June 1986

What Reggie is actually doing, and he would like it set down once, because there will be nobody to explain it to afterwards and Pascual will not read an explanation:

He is seventy-three years old.

That is the whole of it, in six words, and everything in the four boxes follows from it. He is a veterinary surgeon of seventy-three with a knee that will not do stairs and a house on a hill and no English anybody would call fluent under pressure, and in about four months he is going to be the sole surviving officer of an estate he cannot read.

There will be returns every month, because they have come to that house every month since 1952. There will be the Commission, and the endowment, and the notebook royalties, which are not small and are going to Shropshire in a way that requires eleven pages to explain. There will be ninety-four correspondents in fourteen countries, of whom eleven are young and four are going to be good, who will write to this house because they have written to this house for years and who will not be told anything by anybody unless somebody tells them.

And there will be a great many extremely kind people who will arrive with food.

Reggie has thought about the kind people a good deal. He has watched that man carry every difficult thing he has ever had by himself for thirty-two years — the drawer in 1958, the business with Spilsbury in 1957 which he mentioned once as item three of four and never again, all of it — and be the most legible human being on this earth, and tell nobody. He tells *Reggie*. He has a village, and a church, and a wife's worth of Inés, and a Nestor, and a John, and he has never once in thirty-two years put a hard thing into any of their hands, because he has never had to, because there has been a man in the next chair.

So the kind people are going to come, and he is going to feed them, and shout at them about the paraffin, and be entirely and visibly fine, and there is nothing whatever Reggie can do about that from where he is sitting.

What he can do is arithmetic.

---

He makes the four boxes so that not one thing in the next ten years has to be worked out by a man who is grieving.

Page by page, in his own hand, fair-copied: what it is, what it is worth, who to ask, what that person is like, what they will try, and what to say. He has never in his life produced a document with a recommendation on every page — his whole professional practice is to set out the options and the costs and attach nothing — and he puts a recommendation on every single page of this one, because a recommendation is a thing you can refuse and an option is a thing you have to decide, and he is not leaving that man two hundred decisions.

And on the ninety-four cards at the back, under each correspondent's address and subject and the date of their last letter, he writes one line about what they are like.

And on eleven of them, at the bottom, in the smallest hand he has:

*Will write to you and not to the Institute. Let him. He is worth the trouble.*

*Writes at length and means it. Four lines back is plenty.*

*Will need asking what it is for. Nobody else is going to.*

That is the letter. It is not addressed to him and it is spread over ninety-four cards in four boxes and it will take him about two years to find it, if he ever does, and Reggie has costed that too and has decided that he would rather it were found late than said badly.

He has never once in his life been beaten by a thing with a form, and this is the last form he is going to build, and he builds it round a man the way you build a wall round a garden.

---

## 4 September 1986

The last three days are at home, because there was never any question about that and Colón signed whatever needed signing.

He is in and out on the Wednesday and mostly out on the Thursday morning. Pascual is in the chair by the bed with the feed book on his knee, not reading it, and Inés is in the kitchen and has been since Monday and hasn't been asked to be.

At about eleven Reggie's hand moves on the sheet and Pascual takes it.

And Reggie says, quite clearly, in an entirely ordinary voice, the voice of a man opening a file:

"Item one."

Pascual waits.

He waits for a long time. There isn'thing else. The man goes under again at about a quarter past and doesn't come up, and it's finished at twenty past four in the afternoon with Pascual holding his hand and Inés in the doorway.

*Item one.* Thirty-one reviews at a kitchen table on the fourteenth of June and *there is only ever one item*, and he got the two words out and could not get the observation, and Pascual Pobil sits in a chair in a bedroom on the north side and understands that he has been left a sentence with a hole in it that he is the only living person who could fill.

---

## September to December 1986

The kind people come and they are magnificent and he couldn't have got through a single week without them and he would say so under oath.

Inés stays nine days. John Clarke comes at seven every morning for a month on his way to the office, for eleven minutes, with nothing to say, and says it. Jean is in from Florida inside three days and is unforgivable at the funeral, twice, and Pascual laughs out loud in a church for the first time in his life. Nestor comes up the eleven steps, every day, at his age, and doesn't mention them.

The village sends food. Forty-one people write. The Commission sends a wreath and the Director-General sends a letter which is four lines and is the right four lines, which Pascual hadn't expected and has kept.

He isn't alone. That wants saying, because he has a horror of being the old man in the house on the hill and he isn't that man; there's somebody in that kitchen almost every day of the autumn and most of them are people he loves.

And in three and a half months he doesn't put one single hard thing into any of their hands.

---

He notices it in the December, and he notices it because Jean makes him.

She telephones from Florida on a Sunday, which she has done every Sunday since September, and asks him how he is, and he tells her about the roof and about Chumley's grandson and about the man from the Ministry who is a fool, and she lets him do it for about nine minutes and then says:

"Pascual."

"*Sí.*"

"Have you said any of it to anybody?"

"Said what?"

"That's what I thought," says Jean Leslie, who is sixty-two and has never once in thirty-three years said a thing she could not stand behind. "I'm going to say something and you can put the telephone down if you like. In thirty years I have known everything about you within about four seconds of your knowing it, because you're the most readable creature I have ever met, and I have never once in my life known anything you actually minded. Not one thing. Not the drawer, not whatever it was in 1957, and I knew there was something in 1957. You told him. You told him and nobody else, and you had somebody to tell for thirty-two years and now you have got about forty of us and not one of us knows how."

There's a silence on a bad line from Florida.

"I don't know how either," says Pascual.

"No," says Jean. "I don't suppose you do. You've never had to learn. He had to learn — he was appalling at it and he learned it standing up in a kitchen over about twenty-seven years, and you're the one who taught him, and you have never done it once yourself." A pause. "I'm going to ring you every Sunday until you do, and I'm not going to be clever about it, because he told me what the trick was in 1959 and I have never forgotten it."

"*Ay*," says Pascual, and finds he cannot say anything else for about four seconds. "Jean."

"Mm."

"What is the trick?"

"You ask, and then you shut up and wait, and it takes about four minutes," says Jean. "Are you going to tell me you can't leave a result alone?"

And Pascual Pobil sits down on the step of his own kitchen with the receiver in his hand and laughs until he can't breathe, which turns out to be the other thing.

---

## 1987

Here's what he can't do, and he sets it out because he has spent his life keeping records and it's the only method he has got.

He can't read a return. He has tried. They come every month, because they have come to that house every month since 1952, and there are four columns he understands perfectly and nine he doesn't, and there's a column of John's for observations not reducible to figures which he can read and which is the worst of the lot, because it is the only column in the world that was designed for the pleasure of one particular man and that man isn't going to read it.

He can't answer a letter from Copenhagen. He can answer it courteously in three lines and he can't answer the question in it, and the question in it is the point, and he knows it is the point because a page in the second box says so in a small upright hand.

He can't do the review. It's the fourteenth of June and he gets the folder out and puts the costing sheet on the table, soft at the folds, with *1984* struck through and *2014* underlined twice in his own blunt hand from 1984, and he says "Item one" out loud at eight o'clock in the evening in an empty kitchen, and that's the end of it. He can't do the observation. The observation was always Reggie's; it was Reggie's for thirty-one years; the whole instrument was a man doing arithmetic on his own life in front of a witness, and the witness can't operate it and never could.

He puts the sheet back in the folder.

---

On the ninth of July he does one thing, and it takes him four days to work up to and about forty seconds to do, and he does it in the lecture hut with the leak because that is where John Clarke still works.

"John."

"Mm."

"There's a column in this return I can read and twelve I can't, and the one I can read is the one he wrote it for." Pascual puts the sheet on the desk. "I'm not asking you to stop sending them. Don't stop sending them. I'm telling you what happens in my kitchen on the ninth of every month, because Jean says I have got forty people and I have never given one of them anything, and she's right, and you are the nearest."

John Clarke looks at the sheet for a while and then at him.

"Right," he says. "Then I'll put the twelve into English at the bottom, and you'll tell me when I've got it wrong, and you will, because you always do."

"*Ay.* That is not what I came for."

"No," says John. "It's what you're getting."

---

And here's the one that takes him until the autumn to see, and which is the actual size of it.

For twenty-eight years the most important thing Pascual Pobil did with his days was ask one man what a thing was for.

Not the practice. The practice was good work and he was good at it and it kept four hundred animals alive a year, and it's over now anyway and the young woman in Sangre Grande is better at it than he was at forty. And not the Institute, where he is an emeritus nuisance who is very much loved and whose opinion is asked about drains.

It was the question. He is the only man who ever found the handle on a dangerous, first-class, unstoppable mind, and he found it by accident in December 1958 in the worst week of his life, and he worked it for twenty-eight years, and it was the single most useful thing any human being on this earth ever did for anybody, and he is the only person who knows how it is done.

And there's nobody to do it to.

He isn't being modest about this and he isn't being grand about it. It's a plain accounting. He has a village and a church and forty people and a knee, and he is seventy-four years old, and the job is gone.

---

## October 1987

He takes the boat out on the ninth, alone, which everybody has an opinion about and nobody says to his face.

*Doris* is thirty-four years old and unlucky to rename and the engine is the engine Cyril's boy looked at in 1985 and she goes out past the head at about four knots and stops where the water changes.

And Pascual Pobil sits on the thwart with the engine off and the island a mile and a half behind him and says it out loud.

He doesn't do it well. He has never in his life prepared a sentence and he has been preparing this one since the fourth of September 1986, which is thirteen months, and he says it four times before he gets it in the order he means.

"I am going to say four things and I have had thirteen months to get them right and I am going to say them properly because nobody can hear me, including you.

"One. It was thirty-two years and I would not take one day of it back, including the sixty-four, and you said that to me at half past midnight on the ninth of August 1986 with your hand on a box and I did not answer you. I have had thirteen months of not answering you. I am answering you now. It was thirty-two years and I would not take one day of it back either, and I never said so out loud, not once, in thirty-two years, because I thought that saying it would be a way of counting it and that you were the one who counted things. That was cowardice and I have found it out late.

"Two. I read the boxes. All two hundred and nineteen pages and all ninety-four cards, and it took me until June, and I was angry with you for nine months and I was wrong. You built me a wall. There is not one decision in ten years that I have had to make on my own and I have made none of them badly, and every time I open that box I am being looked after by a man who is not here, and I would rather have had four words and I know now what four words would have cost you, and I am not asking for them any more.

"Three. I am afraid I am going to stop being able to remember what you sounded like. That is the thing I am actually afraid of and I have not said it to anybody, and Jean has been telephoning me every Sunday for a year to make me say something and I have given her the roof, thirteen times.

"Four." He stops.

"Four. *I love you.* In English, because your Spanish was appalling and you knew it and you did it anyway for thirty-two years, and I am not going to do the same to you now."

And then Pascual Pobil sits in a boat a mile and a half off the north side with his hands on his knees and cries for about eleven minutes, which he hasn't done since the fourth of September 1986, and afterwards blows his nose and starts the engine and goes in, and is late for supper, and Inés is furious with him, and it's the best evening he has had in thirteen months.

## March 1989

The letter comes in the ordinary post on the ninth of March.

It has a Wellington postmark and it's four pages and it's addressed to *Dr P. Pobil* at the house on the north side, which isn't the Institute and not the Commission, and it begins:

*Dear Dr Pobil,*

*I am writing to you rather than to the Institute because Dr Tar told me to. He wrote it at the bottom of a letter in 1985 and I did not understand it at the time and I have understood it since about last August, and I am sorry to be writing to you about this at all and I have no one else.*

*My name is Ruth Marchetti and I am twenty-nine. I run four animals at Kaikōura on a government grant and I have a design I am going to put in on the first of May and it is the best thing I have ever done and I cannot sleep and I have stopped being able to tell whether I want to run it because it will answer the question or because I want to see what happens.*

*I have read every paper you have your name on. I know what you did in May of 1954 and I know what the discussion in the 1959 paper says about non-blindability and who actually wrote it. There is nobody in this country who will tell me no. My head of department thinks I am the best thing that has happened to him in twenty years and he is right and that is the difficulty.*

*The design is enclosed. It is four pages.*

*I am not asking you to approve it.*

---

Pascual reads it twice at the kitchen table.

Then he reads the four pages of the design, which he doesn't understand in nine places and understands perfectly in the two that matter, because the two that matter are about an animal and he has been looking at animals since 1938.

Then he sits back and puts both hands flat on the table and finds that his face has done something it hasn't done in two and a half years, and that he knows exactly what it has done, because he saw it on his own face in a mirror in a sick bay in 1954 and has never seen it since.

He gets up and gets the second box off the shelf and finds the card in about ninety seconds, because the man numbered everything.

*MARCHETTI, R. — Wellington, then Kaikōura from '85. 28. Physiol./behav. Second-best design I have seen from anybody under 40 and the best is not hers yet. Writes once and then not for eight months, and the eight months are when she is doing the work.*

And at the bottom, in the smallest hand he has:

*Will need asking what it is for. Nobody else is going to.*

---

Pascual Pobil sits in a kitchen on the north side at seventy-six years of age with a card in one hand and four pages from New Zealand in the other.

"*Ay*, Reggie," he says. "*Ay, Dios mío.* Three years."

Then he gets a pen, and a sheet of paper, because he has never in his life been able to write a letter that said anything and he is going to have to learn how at seventy-six, and it takes him the whole of the afternoon and the whole of the evening and he gets eleven lines.

The first ten are about the animal at Kaikōura, and the header tank, and what she has got wrong on page three, which isn't the design and is the housing, and which she will hate him for.

The eleventh is:

*I am not going to tell you no. Nobody can tell you no; I knew a man for thirty-two years and it never once worked on him. I am going to ask you one thing instead and you are not to answer it to me. What is it for?*

*Take four minutes over it. It always takes four minutes.*

*P. Pobil*
