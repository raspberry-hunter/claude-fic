# A Sample of One

## February 1950

The afternoon is the third Thursday in February. It runs from about half past one until about ten past six, there are four people in the room and a schedule on the board, and what Reggie Tar does in it is not going to be set down here.

At ten past six he washes his hands for a long time.

At about seven he is sitting on the edge of a bench in the anteroom with his glasses in his hand, and he cannot get his breathing to do the ordinary thing, and he understands that he has just found out something about himself which he is going to have to act on.

That he was not carried away. That he was not obeying anybody. That he had enjoyed it, steadily, from about two o'clock, in the way he enjoys a good number.

---

He does not go through the doorway where the door used to be.

He goes back to his own room and puts the light on and takes a sheet of the departmental paper and writes eleven lines, and they are dry and correct and give three months, and he signs them, and then he sits and looks at them for perhaps forty seconds.

Then he takes them down two flights and along the covered way to the registry, where there is a box with a slot in the lid and a clerk who empties it at eight in the morning and stamps everything in it with the date.

He knows exactly what he is doing and he would like that on the record.

He is not a brave man and he does not become one that evening. What he is, is a man who has spent four and a half years watching Alec Spilsbury take an argument apart, and who has therefore no illusions whatever about what would happen if he went and stood in that other room at five past seven with a piece of paper in his hand and said *I am thinking of resigning*: that it would take about eleven minutes, that every line of it would be true, that he would be at the bench in the morning, and that he would still be at that bench in 1970.

So he does the only thing available to a man who cannot be relied on, which is to make the decision somewhere the decision cannot be reopened.

The lid goes down. It makes a noise like a letter box.

Then he goes back up two flights and through the doorway and tells him.

---

He does it in the flattest voice he has, standing up, with his eyes on the dark square of the window, and it takes about a quarter of an hour: what the afternoon was, and what he did in it, and what he has been doing in the twenty months before it, and the three occasions on which a decent man would have stopped and he did not, and the count, which he gives, because a thing has a number and the number is the only part of it he can pronounce.

He does not soften any of it and he does not explain any of it. There is no explanation. There is the fact that he was curious and that being curious was enough.

Then he says: "It's in the registry box. It'll be stamped at eight. I'm telling you afterwards on purpose and I'd like you to know that, because I'd rather be a coward in a way you can see than in a way you can't."

He turns round.

Spilsbury is on the stool with the pen still in his hand and his head slightly on one side, and he has not moved at all, and he is not appalled, and he is not angry.

"Right," he says.

---

There is a pause of about four seconds.

"Well, that's a nuisance," says Spilsbury. "The November series wants a second pair of hands and Halliwell's no good at it. Where are you going?"

Reggie stands in the middle of the floor.

"I don't know."

"Then don't take anything for six months and do the mortality work. You've had the returns since 'forty-seven and nobody else on this earth can read them and it's a book, not a paper." He is already reaching for the pad. "I'll write to Brayshaw and to the people at Plymouth, and I'll say what I think, which is that you're the best instrument in this building and that they'd be fools. You may read both letters before they go if you want to and I'd rather you didn't, because you'll take the adjectives out."

"Alec."

"Mm."

"That's not — " He stops. He is thirty-one years old and has never once in his life been unable to construct a sentence. "I'm not only talking about the post."

Spilsbury looks up.

Perhaps three seconds. Not startled. Not hurt. And not, this is the thing, not *surprised*.

"No, I know you're not," he says, quite pleasantly. "Suit yourself."

And then: "Bring the November plates through before you go, would you. I want the eleven o'clock one."

---

He brings the November plates through.

They work until half past ten, and it is ordinary, and it is good work, and at some point Spilsbury says something extremely funny about Halliwell and Reggie laughs before he can help it.

At the end Spilsbury caps the pen and says, "That's the best thing you've done since the spring and I shall say so in the letter whatever you think about it," and means every word, and goes to bed.

---

He gives three months and he works all of them.

Nothing is withdrawn. That is the part he has never been able to put in an order.

The door stays off its hinges. The other thing goes on, unaltered, about twice a week, for the whole of March and the whole of April and the first three weeks of May, and it is exactly what it was in 1947 — unhurried, and attentive, and it goes to the third drawer down without ceremony — and on the last night it is not different in any particular that Reggie can identify, and he lies awake afterwards for about two hours looking for one.

On the morning of the eleventh of June there is a car at twenty past eight and Spilsbury comes down to it in his shirtsleeves with the marmalade still on the table and shakes his hand and says, "Plymouth will have written by Friday. Send me the mortality book when it's done and I'll fight you about chapter four."

"You will."

"I shall be right," says Spilsbury. "Goodbye, Tar."

And goes back in, because the eleven o'clock plate wants doing.

---

## 1950 to 1952

He writes the mortality tables in eleven months and they are the best thing anybody has done in the subject.

He also writes something else, in the March of 1951, in a lodging house in Devon at about two in the morning, on the back of an envelope, and he does not throw it away; he copies it out fair and puts it in the front of the notebook he keeps his own figures in, where he will see it every time he opens the notebook, which is four times a day for thirty years.

*Rule. Not again, not with anybody, at any price. The whole of it goes or none of it does, and the whole of it is not survivable twice. Prespecified 14.iii.51, in cold blood, with the result in hand. Not to be revisited on the day.*

He is thirty-two and he has just applied to his own life the only piece of method he has ever been entirely reliable about, which is that you decide the stopping rule before you look at the data.

It holds. Of course it holds. He has never once in his life been beaten by a thing with a form.

---

## February 1953

The veterinary surgeon comes in February and is at the pens within about forty minutes of landing, with his sleeves rolled, arguing with Jean about a hose.

He is just shy of six feet and built like a docker and forty years old, and he has the most legible face Reggie has ever seen on a human head — everything goes across it like weather across a field — and on the second morning he tells the senior scientist of the Institute, in front of two juniors, that the winter feed schedule is a disgrace and that whoever wrote it should be ashamed.

Reggie wrote it.

"I wrote it," he says.

"Then you should be ashamed," says Pobil. "It's got them on four ounces in January and six in June and there isn't a fish in this ocean that does that. Who told you they eat less when it's cold?"

"The literature."

"*Ay.* Then the literature's never fed anything," says Pobil, and goes back to the balance.

"It hasn't," says Reggie. "Write me a better one by Friday and I'll sign it, and you can have the credit and the blame."

---

It takes nine days.

Reggie would like that entered somewhere, because it is the single most useful figure he has ever produced about himself and there is nobody to give it to. Eleven months is what it would have taken him once, and he knows that, because he has watched himself do a version of it before at a slower speed with less information. Nine days.

Ninth day, at the rail, at twenty past six in the morning: the man is squatting on the step with his hand in the water talking to an animal in Spanish in a low rough voice, and he looks up and says something about the calf's respirations, and Reggie hears about a third of it, and thinks, with perfect clarity and no warning whatever:

*Ah. There it is. That is what it is going to be.*

And goes up the path and into the office and opens the notebook and reads the front page of it, which he wrote in Devon in 1951 in cold blood with the result in hand, and closes the notebook, and does the roster.

---

It is not a struggle. He wants that understood, because he has read a great deal of sentimental rubbish about men struggling, and this is not that.

He made the decision in 1951 and he made it properly, and a properly made decision does not require to be made again every morning; that is the entire point of making it properly. What he does for the next four hundred and seventy days is simply not do a thing, in the way a man does not put his hand on a hotplate — without effort, without drama, and with the absolute confidence of somebody who has been burned once and has the figures.

What it costs is something else, and he does not have a column for it, and so it goes uncosted for three years.

---

## May 1954

The trial runs eleven days.

Reggie designs it in about two hours and it is, he thinks, quite the best thing he has ever put on paper. Charles Cholmondeley is moved to Gamma's pen. Beta is left alone. The point of it is to establish whether the arrested decline is the man or the pairing, and there is no other design that would answer it, and Reggie knows that, and says so, twice, patiently, to a veterinary surgeon who has never in his life been able to hold a design in his head for longer than it takes him to look at an animal.

Pobil objects on the second day.

"He's an adult male five months into a decline and you've taken away the only thing that's put a pound on him since January," Pascual says, on the fourth, in the office, at volume. "I'm not arguing with the question. I don't care about the question. I'm telling you what that animal's going to do about it, and you haven't got a figure for it and I have, and it's in my book, and you won't look at my book."

"I've read your book. It's the best clinical record in this hemisphere and it doesn't answer the question I'm asking," says Reggie, and gives him three answers, and the three answers are correct.

And on the sixth day Pobil comes down the path with the feed book in his hand, and stops at the corner of the store, and does not come round it.

He stands there for about ninety seconds. Reggie, at the rail, sees him do it, and does not know what it is, and files it.

---

What Pobil has got, and has had since about the fourth of May and cannot use, is a reason that is not about the animal.

He knows perfectly well that the animal is the argument. He has made the animal argument four times and lost it four times, on the merits, to a better man, and he will go on making it. What he has underneath it is the other thing, which is that he has watched Reggie Tar for fifteen months and is now entirely certain that the design is a *want* wearing a coat, and that the want is the thing, and that if he says so it will be the first true sentence anybody on this island has said out loud in a year and it will also be — and he can hear exactly how it will sound in his own mouth, in front of Jean, in front of the boy —

He does not say it.

He stands at the corner of the store for ninety seconds and works out four ways to say it that are not about himself, and there are none, and he goes up the path with the feed book.

Reggie will not learn any of that for two and a half years.

---

Beta gets out on the night of the eighth of May.

He is found on the concrete at about half past five in the morning. It is rhabdomyolysis and renal crisis and it takes eleven days and Pascual Pobil does not sleep for the first four of them, and the water in the catheter bag goes from port wine to clear on the ninth day, which is the seventeenth, and the animal lives.

The row is on the verandah on the tenth, in front of the entire station, and it is heard from the water.

*You have your results, Reggie. You don't need more.*

And Reggie goes white and does not answer and does not stop the trial until the following morning, because he needs a night.

---

He gets him away on the nineteenth.

That does not change, and he would like it recorded that it was never going to, because it has nothing whatever to do with the other business and everything to do with a boy of twenty-four who had packed a knapsack days before and was going to go, with permission or without it, and would have gone off the end of the cay in the dark with a rucksack.

So: Jean's chart, drawn in the lecture hut on the fourteenth with the door shut. The tin out of the chandler's at Bridgetown with the matches taken out of it. A name. An arrangement. Money. *Colonel Bevan does outrank me.* And, at the end, because there is nothing else available to say to a boy he is sending into the sea:

"I shall be quite cross with you if it doesn't work."

"It will work," says Charles Cholmondeley, who is not sure of one single thing on this earth, and goes.

---

Then Reggie goes to the sick bay at a quarter to ten at night and tells Pascual Pobil what he has done, and Pobil shoots the bolt behind him, and they are in a room eight feet by ten with a smell of formalin and one high window and nobody on this island looking at either of them.

And Reggie tells him about the labs.

He does it standing up, with his hands behind his back and his eyes on the window, in the flattest voice he has: what the place was, and what it was for, and what he did there for four years, and what he was very good at, and what he went on being good at for twenty months after the point at which any decent man would have stopped. It takes about a quarter of an hour. It comes out level and ordered and complete, like a paper being read aloud.

At the end there is a very long silence.

Then Pascual says, "How many?"

Reggie tells him that too.

"Turn round."

He turns round. Pascual is sitting on the low chair with his elbows on his knees and his face showing everything, and what is on it is not horror and not pity and is worse than either, because it is grief, and none of it is for Reggie.

"I'm not going to forgive you for that. I couldn't if I wanted to, and I'm not one of the people who could, and some of the people who could aren't anywhere at all now." He stands up. "That's the first thing.

"The second thing is that you've carried it about for eleven years by yourself and made yourself into a man who can't be in a room, and everyone on this station thinks you're cold, and I've thought it myself for a year. And you've just handed me the whole of it standing up, without one word of excuse, on the day you had the chance to say nothing at all." He crosses the floor. "Here's the third thing. I'm not going anywhere. Not tonight and not because of that and not ever, and if you've been standing there waiting for me to open that door and put you through it, you can stop."

And he gets hold of Reggie by the front of the shirt, both fists, not hard.

And Reggie takes hold of both his wrists and takes them off.

---

He does it quite gently and it is the hardest thing he has ever done with his hands.

"No," he says.

Pascual stands there.

"I'd like to," Reggie says. "I want that said, because you'll be up all night with it otherwise and I am not having that on top of the rest. I would like to a very great deal, and I have wanted to since about the fourteenth of February 1953, and I am not going to, and it is not about you, and there is nothing wrong with you, and I am not going to explain it tonight."

"Reggie —"

"I've given you four hundred honest sentences in the last quarter of an hour and I cannot give you a fifth. That is the whole of what I have got." He puts his hat on. "Thank you for the animal. You were right on the fourth of May and you were right on the tenth, and I didn't say so, and I'm saying it now."

And he goes out, and across to his own room in the block, and does not sleep, and is at the pens at half past five.

---

Behind him, in a room eight feet by ten, Pascual Pobil sits down on the low chair and puts his face in his hands for about a minute.

Then he says, out loud, to nobody:

"*Ay.* That wasn't a confession. That was a *fence*."

---

## 1954 to 1956

It goes on for two and a half years and it is not dramatic and nothing whatever happens.

They work extremely well together. They write the big paper with Jean and it goes off on the third of July and it is the best thing either of them will ever put their name to. They row — properly, at volume, on the verandah, in front of everyone — about the drains and the pen dimensions and the Whitley return and the paraffin, and Pascual wins about half and both of them enjoy it more than anything else in their week. Reggie makes him laugh perhaps four times a year and counts them.

Pascual does not raise it. Not once, in thirty months.

That is the thing Reggie finds he cannot account for and turns over about eleven times a year. He had prepared, on the twentieth of May 1954, for three conversations of increasing difficulty, and has had none of them. He has been treated for two and a half years with perfect and undiminished warmth by a man who was told no, standing up, in a sick bay, with a reason withheld, and who has never once asked for the reason and has never once behaved as though he was owed it.

Reggie files this under *decency* for about a year and a half, and then one morning in the autumn of 1955, at the rail, watching the man put a hand flat on the side of Nestor Aguilar's face because Aguilar's boy has been ill, he understands that it is not decency at all.

It is that Pascual Pobil worked out the answer on about the twenty-first of May 1954, on his own, in about two days, and has been waiting since then for Reggie to be able to say it, and would wait until 1980.

Reggie goes into the office and shuts the door and sits down and does not do any work for about forty minutes.

Then he opens the notebook to the front page and reads eleven words in his own hand from a lodging house in Devon, and closes it, and does the roster.

---

## December 1956

Spilsbury lands on the fourteenth of November with two cases and a crate of books, and is fifty-one now and greyer and otherwise entirely unchanged, and Pascual dislikes him inside about four minutes and is perfectly aware that four minutes isn't enough to go on and dislikes him anyway.

It takes five weeks to find out he was right for the wrong reason.

---

Here's what he watches, because watching is the only thing he's got.

He watches the two of them work, and it's the best six weeks of science he's ever been near and he'd say so under oath. He watches Spilsbury hand Reggie a page and Reggie hand it back with three things crossed out and Spilsbury say *yes, obviously, damn* and mean it. He watches Reggie be better in that man's company than Pascual has ever seen him be in anybody's, including his own.

And he watches Reggie eat breakfast at a table with him every morning for forty-one days and never once touch the marmalade, which he has had every morning since 1952.

He watches Spilsbury say *you look well, Tar* on the first afternoon, without looking up, in the tone of a man remarking on the weather, and he watches what that does, from eleven feet away, for about a second and a half, and then not be there any more.

He watches Spilsbury mention the old building — *the winter of 'forty-eight, when you had that row with Halliwell about the bath* — perhaps nine times over six weeks, comfortably, affectionately, at supper, in front of five people, with no flicker of any kind.

And on the eleventh of December, at the rail, in the half-light, with nobody about, he watches Alec Spilsbury put a hand on Reggie Tar's shoulder for about two seconds, in passing, on his way to the store, the way a man does to somebody he's worked with for years.

And Reggie stands at that rail afterwards for eleven minutes without moving.

---

Pascual goes back to the sick bay and sits down and gets there in about four minutes, and then sits for another two hours making himself be sure, because he's about to say a thing out loud that he can never take back and he's not going to say it on a feeling.

Here's what he's got, and he lays it out the way he'd lay out a case history.

A man who was at that building four and a half years and left it at thirty-one in the middle of a series, three months' notice, for no post. A man who has never once in five years said the name of that place in public. A man who was told *no* at twenty-four, presumably, or was never told anything, and who has spent thirty months being warm and short and funny and entirely, immovably, untouchable, with a reason he could not give.

And a man in a guest hut who has never in six weeks done one single thing that a decent colleague wouldn't do, and who is not cruel, and is not lying, and has not the smallest idea, and has never had the smallest idea, and whose hand on a shoulder for two seconds in passing can put Reggie Tar at a rail for eleven minutes in the dark.

*Ay, Dios mío*, Pascual thinks. *He had him.*

And then, about forty seconds later, the second one, which is worse and is the one he'll have for the rest of his life:

*And he gave him back, and did not notice that he had done it, and is eating his supper.*

---

Spilsbury sails on the twenty-second.

Pascual gives it four days, because he's not going to do it while the man's boat is still on the water and be a person who did that.

He does it on the twenty-sixth, at half past eight in the evening, in the office, with the door shut, and he does the thing he has never once done in his life, which is that he has it written out.

Not on paper. He hasn't written it down; he wouldn't. But he has said it to himself in the sick bay, in order, eleven times over four days, until every sentence is exactly the sentence he means, and when he comes through that door he does not start hot and he does not grumble and he does not interrupt himself, and Reggie Tar, who has known him for three years and ten months, sits up straight the moment he opens his mouth, because he has never heard this voice before and does not need to be told what it is.

"I am going to say four things and I have had four days to get them right, and I would be obliged if you did not answer any of them until I have finished all four.

"One. You had that man. I do not know for how long and I am not going to ask and it does not signify. You had him and he had you and it ended and you left that building, and you have not said one word of it to me or to anybody on this island, and I have known it since the eleventh of December at twenty past six in the morning and I have not said so until tonight because I did not want to say it while he could still be reached by boat.

"Two. He does not know. That is the part I have had to sit with. He is not cruel and he did not use you and he did not lie to you, and if he were any of those things I could do something with it. He simply never understood that there was more of you to be had than what he already had in his hand, and he never will, and he put his hand on your shoulder on the eleventh of December on his way to the store and it cost him nothing whatever, and you stood at that rail for eleven minutes.

"Three. I am not jealous of him. That is a lie and I am striking it out. I am extremely jealous of him and I have been since the fourth week and I am ashamed of it and I have decided that I would rather be ashamed in front of you than manage it for another year.

"Four." He stops for the first time. "Four. You wrote a rule."

Reggie does not move.

"I do not know what is in it and I do not need to. I know you decided something once, in cold blood, about not doing this again, and that you have kept it for six years without one day off, because that is what you are, and it is the best thing about you, and it is what makes you unbearable.

"So here is the whole of it, and then you may throw me out of this office.

"You ran it once. One man, one building, one result. And you took that result and you wrote the rule out of it and you have applied it to every man on this earth for six years and you have never once gone back and looked at the design, and you know exactly what is wrong with it, because you have written it in three papers and said it to Jean about the salt ponds and you said it to me in this room in September about the Whitley animal, and the words you used were *on a sample of one*.

"*Ay.* That is it. That is the whole of the objection. You would fail a student for it. You would fail *me* for it and I would deserve it.

"I have finished. Now you may talk."

---

## December 1956

Reggie sits in his own office with the generator going and finds that he has stopped being able to breathe properly.

He does not say anything for about ninety seconds and Pascual lets him have all ninety.

"The rule's dated the fourteenth of March 1951," he says. "It is in the front of my figures notebook, in ink, and I have read it four times a day for six years, and there is a line in it that says *not to be revisited on the day*, which is the only clause that ever mattered, because I put it there to stop myself doing exactly what I'm about to do now."

"Then don't do it now," Pascual says. "*Ay.* Reggie. I've waited two and a half years, I can wait until Thursday."

"No."

"You're going to tell me you can't leave a result alone —"

"I am going to tell you," Reggie says, "that you've just handed me a methodological objection to a stopping rule, and that it is correct, and that a rule which cannot be revised when a fatal objection to its design turns up is not a stopping rule. It is a *superstition*, and I have been operating one since 1951 and calling it method." He takes his glasses off. "And I am not revisiting it on the day. You gave me four days' notice. You rehearsed it for four days in a sick bay to make sure every sentence was one you meant, and I've never once in my life seen you prepare anything, and I've been sitting here for ninety seconds working out what that cost you, and the answer's more than the whole of the rest of it put together.

"So no. I'm not going to be careful with you, and I'm not making you wait until Thursday, and I am not telling you the rest of 1946 tonight, because it would take three hours and you have had four days of it already and you look terrible."

He stands up.

"You may have the whole of it tomorrow and every day after that for as long as you want it," he says. "What I've got tonight's this. You were right about the design, and I've been wrong for six years in a way I wouldn't tolerate in a first-year student, and I'd have spotted it in anybody's work but my own. And that I've wanted this since the fourteenth of February 1953, and I told you so once, standing in a sick bay, and then I walked out of the room, and I've thought about that every day since. And that I'd like about thirty years to make it up to you."

There is a silence of about four seconds.

"*Thirty years*," says Pascual.

"That's the figure. I have had it since 1954. It is not negotiable and I'm prepared to be extremely tedious about it."

"*Ay, Dios mío*," says Pascual Pobil, and crosses the floor.

---

## 1957

The costing sheet is drawn up on a Saturday in March of 1957 at the kitchen table of a rented house on the north side, because Reggie is a man who brings a folder to things and Pascual has given up.

It has the boat on it, and the house, and a great deal of arithmetic, and at the bottom, underlined twice in Pascual's blunt hand, a year.

They will review it every March.

Reggie does not put the 1951 rule in the stove, because he has never destroyed a record in his life and is not going to learn how at thirty-eight. He puts it at the back of the same notebook, out of the front, with a line through it and a date beside the line, and one sentence underneath in his own small upright hand:

*Withdrawn 26.xii.56. Design fault: N = 1. Objection raised by P. Pobil, who had it right on 21 May 1954 and waited two and a half years for me, which is the actual finding and is not mine.*

---

He had expected it to be the same and worse.

That is the honest form of what he was braced for, and he would like it on the record, because he was wrong in every particular and the way he was wrong is information. He had three and a half years of it with a man who was better at it than anybody in England, and he arrived at a kitchen table on the north side at thirty-eight with a technique, and he had assumed — in the way one assumes that a method which worked once will work again — that the technique was the valuable part, and that what he was bringing to this was an advantage.

It is not an advantage. It is the first thing he has to put down and it takes him about eleven days.

Here is what he had in England. A man who found out how it worked in a fortnight in 1947 and was thereafter extremely good at it, and who was unhurried, and attentive in a way that went through Reggie like current, and who noticed everything once and never needed to notice it again. Nothing ever went wrong. In three and a half years, not once — no fumbling, no misjudgement, nothing that had to be laughed at and started over. Reggie had taken that for excellence and it was excellence, and it is also what a thing looks like when the man doing it is not, at any point, in any degree, at risk.

And he could ask for anything. That is the part he has had to take apart. He asked freely and often and was given all of it, competently, without comment, and he had thought for years that this was a species of generosity. It is not. A request costs the man receiving it exactly what he has at stake, and Alec Spilsbury had nothing at stake, and so it cost him nothing, and so Reggie could say anything he liked at two in the morning and be accommodated, the way one specifies a parameter.

He cannot ask Pascual Pobil for a glass of water.

That is not true by February and it is true in January and it very nearly finishes him, because he does not understand it, and because he has spent six years believing the thing he believes about himself, which is that he cannot ask anybody for anything, and here is the evidence arriving right on time. It takes him until about the third week to see that it is the opposite. He cannot ask because it will *land*. Because there is a man in this bed who will mind, and be moved, and remember it, and bring it up at breakfast in front of the feed book with his eyebrows up. The asking has a recipient now. That is the whole of the difficulty, and it is not a deficit, it is a price, and he has never once in his life been charged one for this.

The other things, in order, because he keeps them in order.

It goes wrong. It goes wrong about one time in four, in the first months, for reasons that are almost always ridiculous, and the going wrong is not a failure of anything; it is where the laughing is. Nobody laughed in England. He did not notice that at the time and cannot now stop noticing it.

Pascual stops in the middle of things to look at him. Not briefly. He will stop — at some cost, and with an expression of complete and undisguised absorption, and for no purpose Reggie can identify — and simply look at his face, and Reggie has had to learn to let him, and it is worse than anything else in the house and he would not give it up for the freehold. It is inefficient. There is nothing to be found out. And that is precisely the difference, because the man in England never stopped to look, and it was not coldness: there was nothing he did not already have.

And there is the sound.

In three and a half years Reggie Tar did not once make a noise he had not approved first. He had taken that for a fact about himself, the way one takes one's handwriting for a fact about oneself, and had been rather pleased about it in an unexamined way, because he is a man who prefers to be in command of the instrument.

He finds out otherwise on about the fourth night, with the shutters open and the generator four miles off, and he gets his mouth shut about a second and a half too late, and Pascual stops dead and puts his forehead down on Reggie's shoulder and says, muffled, wrecked, delighted, *ay, Dios mío, do that again* — and Reggie lies in the dark at thirty-eight years of age with his hand in the back of a man's hair and understands, with the completeness he usually reserves for a result, that he was never in command of any instrument. He was simply never in a room with anybody who could get anything out of him.

---

And then there is afterwards, which he had not known was a category.

In England, afterwards was four minutes. He can be precise about it because he counted, for three and a half years, the way he counts everything: from the point at which Alec Spilsbury stopped to the point at which his breathing changed, four minutes, and twice in three and a half years five. It was not rudeness and it was not a slight. The man slept the way he did everything, immediately and thoroughly and without waste, and Reggie had lain beside him perhaps four hundred times, wide awake, with the whole of the evening still going round in him and nowhere to put it, and had told himself this was restful.

On the second night in the house on the north side, Pascual Pobil does not let go of him.

That is the part Reggie has no procedure for. The man simply stays where he is, turned in, an arm across him, heavy and unembarrassed and appallingly warm, and does not appear to be going anywhere or to have noticed that anything requires deciding; and Reggie lies in the dark and applies himself to it, because he has never been in this position before in his life, and he is a man who works at a thing until he has it.

He is about ninety seconds into working at it when the breathing changes.

And he knows that sound. He has three and a half years of that sound. It is the shallowing and the lengthening and then the small catch, and it means four minutes, and he takes hold of it with both hands and is very nearly grateful, because it is the one quantity in this bed he recognises. *Ah. There it is. Four minutes.* He has the number. The number is familiar. A familiar number is the nearest thing to solid ground he has been offered since eight o'clock.

Then the hand starts moving.

Up and down his spine, slowly, not very accurately, going out of true at the top and correcting; and the breathing goes on shallowing all the while, so that the two things are happening at once, and the second one does not stop when the first one finishes. It goes on for another minute and a half after the man is unmistakably asleep. Then it stops in the middle of a stroke and stays where it stopped, palm flat, between his shoulder blades.

Reggie Tar lies absolutely still in a rented house at thirty-eight years of age with the four minutes in one hand and no use for them whatever, and has to work quite hard for about a quarter of an hour.

---

So the duration was never the variable. That is the finding, and it takes him some months, because he arrives with a stopwatch, as he arrives everywhere, and the numbers refuse to do anything. Four minutes. Ten. An hour and a half. Twenty minutes and then asleep in the middle of a sentence about the drains. He looks for a relation to the day of the week, to how good it was, to which of them started it, and there is nothing there at all.

There is exactly one variable that predicts it, and he identifies it in about the April, and it is how tired the man is.

Reggie Tar, who has published on the non-independence of observations in three languages, sits at a bench with a column of times in front of him and establishes, at thirty-eight, that a veterinary surgeon who has been up since half past four with a colicking donkey goes to sleep sooner than one who has not. He does not write it down anywhere. He does think, with some feeling, that it is the most fatuous hour he has ever spent on a series, and that he would not give it back.

What does not vary is that he is touched.

That is the whole of it. Every single time, without exception, in all weathers, from the first night: an arm over him, or a hand flat between his shoulder blades, or the knots in the right shoulder — which Pascual found on about the fourth occasion and has an opinion about, and which he works on with his thumb while complaining that a man who sits at a bench like that at thirty-eight will be a hoop at sixty.

And then, distributed across the spectrum and entirely unpredictably, the rest of it. Sometimes he wants to talk, and does, for an hour, about nothing whatever. Sometimes he says something about what has just happened — plainly, without embarrassment, in the tone he would use about a good result, and once in the November about a particular thing he had not expected to like and did, which Reggie has taken out and looked at rather more often than he would care to have entered anywhere. Sometimes he says, with enormous satisfaction, that Reggie is the most infuriating man in the Caribbean, and gives a reason, and the reason is always specific and always correct. Sometimes he gets up and comes back with water for both of them. Sometimes he says nothing at all for forty minutes and neither does Reggie, and the hand stays where it is.

And sometimes he is asleep in four minutes with his thumb still on the shoulder, and Reggie lies there in the dark being held by a man who is not even present for it.

That is the one that finishes him. He had thought, for six years, that what he lost in 1950 was the four minutes, and every time Pascual Pobil goes out like a light with an arm across him he finds out again that he had never had the thing he was grieving.

---

He tries to say it in about the March and it is the worst piece of speaking he has ever done in his life.

They are in the dark and Pascual has got a hand on the back of his neck and has been quiet for some while, and Reggie says, into the pillow, "I want to put something to you and I'm going to do it very badly."

"*Ay.* Go on, then."

"The — after. This." He stops. He is a man who has read three papers aloud to the Royal Society without a note in front of him. "You go on being here."

"Where would I go?"

"That isn't —" He tries again. "It is not a complaint. I want it *entered* that it is not a complaint. I am trying to tell you that I did not know it was a thing that was done. That there is a part afterwards, and that people have it, and that I had not got it and had not known there was anything to be got, and that I have been having it since December and I do not know what to do with my hands.

"And I would like to say something about it that is proportionate," he says, "and I have been working on it for about eleven weeks, and everything I have got is either three words long or forty minutes long, and I am going to go with the three words, which are that I had not got it, and that I have got it, and — that is five words. I have miscounted. I am now going to stop."

There is a silence.

Then Pascual gets up on one elbow and turns the lamp on, which is not what Reggie wanted, and looks down at him with his whole face going, and says:

"Say it again."

"Absolutely not."

"*Reggie.*"

"I've said it once and it took eleven weeks and you may have it once."

"You have just told me," Pascual says, "that in three and a half years nobody ever put a hand on you afterwards. Not once. *Ay*, and you did not even tell me that; you told me you hadn't known it was a thing that was done, which is worse, and I don't think you can hear how much worse it is." He is not shouting and it is very close. "And then you lay in this bed since December working out how to thank me for it. You got it down to three words and then you miscounted."

"Because it costs you nothing," Reggie says. "That's the whole of the difficulty, and I'd rather say it than have you work it out. You do it going to sleep. Half the time you aren't awake for it. It's the cheapest thing in this house, and I've spent eleven weeks composing a speech about a thing you're not even present for, and I'm aware of how that looks, and I'd like the lamp out."

And Pascual does not put the lamp out.

"No," he says.

"Pascual —"

"*No.* Listen to me, because you've got it wrong, and you've had it wrong since December, and I'm not having you carry it about for another night." He gets his hand round the back of Reggie's neck, which is not gentle. "It isn't cheap. It isn't a thing I do for you. *Ay, Dios mío*, Reggie, it's the best part. It's the part I *want*. If you told me tomorrow that you didn't like it I'd stop that night and I'd mind it for the rest of my life, and that's the whole difference, do you understand me? I'm not being kind to you. I'm *having* something. Every time. And I've been having it since December, and you've been lying here thinking you were being done a favour."

---

And that finishes him.

Not the hand, and not the eleven weeks, and not anything he had braced for. He had built the whole of 1951 on a premise so sound that he had never once examined it — that what a man gets is what somebody consents to give him; that the arithmetic therefore runs one way only; and that his own job is to keep the bill small. Everything he has been since Devon rests on it.

And there is a man in this bed telling him, at volume, with a hand round the back of his neck, that the transaction has been running the other way as well, from the beginning, in quantity, and that he has been too stupid to notice.

Reggie Tar does not say anything at all, because there is nothing in him to say it with. He gets one hand up and takes hold of the front of Pascual's shirt, which is not on him, and so takes hold of nothing, and Pascual catches the hand and keeps it.

---

Pascual watches the whole of it happen from about eleven inches away with the lamp still on.

And what he feels, and he'll remember the exact sensation for the rest of his life, isn't tenderness. It arrives about a second and a half later than it should and it's enormous and it's entirely physical, and it's that he'd like to get on a boat and go to Massachusetts and find a man in a laboratory and take him apart with his hands. Not for the coldness. There wasn't any coldness; he's been over that and over it. For the *waste* — for three and a half years of a man who'd come apart like this, reliably, at the first indication that anybody wanted him, sitting eleven feet down a corridor with the door off its hinges, unopened, because nobody ever thought to look.

That isn't available. It never was and it isn't going to be.

What's available is to put the lamp out, and lie down, and pull him in, and be here for the next thirty years, every single time, until the man stops being astonished by it.

It takes about nine years.

---

There are two things in that house that do not go away, and both of them are his, and he sets them down because he is a man who writes things in columns.

The first is that he cannot be astonished by the idea of being left.

In another life — he has no evidence for this and is certain of it anyway — there is a version of Reggie Tar who would look at Pascual Pobil across a kitchen table in 1958 and be honestly amazed to hear that the man was frightened of losing him; who would say something about the tide, and mean it, and be astonished twice a year for five years, and be a fool about it, and be loved for it.

That is not available to him. He has watched it happen. He knows precisely how a man is given the whole of another man and does not notice the transaction, and he has the figures, and the question can absolutely be asked and he has never once found it surprising.

So what he gives Pascual instead, on the second night, at the kitchen table, in about eleven words, is the thing that in that other life would take him until February of 1959 and a doorway at twenty to twelve:

"It isn't a property. It's a choice, and I've seen what it looks like when somebody chooses the other way, and I am going to choose you every day for thirty years and say so out loud on the days it's hard."

And Pascual puts both hands over his face and does not take them away for some time, and afterwards says that it is worth more than anything else anybody has ever offered him and that he would like it in writing.

The second thing is Pascual's and is not Reggie's to write down, and he writes it down anyway, in the back of the same notebook, in 1959, because he has noticed it and will not pretend otherwise:

*P. is jealous of a man in Massachusetts who never knew he had me and never will, and has told me so once, in an office, in 1956, as item three of four, and has never raised it again and never will, and minds it every year. That is a thing I did to him before I met him. It does not come off the sheet.*

---

## November 1977

The telegram comes to the Viale Aventino at twenty past nine on the fifteenth.

*DR SPILSBURY DIED EARLY MONDAY AT HIS DESK. HEART. HE LEFT WORD THAT YOU ARE TO HAVE THE BOX. I WILL BRING IT MYSELF. ALDRIDGE.*

Pascual is home from Naples by seven, because Mrs van Wijk telephoned, and puts the bag down and sits at the table across from him and does not say anything at all, and Reggie reads the Bergen returns for another hour with the man sitting there and does not once ask him to go.

---

Aldridge comes on the twenty-first with two shoe boxes and an envelope out of the wallet.

There are one thousand one hundred and six cards. He reads them over nine nights. There are ninety-one under B and nineteen under M, *things decided against*, and a card near the front of the second box that says *POBIL — asks what it is for —* with the dash he used when a thing was not solved.

Under R there are forty-one.

*R.T. — 31 — resigned. Three months, no post, mid-series. — Would not give a reason. — Gave one and I did not take it. — Not the labs. — He said it twice.* That one is dated June 1950 and has been gone back to, by the ink, five separate times, the last of them in 1974.

*R.T. — Plymouth. Good. — Does not write about anything but the work. — Deliberate?* 1952.

*R.T. — Pobil. — He has somebody. Good. — Should have expected this and did not. Why not?* December 1956.

And the last one, dated March 1977, eight months ago: *R.T. — Best appointment I ever made. Did not make it for that reason.*

---

He opens the envelope on the ninth night.

There is one card in it, soft at the corners and gone a little brown, and it has been in a man's wallet for twenty-seven years, next to a driving licence and a photograph of his mother.

*R.T. — 1950 — suit yourself. —*

The dash. Unsolved.

---

He sits at the table under the window in Testaccio at about one in the morning and looks at it for a long time.

Twenty-seven years. A man who never in his life let an option go by unrecorded, who had four hundred and six cards in a shoe box by 1958 and one thousand one hundred and six by the end, carried that one about in his wallet, next to his mother, through Boston and Bergen and Rome and every committee either of them ever sat on, and got no further with it than he had got on the eleventh of June 1950 in his shirtsleeves at twenty past eight with the marmalade still on the table.

He wanted to know what those two words had been for.

He never found out. He was not able to. There was nothing in him for it to reach; he said so himself, in a different world, in the dark outside a bungalow, and was fifty-four and knew it as a fact and not as a boast.

And he kept the card.

---

Reggie gets up and goes through to the other room, where a man of sixty-four is lying with the door open, which is not the same as being asleep and never has been.

"Say it," says Pascual.

So Reggie says it. All of it, badly, out of order, standing in a doorway in his shirt at one in the morning with a card in his hand, and has to go back twice.

Pascual listens to the whole of it.

Then he holds his hand out for the card and reads it, and turns it over, and finds the back blank, and reads the front again.

"*Ay*," he says.

"Yes."

"Twenty-seven years."

"Twenty-seven years."

Pascual gives it back. He is not crying and his face is doing everything, and what settles on it, at the end, is not triumph and is not pity and is the thing Reggie has been reading off that face since a rail in February of 1953.

"I was jealous of that man for twenty-one years," he says. "I told you so once in an office in 1956 and I've never said it again, because I said I wouldn't, and I'm saying it tonight because tonight it's finished." He puts both hands flat on the blanket. "He had you and he didn't know what he'd got. I've known exactly what I've got every single day since the twenty-sixth of December 1956, including the bad ones, and I've never once had to be told. There's nothing in that box to be jealous of. There's a man who kept a card in his pocket for twenty-seven years because he couldn't work out why somebody left him, and I'd have written him the answer in 1957 on the back of a feed sheet in four words."

"What four words?"

"*He wanted to be asked*," says Pascual Pobil. "Come to bed."
