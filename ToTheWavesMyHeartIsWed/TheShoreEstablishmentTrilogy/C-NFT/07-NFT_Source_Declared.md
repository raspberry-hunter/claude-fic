# Source Declared

The flat is on the third floor of a building in Testaccio with no lift, and it has two rooms and a kitchen and a bathroom with a geyser that makes a noise like a mortar, and it has a window.

Reggie measured the window in October. He came out to Rome for four days to sign the lease and he stood in the empty front room with a rule and measured it, sill to lintel and jamb to jamb, and wrote the figures in the back of his diary, and has not mentioned them to a living soul.

Pascual comes up the stairs on the eighth of January behind the porter's son with the second trunk, and puts his bag down in the front room, and stands there.

The window is five feet by three and a half and it looks over the roofs of the market to a line of cypresses and a hill, and at four in the afternoon in January the light comes through it flat and gold across the whole length of the floor.

"It's a window," Pascual says.

"The Colonel said a window."

Pascual does not say anything else for a moment. Then he goes and puts both hands on the sill and leans on them and looks out at a city he has never been to, over a market he cannot name, in a language he does not speak, and his shoulders go down about an inch, which is the thing Reggie measured the window for.

"*Ay, Dios mío*," Pascual says, to the glass. "You measured it."

"I did not —"

"You measured it. You stood in this room in October with a rule." He does not turn round. "I can see the pencil mark. It's on the jamb."

---

The porter's wife is called Signora Bruni and she speaks Roman, which is not Italian, and Pascual speaks Spanish, which is not Italian either, and by the end of the first week the two of them are conducting long, energetic, mutually incomprehensible conversations on the stairs about the geyser, the price of artichokes and the moral character of the tenant on the second floor, and appear to understand each other perfectly.

Reggie has had Italian since school and speaks it correctly and slowly, like a man reading a menu, and Signora Bruni is courteous to him and does not like him at all.

"She says you are *freddo*," Pascual reports, coming up with the bread.

"I am not cold."

"She says you're cold and I'm a saint for putting up with you." Pascual puts the bread down. "It took her six days. Mrs Hanley took a fortnight. The standard of observation in this city is extremely high."

---

The International Commission for Captive Sirenians occupies two floors of a building on the Viale Aventino lent by the Italian government, with eleven flags in the hall, a Dutch deputy called Mrs van Wijk who is the most frighteningly competent person Reggie has met since Hester Bevan, a registry, a typing pool of four, and a view, from the Director-General's window, of a wall.

"It's a wall," Pascual says, on the first day.

"It is a very old wall."

"You've given me the window and taken the wall."

"I don't need a window," says Reggie. "I've got the returns."

---

He has the returns.

The schedule is Hester's schedule, grown up. Every holding in eleven countries files quarterly with the registry on the Viale Aventino, on a form with the columns ruled and the headings printed, and the form has got a column for *observations not reducible to figures* that Chettle asked for in 1956, and it has got a column headed *Source*, with three sub-columns: *water*, *vessel*, *date taken*.

Condition three, June 1955, on the back of a spoiled sheet, in a small room with an iron cot.

He built that column because he wanted to know where the animals were coming from. It will now tell anyone who can read a table where a great many things are, and it is his, and he knew in October at a kitchen table on the north side of a harbour what it was going to show in March.

---

He writes Schedule C himself, in February, and it takes him three weeks, and it is the most careful piece of drafting he has ever done.

Schedule C is closed waters. It says where no animal may be taken out of the water, by anybody, under any licence, for any purpose, including survey.

Some of it is easy, because Jean has done it for him. Her four hundred miles of the delta give him three breeding grounds with animals counted in the water, and he closes all three on her plates, by name, with her figures in the annex, and it is the first thing the Commission does that will keep an animal in the sea on the strength of evidence, and he is extremely happy about it for a whole afternoon.

And then there is the other water.

He could name it. Pascual said at the kitchen table in October that the circle is drawn and the man who matters has already read it, and Pascual was right. But Spilsbury is not the only man who will read Schedule C. The broker will read it. Every boat out of Cedros will have it read to them by somebody. A named closure of the eastern mouths behind Pedernales, with no plates and no counts and no reason given, will tell every one of them that the Commission knows something about that water, and a man paid by the head is very interested in what a Commission knows.

So he does not name it.

He writes a rule instead. *Precautionary closure: any water from which no animal has been declared taken in the five seasons preceding the date of this schedule shall be closed, pending survey, and no licence for survey shall issue except on the unanimous recommendation of the Scientific Committee.*

There are nine such waters on the coast between the Gulf of Paria and the Pomeroon. The broker's boats gave up on seven of them for the ordinary reasons, which are mud, and distance, and sandbars that have moved. The rule closes all nine. It names none. It is defensible on every line and it is genuinely good conservation, because a water nobody has fished in five years is a water nobody knows anything about, and it hides one channel among eight others that deserve closing on their own account.

And the last clause — *unanimous recommendation of the Scientific Committee* — means that no survey licence for any of the nine can issue if one man on that committee says no.

He shows it to Pascual at the table in the flat, on the twenty-sixth of February, before it goes to the typing pool.

Pascual reads it twice with his glasses on.

"*Unanimous*," he says.

"Yes."

"There are seven men on that committee."

"There are. One of them is Bassani, who would not license a survey of a water he could not see the point of if the Pope asked him to, and another is Chettle, who would ask me why before he voted on anything, and would take my answer." Reggie takes his glasses off. "And one of them is the chairman."

"And he'll vote for it."

"He will vote for it," Reggie says, "because it closes the water to everyone else."

---

Pascual shuts his first holding on the nineteenth of February, in Marseille, with the badge.

He comes back on the twenty-second on the night train and comes up the three flights with his bag and puts it down in the front room under the window and sits down on the floor, in his coat, with his back against the wall, and laughs until he has to wipe his face.

"A private aquarium," he says. "Very grand. Chandeliers. Eleven tanks and a marble floor. Registered with the French government in November. And a female, three years in a pen, on her own in a back room behind the seal tank for *quarantine*, since August. Since *August*, Reggie. The director showed me the form. He'd filled it in beautifully. *Quarantine* in the observations column, every week, in green ink."

"What did you do?"

"I stood in the back room and I looked at her going round the bottom of that tank, and I took the badge out of my pocket and I put it on the edge of the tank where he could see it, and I told him in Spanish that if she wasn't in the main tank with his male by six o'clock that evening I'd close every tank in the building and he could sell his chandeliers." Pascual wipes his eyes with the back of his wrist. "He didn't understand one word. He understood the badge. He cried. She was in with the male by five. She took fish at half past."

He sits on the floor under the window.

"It's quite a small badge," he says. "It works."

Reggie sits down on the floor beside him, in his good trousers, with his back against the same wall, and does not say anything for some time, and does not need to.

---

The first returns come in during the last week of March.

He reads them himself. He said in April of 1956, at a pen rail in the dark, that what the whole business wanted was somebody who read the returns the week they came in, and he has now got a registry and a typing pool and Mrs van Wijk to do exactly that, and he reads every one of them himself anyway, in the office with the wall, over nine days, and Mrs van Wijk watches him do it and says nothing and, on the fourth day, begins bringing him coffee.

Two hundred and six animals in eleven countries. Nineteen deaths in the quarter, of which eleven would have been avoided by anybody who had done the eight-week course, and he writes to all eleven holdings himself. Four pregnancies. One of them Mabel's.

And in the *Source* column, the broker's declared takes for the 1961 season, water by water, and nine waters with a nought against them for five seasons running, and one of them the eastern mouths behind Pedernales, in a list, in a published table, under a precautionary rule, over his own signature.

He looks at that nought for a long time.

---

In April a manuscript comes to the Commission from the editor of a journal in London, for an opinion, because the editor does not know what to do with it.

It is a study of graduated separation. It was done at a private station with a great deal of money in a country that is not one of the eleven, and the design is almost exactly the one Reggie wrote on page twenty-three of the letter to Lisbon in October of 1959 and took out again at a kitchen table. The animal moved at night by a man who never attends the rail. The subject scored before he is told anything. The distances drawn by lot.

Except for the one thing Pascual's paragraphs say cannot be got round, which is that the animal can still be heard. They got round it with a wall. It did not work.

The results are uninterpretable. The editor has already written the rejection. He quotes pages thirty-one and thirty-two in it, and the man he quotes by name is Pobil.

Reggie reads the methods section with a pencil and counts the animals. There were four. There are two.

He writes that figure down on a slip of paper and puts it in the drawer with the Whitley return, and does not show it to anybody for three days; and on the third day he shows it to Pascual at the table in the flat, because that is the arrangement, and Pascual reads the rejection and the figure and puts his hands flat on the table.

"It's stopped," Pascual says.

"It's stopped. Nobody will print it. The editor has said so in a letter I'm going to have copied to every journal on the list." Reggie takes his glasses off. "It took two animals. It took two animals to find out it was worthless, and your paragraphs are why it's worthless, and it's going to be worthless for everybody who tries it after him. I think it's the last."

"It isn't the last."

"No. It isn't. But it is the last one anybody will ever be able to publish."

Pascual looks at the figure on the slip for a while.

"Put it in the drawer," he says.

---

The Scientific Committee meets for the first time on the twenty-first of May, and its chairman arrives from Massachusetts on the Sunday, and on the Monday at ten o'clock Alec Spilsbury is sitting at the head of a long table on the Viale Aventino with seven men and a carafe of water and Schedule C in front of him.

He moves it. He moves the whole of it, all three closures on Jean's plates and the precautionary rule and the unanimity clause, in about four minutes, from the chair, and it goes through without a word against, because Chettle asks one question about the definition of *season* and is answered by Reggie in a sentence, and Bassani says it is the best schedule he has ever read and looks at Reggie across the table in a way Reggie will think about later.

It is in the afternoon session, over the returns, that Spilsbury is warm at him.

---

They are going through the tabulation. Reggie built it himself: two hundred and six animals down the left, and across the top the things that matter, and at the right the source columns; and a second sheet that he did not have to build, which sets the declared takes by water against the closures in Schedule C, so that a man can see at a glance which closure touches which boat.

Spilsbury looks at the second sheet for about a minute and a half.

Then he looks up, down the length of the table, past six men, and says:

"That's beautiful, Tar. And the precautionary rule's the best piece of drafting in the schedule. It closes the one water you want closed without ever having to say which. Mrs Bevan couldn't have done it better." He turns the page. "Right. Naples. Bassani, your intake columns."

---

And it lands.

It lands exactly where it has always landed, in the same place under the breastbone, and it is warm, and for about four seconds Reggie Tar is twenty-seven and standing in a corridor with a plate in his hand, and the rest of the long table on the Viale Aventino goes somewhere a long way off.

And then — and this is the part he will try to describe to Pascual at half past seven, and get about half of — it goes on landing, and he is still there, watching it.

He can see it. That is what is different, and he has not had it before. He sits at the table with his pen in his hand and feels the whole of the old thing come in like a tide over a flat, and at the same time, from somewhere a little further up the beach, he watches it come in; and he watches the man at the head of the table turn the page to Naples; and he hears, as clearly as if it had been said in another voice, the second half of the sentence.

*It closes the one water you want closed without ever having to say which.*

He has just been told, in front of six men, warmly, in a compliment, with Mrs Bevan's name in it for good measure, that Alec Spilsbury knows exactly what the precautionary rule is for.

And the warmth does not stop because of that. It goes on being warm. The two things are in the room together, the warm one and the one with a blade in it, and Reggie sits there with both of them for the rest of the Naples intake columns, and does not do anything at all.

---

He goes back to the flat at half past seven. He has been going over it since four.

Pascual is at the stove with the geyser making its noise and the window gold, and he turns round at the door, and looks at him, and puts the spoon down.

"Sit down," he says.

"I'd rather stand."

"*Ay.* Then stand." Pascual wipes his hands. "Go on."

"He was warm at me this afternoon. In the committee. In front of six men." Reggie has his hands behind his back and he is looking at the window and not at Pascual, which he has been told he does and cannot stop. "About the tabulation. He said it was beautiful. And then he said the precautionary rule was the best drafting in the schedule, and that it closed the one water I wanted closed without having to say which."

Pascual goes still.

"So he told you he knows."

"He told me he knows, and he told me in front of Bassani, and he put Hester's name in it, and it was a compliment, and it was true." Reggie takes a breath. "And I want to tell you the other half, because that's the arrangement, and because it's different, and I don't know yet whether different is better."

---

"It landed," he says. "The way it lands. Same place. Same size. I was twenty-seven for about four seconds at a table on the Viale Aventino, and I'd like that recorded, because I'm forty-three and it's 1962 and I'd hoped by now it would have got smaller, and it hasn't got smaller. It's exactly the size it was on the seventh of December 1958."

"Yes," says Pascual.

"And I could see it."

He turns round.

"That's new. I sat there and it came in and I watched it come in. From further up the beach. It didn't stop being warm. But I could see the man at the head of the table while it was happening, and I could see what the sentence was for, both halves of it, in the same four seconds, and I've never once had that. In 1958 I stood in the office and took it in like water and had to go and hold on to the wall of the store, and I didn't know what the sentence was for until you told me eleven words of it in the kitchen. This afternoon I knew what it was for before he'd turned the page." He puts his hands behind his back again. "It's the same size. I'm further off. I don't know what to call that."

Pascual looks at him for a long moment.

"I'd call it four years," he says.

"Is it better?"

"I don't know, Reggie. I'll tell you in another four." He comes round the stove, and wipes his hands again on the cloth, which he does not need to do, and puts one of them on the side of Reggie's face. "You came up three flights of stairs and stood in that doorway and said it. On the day. Going red. That's the part I know about."

Reggie puts his hand up to answer it.

"He's going to ask me for something," he says, against the palm. "Tomorrow. Or Wednesday. He's not in Rome for the committee. He could have chaired that committee by post. He said the thing about the precautionary rule so that I'd know, before he asks, that he knows. So I'd come to it with nothing to hide behind."

"*Sí*," says Pascual. "I know. Eat first."

---

Spilsbury asks for an hour on the Wednesday evening, and asks for it not in the office but in the Giardino degli Aranci, on the Aventine, which is ten minutes' walk from the Viale and has a terrace with a parapet and orange trees in rows and a view of the whole of Rome going gold and then blue, and which is, Reggie thinks, walking up the hill at a quarter to seven, exactly the kind of place a man would choose if he wanted the other man to feel the size of what he was being offered.

Spilsbury is at the parapet with his hat off.

"Tar."

"Spilsbury."

"Look at that." He does not turn round. "I've sat on committees in this city eleven times since 1938 and I've never once come up here. Fifty-six years old and a man from the Commission had to tell me about it. Bassani, as it happens. He said it was where Romans come to propose marriage and I said I'd bear it in mind." He turns round. "I'll be quick, and I'll be complete, and you'll want to set every word of it beside what you know of me, so I'll try to make that easy."

---

He does it in about seven minutes. Reggie will be able to recite most of it for the rest of his life.

"Cholmondeley's alive. You know that. I know that. I've known since November of 'sixty; you've known since Dr Leslie's letter, which I'd guess was October of last year from the date the precautionary rule turned up in the drafts. He's in the eastern mouths behind Pedernales with the animal he went in with and that animal's people, and he's been there since the summer of 1954, and he's been cutting the broker's nets since 1955, and the boats won't go in and call him the drowned man.

"He was twenty-four when he went in. He's thirty-two. He's been living in a mangrove delta for eight years without a doctor or a roof, on whatever a man can get off the edge of a fishing village by trade, and I've looked up what the delta does to Europeans who try it, and if he's alive at forty it'll be a very remarkable thing.

"So here's what I propose.

"One survey licence. For one of the nine precautionary waters. Under the Commission's own authority, led by me, with a Commission officer present — you, or Pobil, or both, and I'd prefer both. Its stated purpose is to establish whether a British subject presumed drowned in 1954 is living in distress among a wild population in a closed water, and if he is, to offer him a place.

"And there is a place. Mrs Bevan wrote it. Article nine of the Martin's deed: a post for life for any member of staff found to have formed an attachment to an animal, the animal's company at his own, not to be withdrawn for cause. He was a member of staff. The animal comes in under licence to the reference station — which already holds a pair and a pregnant female and would gain its second male, which I'd point out doesn't hurt Mrs Bevan's clause in the slightest. He gets a legal life, a roof, a doctor, the company of the animal, and for as long as he lives nobody can move either of them.

"And nobody will ask about the certificate. Men presumed drowned in these waters turn up on beaches alive every decade, cast away and half mad, and the Colonial Office has a form for it. I'll write the report myself. It'll say what happened in the plainest terms and it'll be entirely defensible and there won't be a line in it anybody could use against Pobil or Mrs Bevan or you. I've thought about that part longer than any other, and you may have the draft tonight if you want it.

"And then there's what I want.

"I want him. I'll not dress that up for you: I want him somewhere I can talk to him, for as long as he'll talk, for years if he'll give me years. Not a series, not a reel, not a card. I read the passage out of his Andersen in 1954 with you and Clarke standing there, and I got into that pen at half past five and got nothing, and I've spent eight years trying to find out what he had, and he's the only man alive who can tell me. I'd like to sit in a room with him. That's my price, and it's the only thing I want out of any of this, and I'd like it understood that I'd be doing the rest in any case if I could find a way to do it without you."

He puts his hat back on.

"That's all. You'll want to take it home. I'd think less of you if you didn't."

---

Reggie stands at the parapet with the whole of Rome going blue below it and does not say anything for some while.

The terrible thing about it is not that it is clever, though it is. It is not that it is generous, though it is that too: every single piece of it has been built to protect somebody — Pascual, Hester, the certificate, the clause — and built well, by a man who has spent a long time on it.

The terrible thing is that he wants it.

Not the way he wanted the loud end, in 1959, at a kitchen table, with his hands flat. He has been ready for that want since the Wednesday and it has not come. What has come instead, and come up through him so hard that he has had to put his hands on the parapet, is a man of thirty-two in a mangrove delta with no doctor and no roof; and a boy who could not remember to eat, whom Pascual had to send to bed; and a post for life, and a roof, and a doctor, and the animal's company, and nobody able to move either of them.

He wants that the way a man wants a person he is responsible for to be safe. And it is a far worse want to be offered than the other one, because there is nothing in it he would be ashamed of.

"I'll answer you tomorrow," he says. "Here. At seven."

"Take him home," says Spilsbury. "I mean that too."

---

He takes it home.

He says all of it standing up in the front room of the flat under the window with the lamp not lit, in order, in about eleven minutes, and Pascual sits at the table and listens to all of it without once interrupting, and at the end Reggie says: "And I want it. Not the way I wanted to see the loud end in 'fifty-nine. I want him to have a roof and a doctor and nobody able to move him, and I'd like to go and get him, and I'm telling you so on the day, because it's the worst want I've had in eight years and there isn't one piece of it I'm ashamed of."

"Sit down," Pascual says.

Reggie sits down.

Pascual gets the notebook out — not the feed book, which is on a shelf four thousand miles away, but a black notebook he has been keeping the inspections in — and opens it flat on the table and puts his hands on it, and does not write anything, because that is what the notebook is for tonight, and they both know it.

"Say what it's for," Pascual says.

---

"It's for Spilsbury," Reggie says.

"Yes. What else."

"It's for — it's so that Charles has a roof."

"Did he ask for a roof?"

Reggie does not answer.

"Reggie." Pascual has not raised his voice. "He packed a knapsack. You told me that at the table on the north side in October of 'fifty-seven, and you told me you'd mislaid it for three and a half years, and I'm not going to let you mislay it again in a flat in Rome. That boy put food and water and a knife and a tin of zinc paste into a knapsack about a week before he went, without asking anybody, and he walked into the sea with an animal on the twentieth of May. With your chart. And he got there. And he's been cutting nets out of that water for seven years so that nobody could do to that animal's people what was done to that animal." He takes his hands off the notebook. "A roof is what *we* would want for him. A doctor is what I would want. I'd give a year of my life to get my hands on that boy and look at his feet and his teeth and make him eat. And he didn't ask me, and he didn't ask you, and he didn't ask Mrs Bevan. And nobody's asked *him*. Not one of us. Least of all the man in a hotel on the Via Nazionale who's built a roof and a doctor and a draft report about a certificate for somebody he's never said one word to."

---

"And if he's dying?" Reggie says. "In the delta. At thirty-two. If he's ill, and on his own at the edge of some village, and it would save him —"

"Then he'll come out," says Pascual. "He came out of a hut and walked across a cay to the sea with a grown animal on a tarpaulin in the dark because he'd decided to. If that man decides he needs a doctor he'll find one. And if he doesn't decide it, it's not ours to decide." He is quiet for a moment. "I hate that. I want you to know I hate that more than anything I've said at a table since the broker's letter. I've been a doctor for twenty-six years and everything in me wants to go and get him. And it's still not ours."

Reggie sits with his hands on his knees.

"No," he says. "It isn't."

"Then we say no."

"We say no." He takes his glasses off. "And he'll go anyway."

---

"He'll go anyway," Pascual says. "Yes. Not with a licence, because the licence needs seven votes and one of them's Bassani, and after this afternoon one of them's you. He'll go as a private man on a holiday. To Cedros. And he'll try to hire a boat to take him across the Serpent's Mouth into a closed water."

"And no boat out of Cedros will go into that water. Jean tried five."

"No boat Jean offered money to." Pascual turns the notebook round on the table. "Jean's a woman from Florida with a permit. He's a man who bought eleven years of consignment records for forty pounds. He'll find the boat that goes. There's always one. And the boat that goes into that water for a gringo with money is a boat that's been waiting eight years for somebody to pay it to go back in, and it'll have a net in the bottom of it."

The geyser does what it does. Down in the square a tram goes by.

"So we have to tell Cedros," Reggie says. "And we can't tell Cedros. A letter is a record. A cable is a record. An enquiry by the Commission's Director-General about a closed water in its first year is the largest record I could possibly make."

"Nestor's cousin Tito has fished out of Cedros every dry season since the war," says Pascual.

Reggie looks at him.

"And Nestor's wife's sister is married to a man at Icacos," Pascual says, "which is the next village along. And I'm the Chief Veterinary Inspector of the International Commission, and I've got an inspection of the Caribbean holdings to do in July, and one of the holdings is the Martin, and I'll be sitting on a step at twenty to six in the morning with a man I've known nine years, speaking Spanish." He shuts the notebook. "And nothing'll be written down anywhere. Not by me, not by Nestor, not by Tito. It'll be a thing said on a jetty. That's how that coast has kept a drowned man for eight years without anybody asking it to. We're only going to ask it to go on."

---

Spilsbury is at the parapet at seven on the Thursday with his hat in his hand.

Reggie has thought, walking up, about how to do it, and has decided that he will not do it well. There has been a great deal of doing things well in this business, over eight years, on both sides, and it has not once helped.

"No," he says.

"Give me the reason."

"The reason is that he didn't ask. He packed a knapsack. He went with a chart. He has been in that water eight years by his own choice and nobody has the smallest standing to decide he'd be better in a pen, and that includes me, and it includes you, and it includes Hester Bevan's article, which she wrote for people to whom the thing had happened and who wanted a place, and not for fetching them in."

He hears his voice go flat and level and lets it.

"I will not license a survey of any of the nine waters under the precautionary rule. I will vote against it in committee and the committee is unanimous or nothing. I will not come with you and I will not send Pobil, and I will not tell you I am sorry about the certificate report, because it is a very good report and I have no doubt at all that you would have written it exactly as you say. That is the whole of it. I am not going to be moved on it and I would rather you did not ask a second time, because I have found in the last eight years that I am able to refuse you, and I have found this week that I would do it every day."

---

Spilsbury looks at him for a long time, in the last of the light.

Then he says a thing that Reggie has not expected, and it is not an argument.

"I don't understand it."

"No."

"I'm not asking you to make me. I'm telling you as a datum." He turns his hat over. "In November of 1958 you sat at the end of a verandah table on your own, in the dark, and I put my four sentences in front of you, and you took them. By yourself. You didn't send for anybody and you didn't say you'd answer me in the morning. I watched you do it and I thought — I'll tell you what I thought, it's not my business, I'm going to anyway — I thought, *there, he's come into his own.* Out from under. I thought well of it. I went up that path pleased about it." He puts the hat on. "And this is the best offer anybody's ever put in front of you, on every single axis you care about, including the ones you'd be ashamed to say, and you've taken it home, and come back, and refused it, and I'd stake the whole box that you didn't refuse it on your own."

"No," Reggie says. "I didn't."

"Then I don't understand it."

"I know. You wouldn't." Reggie puts his hands behind his back. "In November of 1958 I didn't take your four sentences on my own. I took them wrongly. And I paid for it for nine weeks, in a house, and so did somebody else, and I'm not going to tell you about that, and it's the reason you don't understand this. You saw a man come into his own. You were looking at a man making the worst mistake of his life, and you went up the path pleased."

---

The lamps are coming on all down the hill.

"Right," says Spilsbury, after a while, quite pleasantly. "Then I'll take a holiday. Trinidad's said to be very agreeable in July."

"You gave me your word in November 1957 that you wouldn't go round me on him."

"I gave you my word that I wouldn't put two lines back on your list. I haven't. He isn't on your list. He's in the Commission's returns." Spilsbury looks at the view once more. "I shan't take him if he doesn't want to come, Tar. You may have that on my word, for what you tell me it's worth. Of course I want him. I've wanted him since a crate in a hut, and you know it, and I'd have thought a good deal less of you if you'd imagined anything else. But a man can't be made to want to come by being asked from Rome. He has to be asked to his face. A man may stand on a jetty in Cedros and look at the water. There's no schedule on earth that closes a jetty."

"Then I'm asking you," Reggie says. "Not on the list. Not on your word. I'm asking you not to go."

Spilsbury considers it. He considers it properly, for about as long as he considered anything Reggie ever brought through the door that did not shut, and Reggie watches him do it, and watches him get to the end.

"No," says Spilsbury. "Good night, Tar. Give my regards to Pobil. I imagine he was the one who said the boy packed a knapsack."

And he goes down the hill.
