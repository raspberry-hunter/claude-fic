# Provision

The letter to Lisbon is thirty-one pages long, and he reads the whole of it aloud at the kitchen table on the fifteenth of October, as agreed, and Pascual lets him get as far as page twenty-three.

It has taken him three weeks. He has written it exactly as he was told to write it, which is long and dull and accurate, with every figure in it and the derivation of every figure, and he has read it through twice at the office and once in the boat four miles off the north reef with the engine cut. It is, he is reasonably sure, the most complete statement of the position that anybody has put on paper, and he has been looking forward to reading it aloud for a week in a manner he is aware is not entirely creditable.

Pascual sits across from him with his reading glasses on and his arms folded and does not interrupt for twenty-two pages.

He does not interrupt the summary of the contact series. He does not interrupt the section on the 1954 table, or the eleven paragraphs on intake lag, or the passage on the non-blindability of complete separation, which is his own and which Reggie reads at a slightly slower pace than the rest, on purpose, and watches him not react to. He takes his glasses off once, at page nineteen, and puts them back on.

At page twenty-three he says, "Read that again."

---

Reggie reads it again.

It is the paragraph on graduated separation, and it is the best paragraph in the letter. It sets out why the design Dr Teixeira has proposed will not serve: that a man at the rail can hear an animal in the next pen as well as see it; that he knows the schedule; that his scores will carry his knowledge of the distance as surely as they would carry his knowledge of an empty pen. And then — because the problem is a good problem and Reggie Tar has never in his life been able to leave a good problem half-solved on the table — it sets out, in four clear sentences, the three conditions under which a graduated design *would* be interpretable. The animal moved by night, by a man who never attends the rail. The subject scored before he is told anything at all. The distances drawn by lot and sealed.

"Now read me the part where you tell him not to," Pascual says.

"It's on page twenty-four."

"I heard page twenty-four. Page twenty-four says it's inadvisable." Pascual unfolds his arms. "Page twenty-three is a methods section. You've spent three weeks writing a man a refusal and in the middle of it you've designed his study for him, properly, better than he could do it himself, with the confound taken out. Every word of it is true. He'll have it pinned over his bench by Christmas."

Reggie looks at the page.

He can see it perfectly well. That is the unpleasant thing. He can see it the way one sees a column of figures that has been added wrong in one's own hand, and he can see precisely the moment at which he wrote it: a Tuesday afternoon, the office, the problem suddenly giving at the joint, and a feeling in the chest like a window opening, and four sentences going down on the page before any other part of him had been consulted.

"Yes," he says.

"Don't say yes like that."

"I'm not saying it like anything. It's a methods section. I wrote it because the question was interesting and I found the answer, and I didn't stop to ask what the answer was for, and you've caught it in about eleven minutes." He takes his own glasses off. "Pages twenty-three to twenty-seven come out."

"All five?"

"Twenty-five and six build on it. Twenty-seven is a joke about Uppsala that I'm not prepared to lose, but it rests on twenty-six, so it'll have to go too." He is already folding them. "What goes in?"

"What it's *for*." Pascual leans back. "Not what it would show. He's asked you what it would show and you've told him. Tell him what it's for, and then tell him there isn't anything."

---

It goes in as one paragraph and it takes him forty minutes, at the kitchen table, with Pascual reading the paper and not looking at him.

*The only question a graduated design can answer is how far an animal must be taken from its companion before a man at the rail can feel the difference. I have tried for a fortnight to think of an animal in any holding in the world who would be better off in any year for that answer, and I have not found one, and I do not believe Dr Teixeira will find one either. I will not supply a sentence for a methods section, and I should decline to referee the paper, and I should say why.*

He reads it out.

"Yes," says Pascual, from behind the paper.

"Is that all?"

"That's all. It's right. Four hundred words shorter and right, and you know it's right because you've gone red." He folds the paper. "Right. I'm finished."

And something in Reggie's chest goes over, the way it has been going over at those two words for ten months, all the way down, like a step that is not there in the dark.

He does not do anything about it. He has had ten months of practice in not doing anything about it and he is, by now, quite good. But Pascual has known that face since May of 1954, and he stops with the paper halfway to the dresser and looks at it.

"With the letter," he says. "*Ay.* With the letter, Reggie."

"I know."

"I know you know." He puts the paper down and comes round the table, and puts a hand on the back of Reggie's neck in passing, and leaves it there for perhaps three seconds, which is longer than passing, and then goes to put the kettle on.

Reggie sits with the twenty-six remaining pages squared in front of him and his neck warm and thinks: *four years, I said. I expect about four more years of that.* It is the fifteenth of October and he has, on current evidence, about three and a quarter to go.

---

Gamma's intake goes down eleven per cent in the last fortnight of October and does not come back up.

It is not a cliff. It is nothing like Pickersgill's column, or Alpha's, or any of the columns in the drawer. It is a slope so gentle that a man who was not looking for it would draw a straight line through it and call it seasonal, and Reggie looks for it every morning at eight with the book open on the office desk, and it is there every morning, and it is not seasonal.

Pascual says the same thing at the rail on the second of November, in the voice he keeps for owners.

"There's nothing wrong with her."

"No."

"Her bloods are clean. She has no infection I can find. Her surfacing is long and her heart's slow, and the calf does most of the moving in that pen and she lets him." He has his forearms on the top bar. "She's old. That's the whole of the finding, and there's no animal of her kind anybody has ever been able to call old, so I haven't got a word for what comes next, and I haven't got a curve."

"How long?"

"You asked me that about the Colonel."

"And you told Hester the truth."

"I told Hester I didn't know." Pascual watches the water. "It may be March."

Down at the shallow end Chumley comes along the wall at the speed he always comes along walls, which is too fast, and stops beside her, and puts something into her hand. Reggie cannot see what. Gamma closes her fingers on it and does not look at it, and the calf — he is not a calf; he has not been a calf for some years; he is bigger than she is by a foot and heavier by a great deal, and every person on the station still calls him the calf and will do until he dies — lies against her side with one arm over her back and stays there.

He used to bring her things four or five times a day. John has the plates. It is eleven times a day now, and Nestor has begun to write the number in the margin.

---

Reggie builds the table anyway, because that is what he is for.

He sets it up on the fourth of November in the ruled book, the way he set up the mortality tables in 1950 and the arrested-decline table in 1954: intake by day, residue by day, surfacing intervals three times a week, Pascual's weights on Sundays. He puts the column headings in. He dates the first line.

And then there is nothing to set against it.

That is the thing he sits with at the office desk, at nine at night, with the lamp. Every table he has ever built has had a second column, somewhere — another animal, another year, another holding, a published figure, a man in Whitley. This one has not. No mermaid has ever been old in a pen. Every row he writes is the only row of its kind that anybody on this earth possesses, and it describes an animal going gently down a slope towards a place nobody has a word for, and there is no comparison, and he cannot make one, and he is going to go on writing the rows.

He finds, somewhat to his surprise, that he minds this more than he has minded most things.

---

Nestor has been sleeping on the island two nights a week since the middle of October.

Nobody arranged it. He began coming across on the quarter past five launch in September, which is the one the fish come on, instead of the half past six, which is the one he is paid to come on; and then in October he stopped going home on the Tuesday and Thursday, and there is a sack and a blanket in the corner of the pen store, folded, and nobody on the station has mentioned it to him or to anybody else.

Reggie asks Pascual about it once, at home, in the dark.

"Does he know about the hand?"

"I told him in April of 'fifty-eight. The week after Hester came to the house." Pascual is lying on his back. "In Spanish. One sentence. That he wasn't to put his hand on the side of that animal's face, ever, for any reason, and that if the animal put a hand on his, he wasn't to answer it."

"What did he say?"

"He said *sí, doctor*, and went back to the weights." A pause. "And about four minutes later he said that his mother had told him the story of the little mermaid when he was six and he'd never liked it, and I said nor had I, and that was all."

"Does he know why?"

"He knows it's in the stories. He knows I don't say things like that about stories." Pascual turns over. "He doesn't know what it costs. Nor do I. There's one person on this island who does, and she has never once gone near him, and I don't think that's an accident."

---

The deed is in the second drawer of the office desk under the Whitley returns, where he put it in April, and he takes it out on the nineteenth of November at ten at night, for the first time in seven months, and reads article four.

He knows what it says. He read it twice at his own kitchen table in April of 1958 with Hester Bevan sitting opposite him and her tea going cold, and the only question he asked about any of the nine pages was where the money would come from. He did not argue article four. He did not so much as mark it. It seemed to him at the time a piece of excellent drafting in a document whose purpose he entirely shared, and he has had the purpose in his head for eighteen months and not the drafting, which is its own piece of information about him.

*Upon the death or loss of any specimen whereby the number of specimens in the care of the Institute falls below two, the Trustees shall forthwith, and in any event within one calendar month, take all lawful steps to procure a specimen to replace it. The Trustees shall act upon the notice of the Secretary. The Director shall not be consulted.*

He reads it three times.

*The Director shall not be consulted.* Six words, in Hester Bevan's hand, and not one of them wasted, and he sits in his own office at ten at night and understands that they were written with a precise and unsentimental knowledge of what he would be like on the morning after an animal he was fond of had died.

The Secretary of the Trust is Mrs H. Bevan. The Trustees are two solicitors and a retired Treasury man in Bridgetown who have never seen a mermaid and never will. The notice goes by cable.

*Lawful steps to procure.* Article one defines the term. *Obtainable* means lawfully obtainable from any source whatever, by purchase, exchange or transfer.

*Save where no specimen is obtainable.*

---

He does the transfer arithmetic first, because it is the arithmetic that costs nobody anything.

There are fourteen holdings on the schedule and thirty-one animals, and he has every return from every one of them in the drawer, and he goes through all fourteen at the desk with a pencil between ten and a quarter to one. Naples, a pair. Whitley, a pair. Florida, a pair. Pickersgill, a pair. Roque, a pair, late and correct. Bergen, two pairs and a juvenile. Lisbon, a pair, which is what started the letter. Four holdings he has never visited, pairs. Ames's ministry station, a pair. The Martin, a pair.

Every one of them intact. Every animal in every holding in the world is at this moment accompanied, which is the single best fact about the species that anybody has been able to state since the millionaire died, and it is the fact that shuts the door. There is not one widowed female on the schedule. There is nobody to transfer.

That leaves the other word. *Lawfully.*

A specimen is not lawfully obtainable from Port of Spain if the Colonial Office has suspended the licences under which the broker exports. Trinidad is a Crown colony with a governor and a gazette, and the licences are issued in the Governor's name, and they can be suspended by a notice in the gazette on the advice of the Secretary of State, and the Secretary of State takes advice on scientific questions from exactly one source, which since 1958 has been the interim committee on captive sirenians that the ministries set up to keep the chair warm for the Commission.

The interim committee is four men.

Reggie sits at the desk in the lamplight with a pencil in his hand and goes through the field in his head, the way he went through it in April of 1956 at a pen rail in the dark; and it is a much shorter exercise this time, because this time he knows the answer before he starts and is merely being thorough.

Three of the four men on that committee have dined with him. The fourth chairs it.

He puts the pencil down.

---

He goes to Hester on the twenty-fourth, a Tuesday, at four, in the bungalow sitting room with the fans going and the shutters half over, and the Colonel next door with the drains file and a rug.

He does not dress it up. He says that Gamma is going, that Pascual gives it months, and that article four will send an order to Port of Spain within a calendar month of the day she stops, and he asks whether it can be suspended for this one death.

Hester Bevan puts her cup down.

"No," she says.

"Hester —"

"I should like to explain why, because you are entitled to the reason and because I have been expecting this conversation since April of last year, and I rather thought you would come to it sooner." She folds her hands. "I drafted article four so that it could not be suspended by a Director on an afternoon when he was fond of an animal. I did not have you in mind in particular. I had in mind whoever would be sitting in that office in 1971. But I will not tell you that I did not think of you, because I did, and I made it a sentence shorter on your account."

"It'll buy an animal. And about one in three survives the passage, and you know that, because I gave you the figure myself."

"Yes. It will buy an animal." She does not look away from him. "It is not for Gamma, Dr Tar. Gamma will not know. It is for whoever is left in that pen on the morning after, and for whoever is standing on the concrete next to him. I built this Institute so that a creature to whom that has happened should not be obliged to go on in a box by itself until it stops, and I have watched three of them do it, and I do not propose to watch a fourth because the Director has done a sum."

Next door the Colonel says something about a culvert, loudly, to nobody.

"Yes, my dear," Hester says, without raising her voice or turning her head, "I am coming," and does not move.

---

"There's one door," Reggie says. "In your own article one. *Lawfully obtainable.*"

"I know where the door is. I put it there."

"Did you know who's got the key?"

Hester is quiet for a moment.

"I did not know in April of 1958," she says. "I have known since about the September of this year, when the interim committee was gazetted and I read the names." She picks up her cup again, which she does when she has decided something. "I shall not use it and I shall not stop you using it, and I shall not be asked to choose between those, because it is not my decision and I have taken great care that it should not be."

"Whose is it?"

"Whose do you suppose?" says Hester Bevan. "You were not in the room when I drafted it, Dr Tar. You were in the room when I read it."

He sits there.

"And Mr Aguilar," she says, in the same voice. "Does he know?"

"Pascual told him the rule. In April of 'fifty-eight."

"Then he has the rule and he has not got the reason." She sets the cup down. "I shall give him the reason myself, when it is needed, and not before. A man cannot be told what a thing costs while he is still able to believe that it will not be asked of him. He only hears the price." She stands, which means he stands. "Thank you for coming to me first. I should be obliged if you would not come to me with it a second time."

At the door, with the Colonel's voice going on about the culvert behind them, she puts her hand briefly on the frame, the way her husband used to, and Reggie adds it to the record he keeps and has never written down, which has about forty entries in it now.

---

The letter comes on the tenth of December, which is a Thursday, in the second sack, in a fast clear hand, and he reads it standing up in the office at eleven in the morning.

Then he reads it again, and puts it in his inside pocket, and takes the half past twelve launch home, because Pascual is at the practice on Thursdays and will be in at six, and Reggie made a promise in February about what he would do on the day, and he has decided somewhere between the office and the jetty that it covers letters in that hand as well as warmth.

He sits at the kitchen table from one o'clock until six with the letter face down in front of him and does not read it a third time.

Pascual comes up the eleven steps at ten past six and puts his bag down and looks at him.

"How long have you been sitting there?"

"Since one."

"*Ay.*" He does not take his coat off. "Who?"

"Read it," Reggie says. "Out loud. I'd rather hear it in your voice than go on having it in his."

---

> Dear Tar,
>
> The Martin's September return reached the committee on the second of the month, and I have read the intake column for your old female about as many times as you have. It is a beautiful column. There is nothing in the literature to set beside it and I expect you have noticed that and minded it.
>
> I have also read Mrs Bevan's deed, which you showed me in November of last year, and which I said then was the best thing to come out of your station in two years. I still think so. Article four is exquisitely made. The morning after that animal dies it will send an order to Port of Spain, and there is nothing you or Pobil or the Director can do to prevent it, which I take to have been the intention.
>
> Except one thing, which you'll have found by now, because you are not slow. *Lawfully obtainable.*
>
> I can have the Colonial Office suspend export licences for the species from Trinidad for eighteen months, pending the Commission, on the recommendation of the interim committee. It will take ten days from a cable. It would have come in 1962 in any case; this brings it forward by two years. It will keep something over forty animals in the water across the field, which Pobil will have worked out before the end of this paragraph, and for exactly as long as it runs it will make Mrs Bevan's article inoperative, because no specimen will be lawfully obtainable from anywhere.
>
> There is nobody to transfer. I went through every return on the schedule in November. You may exempt transfers from the suspension if you wish and I shan't object.
>
> Here is what I want for it, and you may set it beside what you know of me.
>
> I want to be present. At my own expense, for as long as I like, at the rail. No camera, no series, no subjects, no session of any kind, and Pobil's stop over anything I do on the island, including standing there. I want to watch Aguilar.
>
> I shan't ask him to do anything. I shan't need to.
>
> If the answer is no, cable me no and I'll take it without a word, and the offer will stand until the day your trustees write to Port of Spain, and not one day after.
>
> Yours,
> A. Spilsbury

---

Pascual puts it down on the table and takes his glasses off and does not say anything.

"He's written every single thing," Reggie says, "except the verb."

"I heard it."

"Every item. The mechanism, the date, the forty animals, the transfer, the price. What he wants and where he wants to stand while he gets it. He's written *I want to watch Aguilar* in a letter to the Director of the Institute that employs Aguilar, in ink, over his own name, and he hasn't written down one word about what he expects Aguilar to *do*." Reggie has his hands flat on the table. "Because he doesn't need to. He read it aloud out of a crate in 1954."

"Yes."

"Now I'm going to tell you what I want, before you tell me what it costs, because that's the arrangement, and because I've been sitting at this table since one o'clock and I've got it in order."

Pascual pulls the chair out and sits down opposite him with his coat still on.

"Go on."

---

"I want the suspension. I want forty animals left in the water and I want them left there two years before anybody else would have left them. I want Hester's article not to send a boat out in March. I have wanted a suspension of the Trinidad trade since the fifteenth of June 1955 and I have never once been in a position to ask for it, and it is on the table in front of me in a fast clear hand for the price of a man standing at a rail.

"And I want to see it.

"That is the part I am telling you on the day. I want to stand at that rail on the morning after she dies and see what comes off a solitary adult male in the first hour, and the first week, and the first month, with a man beside him who can feel it. Nobody has ever seen that. One boy saw the end of it and wrote it down and I have not read what he wrote and I have wanted to since the ninth of October 1957, and I am the man who sat at that desk and wrote *both animals are well* underneath it as though it were a comfort and not a regret.

"I am not better than he is about this. I have been sitting here for five hours trying to find the place where I am better than he is and there is not one. The only difference between that letter and what I want is that he has written it down.

"That is all of it. You may say what it costs."

---

Pascual does not raise his voice. Reggie had not expected him to and is still, obscurely, braced for it, and it does not come.

"He's asking us to keep an animal alone so he can watch a man stand next to it," Pascual says.

"He's asking us not to buy one."

"It's the same sentence." He puts one finger on the letter, not hard. "It's the same sentence, Reggie, and you've just said so yourself, at some length, and you're right, and I'm glad you said it, and I would rather have had it than not. And it does not change the answer."

"Which is."

"Which is that it isn't ours." He takes his finger away. "It isn't yours and it isn't mine and it isn't Hester's, and she knows that, or she would have been across on the launch this afternoon. It's Nestor's. Nestor's the one who'll be on that step on the morning after. It's his hand and his face and his four children at the top of the village and his wife, and every single thing that man wants to buy off us is going to be paid for by somebody who is on eleven pounds a month more than he was in 1957 and has never once been asked a question he could refuse."

"Then we ask him."

"Not while she's eating." Pascual's jaw has set. "I'm not doing arithmetic over a live animal. I'm not going up that hill to a man's kitchen in December and asking him what he'll do when an animal who's still taking fish off his hand is dead. I did that sum over Beta for eleven days and I have never been clean of it. Cable him no."

Reggie looks at him for a while.

"He's built it," he says, "so that we'll be asking him in February."

"Then we'll be asking him in February," says Pascual. "Not in December. And if it is February and I'm at that table asking, you'll be sitting across from me with your face doing that, and we'll do it together, and I'll hate it, and so will you." He stands up and takes his coat off at last. "Cable him no. And then come here, because you've been sitting in that chair for five hours and you have kept a promise nobody asked you to keep, and I am not having you do it on your own twice."

---

The cable goes on the eleventh. It says *NO. TAR.*, which is the shortest thing he has ever sent that man in thirteen years, and which Pascual reads over his shoulder at the office and describes as eloquent.

The reply comes on the fourteenth.

*UNDERSTOOD. STANDS AS STATED. MERRY CHRISTMAS BOTH. S.*

---

The Colonel wants to see his pens.

He says so at breakfast on the first of January, to Hester, in the voice of a man who has been told for twenty-three months that he may not go down the path and has now had a sufficient quantity of it. Hester says, "Yes, my dear," and sends for Nestor and for Juan's brother, and on the second of January at half past four in the afternoon, when the heat is going off the concrete, Colonel Bevan is carried down the path to Gamma's pen in a cane chair with poles lashed to it, at a slow walk, with his wife beside him at exactly his pace.

Reggie is at the rail. He has not been asked to be and he has not been told to go, and he stands at the far end with his hands behind his back and does not come any closer.

They put the chair down eight feet from the edge. Nestor puts a rug over the old man's knees and Hester takes it off again, because it is eighty-four degrees, and the Colonel sits and looks at the water for a long time.

Gamma is at the shallow end, where she is almost always now. Chumley is against her.

"Tar," the Colonel says, without turning his head.

"Sir."

"How old is she."

"Nobody knows, sir. Older than any animal of her kind has ever been in a pen. That's all anybody knows."

"Hm." The Colonel watches the water. "So am I."

Nobody says anything for about a minute.

Then Chumley goes off down the length of the pen at the speed he goes everywhere, and turns at the far wall, and comes back, and stops beside her, and puts a fish into her hand. And Gamma, who has refused two fish off Nestor since noon, lifts it and turns it over and eats it, slowly, the whole of it, because it has been brought to her.

"*Well*," says the Colonel.

Hester's hand is on his shoulder. Reggie has not seen her put it there.

"That clause of yours," the Colonel says, to the water. "Eleven. I always rather liked that clause."

"Yes, my dear," says Hester. "I know that you did."

He sits a little while longer. Then he says, "Aguilar," and Nestor comes and bends down, and the Colonel says something to him too quietly for Reggie to hear, and Nestor says, "*Sí, coronel*," and straightens up; and then they lift the chair between them and carry the Colonel of the William Martin Institute for Marine Studies back up the path to his verandah, at a slow walk, with his wife beside him, and Reggie stands at the rail for some time after they have gone.

Down at the shallow end the old female lies on the bottom with her tail under her, and the young male lies against her side with his arm over her back, and neither of them moves, and the light goes off the water.
