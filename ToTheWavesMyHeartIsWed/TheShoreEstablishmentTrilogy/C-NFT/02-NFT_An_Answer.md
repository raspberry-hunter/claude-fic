# An Answer

Inés dries her hands on her apron before she touches him.

She's done it for twenty-one years. She did it the first week they were married, in her mother's kitchen, with her hands wet from the pot, and he saw her do it and didn't say anything, and she has done it every time since — at the washtub, at the sink, coming in from the rain — a quick wipe on whatever's nearest before she puts her palm on the back of his neck or his arm or his face. She has never once asked him why. He has never once told her.

At twenty to five on the eighth of February she dries her hands and puts one on his shoulder in the dark kitchen and says, "*Otra vez?*"

"Otra vez. She's slow."

"She's been slow since September."

"She's slower."

Inés takes the hand away and gives him the bread wrapped in a cloth and doesn't say anything else, and he goes down the hill in the dark to the quarter past five launch, which is the fish launch, which is not the launch he's paid to be on.

---

The old one has been slow since September and he said so to Dr Pobil in October at the balance, and Dr Pobil said *I know*, and they've neither of them said anything more about it since, because there isn't anything to say.

He's weighed her fish every morning since the spring of 'fifty-four. He can tell by the weight of the bucket at four o'clock how she's been without looking in it. And since the end of the year he's been coming across on the early boat so that there's somebody at the pen before the light, which nobody asked him to do and nobody's told him to stop, and twice a week he doesn't go home at all, and there's a sack and a blanket in the corner of the store, and nobody on the station has once said a word about it to him.

That's how this place is. He's worked here seven years and he's come to understand that it runs on a great many things that nobody says a word about.

---

He sits on the step with his hand in the water.

That's the other thing nobody says a word about. There's a step cut into the wall of the pen just above the waterline, eight feet in from the rail, where a towel used to be left, and at twenty to six most mornings Nestor Aguilar sits on the edge above it with his trousers rolled and puts his right hand in the water up to the wrist, and keeps it there for about ten minutes, and then gets up and goes to do the weights.

Dr Pobil knows. Dr Pobil has known since the spring of 'fifty-eight and has never told him to stop, and once, in the August, came and sat down on the concrete a few feet off and asked him in Spanish what it was like, and listened to the answer, and nodded, and went away.

What it's like is this.

The water in that pen has two things in it, and it's had two things in it for as long as Nestor has been putting his hand there. The old one is slow and warm and very large, the way a room is when there's a fire lit in it — somebody else's fire, in somebody else's room, that you're sitting near but isn't for you. And the young one is bright and quick and all over the place, like a dog that's heard the door.

And between the two of them there's a third thing, which he's never had a word for and has never tried to get one.

It isn't in either of them. It's in the water between them, or it's in the two of them at once — he can't tell which and it doesn't matter. It's like a rope between two boats in a harbour: you don't notice the rope, you notice that the two boats move together.

---

He was nine the first time anybody told him he was wrong about what he felt, and it was his mother, at the washtub, with her hands wet.

She put her wet hand on his arm and he said, *why are you frightened, Mamá?*, and she said she wasn't, and she was, and it was three days before his father came back from the boat. And after that it happened perhaps a dozen more times, and she told him every time he was imagining things, and he learned what boys learn, which is to stop saying.

He learned the rest of it by himself. That it was worse when things were wet. That he couldn't hold his sister when she'd been bathed without knowing she was hungry before she did. And at eleven, on a Sunday, when his cousins took him out past the reef and he went into the sea up to his chest, that the sea was full — full, the way a church is full at Easter, every voice in it going at once and none of them to him — and he came out on to the sand and was sick and never went in again.

His father called him a coward for about four years and then stopped, and he became the one who stayed ashore and mended the nets and was good with his hands, and married a girl who dried her hands, and had four children, and wrote a letter in 1953 in his careful English to a Mrs Bevan at the Institute to say that he could not swim but was very steady with animals and would work at anything.

He's never known why they took him over the other men. He supposes his hands.

---

The old one surfaces at twelve minutes to six, which is long.

The young one comes up beside her and blows, and they both go down again, and the young one lies along her side on the bottom with his arm over her the way he's lain every night since before Christmas. He's bigger than she is now by a great deal. He was a thing the size of a dog when the Navy brought him in a canvas tank in 'fifty-four, and Nestor was on the derrick rope that afternoon, and remembers the weight of him, and not much else.

The fire's very low this morning.

He's felt it going down since September, and it hasn't gone down the way a thing goes down when it's ill. He's felt that. He felt it in the calf in March of 'fifty-eight when Luisito had the fever and Nestor was kept home two days, and it wasn't this. This is the way a fire goes down at the end of an evening when nobody puts more wood on, because the evening's over and everybody knows it and there's no hurry.

He sits there with his hand in the water and thinks about nothing in particular. About the bread in the cloth. About whether Rafael has gone to the boat or gone back to bed.

---

It happens at ten to six.

It happens first in the young one, which is how he knows. The dog-that's-heard-the-door goes all at once into something he's never felt off that animal in six years, sharp and close, and the young one comes up off the bottom with the old one's weight across his arm and gets under her and *pushes* — up, up through the water, to the top, the way men say dolphins do with their own — and her head comes out and she breathes, once, long.

And they go down.

And the fire goes out.

Not down. Out. Nestor has sat by a great many fires and there's always a last bit that glows on after, and this hasn't got one. It's there and then the room is cold and there's nobody in it.

And then the rope goes.

---

It doesn't slacken. He's had seven years of that rope, and he'd have said, if anyone had ever asked him, that if it went it would go the way a rope goes when a boat comes loose — a slackening, a drift, the other boat getting further off. It doesn't. It goes the way a rope goes when somebody cuts it with a knife.

And the young one is on the other end of it, and what comes up Nestor's arm is not a hum.

White. It comes up through the wrist and the elbow and the shoulder and goes off in the top of his head like a lamp in a dark room, and there's no down any more and no concrete and no step, and a noise that isn't a noise, a *calling*, the same thing over and over, into a place where there used to be an answer and now there's nothing, nothing, not an echo, not a wall to come back off —

He's on his back on the concrete.

He doesn't remember getting there. He's lying flat eight feet from the rail with his arm across his chest and the sky going grey over the tarp, and his ears are ringing, and his mouth is full of the taste of pennies, and he thinks very clearly, the way a man thinks when he's been knocked down by a wave: *so that's what it is. So that's what Mrs Bevan has.*

---

He gets up on his knees.

In the pen the young one has let go of her and she's on the bottom at the shallow end, on her side, not moving, with her tail under her. And the young one is at the ladder, hitting it — twice with the flat of his tail, hard, the way he does when he wants somebody to come, and then again, and again, and the iron's ringing all down the wall.

Nestor crawls back to the step.

He knows what'll happen if he puts his hand back in. He's just had it. He knows it the way you know a stove is hot after the first time, and he sits on the edge for about the length of a breath with his hand over the water and his whole body telling him not to.

Then he puts it in.

It's like putting a hand on a stove.

It's the white again, the calling, and he holds it there and counts to ten out loud in Spanish, and on *seis* the young one stops hitting the ladder and turns round in the water.

"*Aquí*," Nestor says. His voice comes out wrong. "*Aquí, muchacho. Aquí estoy.*"

He gets to ten and takes his hand out and puts his forehead down on his own knees and breathes.

---

Dr Pobil comes out of the store at a run.

He's been asleep on the sack in the corner, because he said last night at eleven *tonight or tomorrow or a week, I don't know, go to bed*, and didn't go to bed himself, and Nestor didn't either. And he comes round the end of the wall in his shirt with no shoes on and sees the old one on the bottom and the young one at the ladder and Nestor on the step, and his face does what Dr Pobil's face does, which is everything, all at once, where anybody can see it.

He doesn't say anything. He goes straight past Nestor and over the edge into the water in his shirt, and down, and puts both his hands flat on the side of the old one's chest and holds them there, and Nestor watches him hold them there for a long time.

The young one lets him. That's the thing Nestor will think about afterwards. The young one comes and hangs in the water a yard off and watches the doctor with his hands on her, and doesn't go for him, and doesn't do anything at all.

Dr Pobil comes up. He looks at Nestor across eight feet of water and shakes his head, once.

Then he puts his hand on the old one's head, on the top of it, where the hair's gone thin, and says, "*Ya está, vieja. Ya está,*" in the voice he uses on Nestor's children when they've fallen over, and comes up the ladder.

---

He sits down on the concrete next to Nestor, dripping, with his hands on his knees.

"Did you have your hand in?"

"Sí."

"When she went?"

"Sí."

"*Ay, Dios.*" Dr Pobil puts his wet hand on Nestor's back, between the shoulders, through the shirt, and Nestor lets him, because his shirt's dry and the doctor has nothing in him that Nestor can feel and never has; he's the quietest man on this island to be touched by, which Nestor has always privately thought is the strangest thing about him. "How bad."

"It knocked me down."

"I can see it did. Your mouth's bleeding." The hand stays where it is. "Is it still — if you put it back in —"

"Sí. The same. I put it back."

Dr Pobil takes his hand away and turns round on the concrete so that he's looking straight at him, and his face has changed. It's still doing everything. But it's put all of it behind something, the way a man puts his hand over a candle in a wind, and when he speaks he speaks in Spanish, very slowly, and he doesn't take his eyes off Nestor's face once.

---

"Listen to me. I am going to say this one time and I want you to have all of it.

"You will not go into the water above the waist. Not for any reason. Not if he calls you and not if he hits that ladder all day and not if you think it would help him. It will not help him and it will put you on the bottom of the pen.

"You will not put your hand on his face. If he puts his hand on yours, you will take it down, gently, with your own hand on his wrist, and you will not put yours up. You know why. I told you in April of 'fifty-eight and it is truer this morning than it was then.

"You will come out of it every hour for ten minutes, whether you want to or not. I will be on the rail and I will tell you when.

"You will eat when I bring food.

"And if you cannot bear it — at any time, today, tonight, next month — you will say so, and I will not think less of you by one hair, and I will take you home to your wife myself in the launch. That is not a kindness. That is the rule. Say it back."

Nestor says it back.

"Good," says Dr Pobil, and gets up, and goes up the path in his wet shirt with no shoes on to find Dr Tar.

---

Dr Tar comes down the path at a run about four minutes later with no collar and his glasses in his hand, and stops at the rail.

He doesn't come any closer. He puts the glasses on and looks at the pen — at the old one on the bottom, and the young one going along the wall, and Nestor on the step — and then he looks at Dr Pobil, who's come down behind him, and Dr Pobil looks back.

Nestor has watched those two men look at each other for seven years. He worked out what they were to each other in about the spring of 'fifty-four, before either of them had, he's fairly sure, and he told his cousin Tito that anybody on the boats who said a word about the doctors would have him to answer to, and nobody ever has. It isn't his business. It's just that he can see it, the way he can see the rope in the water; not with his hands, only with his eyes, but it's the same shape.

Dr Tar takes his glasses off again and holds them.

"Nestor," he says.

"Doctor."

"Pascual says you had your hand in."

"Sí."

Dr Tar looks at him for a long moment, and doesn't say *are you all right*, which is what anybody else would say and which there isn't any answer to, and Nestor is grateful.

"I'm sorry," Dr Tar says. "About the old one. I'm sorry."

"Sí," Nestor says. "Me too."

---

The Señora comes down at twenty past six.

She comes down the path in her hat the way she's come down eleven or twelve times since the spring of 'fifty-eight, which is a thing nobody on the station says a word about either, and on those mornings Nestor and Dr Pobil do the weights at the far end with their backs turned, because that's what Dr Pobil told him to do the first time, and he'd have done it anyway.

This morning nobody's doing the weights.

She stops at the rail. She looks at the old one. She looks at the young one, who's gone down to the bottom of the pen and begun — slowly, and then not slowly — to go round it. Round and round, along the bottom, close to the wall, as if on one of these times round there'd be a door.

Nestor has seen that before. He saw the other one do it, the bad one, Beta, in the summer of 1953, from the rail, dry, and felt nothing, because he was dry. He's been glad of that for seven years.

The Señora watches the young one go round three times. Her face doesn't do anything. And then she does something Nestor has never seen her do, which is put her hand on the rail and hold on to it.

"Mr Aguilar," she says.

"Señora."

"Is it very loud?"

Nobody has ever asked him that.

Not his mother, not Inés, not Dr Pobil, who asked what it was *like*, which isn't the same. Nobody in forty-five years has ever asked Nestor Aguilar whether a thing he felt was loud, as if it were a sound, as if it were a real thing that somebody else might have heard — and he sits on the step with his hand in his lap and finds that he can't answer her for a moment.

"Sí, señora," he says. "Very loud."

"Yes," says Mrs Bevan. "It will not be quieter for some time. I shall be here."

And she stays at the rail, in her hat, with her hand on it, until nine o'clock.

---

At nine she goes up the path, and Dr Tar goes after her, and Nestor hears them at the top where the path turns by the store, because the generator's between its changes and the air's still.

"Hester. The cable —"

"Will go at nine, Dr Tar. As drafted." The Señora's voice doesn't change at all. "The Secretary is to give notice within the day. I am the Secretary. I wrote the article, and I am not going to be the first person to break it."

"The trustees will write to Port of Spain."

"The trustees will do what the deed obliges them to do, within one calendar month, and not an hour before. That is thirty days." There's a pause. "You have thirty days. So has Dr Pobil. So, I think, has Mr Aguilar, though nobody has told him so, and I should be obliged if one of you would."

Nestor doesn't know what that means, and puts it away, and puts his hand back in the water.

---

They leave her in till the afternoon, because the young one won't let them near.

He's stopped going round by eleven. At eleven he goes back to her at the shallow end and lies along her side with his arm over her, the way he's lain every night since Christmas, and puts his face against her shoulder, and stays. At a quarter to twelve he goes off down the pen and comes back with the brass hose fitting he's been carrying about since 'fifty-seven, and puts it in her hand.

Her hand doesn't close on it.

He takes it out and puts it in again.

Nestor watches that from the step with his hand in the water and the white going up his arm, and he thinks he would give a year's pay — the new pay, Mrs Bevan's pay, that goes up every April — never to have watched it.

---

Don John comes down at noon with his book and his pencils, and stands at the rail with his face gone red, and doesn't open the book for a while.

Then he opens it, and writes the date and the number at the top, and draws. He draws the whole thing, the old one on the bottom and the young one along her and the fitting in her hand, and he draws the tail as well, every bit of it, and dates the sheet in ink. And every so often he has to stop and wipe his face with the back of his wrist, and then he goes on.

When he's done he tears it out and gives it to Dr Pobil and says, in English, "Eleven thousand four hundred and six," and Dr Pobil says, "Thank you, John," and Don John goes and sits on the far side of the pen on an upturned bucket and doesn't draw anything else all day.

---

They take her out at three.

Dr Pobil does it with the derrick and Juan's brother and two men off the fish boat, and Dr Tar on the rail with the line, and it's done the way the doctor does everything with an animal, which is slowly, and with nobody shouting.

Nestor's job is the young one.

He sits on the step with his feet in the water up to the shin, and both hands in, and he talks. He doesn't know what he says. Names, mostly. The children's names. The name of the boat. *Aquí, muchacho, aquí, mira, aquí estoy*, over and over in the voice he used on the children when they had nightmares. And the white goes up both arms at once, and his teeth ache, and at twenty past three the young one leaves the old one's side and comes up the length of the pen towards the step, very slowly, and stops about a yard off, and looks at him.

Behind him the sling goes under her.

"*Aquí*," Nestor says.

The young one comes the last yard and puts the top of his head against Nestor's knee, and stays there.

Nestor puts his hand on his shoulder. Not the face. The shoulder, the big hard slope of it above the water, and the white comes up through his palm so hard he nearly goes over backwards again, and he doesn't, and he keeps it there.

When the derrick takes the weight and she comes up out of the water behind them the young one doesn't turn round.

---

Dr Pobil does the post-mortem in the morning. Dr Tar goes up the path at six to the office and doesn't come down. The Señora sends a basket at seven with no message in it.

Nestor doesn't go home.

Dr Pobil tells him to go home, twice, in Spanish, and then stops telling him, and instead writes a note in Spanish on the back of a feed sheet and sends it up the village with Juan's brother's boy, and at nine the boy comes back across on the late launch with a basket from Inés.

There's bread in it, and a clean shirt, and cold rice and beans in a tin, and at the bottom, folded, a towel.

A dry towel. Nestor sits on the step and takes it out and holds it in his lap and looks at it for some time.

She's never once asked. She's put a towel in.

---

"You'll eat that," says Dr Pobil, from the rail, in English. "All of it. You're no use to him if you keel over."

Nestor eats it.

The doctor sits on the concrete a few feet off with his back against the wall and doesn't talk, and at ten Dr Tar comes down and sits beside him, and the two of them sit there in the dark with their shoulders not quite touching, and Nestor hears them talk once, very low, in English, and hears the word *Port of Spain*, and the word *thirty*, and then another word, which is a name.

He knows that name. It's the Englishman with the camera. The one who stood at the rail with him on a morning in December of 'fifty-eight in the half-light with a cup of tea, and said that he'd been in the water three times and got nothing, nothing at all, not a thing, and said it the way a man says a thing he's proud of, and then didn't say anything else for twenty minutes.

Nestor said he was sorry. He was sorry. He's never been able to work out why.

He puts it away with the other thing, the thing the Señora said at the top of the path, and puts his hand back in the water.

---

At half past eleven the young one hits the ladder. Nestor says his name. He stops.

At ten to one the young one hits the ladder. Nestor says his name. He stops.

At twenty past two he comes to the step and puts his head against Nestor's knee again, and Nestor puts his hand on his shoulder, and they stay like that for a long time in the dark with the generator going.

And Nestor thinks — because there's nothing to do but think, and the white's going up his arm, and it's the middle of the night — about the Colonel.

The Colonel, in January, in the chair with the poles, at the edge of this same pen. When they'd finished looking at the old one and the young one bringing her a fish, the Colonel said *Aguilar*, and Nestor bent down, and the Colonel said, very low, with his hand on Nestor's sleeve, in the voice of an old man who's had forty years of something he's never once said:

*When she goes, he'll want the whole of you. You'll give him a good deal. See he doesn't get all of it.*

And Nestor said *sí, coronel*, and didn't understand it, and carried the chair back up the path.

He understands it now. He sits on the step in the dark with his hand on an animal's shoulder and the calling coming up through his palm, over and over, into the place where the answer used to be, and he understands it the way he understood Dr Tar and Dr Pobil in the spring of 'fifty-four: not because anybody told him, only because he can see the shape.

The young one lifts his head off Nestor's knee.

For a moment he's quite still in the water, a yard off, looking at him.

Then he goes down to the bottom of the pen and begins, slowly, to go round.

Nestor keeps his hand in. Every time the young one passes the step, he says his name.
