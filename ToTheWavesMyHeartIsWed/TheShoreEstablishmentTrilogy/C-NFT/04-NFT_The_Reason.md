# The Reason

Mrs Bevan minutes the Friday meeting of the eleventh of March in eleven lines, and none of them contains the word *suspension*.

*Item 4. The Secretary reported that the obligation under article four of the Trust deed is in abeyance pending the notice of 1 March. Noted.*

That's all it needs to say, and it's all it's going to say, and she writes it in the hand she has written everything in since 1946 and blots it and closes the book.

What it doesn't say is that she spent part of the evening of the second with the deed open on her knee and a glass of the Colonel's sherry, reading article one over and over, the way a woman reads a letter from somebody she didn't expect to hear from. *Lawfully obtainable.* She put that word there in April of 1958 because a document that obliges a thing ought also to say what happens when the thing can't lawfully be done, and because she was trained in 1922 by a man who said that every door you build into an instrument will one day be opened by the person you least expected to have the key.

He was right. She had not, in fairness to herself, expected it to be opened by a letter from Massachusetts that said *I want to watch Aguilar* in so many words, and she finds she thinks the better of the drafting, not the worse.

---

Dr Spilsbury is at the rail by twenty to six every morning, and he stays till nine.

Then he goes to the office and does whatever it is he does with his correspondence, which is a great deal, and at four he comes back down and stays until the light goes. He doesn't bring a camera and he doesn't bring a notebook. He brings a cup of tea in the morning and nothing in the afternoon, and he stands where Dr Pobil told him he might stand, which is wherever he likes, and he has chosen a place about four feet to the left of Dr Pobil's board and has not moved from it in a fortnight.

He speaks to Aguilar in the morning — *good morning, Mr Aguilar* — and in the evening — *good night, Mr Aguilar* — and not otherwise.

She's watched him from the bungalow verandah, which you can do if you know where to look between the store roof and the tarp, and she has watched a great many men wait for a great many things in forty years in colonial administration, and she has never seen a man wait better.

---

She herself goes down on Sundays, at twenty past six, as she has since April of 'fifty-eight, and stands at the rail.

She doesn't sit on the edge. She hasn't sat on the edge or put her hand in that water since the tenth of February, and she won't, and there's no question in her mind about why.

There's a creature in that pen who is calling into a place where there used to be an answer, and she knows precisely what that is from the inside, and she's sixty-four, and in 1949, at fifty-three, an animal not half so loud as that one put her sitting on the concrete for forty minutes and in bed for a day and a half. She stands at the rail in her hat, eight feet from the edge, and watches a man do what she can't, and doesn't pretend to herself that it doesn't cost her something to watch.

Dr Spilsbury says good morning to her on the first Sunday, and on the second, and on the third he says good morning and then, after about a minute, "I wonder if I might have an hour, Mrs Bevan. At your convenience, and not today."

"Tuesday," says Hester. "At four."

---

He comes to the sitting room on Tuesday the twenty-second with nothing in his hands, and she gives him tea, and he takes it and puts it down and doesn't drink any.

"I'll be quick," he says, "because you'll have worked out the shape of this in about four seconds, and I'd rather not waste your time pretending you haven't."

"Go on, Dr Spilsbury."

"I've read your deed."

"Dr Tar showed it to you in November of 1958. You said it was the best thing to come out of this station in two years."

"I said that, and I meant it, and I read it again on the boat coming down, properly, the way I read a paper I've been asked to referee." He sits forward with his elbows on his knees, which he does, she has noticed, when he has stopped performing. "Article four obliges the Institute to keep two animals together in perpetuity whether or not a line is ever published about them. That's not a scientific provision. I've sat on eleven committees that drafted scientific provisions and none of them ever wrote one like that. And article nine, Mrs Bevan, provides a post for life for any member of staff found to have formed an attachment to an animal — at the Institute's discretion, the animal's company at his own, and not to be withdrawn for cause."

"Yes."

"Nobody in the literature has ever described that condition." He says it gently. "Not once. Not in forty years. There's no *attachment* in any paper I've ever read, and I've read all of them. And you've written a pension for it. In 1958. Before the contact series was scored. With a clause about not being withdrawn for cause, which is the clause a person writes when she knows what it would be like to be dismissed from the only place that — " he moves his hand — "well."

Hester says nothing.

"That's not a trust deed," Spilsbury says. "It's a case report, written in the only form a woman in your position could have published one. And I'd very much like to read the case."

---

She had known it would be something of this shape since the Sunday. She had not known it would be this.

"You've been to the pens on a Sunday morning eleven or twelve times since April of 1958," he says, "and never once in the twelve years before it. I didn't have that from anybody. I had it from Clarke's plate sequence. You're in the background of four of his plates, in a hat, at the rail, and every one of them is dated after the twenty-second of April 'fifty-eight, and there's nothing before, not in eleven thousand sheets. Clarke numbers everything. If something goes missing everybody can see it has." He looks at her with an expression of enormous, uncomplicated respect. "You went down there for the first time on the twenty-second of April 1958, Mrs Bevan, eleven weeks after your husband's thrombosis, and you've been going ever since, and you hired Cholmondeley, and I believe you hired Aguilar, and you wrote article nine."

The fans go round.

"I'm not going to ask you what happened," he says. "I'm going to tell you what I'd do with it, if you gave it to me, so that you can refuse it knowing exactly what you're refusing.

"No name. Not a county, not a decade. *A subject, female, now elderly, whose account was given in confidence.* Nobody would ever connect it to this station and I'd see to it that nobody could. And it would be the only case in the literature with a history — forty years of a thing, from one end to the other, told by the person it happened to. The contact series is eleven hundred reels of a calf fetching a hose fitting. Aguilar is three months on a step. You're a lifetime. You're the whole curve." He sits back. "That's the ask. You may refuse it and I'll not raise it again, and you've my word, and you know from Tar what my word is worth."

---

"I refuse it," Hester says.

"Right," says Spilsbury, immediately, and picks up his tea for the first time, and drinks some, and puts it down. "Thank you. May I ask one more thing, which you needn't answer, and which I'll never refer to again?"

"You may ask."

"Was it answered?"

She looks at him.

She has been looked at by clever men across tables for forty-two years, in Georgetown and Bristol and Bridgetown and the Colonial Office, and she learned in about 1919 — in a bad year, in a room she has never recovered — to keep her face so still that a man could read it with a lamp and get nothing but the time of day. She's never been more grateful for that year than she is at twenty past four on the twenty-second of March 1960, with the most dangerous man she has ever given tea to sitting three feet away waiting for her face to answer a question she hasn't let her mouth hear.

It doesn't.

After about five seconds Spilsbury smiles, quite ruefully, and stands up.

"Mrs Bevan," he says, "you've got the worst face for my purposes of any human being I've ever sat across from, and I include Tar, and I'd like it recorded that I mind." He picks up his hat. "The only negative result I ever believed off a face was Pobil's, in his own sick bay in 1954, and that was a lie, and I knew it was a lie, and I believed the lie anyway because the man couldn't manage anything else. You can. I'm obliged to you for the tea."

---

She sits in the sitting room for some time after he's gone.

He has four of the five things. She does the count the way she does an unbalanced column: that she's sensitive; that she went to the pens late and deliberately; that she built a place and a provision for people to whom it had happened; and that it had happened to *somebody* for her to know what to provide. He hasn't got Tom. He hasn't got the Essequibo or the net or the eleven weeks in the spring of 1918 or the two years after. He hasn't got whether it was answered.

He hasn't got it because she wouldn't let her face give it to him, and her face is the only thing standing between that man and forty-two years of the only private thing she owns.

And he'll not raise it again. She believes that. He keeps his word the way a good instrument keeps its calibration, not out of virtue but because it's more useful to be relied on.

What he'll do is *file* it. That's the thing Dr Tar told her about him in 1958, in a house on the north side with a repulsive chair in the corner. He'll put a card in a box, with a question mark on it, and it'll sit there, and one day some other fact will come in by post from somewhere she can't foresee and fit against it.

Next door the Colonel says, "Hester."

"Yes, my dear."

"Was that the fellow?"

"Yes."

"Is he after you?"

She considers the question as it deserves.

"Yes," she says.

"Hm." A pause. "Want him off the island?"

"No, my dear. Thank you."

"Had him off it once."

"Yes," says Hester, and gets up, and goes in to him, and takes his hand, which is dry and cool and has a very slight tremor in it now that it didn't have at Christmas. "I remember."

---

She goes up the village on the twenty-fourth of April, a Sunday, a week after Easter, having sent word by Dr Pobil on the Thursday that she would like to call.

She's never been in a house at the top of the village. She has been the wife of the Colonel of the William Martin Institute for fourteen years and the Institute has employed half the men in that village at one time or another, and she has signed their contracts and their pay and their widows' allowances, and she has not once walked up that hill, because the wife of the Colonel is not asked, and doesn't ask. Juan's brother drives her to the foot of the last stretch where the road gives out, and she walks the rest in her hat, and it takes her eleven minutes, and she's not ashamed of that.

There's a mango tree over the gate. There's a boy of eight on the step, who looks at her, and goes in, and says something very fast in Spanish, and a girl of eighteen comes out and says, "Please come in, Mrs Bevan," in careful English, and she goes in.

Aguilar is at the table. He stands up.

His wife is at the stove, and turns round, and dries her hands on her apron.

Hester sees her do it. She sees the woman do it without looking, the way a person does a thing ten thousand times, and she thinks of a young man in May of 1954 flinching from Jean Leslie's hand on his shoulder, and she has to take a moment before she's able to say good afternoon.

---

"Mrs Aguilar," she says, when she has sat down and been given coffee and the children have been sent somewhere and have not gone. "Dr Pobil tells me that you asked him a question, and that he told you he did not know the answer, and that there was one person who would. I am that person, and I have come to answer it, and I have come to your house because I did not think you should have to come to mine."

Inés Aguilar sits down at the table across from her. It's a thing Hester will remember: that the woman sat.

"I shall have to tell you both something first," Hester says, "so that you know why I can answer it. I have told it to four people in forty-two years. You will be the fifth and sixth. I should be grateful if it did not go further than this kitchen, and I shall not ask you to promise, because I can see from where I am sitting that you would not repeat it anyway."

"No, señora," says Aguilar.

"Then here it is."

---

She doesn't give them all of it. She gives them less than she gave the two doctors, and she gives it plainer.

That in 1916 she was twenty-one, on the Essequibo, in British Guiana, where her father was in the customs service; that there was a run of mangrove below the landing and an animal in it, and that she went to the water every day for a year and then one afternoon put her hand on the side of his face, and he put his on hers.

"And after that," she says, "for a year and eight months, I was not by myself for one hour. Not waking or sleeping. Not in church, not at my father's table, not in the bath. I cannot describe it to you any better than that, and I have tried for forty years, and that is the nearest I have got. It was not company. It was that there was nowhere I could go where I was alone."

Inés Aguilar's hands are flat on the table.

"In December of 1917 four men came with a net. They were paid by the head. They took him out of the mangrove and I felt him get further away for eleven weeks, and then I did not feel him at all." She says it in exactly the voice she uses for the drains. "And then I was alone, Mrs Aguilar, in a way that I have not got a word for in English and nor, I think, has anybody. I was ill for two years. And then I was well. And in 1923 I married a very good man and I have been fond of him every day for thirty-seven years, and he is in the bungalow this afternoon with a rug on his knees reading the drains file, and I have been in a room with something missing from it every single hour of those thirty-seven years."

---

Nobody says anything. Out in the yard a hen goes about its business.

"Mr Aguilar," Hester says. "If you answer that animal, you will not be by yourself again for as long as he lives. You will be happier than you have ever been in your life, and you will not be able to tell anybody at this table what it is. And when he dies — which he will, in a pen, before you or after, and nobody can tell you which — you will be in a room with nobody in it for the rest of your days. And your wife will be in the room with you. And she will not be able to see what is missing, and she will know that it is."

Aguilar has put his hand over his mouth.

"I am not telling you not to," Hester says. "I want to be entirely clear about that, because I have thought about very little else since February. I built that Institute so that a man could. I wrote article nine so that if you did, nobody could take the place away from you. And I have wanted, for forty-two years, that there should be one other person in the world who knew what I know, and was not alone in it, and I have been sitting at a rail on Sunday mornings for six weeks watching a man with his hand on that animal's shoulder and wanting you to answer him so badly that I have had to go back up the path early."

She stops, because she has said a thing she didn't come up the hill to say.

She lets it stand.

"So you should know that about me," she says, "before you decide anything, so that you may discount it."

---

"And my question," says Inés Aguilar.

"Yes." Hester turns to her. "You asked whether the animal would take him from you.

"It will not take him. He will come home every night. He will be kind to you and to his children, if he is the man I think he is, and he will be here, at this table, every Sunday. What it will take is a part of him that you have never had — that nobody has ever had — and it will give that part to somebody who is not a person, and you will not be able to reach it, and you will know that it is there.

"I cannot tell you whether that is enough. I have been on the other side of it. My husband has been on your side of it since 1923, and I believe he has been happy, and I do not know, because I have never once asked him."

Inés looks at her for a long time.

"Señora," she says at last, "was it worth it?"

And Hester Bevan, who has run an institute and outwitted a navy and kept a thing hidden in a house for forty-two years that her husband knows is there, sits at a kitchen table at the top of a village and finds that nobody has ever put that question to her, and that she knows the answer, and has always known it.

"I would not give back one hour of it," she says. "And I would not wish it on anybody I loved."

---

She stays for another half an hour and they talk about other things, which is a kindness they do her and which she recognises. The eldest girl wants to be a teacher. The boy is on the boats with his uncle and hates it. The youngest has made his first communion in the autumn and would like it known.

When she stands to go, Inés Aguilar gets up too, and comes round the table, and — without any hurry — wipes her hands on her apron, and puts one on Hester's shoulder.

It's dry. On dry land, with a dry hand, through a cotton sleeve, it's almost nothing. It's the faintest thing. Hester has been touched by a great many hands in forty years and has learned to feel almost nothing off any of them, on purpose, and she stands in a kitchen with her hat in her hand and feels — very faint, very far off, like a lamp seen from a boat — a woman who is frightened and has decided not to be, and something else under it, steady as a floor, that Hester doesn't have a word for either.

"Thank you for coming up the hill, señora," Inés says.

"Thank you for drying your hands," says Hester, before she can stop herself.

Inés doesn't ask what she means. She nods, once, as though it's a perfectly ordinary thing to have been thanked for, and walks her to the gate.

---

The cable comes on the twelfth of May, on a Thursday, and it's addressed to the Director and it comes to Hester, because everything does.

*FLORIDA MALE DEAD ENTERIC TUESDAY. FEMALE SOUND ALONE. VANCE CANNOT BUY UNDER NOTICE. TRANSFER EXEMPT. BRINGING HER. ARRIVE JULY. DO NOT ARGUE. LESLIE.*

Hester reads it standing in the doorway of the office.

Then she reads it again, and something happens in her chest that hasn't happened in a very long time, and she has to put her hand on the door frame, because she's laughing — not loudly, not in any manner that could be heard past the door, but helplessly, with her eyes shut, for about ten seconds — at Jean Leslie, who has been in Florida four years and has not once asked anybody's permission for anything, and has not started now, and has put *DO NOT ARGUE* in a cable at a shilling a word.

---

Dr Tar and Dr Spilsbury are both in the office.

They're at the long table with the Bergen offprints between them, and they both look up, and she gives the cable to Dr Tar, because it's addressed to him, and watches Dr Spilsbury while Dr Tar reads it.

Dr Tar reads it and goes a colour and takes his glasses off.

And Dr Spilsbury, who hasn't been shown it, reads Dr Tar's face — she watches him do it, in about a second and a half — and then holds out his hand, and Dr Tar gives it to him, and he reads it.

He doesn't move for a moment.

It's the only time she has ever seen that man receive a fact he had not got in advance. She knows it, because she's been watching him for two months and she knows now exactly what he looks like when a result comes in that he has already put a figure on, which is like nothing at all. This isn't that. This is a man who went through fourteen returns in November and again in February and put *THERE ARE NONE* in a telegram at eightpence a word, and conceded the exemption on the strength of it, and has just found that there is one.

"Leslie," he says.

He says it with enormous admiration. That's the thing she'll remember. There's not a flicker of anything else in it.

"The Florida male was the soundest animal on the schedule," he says. "I'd have staked the box on him." He puts the cable down. "July."

"July," says Dr Tar.

And Hester, in the doorway, watches Alec Spilsbury's face take in a date, the way a man's face takes in the time on a station clock when he'd thought there was another hour before the train — and she understands, before either of the men in the room does, that a want which had eighteen months in front of it has just been told it has eight weeks.

---

He's at the rail at night after that.

Not all night. But she sees the lamp from the verandah at eleven, and at one, on two nights in the last week of May, and at the beginning of June Dr Pobil comes up to the bungalow at nine in the morning grey in the face and says, without sitting down, that the Englishman has asked whether he might stand at the rail in the small hours, and that he has said yes, because he said *wherever you like* in March and he's not going to be the first man to take back a word on this island.

"Is he doing anything?" Hester says.

"No. He stands there." Dr Pobil rubs a hand down over his face. "He stands there and he doesn't say anything and he doesn't go to bed, and Nestor knows he's there, and the animal knows he's there, and I can't find one single thing on the board to call."

"No," says Hester. "You would not. He wrote the board."

---

Aguilar's niece is married on the nineteenth of June, which is a Sunday, and there's a fiesta afterwards at the top of the village that goes on until two in the morning, and Dr Pobil sends Aguilar home for it on the Saturday night and tells him in Spanish in front of the whole pen that if he sets foot on the launch before Monday afternoon he'll be sacked, which isn't true, and which everybody knows isn't true.

Chumley's intake on the Monday is eight pounds.

Hester has the figure by ten from the day book, and she has it again at four from Dr Pobil's face, and she goes down on the Tuesday morning at twenty past six, which is not a Sunday, in her hat, and nobody on the station remarks on it.

---

There are four people at the rail and one on the step.

Dr Pobil, with the board. Dr Tar, at the far end, with his glasses on. Dr Spilsbury, four feet to the left of the board, with no cup. And Hester, who takes the place she always takes, which is at the end nearest the path.

Aguilar's on the step with his feet in. He came back on the Monday at half past four and hasn't gone to the store to sleep, and he's got the look of a man who has been awake a long time, and his hand's on the animal's shoulder.

The animal isn't lying against his knee this morning. He's hanging in the water a foot off, face to face, very still.

His hand comes up out of the water at twenty-six minutes past six, and goes to the side of Aguilar's face, and lies there.

It has done that most days since March. Dr Pobil has it in the margin of the day book with the time, eighty-one times, and Aguilar has taken the wrist and brought it down eighty-one times, gently, and not let go of it under the water, and Hester has seen perhaps twelve of them.

This morning he doesn't take the wrist.

---

She watches him not take it for thirty seconds.

He sits there with the animal's palm flat on his cheek and his jaw and the webbing over his ear, and his eyes shut, and his own hand on the animal's shoulder, and Hester Bevan, sixty-four years old, eight feet away, dry, in a hat, with nothing coming up her arm at all, knows exactly what's going through that man, because she had it in the brown water below her father's landing in 1916, and has had forty-two years to remember it exactly.

It isn't the calling. Not with the hand on the face. It's the calling *stopping*, for as long as the hand is there. It's the whole of that animal turning towards the one place in the world where there might be an answer, and waiting, with everything it has.

Aguilar's hand comes off the shoulder.

It comes up out of the water very slowly. It comes up the way hers came up, in 1916, when she had not decided anything and found that her hand was on the side of a face. It rises past the animal's arm and past his own chin, and turns, palm outward, towards the side of the animal's face, and she can see it shaking from the rail.

And it stops about an inch off.

---

Nobody at the rail moves.

Dr Pobil's hand is on the board. Dr Tar has stopped breathing; she can see it from where she stands. And Dr Spilsbury, four feet to her right, has put both his hands on the rail and his whole body has gone the way a pointer's goes, absolutely still, and she doesn't need to look at his face.

And Hester, at the end of the rail nearest the path, in her hat, finds that she wants that inch closed more than she has wanted anything since December of 1917.

She wants it with her whole body. She wants it for the man, who would never be alone, and for the animal, who is calling, and for herself, God forgive her, for herself — so that there would be one other person on this earth, at the top of that village, who knew. She built a place for this. She wrote a pension for it. She has sat in a kitchen and told a woman who dries her hands exactly what it would cost, and then sat on a rail every Sunday for three months wanting it anyway.

She doesn't make a sound.

She stands there in her hat and holds the rail and keeps her face exactly as still as she kept it for Dr Spilsbury in March, and doesn't make one sound, and doesn't move, because it is not hers. It has never been hers. The whole of the use of her, for forty-two years, has been to build a place where it could be somebody else's, and not to put her hand on the scale.

---

The hand stays an inch off for about the time it would take to say a short prayer.

Then it turns, and comes down, past the animal's face, and closes round the wrist. And brings it down, away from his cheek, into the water, the eighty-second time. And Aguilar bends forward over it on the step until his forehead is on his own knee and his other hand is in the water with the animal's wrist in it, and his shoulders go, and Hester looks away.

Chumley doesn't pull his hand back. He lets it be held.

After a minute Dr Pobil says, "Nestor. Ten minutes," quite quietly, in Spanish, and Aguilar says, "*Sí*," into his knee, and doesn't move for a little longer, and then lets go and comes up off the step, and goes past all of them to the store without looking at anybody, and Juan's brother's boy follows him with the dry towel.

---

Dr Spilsbury takes his hands off the rail.

He stands there for a moment. Then he turns his head and looks at Hester — not at Dr Tar, not at Dr Pobil with the board, at her — and she understands that he has been watching her, too, for some part of that minute, and that he's a man who can watch two things at once.

"He'll not do it now," he says.

"No."

"Why?"

He doesn't ask it the way a man asks a question he'll use. He asks it the way she's heard him ask nothing else in two months — as if he genuinely doesn't know, and would like to, and can't construct it from anything he's got. A sensitive man, a solitary animal, the only positive case the world will ever be offered, and an inch; and nothing on any card in any box in Massachusetts to account for the inch.

Hester looks at him for about as long as it takes to decide.

The reason is the information. Dr Tar told her that, too, in the house on the north side. If she tells this man why, she has told him what it costs; and if she tells him what it costs, she has told him who told Aguilar; and he has four of the five things already.

"The July intake will want housing, Dr Spilsbury," she says. "I shall need to know by Friday whether you are staying."

And she straightens her cuffs, and goes up the path.
