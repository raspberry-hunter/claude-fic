# Exemption

The boat from Florida is a converted shrimper called the *Lady Grace*, and it comes round the head at twenty past two on the nineteenth of July with a canvas tank lashed amidships and a woman standing on top of the wheelhouse, which is not where anybody ought to stand on a shrimper in a swell.

Spilsbury's on the jetty. So is the whole station. Mrs Bevan has seen to that; it's the second time he's been on this jetty with the entire establishment drawn up in a row waiting for an animal to come in a tank, and the first time was the twentieth of May 1954, and he'd been on the boat, and they'd been waiting for him.

He's aware of the symmetry. He'd have been a fool not to be, and he's never been accused of that, and he notes it and puts it by.

Dr Leslie comes down off the wheelhouse by way of a stanchion and a fish hold, and is over the rail before the *Lady Grace* has a line on the bollard, and lands on the planks in a pair of trousers that have been in salt water for eight days, sunburnt to the colour of a brick, with her hair tied up in a handkerchief.

"Reggie," she says. "You look *terrible*."

"Thank you, Jean."

"I mean it. You look like a man who's been standing at a rail since March." She turns round. "Pascual. Come here."

Pobil comes, and she puts both arms round his neck in front of everybody, and he makes a noise like a kettle and lifts her off the planks, and the Colonel, who's been brought down in a chair and put in the shade of the store, says "*Well*," with enormous satisfaction to nobody in particular.

Then Dr Leslie puts her feet back on the jetty and sees Spilsbury.

---

He'd wondered what she'd do. It's one of the reasons he came down to the jetty instead of waiting at the pens, where he'd have had a better view of the tank.

She looks at him for about two seconds. She has, he's always thought, the most legible face on this station apart from Pobil's, which is saying a great deal — everything goes across it like weather across a field — and what goes across it now is, in order, recognition, dislike, a quick hard piece of arithmetic, and something he'd have to call amusement.

"Dr Spilsbury," she says. "You conceded transfers."

"I did."

"In writing. In a telegram. At eightpence a word." She puts out a hand, which he takes. It's extremely firm and it's got tar on it. "*There are none.* Reggie sent me a copy. I've got it pinned over my desk next to the photograph of the mud."

"I went through the schedule twice."

"I know you did. So did I, in November, and I'd have told you the same." Dr Leslie lets go of his hand. "And then a perfectly good male got enteritis on a Tuesday, because the Florida water warms up in May like a bath and nobody in that building would listen to me about the intake pipe. Nobody could have seen it coming. I didn't. You didn't." She smiles at him, and it's not a nice smile, and he likes her for it more than he's liked anybody in a year. "But you *wrote it down*."

---

The female's called Mabel.

That's in the covering letter, which Dr Leslie hands to Tar on the jetty, and which Tar reads aloud at the pens in a voice Spilsbury recognises from 1947 as the one he uses when trying not to laugh.

"*Designation at Florida: F-2. Called Juno by the curator's office. Called Mabel by everybody who has actually fed her, on the grounds that nobody has ever successfully shouted Juno across a pond at six in the morning.*"

"Epsilon," says Tar, "in the day book."

"Epsilon, then," says Dr Leslie. "And Mabel at the rail, or she won't come."

She is perhaps eight years old, which is to say younger than anybody knows how to estimate, sound, lean from the crossing, and about nine feet. They put her into the second pen at four o'clock with the derrick and eleven men and Pobil swearing in two languages, the way they put the calf in with the old female in 1954 while he stood at the rail and watched, and she goes straight to the bottom and stays there.

The second pen is Beta's.

Nobody says so. Spilsbury watches every face on that concrete not say so, and Clarke's goes red, and Tar's doesn't move at all, and Pobil's does everything, and Dr Leslie looks at the water with her arms folded and says, "Well, it's the pen," and that's the end of it.

---

The protocol's Pobil's, and it's the one they teach, and Spilsbury knows it by heart because he helped write the reasoning out of it and then Pobil made them put the reasoning back in.

Four days in the adjacent pen, in sight and hearing. Intake on both. Then over.

For four days Chumley goes up and down the wall nearest the second pen, and hits it, and vocalises — Tar's tapes will have that, Tar's got a microphone on a pole — and the female stays on the bottom of Beta's pen and eats almost nothing.

And Aguilar sits on the step of the first pen with his feet in the water, the way he has sat every morning since February, and the calf doesn't come to him.

Spilsbury watches that on the second day, and on the third.

The animal that has put his head against that man's knee every morning for five months, and put his hand to that man's face eighty-some times, and been held by the wrist and let himself be held, goes past the step without stopping. Up the wall. Down the wall. Up. And Aguilar sits there with his hand in the water, and watches him go, and on the third morning Spilsbury sees his face, and it's the face of a man watching somebody he loves walk away down a road towards something better, and being glad, and it costing him exactly what it costs.

He doesn't write that down. There's nothing to write it on and nothing to score it against.

---

They put her over on the twenty-third, which is a Saturday.

It's quick. It's quicker than 1954, because there's no Navy and no Commander shifting from foot to foot, and because Pobil has done it now in three countries and teaches it twice a year. She goes over the wall in the sling at ten past four and into the water, and goes to the bottom, and the calf — the *male*; Spilsbury has made himself stop calling him the calf, and has noticed nobody else on this island has managed it — comes down the length of the pen at a speed that makes three men at the rail step back.

He stops a yard off her.

Nothing happens for about four minutes, and then she comes up off the bottom, slowly, and goes past him, close, and turns, and goes past him again.

"That's not a fight," says Dr Leslie, to nobody.

"No," says Pobil.

"She did that in Florida. With hers. The first week." Dr Leslie's got both hands on the rail. "Round and past and round. Vance thought she was going for him. I told Vance to go and have his lunch."

---

It's a fortnight and five days, and Spilsbury's at the rail for all of it.

He doesn't stand at night any more. There's no reason to. What he wanted to see at night was a man on a step and an animal calling, and the animal isn't calling; he can see that without a single thing coming up his arm, because the animal's stopped doing the thing with the wall. He stands from twenty to six until nine, and from four until dark, four feet to the left of Pobil's board, and watches two animals learn each other in a concrete box, and it is — he'd say this at any dinner table in Boston and be believed — the most interesting fortnight of behavioural observation he's had in his life.

They play. He's got the literature on play, and the literature on play in this species is four pages long and two of them are his, and he watches it for nineteen days and throws most of the four pages away in his head. They go at each other in the middle of the pen with a noise like a boat going over, and come apart, and she goes round him, and he goes under her, and on the tenth day she bites him on the shoulder, hard, without teeth, and he goes off down the pen in what can only be described as a sulk, and comes back in six minutes with the hose fitting.

He puts it in her hand.

She looks at it for a while, and then she drops it on the bottom of the pen, where it stays, and bites him again.

"*Ay*," says Pobil, beside the board, and has to go and stand behind the store for a minute.

---

Aguilar comes on the half past six now. That's the launch he's paid to be on.

He comes on it from the first of August and does the weights and the residue and goes home on the four o'clock, and doesn't sit on the step, and nobody on the station says a word about it, which Spilsbury has had five months to learn is how this station says the loudest things it's got.

On the eleventh he's early. He comes on the fish launch at a quarter past five, for no reason he gives anybody, and at twenty to six he's on the step with his trousers rolled and his hand in the water, the way he was all spring, and Spilsbury, at the rail, says, "Good morning, Mr Aguilar," and Aguilar says "*Buenos días*," and that's all either of them says.

At eleven minutes past seven the two animals are in the middle of the pen, face to face, about a foot apart, not moving.

Spilsbury's hands are on the rail.

---

She puts her hand on the side of his face.

It's the right hand. It goes flat against his cheek and jaw, the whole palm and the webbing, the way Spilsbury has watched the male's hand go against Aguilar's face eighty-some times since March — and for about a second nothing else happens, and he's aware that his own heart has gone up into his throat like a boy's, which hasn't happened to him in a very long time.

And the male puts his hand on hers.

---

Spilsbury doesn't hear anything.

That's the whole of it, and it's the thing he'll take back to Massachusetts, and he knows it at the time.

There's nothing. There's the generator, which is changing note. There's a gull. There are two animals in the middle of a concrete pen with their hands on each other's faces, perfectly still, perfectly quiet, and nothing whatever coming off them that a man at a rail can perceive — no sound, no movement, not a ripple — and he's put six years and the whole of his standing at this station into being at this rail when this happened, and it's happening, in front of him, at eleven minutes past seven on a Thursday, and it's exactly as silent as the pen was at half past five on the twenty-third of May 1954 when he had his own hand on an animal for a minute and got nothing.

And on the step, Nestor Aguilar's head comes up.

It comes up the way a man's head comes up when somebody says his name across a room. He's got his hand in the water to the wrist, and whatever it is has come up his arm, and Spilsbury watches it arrive in the man's face from eight feet away. He watches Aguilar's mouth open. He watches him laugh — once, not loudly, with his eyes filling — and take his hand out of the water, and look at it, as though he expected there to be something on it.

At the board, Pobil has put both hands over his face.

And at the far end of the rail, Spilsbury sees Tar's head turn. Not to the pen, which Tar has been watching; not to Aguilar; not to Spilsbury, who's nearer. Tar's head turns, instantly, the whole length of the rail, to Pobil. As though that's the place a man looks when the thing he's waited for happens, and there isn't a second place.

Spilsbury notes it. He doesn't know what to do with it, and he notes that too.

---

The animals come apart after perhaps a minute. She goes off down the pen. He goes after her. They go round.

Aguilar dries his hand on the towel. Then he stands up off the step, and picks the towel up, and folds it, and stands for a moment looking at the water; and then he says, "*Ya*," in the tone of a man shutting a gate, and turns round and comes across the concrete and past the rail and says to Pobil in Spanish something that Spilsbury catches about a third of, and the third he catches is *the rope*.

Pobil takes his face out of his hands and says something back, and puts his hand on the back of Aguilar's neck, and Aguilar goes off to do the weights.

Spilsbury stands at the rail.

He finds that what he feels isn't disappointment, which is what he'd have predicted, and has in fact written on a card in his head in advance — *AGUILAR — if pair forms before A. answers, study lost* — some time in May. He's felt disappointment about results a hundred times and he knows the shape of it and this isn't the shape.

The nearest he can get, standing there, is that he'd have liked very much to have heard it.

---

Dr Leslie finds him at the rail at six the next morning.

"Dr Spilsbury," she says. "Has anyone shown you the salt ponds?"

He turns round.

"Dr Leslie," he says, "you showed me the salt ponds on the twenty-second of May 1954, from twenty past eight until twenty to ten, to keep me out of a hut. I've had six years to work it out. It took me about four minutes on the day."

"Did I?" Dr Leslie doesn't blink. "Well, they've changed. They've put a culvert in the third pond and it's done something frightful to the gradient and I want another pair of eyes on it, and you're the only man on this island this morning who isn't busy." She jingles the truck keys. "I'll have you back by ten."

He looks at her.

He considers, in about a second and a half, the question of what on this station somebody might want the pen to themselves for at six o'clock on a Friday morning, three weeks after the silence came out of that water, and which woman has stood at that rail every Sunday since March eight feet back from the edge and never once come closer, and he gets the answer before she's finished jingling.

He could decline. He's perfectly entitled. Pobil said *wherever you like*.

"I'd be delighted," says Spilsbury, and gets in the truck.

---

"You're not stupid," Dr Leslie says, at the first bend, "so I shan't pretend. There's somebody who'd like that pen to herself for an hour and you've been standing at the rail since March."

"I know."

"Then why did you get in?"

"Because I'd rather talk to you for four hours than watch Mrs Bevan put her hand in the water for one," Spilsbury says. "And because I shan't learn anything from watching her that I haven't got already, and I'll learn a great deal from you, and I've never in my life turned down that arithmetic."

Dr Leslie drives for a while.

"That's the most horrible compliment I've ever been paid," she says.

"It isn't a compliment. It's the reason."

"Yes," says Dr Leslie. "That's what's horrible about it."

---

They talk about estuaries for two and a half hours, and it's the best two and a half hours he's had since the reels came back in 1957.

She's got eleven hundred miles of channel in the Ten Thousand Islands and she's done a hundred and sixty of them, and she knows more about the salinity regime of a mangrove estuary than anybody he's ever met, and she isn't a physiologist and doesn't pretend to be and doesn't need to be. She's got the torn net — four feet across, not a propeller, eleven miles up a run with no name — and she's got a theory about it that she won't let him have in full. She's got the Florida female's intake curve against the water temperature at the intake pipe. And she's got a thing she's worked out on the *Lady Grace* in eight days on top of a wheelhouse, which is that a mermaid won't breed in water that doesn't pulse.

"Pulse," he says.

"Fresh water. In and out. Rains and dry. A river that floods and drops. Every estuary that's ever held a population in the literature has a big freshwater pulse on it, and every pen in the world is dead still and full of sea." She's got both hands on the wheel and she's going too fast. "That pen's never going to have a calf in it the way it is. Not with that pair. Not with any pair. Pascual's going to hate me because I want a culvert and a header tank and a sluice, and Reggie's going to love it because it's expensive."

"And you'd like to see a big one."

Dr Leslie looks at him sideways.

"A big what?"

"A big pulse. An estuary with a river on it the size of a country." He's watching the ponds come up on the left. "You've got a hundred and sixty miles of small channel in Florida and a hypothesis that wants a large river, and there's exactly one on this coast, and nobody's surveyed it for anything but oil."

---

She stops the truck.

"The Orinoco," she says.

"Yes."

"I've written to Caracas four times since 1958. I've had two acknowledgements and a form in triplicate." Her face has gone through about four things and settled on fury. "You can't get a permit to put a boat in that delta without somebody with a name writing to somebody with a name, and I'm a woman on a soft-money survey in Florida who used to do Reggie Tar's graphs."

"I'll write," says Spilsbury.

She looks at him.

"What do you want for it?"

"Nothing," he says, and finds, a little to his surprise, that it's true. "I'll write to the Ministry of Agriculture and to a man at the Smithsonian who went shooting with a Venezuelan minister in 1955, and you'll have a permit by the dry season. And I've got eleven years of the Port of Spain broker's consignment records in a tea chest in Massachusetts that I bought for forty pounds in 1957 for the mortality figure and never used for anything else. Every animal that came out of the delta, by channel, by boat, by year. You'll want that for your survey design. I'll send you the tables."

Dr Leslie sits with both hands on the wheel.

"That's the second time you've done something for me for nothing," she says. "The first was Plymouth, in 1954, at supper, in writing, on the spot. I've never understood it."

"I like people who are going to be good," Spilsbury says. "It's the only thing I've ever been sentimental about. It's never once cost me anything."

---

In the truck on the way back, with the heat coming up, he asks her.

He hadn't meant to. He's aware of that, and aware that it's unlike him, and does it anyway, because she's the only person on the island who wasn't here in February, and therefore the only person whose face might tell him something by not knowing.

"Why won't Aguilar do it?"

Dr Leslie keeps her eyes on the road.

He watches her face. It's a very good face for his purposes, the best on the island, and it does exactly what he'd hoped: it goes through surprise — real surprise, not arranged — and then a quick hard look inward, like a woman going through a drawer and finding it empty, and then something very like anger.

"I don't know," she says.

"No. I can see you don't."

"I've been here three weeks." She changes gear too hard. "I know he didn't. Pascual wrote to me in June. He didn't say why and I didn't ask, because Pascual doesn't write *why* in letters, he writes it on the backs of feed sheets and leaves them on tables." She's gone red under the sunburn. "And I don't like not knowing, Dr Spilsbury, and I like it a great deal less when you're the one who's noticed it."

"Guess."

"*No*."

"Then I will," says Spilsbury comfortably, "and you may tell me nothing whatever, which you'll do in any case," and looks out of the window at the ponds going by.

Because he's got it. She doesn't know. Which means the reason isn't old, and it isn't something the station has always known, and it isn't in any letter. It's recent and it's local and it's small, and it happened between February and June, on this island, among perhaps three people, and the woman who'd have been the fourth was in Florida.

---

He gets the rest of it from Aguilar, on the twenty-fifth, and he gets it by asking a question he promised himself in March he wouldn't ask.

Not *do*. He never once asked the man to do anything. But he told Tar in his letter that he wouldn't ask Aguilar to do anything and wouldn't need to, and he's aware, at twenty to six on a Thursday morning at the rail with the pair going round below them, that *why didn't you* is a question in the same family, and asks it anyway.

"Mr Aguilar. I said I'd ask you nothing, and I haven't. I'd like to ask you one thing that isn't that, and you may tell me to go to the devil."

Aguilar is weighing fish. He doesn't look up.

"*Sí*, Doctor."

"Why didn't you answer him?"

The balance settles. Aguilar writes a figure in the book, and then he stands with the pencil in his hand and looks at the pen for a while, at the two animals going round, and Spilsbury watches his face, which isn't a good face for his purposes, and never has been; it's patient and closed and entirely courteous, and it's been standing next to him at this rail on and off since 1957 and has never once told him anything.

"Because somebody told me what it costs," Aguilar says. "Before. So I knew."

"Who?"

Aguilar turns round and looks at him.

And then he says — in exactly the tone he used on the fourth of December 1958, at this rail, in the half-light, when Spilsbury told him he'd had three sessions and got nothing, and Aguilar said he was sorry, and Spilsbury has never been able to account for it — "I'm sorry, Doctor."

And goes back to the fish.

---

Spilsbury sails on the thirtieth.

He goes up the path the evening before, on his own, at about nine, and stands for a minute outside the bungalow in the dark, where he can see the lamp in the sitting room and the shape of a woman reading a file to an old man in a chair.

He's got it. He's got very nearly all of it, and he stands in the dark and lays it out the way he'd lay out a column.

Mrs Bevan went up the village on the Sunday after Easter, in the station car, in a hat, at half past three; he saw the car go and noted it and didn't know why. Aguilar was told what it costs, before, by somebody, recently, on the island, among three people, none of them Dr Leslie. Aguilar took the animal's wrist down eighty-one times after that and stopped an inch off once, in June, with Mrs Bevan at the rail not making a sound. And a person can only tell a man what a thing *costs* if she has paid it.

So Hester Bevan has had it. Not the sensitivity; he's had that since November. The thing. The hand, and the hand back. Some time, somewhere, with an animal who is not, now, anywhere.

That's the fifth fact. He's got it off a technician's apology. He's never going to raise it with her, because he gave his word; and she's never going to know he has it, unless she reads it in his face, which she may.

And it doesn't help him in the least, and that's the thing that keeps him standing in the dark.

Because he still can't see why it *worked*. He can see the mechanism — a man was told a cost and declined to pay it — and the mechanism's no good at all. A man was offered the only case of it the world is ever going to have, with a lifetime of the thing on the other side, and a woman told him that one day he'd be alone, and on the strength of that he sat on a step for five months with his hand an inch off it and took it down. On *actuarial* grounds. Spilsbury would have paid that price in 1928 before he'd finished hearing what it was, and he'd pay it tonight, and so, he's quite sure, would every man worth knowing in his field.

So there's a thing in that man that the cost reached, and there's no equivalent thing in Spilsbury for it to reach, and he's fifty-four and it's the first time he's ever known that about himself as a fact and not as a boast.

He goes down to the guest block and makes a card.

*AGUILAR — told cost (by H.B.) — declined. Why does a cost decide it?*

He files it with *R.T. — block, 4 nights —*, and with one he made on the eleventh, which says *R.T. looks at P. first*, and which he's also got no account of.

---

The room in Massachusetts has the same four hundred and eleven cards in the shoe box and a tea chest under the window that hasn't been opened since the spring of 1957.

He opens it on the fourteenth of November, a Monday, at half past five in the morning, because he told Dr Leslie on a road in July that he'd send her the tables, and he's written to Caracas and to the man at the Smithsonian, and the permit's in hand for February, and he keeps his word the way a good instrument keeps its calibration.

It's eleven years of carbon flimsies in a clerk's hand, in Spanish, tied in bundles by year, and he does what he'd do with anybody's data, which is lay it out on the floor and start.

By boat. By season. By channel. The broker's boats worked the delta mouths from Boca de Serpiente round to the Pedernales channels and down the coast as far as the Pomeroon, and the clerk wrote down where each animal came out of the water, because a buyer in Naples or Florida pays more for a provenance, and Spilsbury tabulates it the way he's tabulated a hundred things, fast and cleanly, onto squared paper, with the lamp on.

It's nearly eight when he gets to the Pedernales rows, and stops.

---

Two animals a season, near enough, from 1949 to 1953. Regular as rent. The Pedernales mouth and the little channels behind it were the best ground the broker had.

One of them, in August 1953, he recognises. *Macho, adulto, cicatriz en la cola* — adult male, scar on the tail — sold on to an institute in the British islands. He's read the Martin's copy of that invoice. He read it in 1956, in the office, in the week he read everything. It was Beta.

Then 1954. One animal, in March. Then nothing after July.

1955. *Red cortada* against the *Santa Rita*, in April. Net cut. Again in June, against a different boat, two animals lost from the net at night, *la red cortada a cuchillo* — cut with a knife.

1956. Nothing taken. A note in the margin that the *Esperanza* won't go into the Pedernales channels any more, and the clerk's own comment, underlined: *superstición*.

1957. Nothing. The margin says the boats won't work the mouth, and gives a word in quotation marks, and he reads it twice.

*El ahogado.*

The drowned man.

---

And 1958. A single entry, in March, not a consignment at all — a report, copied in, from the master of the *Santa Rita*, explaining to his employer why he'd come back with an empty tank. The clerk's hand, careful, a little shocked:

*Seen at dawn in the Pedernales mouth, among the animals, in the water with them: a man. White. Bearded. Not swimming as a man swims. He looked at the boat. The crew would not put out the net. The master says it was the drowned man, as the crews at Cedros say, and that he will not go back.*

Spilsbury sits on the floor of the room in Massachusetts with his back against the tea chest and the flimsy in his hand, and doesn't move for some time.

---

He's aware, eventually, of several things at once, and he sets them out, because that's what he does.

That he's got it, and it isn't proof; it's a clerk's note about a boatman's story, and it'd be laughed out of any room in Boston.

That it's also the only reading of eleven years of consignment data that accounts for every row, and he's never in his life been wrong about a thing like that when it was this clean.

That in May of 1954, in a sick bay on a cay, he looked at Pobil's face while Pobil said *yes*, and knew it for a lie, and knew the animal hadn't died. He'd believed the lie anyway; the man couldn't manage anything else. He'd thought he knew the size of it.

He hadn't.

---

He gets up off the floor.

He goes to the desk and finishes the tables for Dr Leslie, cleanly, every channel, every boat, every year from 1949. And when he comes to the Pedernales rows he doesn't enter them. He folds them into the total for the eastern mouths, under *other*, where nobody will ever pick them out, and writes a footnote saying the eastern figures have been aggregated for reasons of small numbers.

It's the first table he's ever falsified in thirty-two years. He notes that, too. It isn't a lie; the numbers are small. It's a door he's closing behind himself so that nobody else can walk through it, and he's perfectly clear with himself about why.

A man who knows a thing has an option. And Dr Leslie is going into that delta in February with a boat and a permit he got her, and she's the cleverest person he's met in ten years, and she worked beside Cholmondeley for the better part of two years, and she once drove Spilsbury to the salt ponds for ninety minutes to keep him out of that boy's hut.

He posts the tables on the fifteenth.

Then he makes a card. He writes it slowly, which he doesn't do.

*C.C. — Pedernales — alive 1958.*

He doesn't put a question mark on it. And he doesn't file it under anything, because there's no letter in the box for it yet; he stands it up against the lamp on the desk, where he can see it, and sits and looks at it for a long time.
