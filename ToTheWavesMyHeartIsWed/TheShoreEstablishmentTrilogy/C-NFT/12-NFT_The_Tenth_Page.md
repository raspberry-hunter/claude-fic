# The Tenth Page

The bottle comes up out of the Caño Mánamo at eleven minutes past seven on the fourteenth of February 1966, and Jean holds it up to the light, and puts the hydrometer in, and reads it, and reads it again, and says a word that makes Hilario turn round at the tiller.

"*¿Qué pasa?*"

"It's salt."

"It's the *delta*, *señorita*. It's always salt at the mouth."

"We're not at the mouth. We're forty miles up. I took a bottle off that post in 1961 on the fourteenth of February and it was fresh enough to wash your face in." She holds the bottle out to him. "Taste it."

Hilario tastes it. Hilario has been on this water forty years, and his face does something slow, and he spits over the side and doesn't say anything else for about a mile.

---

They finished the dike in November.

She knew they would. She had it from an engineer in a bar in Tucupita in 1961 and put it in a letter to Reggie on page nine, and the Corporación put up a sign in 1964 with a painting on it of rice growing where there had been mangrove, and she photographed the sign and numbered the plate. And in November they closed the Mánamo. A wall of earth and stone from bank to bank, forty miles below Tucupita, to keep the floods off the islands and make farms.

And the Orinoco, which had been coming down the Mánamo into the Gulf of Paria every wet season since before there were people to watch it do it, went somewhere else.

Jean has spent eleven years on a single idea, and the idea is that a mermaid won't live in water that doesn't pulse. Fresh in the rains, salt in the dry, in and out, like breathing. Every estuary that ever held a population in the literature had a big river on it doing that.

Somebody in Caracas has taken the pulse out of the western delta with a wall.

---

She counts.

That's what she's for. She stands up on the gunwale, which Hilario has given up telling her not to do, and puts her hat on and her hand up, and counts.

At the mouth of the caño where she counted six in 1961, she counts two.

At the reach below the old mission, where there were four in 1961 and five in 1963, she counts none, and she waits three hours, and still none.

At the place above Pedernales — outside the closed water, a mile outside, which she has measured on the chart twice, because she doesn't go one yard inside it and never will — she sits in the boat at dawn with the engine cut and the camera in her lap and looks east, towards the mouths behind Pedernales that she's never seen, and puts a bottle over the side on a string, and pulls it up, and it's salter than any bottle she's ever taken on this coast.

She sits there a long time. There's nothing in the water. She takes a photograph of the nothing and numbers it.

*Plate 1,106.*

She thinks about thirty-odd heads in a back channel she's never going to see, and a man with them, and whether he has a bottle, and whether he knows.

---

It isn't only the *sirenas*.

She's a woman on a soft-money survey and she's never pretended to know anything about anything but water and mermaids, and she's not going to start now. But she's been coming up and down these caños with Hilario for five years, and she has eyes.

The moriche palms along the bank above the old mission are dead. All of them, for a mile, standing up grey in the sun like a burnt forest. The soil's gone sour when the water went; Hilario says the ground turned to acid when it dried and the palms drank it. And there's a Warao village on the east bank of the reach where Hilario's cousin married in, where Jean has stopped every season since 1961 to buy fish and fill the water cans, and in February of 1966 the landing's empty and the houses are empty, and the well at the back of the landing that Jean has drunk from four times is brackish.

Hilario's cousin has gone south, with her husband's people, to where the water's still fresh. Hilario says a great many families have. Hilario says it in a voice she hasn't heard from him before, and then he doesn't say anything about it again, and she doesn't ask him to.

She photographs the empty landing. She numbers the plate. She writes underneath it in her book *Well brackish. Village gone south. Ask nobody for a count of this; it isn't yours to count.* And then she sits in the bottom of the boat with her hat off for a while, and Hilario steers, and neither of them says anything.

---

In September she's in Florida, and it's the other way round.

The Ten Thousand Islands are a hundred and sixty miles of mangrove on the bottom of the peninsula, and Jean has done every mile of them since 1956, and in the middle of them, eleven miles up a run with no name, there's a place where in March of 1956 a net came up torn four feet across, and where by 1962, sitting in a skiff at dawn for nineteen mornings running, she'd counted six.

Six. The only wild ones in Florida. The whole reason she's never been able to stop coming back.

And north of the Ten Thousand Islands, in 1962, a company from Miami bought a hundred and seventy square miles of cypress and pine, and began to sell it to people in Ohio over the telephone, and dig canals to drain it.

---

The canals don't take the pulse away. That's what takes her two seasons to see, and she only sees it because she's been standing in Venezuela with a bottle.

They make it *worse*.

Before the canals, when the summer rains came down on Big Cypress, they came down on a hundred and seventy square miles of wet ground, and sat there, and soaked, and seeped out south through the strand into the islands slowly, over weeks — a long soft pulse, fresh coming in a little at a time over the whole wet season, the way breath comes.

After the canals, it comes down the canals.

All of it. At once. The morning after a storm the water at the mouth of the Faka Union goes from sea to river in about eleven hours, and the oysters on the bars below it die in a day, and eleven miles up the run with no name there's a line of dead fish along the mangrove roots like a tide mark. And then in a week it's all run out and it's salt again.

It's a header tank. It's four hundred gallons of rain off the store roof in a single slug, every time it rains, on water that had spent ten thousand years getting it slowly.

She counts. Six in 1962. Four in 1964. In September of 1966 she sits in the skiff at the mouth of the run with no name for twenty-three mornings, and counts two. The same two, she thinks. Old.

In September of 1967 she sits there for thirty mornings.

---

She writes it up in Florida in October of 1967 on Vance's porch with a bottle of gin and the fan going and the whole of eleven years spread over the floor in plates.

And the thing that makes her put the pencil down and walk out into the yard and stand there in the dark for a quarter of an hour is that it isn't two findings.

It's one.

Venezuela took the pulse away. Florida made it violent. One site too salt and the other too fresh and too sudden, and both of them emptying, in the same four years, in exactly the same way. It was never about how salt the water is. It was about the *shape* of the fresh water arriving. Slow and wide and long, like breathing. Anything else — nothing, or a slug — and they go.

She's had the idea since the *Lady Grace*. She's spent eleven years proving it. And the proof is two empty rivers.

---

She sends it to Reggie, and to John, and to Pascual, and to a man in Massachusetts, in that order, and it's the man in Massachusetts who writes back first.

He writes back in eleven days, which means he read it the morning it came, and it's four pages in the fast clear hand, and the first line is:

*Dr Leslie, you have now got there before me three times, and I find I have stopped minding, which is a sign of age.*

And the rest is a plan.

---

The Scientific Committee of the International Commission for Captive Sirenians meets on the thirteenth and fourteenth of November 1967, on the Viale Aventino, and Jean's in the room, because she's been asked to give the counts, and she sits at the long table under eleven flags in her good suit with her plates in a box and her hands wet, and watches Alec Spilsbury do something that she'll never, as long as she lives, entirely forgive him for being so good at.

He's sixty-two. He looks it now, a bit; he's gone thinner and there's a stick against his chair that he doesn't use in front of anybody. He's chaired this committee for six years and he's chaired it perfectly, and he chairs it perfectly on the Monday morning, and moves the business in the order he wants it, and when he gets to her, he says, "Dr Leslie," and nothing else, and sits back.

She gives the counts.

She gives them flat, in order, the way Reggie taught her to in 1952, with every plate, and every bottle, and the empty landing, and the dead palms, and the oysters, and the two old ones in the run with no name who weren't there on any of thirty mornings. It takes forty minutes. Nobody interrupts. When she's finished there's a silence round the table of a kind she has heard before only once, in the office at the Martin, when Reggie read the tables out in 1954.

And then Spilsbury leans forward and puts his elbows on the table.

---

"Right," he says. "Here's what we're going to do."

And for the next two hours Jean sits at the end of the table and watches that man take her eleven years and turn them into a weapon.

He's been to Caracas. Twice, since October. He's had dinner with the Minister of Agriculture, and with a man at the Corporación whose name he gives, and with the American ambassador, who went to school with somebody on the committee in Washington who's responsible for the interstate highway north of the Ten Thousand Islands. He's been to London. He's written to the Governor of Florida in terms that make Bassani put his hand over his mouth. He's got a resolution drafted — he's got it in front of every man at that table, typed, with Jean's name in the second line and her figures in the annex — which commits eleven governments to a finding that the maintenance of natural freshwater flow into estuaries is a condition of the survival of the species in the wild, and to a review of every flood-control and drainage scheme in any estuary on the schedule, and to consultation with the Commission before any new one.

It won't bring back the Mánamo. He says so. It won't fill in a canal in Florida. He says that too.

"But it's on the record," says Spilsbury, "in eleven governments, with a finding and a date. And the next time a man in a ministry wants to put a wall across a river with *sirenas* in it, somebody in Rome will be able to ask him in writing what it's for. Which I'm told" — and he doesn't look at the Chief Veterinary Inspector at all — "is a remarkably effective question."

It passes on the Tuesday morning. Unanimous.

---

And on the Tuesday afternoon, the same man, from the same chair, in the same voice, moves item eleven.

Item eleven is a licence for a study of the formation of the parental bond in captive-born calves.

There have been fourteen calves born in holdings on the schedule since June of 1962. Nobody knows anything about how they bond with their mothers, because nobody's been allowed to find out, and Spilsbury would like to find out, and he's drafted a design, and it's a good design, and Jean reads it in about four minutes and feels her face go cold, because the design is to take one calf, at eight weeks, from its mother, for a graduated period, and see.

"It's worth asking," Spilsbury says, pleasantly, to the table. "I'd be failing in my duty if I didn't. We've got a population in pens for the first time in the history of the species, and it's the only population on earth that's going to be here in twenty years if Dr Leslie's counts are right, and they are. And we don't know the first thing about how a calf of this species attaches, or what happens if it doesn't. I'd like to."

---

Nobody says anything for a moment.

Then the Chief Veterinary Inspector of the International Commission, who has been sitting at the far end of the table all day with his glasses on and his hands flat on the papers, says one word.

"No."

"Pobil —"

"No." Pascual doesn't raise his voice. He doesn't need to; Jean has known him since 1953 and she's never once heard him more frightening than at that volume. "Not at eight weeks. Not at eight months. Not in any holding I can walk into, and I can walk into all of them. You can put it to the vote, and I'll ask what it's for, and when you tell me, I'll shut the pen."

"And the Director-General?" says Spilsbury, turning his head.

"Against," says Reggie, from the other end.

"Then it fails," says Spilsbury. "I can count, and in any case Pobil's got a badge, and I'm told it's quite a small one and it works." He picks up the next paper. "Thank you, gentlemen. It was worth asking. Item twelve. Bassani, your filters."

---

Afterwards, in the corridor, while Reggie's being cornered by a man from Bergen, Jean finds herself next to Spilsbury at the window, and she's so angry she can't see.

"You did both," she says. "In the same *day*."

"I did." Spilsbury's leaning on the stick now, because there's nobody in the corridor but her. "The resolution's the best thing this committee's ever done and it wouldn't exist without you, and item eleven was a good question, and Pobil was always going to say no to it, and he was right, and I asked anyway." He looks out at the wall. "I don't see the difficulty, Dr Leslie. I want the species to exist for another forty years, because I want there to be a field. And I want to know how a calf attaches. Those are both perfectly true. Some days they're the same want and some days they aren't."

"*How* do you live like that?"

"Very comfortably," says Spilsbury. "It's Tar who can't. That's why he keeps Pobil." He nods down the corridor, to where Reggie has got free of Bergen and is coming towards them with his face set, and Pascual behind him, limping very slightly on the left. "Look at him. He's going to tell me that was unforgivable, and I'm going to agree, and he's going to go home and tell Pobil he was angry, and Pobil's going to tell him he wasn't angry enough, and they'll both enjoy it." He straightens up off the stick. "Your counts were very beautiful. I'm sorry about the landing."

---

The paper — the other paper, the one with the baskets — came out in March of 1966, and it's in the box with the plates, and Jean's read it perhaps a hundred times, and nobody else has read it at all.

*Leslie, Aguilar and Clarke. Transfer of information between a sensitive human subject and a paired adult male of Nereopithecus aestuarius under forced-choice conditions.* Nine pages. Thirty-nine of forty, one of them a miss caused by a calf. Nineteen of forty in the control. Eleven voids. One in twenty-seven thousand million. Spilsbury's eleven pages of objections, answered one by one, in an appendix, because he asked for that and it was the right thing to ask for.

Two paragraphs in the science column of *The Times*, in April, that got the species name wrong.

A letter to *The Times* from a bishop, in May, asking whether this meant anything about souls.

A letter the following week from a professor of physiology at Cambridge saying that it did not.

And then nothing. Not a line, not a reply, not a citation in any journal on the list in eighteen months. The world had looked at thirty-nine of forty and a heron, and shut the page, and gone off to do something else.

"It's published," Reggie wrote to her, in June of 1966, when she wrote to him furious. "It's in the record. It's on every shelf on the list with a number and a date. It isn't going anywhere, Jean. Nobody can take it off the shelf. They can only decline to take it down. And one day somebody will." And at the bottom, in a different ink, in a hand she'd have known in the dark: *And the heron was the best sentence in it. P.*

---

In January of 1968 the Commission amends Schedule C.

No licence shall issue for the taking of any animal of the species from the wild, from any water, for any purpose, by anybody.

There's no need. There have been fourteen calves born in holdings since 1962, and nine have lived, and there are more animals of the species in pens in eleven countries than anyone has been able to count in the wild since 1964. The Port of Spain broker hasn't declared a take in two years. Señor Rocha has retired to Maracas and grows orchids.

The trade's over. It didn't end because anybody ended it. It ended because the pens began to fill themselves.

Jean reads the amendment in the lecture hut at the Martin, where she's been since December, because John asked her, and Hester asked her, and there was nobody in Florida left to count. And she thinks about Hester's deed, which she has never seen but has had described to her by Pascual in some detail, and about article four, and about the one door in it.

*Save where no specimen is obtainable.*

It's obtainable. From the pens. From the next pen along. It won't ever be obtainable again from anywhere else.

She'd spent eleven years trying to find out what an estuary has to be so that they'd live in the wild. And what she's found out is that there isn't one left on this coast that is, and the safest place on earth for an animal of the species in 1968 is a concrete box with a header tank on it.

She sits in the lecture hut with the leak going and hates that more than she's ever hated anything.

---

Hester goes down to the pens on the seventeenth of March 1968, which is a Sunday, at twenty past six in the morning, for the last time.

She's seventy-two. She's been in bed since Christmas, mostly. The doctor from the main island has been three times and Pascual has been, and Pascual came out of the bungalow in January and sat on the verandah step with his head in his hands and didn't say anything to anybody, and Jean sat down beside him and didn't say anything either.

On the Saturday Hester sends for Nestor. On the Sunday morning, at six, Nestor and Juan's brother carry her down the path in the cane chair with the poles, the way they carried the Colonel in 1960, at a walk, and Jean walks beside her, because Hester asked her to.

There's nobody else at the pens. There's never anybody else at the pens at twenty past six on a Sunday. Nestor made sure of it twelve years ago and has never had to make sure of it again.

---

Hester doesn't want the chair at the rail. She wants the step.

Nestor puts her there. He lifts her out of the chair himself, as if she weighs nothing, which she very nearly doesn't now, and sets her down on the edge of the pen at the step, eight feet in from the rail, where a towel used to be left, and takes off her shoes and her stockings without a word, as though he's done it every Sunday of his life. And he rolls her sleeve up to the elbow. And then he gets into the water himself, to the waist, and stands below her with his hands ready, and says, "*Ya, señora.*"

Jean stands at the rail. She's been told the rule, by Pascual, in 1958, by letter. She has her back to it.

But she can see the water. You can see the water from the far end of the rail if you stand at an angle, and she stands at an angle, because she has never in her life done what she's told when it mattered.

---

Hester Bevan puts her hand in.

And the pen comes to her.

All of it. Chumley first, because Chumley has been coming to that step since he was the size of a dog. And Mabel after him, and Leslie, who's six now and too big for anything and still thinks everything's hilarious, and the second calf, who hasn't got a name yet because John says he's not naming another one after anybody until he's sure she'll live, and she's two and she has. Four animals, in a half-circle, at the step, in the grey light, very close, very quiet, around an old woman's hand.

Jean can't feel anything. She's never been able to. Hester told her once, years ago, very kindly, that she'd had hopes of her, and been wrong.

But she can see Hester's face.

It's doing something. For the first time in the fourteen years Jean has known her, Hester Bevan's face is doing something, where anybody could see it — not much, and not for long; the eyes close, and the mouth goes, very slightly, at one corner — and it stays like that for about as long as it takes to say a short prayer.

"It is very loud, Mr Aguilar," Hester says.

"*Sí, señora*," says Nestor, from the water. "In a good way. Like a kitchen."

"Yes," says Hester. "Like a kitchen."

---

She dies on the Thursday.

On the Tuesday night she asks for Jean, and not for anybody else, and Jean goes up to the bungalow at nine and sits in the chair by the bed with the lamp turned down, and Hester, propped on the pillows in a white bed jacket with her hair plaited like a girl's, gives her instructions.

She gives them in order, clearly, in the voice she used for the drains.

"The Trust is in order. John has the deed. Dr Tar has a letter, which he is not to open until after the funeral, and which he will open before the funeral, and which Dr Pobil is to take off him if he does." She breathes for a while. "And the notebook."

Jean goes very still.

"Mr Cholmondeley's notebook," says Hester Bevan, "is in the second drawer of the tallboy under my winter things, where it has been since May of 1954. I have thought for fourteen years about who should have it. Not Dr Tar. He knows why, and has said so himself, and he is right. Not Dr Pobil, who would burn page two hundred and six, and be wrong. Not you, my dear, because you would read it tonight."

"I *would*," says Jean, whose face is going.

"Yes. John is to have it. John is to number it, and not open it, and put it in the Institute's safe in a sealed envelope with a date on the outside, and the date is to be the twentieth of May 1984. Or before, if its author should ever ask for it." A long breath. "Thirty years. That was the arrangement I made with myself. I shall not be here for it. I find I do not mind. It will be in the record, and John will know where it is, and nothing can go missing."

---

And then Hester is quiet for such a long time that Jean thinks she's asleep, and reaches over to turn the lamp down the rest of the way.

"Dr Leslie."

"I'm here."

"You asked me a question in 1954. In the sitting room. After Mr Cholmondeley had gone. You asked me how I had known what to say to him, and I did not answer you."

"You didn't. You gave me a biscuit."

"I gave you a biscuit." The corner of Hester's mouth goes again, very slightly. "I shall answer you now, because there is nobody left to whom I owe the not-answering. There was an animal. In 1916. On the Essequibo, below my father's landing. And I put my hand on his face, and he put his on mine." She says it the way she'd say the time. "Dr Tar and Dr Pobil have the rest of it, and Mr and Mrs Aguilar, and I should be glad if you would ask them, afterwards, and not me. I am rather tired. But there is one thing none of them have, because I have never said it aloud to a living soul, and I should like somebody to have it, and I should like it to be you."

"Hester —"

"They give you your name first," says Hester Bevan. "Mr Aguilar has his. He is a heron. I had mine in the October of 1916, a week after the hand, before I knew what it was, and I thought for a week that I was making it up."

She opens her eyes and looks at the ceiling.

"My father kept a lamp at the end of the landing. For the launches, at night. A hurricane lamp, on a post. It was lit every evening of my childhood at six o'clock and it was there every night, and you could see it from a long way out on the water." A breath. "That was my name. The light at the end of the landing. The one that is always there."

---

Jean sits by the bed with the lamp turned down and can't say anything at all for a long time.

When she can, what she says is, "Thank you," and it comes out in pieces.

"You are very welcome, my dear." Hester's eyes have closed again. "They will have one for you. I am quite sure of it. Wherever you have been counting them. You must not be sad that you will never hear it. It will be there all the same."

---

Hester Bevan dies at twenty past six on the morning of Thursday the twenty-first of March 1968, in her own bed, with the shutters open on the harbour, and doesn't make a sound.

Jean's asleep in the chair. She wakes because the room's changed. She sits there for a while in the grey light before she gets up, and then she goes and opens the door, and Pascual's on the verandah step in his coat, where he's been since four, and he looks up at her face and doesn't need to be told.

---

Reggie opens the letter on the Wednesday, the day before the funeral.

Pascual doesn't take it off him. Jean's on the verandah of the house on the north side when Reggie comes out with it in his hand, and she sees Pascual look at it and look at Reggie and decide, in about four seconds, not to.

It's one page. Reggie reads it out, on the verandah, in the dark, to the two of them, because that's the arrangement, and because, he says, Hester will have known perfectly well he'd open it early and written it to be read aloud.

> *Dr Tar,*
>
> *You will have opened this before the funeral. Dr Pobil will have let you.*
>
> *I wrote article four so that it could not be suspended by a Director on an afternoon when he was fond of an animal. I should like you to know that it was never once suspended, and that it was never once needed, because the pens began to fill themselves before anybody had to buy another animal out of the sea on its account, and Dr Leslie tells me they will go on doing so. I built a clause to make a thing impossible, and somebody else made it unnecessary, which is the better way. I should like her thanked.*
>
> *I have given the notebook to Mr Clarke, with a date. You will want to know the date. It is the twentieth of May 1984. You will be sixty-five. I trust Dr Pobil will still be in a position to stop you.*
>
> *There is a drawer in my writing desk with a cable in it from Massachusetts about my husband. I should like it returned to its author, with my compliments, and the information that it was the only honest thing I received that week, and that I kept it on the side table for seven years.*
>
> *The light was on the landing, Dr Tar. It was on every night.*
>
> *H. B.*

---

Reggie folds it. He doesn't say anything.

Neither does Pascual. After a while he takes Reggie's hand, on the verandah, in the dark, where Jean can see, and Jean — who has known about the two of them since about the summer of 1954, and has never once said so, to either of them, or to anyone — looks at the harbour and lets them.

"The light," Pascual says eventually. "What light?"

"It's her name," Jean says, to the harbour. "Her animal's name for her. She told me on Tuesday night. I'll tell you properly tomorrow, after." She wipes her face with the back of her wrist. "I think she put it in the letter so that I'd have to."

"*Ay*," says Pascual. "Of course she did."

And Reggie, who has been sitting perfectly still with the folded letter on his knee, says, very quietly, "She's the only person who ever outmanoeuvred all three of us at once," and then puts his face in his hands, and Jean gets up and goes inside to put the kettle on, because somebody has to, and because it's what Hester would have done.
