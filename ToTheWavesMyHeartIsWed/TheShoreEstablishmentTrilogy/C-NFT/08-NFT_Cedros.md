# Cedros

The calf is twenty-six days old on the tenth of July and she's the size of a large dog and she's called Leslie.

John named her. He cabled Rome on the fourteenth of June at a quarter past nine in the evening, eleven hours after she was born, and the cable said *FEMALE BORN 0610 BOTH SOUND NAMED LESLIE DON'T ARGUE CLARKE*, and Reggie read it aloud at the table in the flat under the window and then had to go and stand on the landing for a while, and Pascual read it four times and then wrote to Jean, and Jean's reply came to Rome ten days later and was one line long and not fit to be read out in front of Signora Bruni.

The first animal of her kind ever born in a pen. Pascual wasn't there. He was in Lisbon, with the badge, shouting at a man about a filter.

He's here now.

---

He's on the step at twenty to six on the tenth, with his trousers rolled, because it's the first morning, and there's a man he's known nine years sitting next to him with his hand in the water.

Pascual puts his own hand in. He does it every time. It gets him what it's always got him, which is warm salt water and a faint smell of fish, and not one thing else, and he's stopped minding that somewhere around 1958, and does it anyway.

Mabel's at the shallow end with the calf against her side. Chumley is going up and down the north wall under the header pipe, where the rain came down in May, doing nothing in particular, the way a man walks up and down outside a door.

"*Tres*," Nestor says, with his hand in. "It's loud in there now, Doctor. In a good way. Like a kitchen."

"She's feeding?"

"She's feeding. He's frightened of her." Nestor takes his hand out and dries it. "The calf. Not the mother. The big one's frightened of the calf. He goes up to look and she opens her mouth and he goes off down the wall."

Pascual laughs, and it goes up off the water in the half-light, and Nestor smiles at the balance.

Then Pascual stops laughing and tells him the rest of it, in Spanish, with nothing written down.

---

It takes about ten minutes. Nestor doesn't interrupt.

When it's done he sits on the step with his hands on his knees for a long time and looks at the water.

"The drowned man," he says eventually.

"Yes."

"Tito's told that story at Christmas for five years. At my table. After the rum." Nestor shakes his head slowly. "*El ahogado de Pedernales.* A white man in the water with the *sirenas*, with a beard, who cuts the nets. My children love it. Luisito makes him tell it twice." He looks at his hands. "I thought it was a story, Doctor. Tito thought it was a story. It's the kind of story fishermen tell."

"It is the kind of story fishermen tell."

"And it's the boy. The one the Señora hired. The one who sat on this step before me." Nestor's face does something slow. "*Madre de Dios.*"

"Yes."

---

"You want me to go to Cedros," Nestor says.

"I want to ask you whether you'll go to Cedros. And say to Tito, and to anybody Tito says, that the Englishman mustn't be taken across. Not in a letter. Not on a telephone. On the jetty."

"They don't go across. They haven't gone across since 'fifty-six. Tito won't go within a mile of that mouth, he says the water's unlucky." Nestor considers. "They'll say that to the Englishman for nothing. They'll say it to anybody."

"He'll offer money."

"Then they'll say it to him for nothing and take his money for a trip to the Bocas and put him ashore at Icacos." Nestor dries his hands a second time, which he doesn't need to do. "Except Faustino."

"Who's Faustino?"

"Faustino Mata. The *Esperanza*. He worked for the man in Port of Spain for twelve years, all the Pedernales water, and when the nets started getting cut he lost two and blamed everybody, and then the other boats wouldn't go in, and then the Commission closed it, and he's had to sell his second engine." Nestor looks at Pascual sideways. "Faustino would sell his mother to a gringo for a new engine, Doctor. And he doesn't believe in the drowned man. He says the nets were cut by Guardia from the other side."

Pascual sits on the step with that.

"I'll go," Nestor says. "Tito doesn't take a word from a cousin by post. He takes it from a cousin. And my wife's sister's husband is at Icacos, and he knows everybody, and he talks too much, which is useful." He stands up. "But I can't do anything about Faustino. Nobody can do anything about Faustino. Faustino's been a son of a bitch since we were boys."

---

He goes up to the bungalow at ten, because there's one more person on this island who has to be told, and he's not going to let her find out by watching his face.

Hester Bevan is sixty-six and has lived without her husband for sixteen months, and the sitting room's the same except for the long chair on the verandah, which has gone, and there's a cable in a frame on the side table that says *BEVAN WAS THE BEST CHAIRMAN I EVER SAT UNDER*.

He doesn't dress it up. He tells her in about four sentences, standing up.

She doesn't move at all. He watches her not move. She's sitting with her hands folded in her lap and her face is doing exactly what she told Spilsbury it would do, which is nothing, and it goes on doing nothing for a long time; and then one hand comes out of her lap and goes flat on the table beside the tea tray, the way it did in May of 1954 when Reggie told her which plates, and stays there.

"Thank you, Dr Pobil," she says.

"Mrs Bevan —"

"I have wanted to be told that for eight years," says Hester, "and I have made quite certain for eight years that nobody on earth could tell me. And now somebody has." She looks at her hand on the table. "Is he well?"

"I don't know."

"No. You would not. Nobody would." She takes the hand back. "Is he alone?"

And Pascual, who's been asked that question once before — by a woman in Shropshire, in a letter, eight years ago, in the second sack — stands in the bungalow sitting room and gives her the only answer anybody's got.

"The boats won't go in because there's a man in the water with the animals," he says. "With them. Among them. That's what the story says. Every version of it."

Hester Bevan shuts her eyes.

"Then he is answered," she says. "Thank you. You may go now, Dr Pobil. I should like to sit here for a little."

---

The broker's holding shed is on the south quay at Port of Spain behind a rum warehouse, and it's licensed under Schedule A of the International Commission for Captive Sirenians since the first of January, and on the sixteenth of July at nine in the morning the Commission's Chief Veterinary Inspector walks into it without notice, with a small badge in his pocket, and finds nothing whatever to shut.

That's the worst of it, and he stands in the shed for an hour and a half and has it.

Three animals, in two tanks, correctly paired. Shade over both. Intake weighed in and residue weighed out, in a book, in a clerk's hand, daily. The returns in a drawer, filed, correct, on Hester's form, back to 1956. The man who owns the shed — Señor Rocha, sixty, in a white suit, who has been paid by the head for forty years and whose boats took Beta out of the Pedernales mouth in August of 1953 — has had every single thing the Martin ever taught him done to the letter since the Commission's schedule came in, because it's cheaper to deliver an animal alive than to lose three to get one, and Señor Rocha has always been able to count.

"Everything is in order, Doctor?" Señor Rocha says.

"Everything is in order."

"I am very glad." He's courteous. He's been courteous for forty years. "Since your Commission, I lose one in six on the passage. Not one in three. I have the figures, if you wish. It has been very good for business."

Pascual stands in that shed with his hands behind his back, because the alternative's to put them somewhere, and says, "I'd like the figures," and gets them, and they're true.

---

At the door, on the way out, Señor Rocha says one more thing.

"You have a colleague in Trinidad, Doctor. An Englishman. He came to my office on Saturday and asked for the name of a master who had worked the eastern mouths before the closure. I told him the waters are closed. He said he was aware of it, and that he wished only to speak to a man who knew them." Señor Rocha spreads his hands. "I gave him a name. It is not forbidden to give a name."

"What name?"

"Faustino Mata." Señor Rocha smiles, very slightly. "Who no longer works for me. He is at Cedros, I believe. So, since Monday, is your colleague."

---

Cedros is the end of Trinidad.

The road runs out at it. It's a long curve of beach with coconut palms down to the water and a jetty and a fish market and a rest house with four rooms, and beyond it, across twelve miles of the Serpent's Mouth that goes brown in the rains with the whole of the Orinoco coming out of it, there's a low grey line on the horizon that's Venezuela, and behind that line, somewhere, the eastern mouths behind Pedernales.

Nestor came down on the bus on the Sunday. Pascual comes on the Wednesday afternoon in a hired car, and Nestor's at the rest house with a man of fifty with no front teeth who's introduced as Tito, and who shakes Pascual's hand and says in Spanish that he's heard about the doctor who shouted at the Americans, and that it's an honour.

"The Englishman's in number four," Nestor says. "He's been on the jetty every morning at five. With binoculars."

"And?"

"And every boat at Cedros has told him the water's unlucky and nobody goes." Nestor's face is quite still. "And Faustino's been drinking with him at the rum shop since Monday night."

---

Pascual finds him on the jetty at five the next morning.

It's not light. There's a grey line on the water where the sky's going to be and the long low line of the delta on the horizon, which you can only see because it's darker than the sea, and there's a man at the end of the jetty in a linen jacket with a pair of naval binoculars round his neck, not using them, with his hat in his hand.

Spilsbury doesn't turn round.

"Pobil," he says. "I thought it might be you by about Wednesday. Tar wouldn't come. He'd not trust himself on the jetty."

"No."

"And you've got Aguilar here. And Aguilar's got a cousin." Spilsbury lifts the binoculars and looks at the grey line on the horizon for a while and lowers them. "That's been very efficient. I've had eleven refusals in four days and every one of them's been in the same words, and the words weren't here on Monday."

Pascual stands beside him on the jetty.

"What can you see?" he says.

"Nothing." Spilsbury hands him the binoculars, without looking at him. "Twelve miles. The Admiralty chart puts the nearest of the eastern channels at nineteen from here. I knew that before I came. I've stood here four mornings. There's a line of mangrove on the horizon and a great deal of brown water." He puts his hat on. "Nothing. Four mornings. You'll have noticed that's my form."

---

Pascual looks through the binoculars at the delta for a long time, because he's never seen it, and because he's aware he's never going to be closer.

There's nothing. There's a low dark line and brown water and a pelican.

"You'll not get a boat," he says, and gives them back.

"I'll get a boat."

"No boat at Cedros'll go into that water. Not for money. You've had eleven of them tell you."

"I've had eleven of them tell me," Spilsbury agrees. "And one of them's not told me anything at all, and has had four evenings at the rum shop on my money telling me about his second engine." He turns round at last and looks at Pascual, and his face is perfectly pleasant, and there's nothing in it. "I said on the Aventine I'd not take him if he didn't want to come, and I shan't. I want to see him, and I want him to see me, and then we'll find out whether he wants to come. There's a man on this beach who'll take me close enough for that, and I'm going to pay him to do it, and there's no schedule in the Commission that closes my eyes."

---

Nestor spends Thursday on the jetty and the fish market and the rum shop and three houses at the top of the beach and one at Icacos, and comes back to the rest house at eight in the evening grey in the face.

"Faustino won't be told," he says. "Tito told him. Rafael from the *Santa Rita* told him, and Rafael's the one who saw the drowned man in 'fifty-eight, and Faustino laughed at him. My wife's sister's husband told him and Faustino threw a bottle." Nestor sits down on the edge of the bed. "He owes money in Port of Spain, Doctor. And the Englishman's paying him more than a season. And he says the Commission's a lot of foreigners in Rome who took his water away and he'll go where he likes."

"Will the others stop him?"

"They'll not fight him. Nobody fights Faustino. He's got a knife and four brothers." Nestor looks at the floor. "They'll stand on the jetty. That's what they'll do. They'll all come and stand."

---

Tito comes to the rest house at half past nine on Friday night and knocks, and doesn't come in.

He says it in the doorway in Spanish in a low voice, fast, and Nestor translates the part Pascual didn't catch, which is most of it.

Faustino's taking the Englishman out at half past four in the morning, before the light, so the other boats won't see him go. Across the Mouth and down the coast into the eastern channels behind Pedernales, as far as the *Esperanza* can get on a tide, and back by dark.

And Faustino's put a net in the bottom of the boat under a tarpaulin. Tito's nephew saw him load it at seven.

"A *net*," Pascual says.

"*Sí*." Tito doesn't take his eyes off Pascual's face. He says something else to Nestor.

"He says," Nestor says, "Faustino told his brother the gringo can look for his drowned man and Faustino will look for something to sell at Güiria. Across the water. In Venezuela. Where your Commission doesn't have a man with a badge."

---

Pascual doesn't sleep.

He sits on the chair in number two with the window open on the sound of the surf and thinks, for about four hours, about a great many things, and the largest of them isn't Faustino Mata or the net.

It's a thing he said at a kitchen table on the eighth of July of 1959 and has had every day since. That he's a man who can pick up the heaviest object in any room, and that it works in four seconds, and that he'd reached for it once, on the thirtieth of November, on purpose, to hurt somebody who didn't deserve it — and that he's going to have to decide not to reach for it every single time, for the rest of his life.

He's kept that. Three and a half years. Every argument, every one. He's felt the weight of it in the room like a scalpel on a tray and not once put his hand out.

And at a quarter to four in the morning on the twenty-first of July, in a rest house at Cedros, he's going to go down to that jetty and pick it up.

Deliberately. At the right man. With both hands.

He sits there and makes himself be sure it's that, and not the other thing — not temper, not the wound, not a man at last getting to hit somebody he's disliked for eight years. He makes himself go through it twice, the way Reggie would, in order.

It's that. He gets up at four and puts his shoes on.

---

There are twenty-three men on the jetty at twenty past four.

Pascual counts them, because he can't help it. Nobody's got a lamp. They're standing along both sides of the planks in the dark in their shirts, from the market end to the far end where the *Esperanza* is tied up with her engine ticking, and not one of them is saying anything, and not one of them moves when Pascual walks down between them with Nestor behind him.

Faustino Mata is in the stern of the *Esperanza* with his hand on the tiller. He's a big man with a moustache, and he looks up at twenty-three men standing along a jetty in the dark and spits over the side.

Spilsbury is sitting on the midships thwart in his linen jacket with the binoculars round his neck and his hat on his knee.

And forward of the thwart, under a tarpaulin, there's a shape Pascual has seen in the bottom of a hundred boats, and the corner of the tarpaulin's come back an inch, and there's the edge of a float and a fold of brown mesh.

---

"Dr Spilsbury," Pascual says.

"Pobil." Spilsbury doesn't get up. "I'd hoped you'd sleep."

"There's a net under that tarpaulin."

"There is."

"Did you know?"

And Spilsbury looks at him — at twenty past four in the morning, in a boat, with twenty-three men on the jetty — and answers him in precisely the voice he used at twenty past five in the morning on the twenty-third of May 1954, dripping, with a towel in his hand, when Pascual asked him whether he'd waited until dark and gone into the pen anyway.

"Yes," Spilsbury says.

---

And it gets out.

It comes up out of Pascual the way it has since he was nineteen, like a boiler, and he doesn't do one thing about it for about fifteen seconds.

"*You knew.* You sat in that boat and you watched him load a *net*, into a boat going into a water you voted to close with your own mouth in Rome eight weeks ago, a water where there are animals nobody in the world has ever counted, with a man in it who's spent eight years cutting nets out of that water with a *knife* so that nobody would do to them what was done to the one he went in with —"

"I'm not responsible for Mata's net," says Spilsbury. "I'm responsible for my eyes."

"— you are responsible for *every animal that comes out of that water in that net*, and you know it, and you've known it since you were twenty-two, and you sit there and tell me about your *eyes* —"

And Pascual stops.

He gets hold of it. It takes him a moment and twenty-three men watch it take him a moment. He rubs his hand down over his face, hard. He puts his hands behind his back.

And then he reaches for the heaviest thing on the jetty.

---

"I'm going to tell you exactly what I'll do," he says, "and I want you to listen to all of it, because I'm only going to say it once, and I don't bluff, and you've known that about me since my sick bay.

"If that boat leaves this jetty I'll write it down.

"Vessel *Esperanza*, of Cedros, master Faustino Mata. The date, the hour, the tide. The water she was bound for, which is a closed water under the precautionary rule of Schedule C. A net aboard. And the name of the one passenger, and the fact that he chairs the Scientific Committee of the International Commission, and that he moved Schedule C himself on the twenty-first of May.

"It'll be the first violation filed under the Commission's schedule in its first year. It'll go on the Commission's register, which is public. It'll go in the first annual report. I'll send a copy to the Venezuelan Ministry of Agriculture, and one to Bassani, and one to Chettle, and one to the chairman in Bergen, and one to the editor in London who quoted my paragraphs in April. I'll sign it as Chief Veterinary Inspector and I'll put the badge number under my name.

"And you'll never chair anything again. Not a committee, not a meeting, not a sub-committee on filters. The field you want forty years of will have your name in it for forty years as the man who went into the first closed water in a boat with a net. Nobody will take your papers. Nobody will lend you an animal. You'll be sixty-one in a room in Massachusetts with a shoe box and nothing to put in it.

"That's all. I haven't left anything out. You may set it beside what you know of me."

---

The engine ticks. A wave comes in under the jetty and goes out.

Spilsbury sits on the thwart and looks at him.

It takes him about as long as it took him to consider Reggie's question on the Aventine, and Pascual watches him do it: watches the man take the thing Pascual has just put in front of him and turn it over, and go round the edges of it, and find there's no edge; watches him weigh a man he wants to see against forty years, the way he weighs everything, cleanly, on a card, without pretending it's anything else.

Pascual can see it land. He's always been able to see things land.

Spilsbury stands up.

He puts his hat on, and picks up the binoculars, and steps up out of the *Esperanza* on to the jetty, which takes him a moment, because he's fifty-six; and Faustino Mata shouts something from the stern that Pascual understands perfectly well without any Spanish at all.

And then Faustino Mata looks up at twenty-three men standing along both sides of a jetty in the dark, not saying anything, not moving.

He looks at them for a long time.

Then he reaches down and cuts his engine.

---

Pascual and Spilsbury stand at the end of the jetty while the light comes, and the men go off it one by one without a word, and Nestor goes up the beach with Tito, and the *Esperanza* sits at her ropes with a net under a tarpaulin.

"You'd have done it," Spilsbury says. "Filed it."

"Yes."

"I knew you would by the second sentence. I went on listening because it was very well put." He looks at the delta, which is a line now, grey-green, with the light coming up behind it. "I'd have gone in, Pobil. I want you to have that. And I'd have let Mata put that net over, and I'd have watched him do it, and I'd have been sorry for the animals in an abstract sort of way, and I'd have looked for the man."

"I know."

"And you'd have ruined me."

"I would."

"Then we understand each other," says Spilsbury, "for the first time in eight years."

"No," Pascual says. "We don't. And we're never going to, and you're going to spend the rest of your life on a card about it."

---

Spilsbury laughs. It's a real laugh, surprised out of him, short.

"Probably," he says. And then, still looking at the delta: "What did Tar say to you? In the flat. On the Wednesday night. He came back up the hill on the Thursday with a refusal that I'd stake the box he didn't make on his own."

Pascual doesn't answer.

"I'm not asking for leverage. I've no use for it; I'll be on a plane on Monday and I'll not come back to this jetty. I'm asking because I don't understand it, and I told him so on the Aventine, and I'm fifty-six, and there are two things on this earth I've been unable to account for in the last three years and they're both about the same man." He puts his hands in his pockets. "What did he say?"

"That's his," Pascual says.

Spilsbury nods, slowly, as though that's an answer, and files it, and Pascual watches him file it.

"Good morning, Pobil," he says, and goes up the beach to the rest house in the early light with his binoculars round his neck, and doesn't look back at the water once.

---

He's on the Monday plane from Piarco. Pascual's at the airport and watches him go up the steps, because he said last May he'd be glad he'd been on the jetty, and was, and wants to be sure.

Then he drives back to Cedros, because there's a bus for Nestor on the Tuesday and he's not going to let the man ride it alone.

---

Tito comes to the rest house on the Tuesday morning at six, while Pascual's shaving, and knocks, and this time he comes in.

He's got something in his hands wrapped in oilskin and tied with fishing line. He puts it on the end of the bed very carefully, the way a man puts down a thing he's been told to put down carefully and doesn't understand, and says something to Nestor in Spanish, fast.

"He says it came across," Nestor says. His voice has gone strange. "From the other side. Last night. In a canoe, from the Pedernales side, to the man at Icacos, the one who talks too much. The man in the canoe said it was for the doctor. The one who stood on the jetty. He said the drowned man sent it."

Pascual puts the razor down.

He sits on the bed with lather on half his face and unties the fishing line, which takes him a long time, because his hands aren't doing what he tells them; and unwraps the oilskin, and inside it there's a tin.

A round tin with a screw cap, gone to rust at the rim, with the paint off. He's never seen it in his life.

He unscrews the cap.

---

Inside it there's a rolled paper, gone soft as cloth, brown at the edges, and he unrolls it on his knee with the tips of his fingers.

It's a chart.

A hand-drawn chart of a reef and a cay and the water off it, and he knows the shape of that coast, because he's lived on it for ten years. *Reef opens here.* *Fresh water.* *Keep clear — ordnance.*

The hand is Jean Leslie's.

Pascual sits on the end of a bed in a rest house at Cedros with a chart of his own island on his knee, and he can't see it very well.

He turns it over.

On the back, in pencil, in a small, terrible, cramped hand that he last saw in a feed log at a pen rail in May of 1954 — *Behaviour: no change* — there are two words.

*It worked.*

---

He doesn't know what it means.

He sits there with the paper and turns it over and back, and it doesn't mean anything to him — not the words, not the joke in them, because there's a joke in them, he can hear it, it's got the shape of a joke the way a key's got the shape of a lock. It's an answer to something somebody said. He wasn't there when it was said.

But he knows who it's for.

He rolls it back up exactly as it was, and puts it back in the tin, and screws the cap on, and wraps the tin in the oilskin. And then he unbuttons his shirt and puts the whole thing inside it, against his ribs, on the left side, where he carried two sheets of a clinical file through May and June of 1954, and buttons the shirt over it.

Nestor's sitting on the chair by the window with his hands over his face.

"Tito," Pascual says, in Spanish. "Tell the man at Icacos to tell the man in the canoe, to tell whoever sent it, that the doctor got it. That's all. Nothing written down."

"*Sí, doctor*," says Tito, and goes.

---

Before the bus, Nestor walks down to the end of the Cedros jetty on his own, and Pascual follows him at a distance, and watches him get down on the last step above the water and put his hand in the Serpent's Mouth up to the wrist.

He stays like that for about a minute, with the brown water of the Orinoco going past his wrist on the ebb.

Then he takes it out and dries it on his trousers and comes back up.

"Anything?" Pascual says.

"Fish, Doctor." Nestor shakes his head. "Only fish. It's twelve miles." He looks back at the grey line on the horizon. "I wanted to try."

"So did I," says Pascual, with his hand flat on his shirt, and they go and get the bus.
