# Forced Choice

The house on the north side has been kept for three years by the William Martin Trust for the use of the Chief Veterinary Inspector when in the region, and the Chief Veterinary Inspector comes up the eleven steps from the harbour road on the fifth of April 1965 at half past four in the afternoon, and stops on the ninth.

Reggie is behind him with both bags. He stops too.

"It's nothing," Pascual says.

"I didn't say anything."

"You stopped."

"You stopped. I stopped because you were in front of me." Reggie shifts the bags. "It's a flight of steps, and you're a man of fifty-two who's been in eleven countries since January and on a boat since Thursday, and you stopped on the ninth, and I'm not going to say anything about it."

"You are saying something about it *now*."

"I'm describing my reasons for being on the eighth step," says Reggie, and Pascual says something in Spanish that Signora Bruni would have enjoyed, and goes up the last two, not stopping, with his jaw set, and Reggie comes up behind him and does not look at the man's left knee at all.

---

The garage has *Doris* in it.

She has been under a tarpaulin since December of 1961. And on a nail inside the door, where Reggie used to hang the tide tables, there is a school exercise book with a pencil on a string, and in it, in Nestor's careful hand, on the first Sunday of every month for three years and four months, a line.

*Motor encendido. 11 minutos. Bien.*

*Motor encendido. 11 minutos. Un poco de agua en el filtro. Limpiado.*

*Motor encendido. 11 minutos. Bien.*

Forty lines. Reggie stands in the garage with the book open and reads every one of them, and when Pascual comes to the door to see where he has got to, he closes it and hangs it back on the nail and does not say anything, and Pascual looks at his face and does not ask.

---

The design came to Rome in February, in Jean's hand, eleven pages, with a covering note that said *Reggie, don't you DARE improve this*.

He read it at the Director-General's desk with the wall outside the window, and when he had got to page four he put it down and went and stood at the wall for some time, because it was the most beautiful piece of experimental design he had seen since 1954 and he did not want Mrs van Wijk to see his face.

It is very simple. It is so simple that he sat at the desk afterwards trying to work out why nobody had done it in eleven years, and the answer was that the only people who could have thought of it had spent those eleven years trying very hard not to think about it at all.

The confound in every study of this signal since the Andersen has been the same confound. The man knows. He knows whether the pen holds one animal or two, and his knowing gets into his scores. And the reason nobody has ever been able to ask what, if anything, comes *through* the signal — not whether there is a signal, which the contact series settled, but whether there is anything *in* it — is the same confound turned inside out. An image in a sensitive man's head, arriving while his hand is on an animal, cannot be told apart by anybody on earth from an image he made himself. Cholmondeley thought for months that his were sunstroke.

Jean's design does not try to tell them apart.

It gives the animal something to know that the man does not.

---

Two baskets, upturned, on the concrete at the far end of the pen, behind a canvas screen that hides them from the rail and the step but not from the water. One basket painted red and one left plain. A brass hose fitting that has been Chumley's since 1957 goes under one of them. Which one, and whether the red one is on the left or the right, is drawn by lot the night before and sealed in forty numbered envelopes by somebody who will not be at the pen. The man who places the fitting opens one envelope at a time, places, and leaves the concrete before the subject is told to begin. The subject is in the water at the step, thirty feet away, blindfolded, with his back to the far end and his hand on the animal. He says one word, *roja* or *blanca*, and a scorer who has not seen the envelope writes it down.

Nobody within thirty feet of the subject knows the answer. Nobody who knows the answer is on the concrete. The only creature in that pen who has seen where the fitting went is the one with the subject's hand on its shoulder.

Chance is twenty of forty. Anything a man could do by guessing, or by reading faces, or by hearing a footstep, is twenty of forty.

He had it by page four. By page six he had the arithmetic for every result from twenty to forty, done in pencil in the margin, and by page eleven he was aware that he wanted it so badly that his hands were not quite steady on the paper, and he took it home that evening and stood in the doorway of the flat and said so.

---

"Say what it's for," Pascual said, that night, at the table under the window.

"It's for the question nobody has asked. Whether they know things, and can show us." Reggie had his hands flat. "Hester knows. Charles knows. Nestor knows. You know, because you believe Nestor. Everybody who has ever had a hand in that water knows, and the field has built fourteen years of husbandry on the working assumption that they are animals that do badly alone, because that is the thing that can be published. If it is shown, properly, with a design nobody can get round, then there's a floor under everything the Commission does that isn't a mortality table."

"And who asked for it?"

"Nestor did," said Reggie. "Jean says so on page one. He said to her at the rail in January that the English papers call Chumley a fish that likes company, and that he's not a fish, and was there a way to show it." He took his glasses off. "It's his. He asked. That's what it's for."

Pascual had read all eleven pages twice, and then put his hands on them.

"All right," he said. "My board. My stop. Not above the chest. Ten minutes out in every forty. Nobody separated from anybody. And if that animal so much as goes off his feed I end it and nobody asks me why. I don't care who's chairing what." He looked at Reggie across the table. "And you tell me what else you want out of it. Not now. When it comes. You'll know when."

---

It comes on the seventh of April, at twenty-six minutes past ten in the morning, on trial thirty-one.

---

Hester Bevan is at the rail in a cane chair that Nestor carried down the path, because she is sixty-nine and walks with a stick now, and will not discuss it, and has brought her hat and her gloves and a rug that she has not put on her knees. She has been there since eight.

John is behind the screen at the far end with a box of forty envelopes, which Mrs Aguilar sealed at her own kitchen table the night before by lamplight, on the grounds that she was the only person on the island who could not possibly care which basket was which. John opens one envelope, places the fitting, puts the envelope face down under a stone, and walks off the concrete and round behind the store, and blows a whistle, and does not come back until Jean blows hers.

Jean is at a card table fifteen feet from the step with a ruled sheet and three pencils and her hair tied up in a handkerchief, and she writes down one word forty times and does not look at the far end once.

Pascual is at the board with his hands behind his back.

Spilsbury is at the rail four feet to the left of the board.

He came off the Tuesday boat uninvited, with one case, having been notified as chairman of the Scientific Committee under a rule Reggie wrote himself, and he walked down the path and stopped at the top of the concrete and said, "Clarke. I know. The rail. I shan't set foot past it," before John had opened his mouth, and John went red and said, "Good," and that was all.

And Reggie is at the other end of the rail, with his hands in his pockets, doing arithmetic.

---

Nestor is in the water at the step up to his chest, with a strip of black cloth over his eyes and his right hand on Chumley's shoulder, and has been for thirty trials.

He does it very simply. John's whistle goes. Chumley, who has been lying against Nestor at the step, turns his head and looks down the length of the pen at the far end, where the canvas screen is; he can see what the rail cannot, because he is in the water and the screen stops at the edge. He watches John place the fitting. He watches John leave. Then he turns back and puts his head against Nestor's chest, and Nestor stands with his eyes covered and his hand on the animal, and after a time that is never less than about four seconds and never more than about twenty, he says one word.

*Roja.* *Blanca.* *Blanca.* *Roja.*

Then Jean blows her whistle and Chumley goes off down the pen like a shot and noses under the right basket and comes back with the fitting in his hand, every time, and gives it to Nestor, and Nestor holds it up without taking the blindfold off, and Jean writes one more thing in a second column, and John comes back round the store and opens the next envelope.

Mabel is at the shallow end with the calf. Leslie is nearly three and the size of a pony and has been trying to get the fitting off her father since trial four.

---

Reggie has been keeping the count in his head since trial one. He is not supposed to. Nobody on the rail is supposed to know the score until the envelopes are opened; that is the point of the envelopes. But Nestor holds the fitting up at the end of every trial, and Reggie can see from the rail which basket Chumley went to, and Nestor says his word out loud, and Reggie Tar has never in his life been able to not add up a column that is being written in front of him.

After ten, it is ten.

After twenty, it is nineteen, and he knows exactly why it is not twenty, because he saw it from the rail. On trial seventeen, while John was walking off the concrete, Leslie went down the length of the pen like a thrown stone, put both hands up on the edge behind the screen, tipped the plain basket over, found nothing under it, and tipped it back, in about four seconds, where only the water could see. Chumley watched his daughter do it. And Nestor stood in the water with his hand on the animal and his eyes covered and said *blanca*, and was wrong, and Jean wrote something in her margin, and Pascual wrote something on the board, and Reggie at the rail thought that it was the most perfectly reasonable error ever made by a subject in the history of experimental science.

After thirty, it is twenty-nine, and he stands at the rail with his hands in his pockets and his face perfectly still and works out the probability of twenty-nine or better in thirty by chance — thirty-one ways of doing it, over two to the thirtieth, which is a little over a thousand and seventy-three million — which takes him about a minute and a half without a pencil, and comes to about one in thirty-five million, and feels the thing in his chest that he has not felt at full strength since the ninth of October 1957.

And then trial thirty-one.

---

John's whistle. Chumley looks down the pen. John leaves. Chumley turns back and puts his head against Nestor's chest.

And Nestor does not say anything.

He does not say anything for twenty seconds, which is the longest he has taken. And then thirty. Pascual's hand comes out from behind his back and stays at his side. And then Nestor's face under the black cloth does something Reggie has seen it do once before, in August of 1960, when two animals put their hands on each other's faces in the middle of this pen — it opens, all of it, at once — and he makes a sound that is not a word, and then he laughs.

"*Una garza*," Nestor says. Not to Jean. To nobody. "*Doctor. Una garza.*"

Jean's pencil stops.

"That's not *roja* or *blanca*, Nestor," Jean says, very gently.

"*Roja*," Nestor says, still laughing, with the tears coming out from under the black cloth. "*Roja*. It's under the red. I'm sorry, *señorita*. It's *roja*. But he showed me —" He puts his free hand over his mouth. "He showed me a heron. Standing at the edge of the water. On one leg. Not going in." He takes the hand away. "That's me. That's what I am. To him. That's his name for me."

---

Nobody moves.

Down at the far end, Jean blows her whistle, a little late, and Chumley goes off down the pen and noses under the red basket and comes back with the fitting and puts it into Nestor's hand, and Nestor holds it up, blindfolded, laughing, with his face wet.

And from the cane chair at the end of the rail nearest the path, Hester Bevan says, quite quietly, to nobody in particular:

"Yes. They give you your name first."

Reggie is the only person near enough to hear it. He turns his head. Hester is sitting perfectly upright in her chair with her gloves in her lap and her face doing nothing whatever, and her eyes are shut.

---

And that is when it comes.

It comes the way it always comes — through the chest, like a window going up in a hot room — and Reggie stands at the rail with his hands in his pockets and watches it arrive and knows, the way he learned to know on the Viale Aventino, exactly what it is and exactly what it is for.

He does not want the baskets. The baskets are done; the baskets will be thirty-nine or forty of forty and it will be a figure with ten noughts after the decimal point and it will go in a paper that Jean and Nestor and John will write, and it will be unanswerable, and he is glad, and that is not it.

He wants to ask Chumley about the old female.

He wants a second design — he has it already, God help him, he had it by the time Nestor stopped laughing — in which the animal is shown something and the man in the water is asked not *which basket* but *what*. And he wants to put that to an animal who lay at the shallow end of this pen on the morning of the eleventh of February 1960 with his arm over a body, and ask him what it was. What it looked like from inside. What the silence was. Whether there are pictures of it. He wants the loud end, not in intake columns and not off a man's face, but *in the animal's own images*, and he wants it so much that he has to take his hands out of his pockets and put them on the rail.

He does not look at Pascual. He does not need to. He can feel the man at the board thirty feet away the way Nestor can feel the rope.

---

"There," says Spilsbury, beside him.

Reggie had not heard him move. He has come along the rail — the whole four feet from the board and the other thirty besides — and is standing at Reggie's shoulder with his hat in his hand, looking at the pen, and not at Reggie at all.

"You got there by about trial twelve," Spilsbury says, pleasantly, under the noise of the water. "I watched you. You'd been adding it up since the first whistle, which you're not supposed to do, and you got there at twelve, and you forgave the calf at seventeen before Dr Leslie had finished writing, and you've been running the next forty trials in your head since about twenty. What have you got at thirty? One in thirty-odd million?"

"Thirty-five."

"About that. And then the heron." Spilsbury turns his hat round. "And now you want to ask him about the old female."

Reggie does not say anything.

"You needn't look like that. I've known where your head goes for nineteen years; I used to arrange for things to be in the places it went." Spilsbury smiles at the water. "You want a design where Aguilar's asked *what* instead of *which*. You had it by the time he stopped laughing. And the first *what* you want to put to that animal is what it was like on the morning she died."

"You want it too," Reggie says.

"Of course I want it." Spilsbury doesn't even pause. "I want it more than you do, Tar. I've wanted it since a crate in a hut. I'd give the whole box for one picture of that morning off that animal." He turns his hat round again. "The difference is that I've done the arithmetic on who'd put his head in that water and ask him for me, and it comes to nobody. Aguilar declined me on a card in 1960. Clarke won't let me past the rail. Dr Leslie would sooner drown me in the header tank. A want with no route to it isn't a want, it's weather." He looks down at the step. "You've got a route. You've got a design by Tuesday and three people on this concrete who'd do nearly anything you asked. That's what makes yours dangerous and mine merely sad."

---

"You're enjoying this," Reggie says.

"*Enormously*." Spilsbury puts his hat on. "I've had nineteen years, Tar, of watching you be unable to leave a thing alone, and for eleven of them I had to do something about it — point it, and feed it, and keep it off the things I didn't want you near — and it was a great deal of work. Now I get to stand at a rail and watch you want something with your whole body and know that I needn't lift a finger." He nods down the rail, towards the board. "Because in about seven hours you're going to go up eleven steps and stand in a doorway and tell Pobil. And he's going to ask you what it's for. And you're going to say *for me*. And he's going to say no."

"You don't know that."

"I know it the way I knew Aguilar would take the wrist." Spilsbury looks at him for the first time, and his face is perfectly kind, and there is not a single thing in it that is not amused. "And you're going to be so relieved you'll be unbearable at breakfast. I'd give a good deal to see it. It's very restful, you know, being the man who doesn't have to be the brake."

"You were never the brake."

"No," Spilsbury agrees. "I was the other thing. That was even more work."

---

Trial thirty-two. John's whistle.

"*Blanca*," says Nestor, after about six seconds, and wipes his face with his free hand.

"And I'll referee it," Spilsbury says, going back along the rail to his four feet. "Dr Leslie's paper. Tell her. I'll be the most unbearable referee she's ever had. I'll send her eleven pages of objections and every one of them will be the one an enemy would make, and when she's answered them there won't be a man alive who can get a knife into it." He stops by the board. "Dr Leslie's got there before me twice now, Pobil. The pulse and this. I'd like it noted that I mind."

"Noted," says Pascual, without turning his head.

---

It is thirty-nine of forty.

John opens the envelopes at the card table at noon, one at a time, in front of everybody, and Jean reads each word off her sheet and John reads the envelope and Hester, from the chair, keeps the tally on the back of the Whitsun roster because she asked to, in the hand she has written everything in since 1946.

Thirty-nine.

Reggie does the figure properly that evening, on paper, with the table, because he will not put a number in a paper that he has only done in his head at a rail. The probability of thirty-nine or better in forty by chance is forty-one in two to the fortieth, which is about one in twenty-seven thousand million. He had it at twenty past ten to within a factor he is not prepared to discuss.

---

The control run is on the eighth, and the control subject is the Chief Veterinary Inspector of the International Commission for Captive Sirenians, because the design requires a man in the water who is known to feel nothing whatever, and there is exactly one such man on the island who has had his hand in that pen more often than anybody alive.

It is not dignified.

Pascual gets into the water at the step at nine, in a pair of Nestor's old trousers and a shirt, with the black cloth over his eyes, and swears in Spanish at the temperature, which is eighty-one degrees, and Chumley comes and lies against him with evident pleasure, because Chumley has known this man since he was the size of a dog.

On trial three, before Pascual has said anything, Chumley leaves him, goes down the pen, noses under the red basket, comes back, and puts the hose fitting helpfully into Pascual's hand.

"*¿Qué?*" says Pascual, blindfolded, holding it. "What's this? What's he given me? Is this the — *it's the thing*. He's brought me *the thing*. Jean. Jean, he's brought me the answer, is that allowed —"

"No," says Jean, who has put her face down on the card table, and whose voice is very nearly steady, and whose shoulders are going. "Void. That's void. John, next envelope."

"*He brought it to me* —"

"Because you *can't hear him*," says Nestor, from the rail, where he is standing with the towel round his neck and has been having the best morning of his life. "He's helping you, Doctor. He thinks you're stupid."

---

It takes fifty-one trials to get forty, because Chumley voids eleven of them by bringing Pascual the fitting, with increasing impatience, and on the last two occasions pressing it firmly against the man's chest, and on the forty-seventh putting a hand flat on top of Pascual's head and holding it there, as though trying to get something in by force.

Nineteen of forty. Chance.

Pascual comes out of the water at half past eleven with the fitting still in his hand, because Chumley would not take it back, and he comes up the ladder, and on the second rung from the top his left knee does something, and he stops.

It is about a second and a half. His face does not do anything. That is how Reggie knows.

---

Reggie counts it.

He does not mean to. He counts everything. He has been counting it since the ninth step on Monday, and he counts it on the ladder, and at lunch when Pascual gets up from the bench, and on the path when Pascual goes up it at four to do Leslie's weights. By six o'clock in the evening it is eleven.

At seven, in the kitchen, Pascual says, "I'm doing the calf tomorrow. Bloods and a proper look at that tail. She won't let Nestor near her tail, she thinks it's hilarious."

"In the water?"

"In the water. She's three. She's not going to come up on a sling for a tail."

Reggie puts the kettle down.

"No," he says.

---

Pascual turns round from the stove very slowly.

"I beg your pardon?"

"You are not going into that pen tomorrow with a calf the size of a pony who thinks your tail examination is hilarious," says Reggie, "on that knee."

"There is nothing wrong with my —"

"Eleven."

"*Eleven* what?"

"Eleven times since Monday. The ninth step. The ladder. The bench at lunch, twice. The path at four. Getting out of the launch. Getting into the launch. The chair at Hester's. The step down into this kitchen, just now, when you came in, which is a step you have been coming down since 1955, and you put your hand on the door frame." Reggie folds his arms. "I haven't said anything for four days. I'm saying something now. You're not getting into the water with that calf."

Pascual stands at the stove with the spoon in his hand and his mouth open.

"You *counted*," he says.

"I count everything. You've known that since a hut. It was always going to be used against you eventually."

---

"You are *braking me*," Pascual says. His voice has gone up. "You. *You*. You are standing in my kitchen, a man who stood at that rail yesterday wanting to ask an animal what it was like when his mate died, telling *me* what I may do in a *pen*, with a *calf*, of which I have examined, in eleven countries, over *twenty-nine years* —"

"I told you about the rail. I told you at nine o'clock last night, in that doorway, and you said *what's it for* and I said *for me* and you said *no*." Reggie is aware that his voice has also gone up. "And you were right, and I was so relieved I could not sleep, and Spilsbury said I'd be unbearable at breakfast, and I was, and you said so. That's the arrangement. It is a two-way arrangement. I have been reading the terms."

"It is not a *two-way* —"

"Everything," says Reggie, "is a two-way arrangement if you read the terms closely enough, and I'm the Director-General of the International Commission, and I read terms for a *living*."

---

"*Ay, Dios mío*," says Pascual, to the ceiling, with both hands and the spoon. "It's contagious. It's *catching*. Eleven years I have been doing this for him and now he has caught it off me and he has turned it round like a — like a — Nestor was *right*, you all think I'm *stupid* —"

"Nobody thinks you're stupid. Chumley thinks you're deaf."

"— and he is going to stand in my kitchen and *itemise my knee* —"

"Nestor holds her," Reggie says. "In the water. She likes Nestor. You sit on the step with your feet in and do the bloods from there and look at the tail when he brings it to you, and if she wants to be hilarious she can be hilarious at him."

Pascual stops.

"From the *step*."

"From the step. Like Hester. Like Nestor for six months. There's a long and very distinguished history of people doing extraordinary things from that step." Reggie unfolds his arms. "Please."

---

There is a silence in the kitchen on the north side of the harbour of a quality Reggie has not heard in it since about 1958.

Pascual puts the spoon down on the stove. He looks at Reggie for a long time. His face is doing everything, as it always does, and Reggie watches it go through outrage and indignation and a very brief, very clear moment of fear — of fifty-two, and a knee, and a pen, and a calf, and twenty-nine years, and all the years after — and then something that is not any of those, and that comes up through the rest of it like a man coming up for air.

"You said please," Pascual says.

"I did."

"You've never once said please to me in an argument. In eleven years. I've kept count too."

"Then it's the first time," says Reggie, "and I'd like it recorded."

Pascual laughs. It comes out of him helplessly, the way it did on the floor of the flat under the window after Marseille, and he puts his hand over his eyes, and it goes on for a while.

"From the step," he says, through it. "*Ay.* All right. From the step. Nestor holds her. And if she bites him you're telling his wife."

"Done."

"And you're never counting my knee again."

"I'm going to count your knee every day for the rest of your life," Reggie says, "and I'm going to tell you the figure on Sundays."

---

They do not have supper. Supper is on the stove and stays there.

Pascual comes round the table, not fast — Reggie watches the knee and does not say so and Pascual sees him watching and says *don't* — and puts both hands on the sides of Reggie's face, and Reggie puts his own up to answer, and they stand in the kitchen like that for a while, the way they have stood in kitchens in two countries, and then Pascual kisses him, and it is not a kiss for a kitchen.

Reggie kisses him back.

He has had eleven years of this man's mouth and he has not, it turns out, got used to it; he does not expect to. It is warm and it tastes of the coffee from four o'clock and it is laughing still, a little, at the corners, and he puts his hand flat on the back of Pascual's neck under the collar where the skin is hot from the day and holds on.

"Bed," Pascual says, against his mouth. "The stairs in this house were built by a sadist."

"There are no stairs between here and the bedroom."

"There is a step. I have a knee. You counted it."

---

The bedroom is the same room. It has the same shutters and the same fan and the same repulsive armchair in the corner that Hester Bevan remarked on in 1958 and that neither of them will allow the other to throw away, and the light through the shutters is going red.

They are slower than they were. Reggie notices that and is glad of it. There was a year, a long time ago, when this was a thing done as though somebody might come through the door, and a year after that when it was done as though it might be taken away; and it is neither of those now. It is two men of forty-six and fifty-two with the shutters closed in a house on the north side of a harbour, taking each other's shirts off with a good deal of attention, and no hurry at all.

Pascual's shoulders are brown to a line and white below it. There is a scar on the left one from a horse in 1938 that Reggie has put his mouth on a great many times, and puts his mouth on again, and Pascual makes the sound he always makes and says *Reggie* in the voice he does not use for anything else.

He is broader than he was. So is Reggie; Rome has done that to both of them, and neither of them minds. Reggie gets his hands on the man's back and pulls him down on to the bed, carefully, on the side away from the knee, and Pascual laughs into his neck and says *you are going to be impossible about that knee for the rest of my life*, and Reggie says *yes*, and then neither of them says anything for some time.

---

It is very good.

It is very good the way things are when two people have learned each other over eleven years and still find there is more: slow, and then not slow; Pascual's hands on his hips, holding, the way they hold an animal, with the whole of his attention; the heat of the man's chest against his back; the weight of him. At one point Pascual stops, and Reggie opens his eyes and finds the man propped over him on one elbow in the red light, just looking at him, with his face doing the thing it does, which is everything, where anybody could see it — and Reggie, who has spent a good part of his life making certain that nobody could see anything on his, lies there and lets himself be looked at, all of it, for as long as the man wants, and does not look away.

"*Te quiero*," Pascual says.

"I know," Reggie says. And then, because it is 1965 and they are neither of them young and there are only so many years in anybody, he says it back, in the man's own language, badly, with the accent wrong, and Pascual makes a sound that is not a word and puts his face in Reggie's shoulder, and after that it is not slow at all.

---

Afterwards they lie with the fan going round and the shutters gone from red to dark, and Pascual's head on his chest, and Reggie's hand flat on Pascual's ribs on the left side, which is where it goes.

"It's the tail," Pascual says, into his chest, some while later. "I want to see the tail. There's a thing with the flukes on that calf that I don't like and I want my own hands on it."

"Nestor will bring you the tail."

"Nestor will bring me the tail." A long breath. "*Ay.* From the step."

"From the step."

"A *heron*," Pascual says, a little after that, in a different voice. "He's been standing at the edge of that water for twelve years and the animal knew. The whole time. Had a name for him."

"Hester said they give you your name first."

Pascual goes quiet under his hand.

"I didn't hear that," he says.

"You were at the board. I was near her." Reggie looks at the fan. "She said it with her eyes shut. I don't think she knew she'd said it out loud."

Pascual does not answer for a long time. And then he turns his head and puts his mouth against Reggie's chest, not a kiss, just resting there, and says, "Then she's heard hers. Once. From somebody," and Reggie says, "Yes," and neither of them says anything more about it.

---

At half past five in the morning Reggie takes *Doris* out.

Not over the reef. That was conceded at eleven o'clock the previous night, in the dark, in exchange for the knee. Through the harbour mouth, and a mile along the north shore, and back.

Nestor's forty lines have kept the engine sweet. She starts on the second pull. Pascual sits in the bow in a jersey with a thermos, facing aft, looking at Reggie and not at where they are going, which he maintains is the only safe way to be in a boat with this man.

And Reggie takes her out through the harbour mouth in the grey light with the tiller under his hand and the swell coming in long and slow off the reef, and for the first time in about three years there is nothing in his head at all.

No returns. No design. No second design. No old female, no *what*, no twenty-seven thousand million. There is the tiller, and the swell, and the set of the water off the point, and a man in the bow with a thermos who has a knee, and the light coming up.

"You're smiling," Pascual says, from the bow.

"I'm handling a boat."

"You're handling a boat and smiling. I'm writing it down."

"You don't write things down."

"I'm going to start," says Pascual, and pours him coffee, and passes it aft, and Reggie takes it with one hand and keeps the other on the tiller, and brings her round off the point, and home.
