# Obtainable

He opens her at six on the twelfth, in the sick bay, with the door propped and John on a stool by the bench with the book on his knee, because a post-mortem on that animal was always going to be John's as much as his, and neither of them said so.

It takes four hours. He goes slowly because there's no reason not to, and because he's never in his life had one of these on the table that he wasn't looking for a reason in.

There isn't one.

He goes through her the way he'd go through anybody. The lungs are clean. He looks twice, and then a third time, with the lamp pulled down, because for eight years every animal of her kind that anyone opened was full of the thing Jean wrote down as pneumonia, and this one isn't. The heart is big and tired and the valves have gone thick at the edges the way an old dog's go. The joints of the tail are worn. The teeth on the left side are down nearly to the gum. There's nothing wrong with her anywhere that isn't simply the number of years she's had.

"John," he says, at a quarter past ten, with his hands in her. "Write this down exactly."

John writes it down exactly.

*No lesion sufficient to account for death. Cardiac enlargement and valvular thickening consistent with age. Dentition, articular surfaces, likewise. Cause of death: senescence.*

He's never written that word about an animal of this kind in his life. Nobody has. No mermaid in any pen anywhere has been let live long enough to die of being old, and it's taken a boy sitting on a rail with his feet in the water, and a calf off a Navy lighter, and a Colonel's wife with a teapot, and six years, and he stands at the bench with his gloves on and reads the word back to himself upside down in John's round hand.

Then he goes to the basin and washes, and while he's washing his face goes, and he lets it.

John doesn't say anything. John dates the sheet and numbers it and draws the heart, very carefully, every part of it, the boring parts too. After a while Pascual hears the door and doesn't turn round, and knows by the way the man stands in it that it's Reggie, with the intake book, and that Reggie is going to stay exactly where he is and not come in until the tap's off.

He does.

---

Chumley takes four pounds of fish on the day she died. Seven on the Friday. Eleven on the Saturday.

Pascual's got six years of that animal's intake in the book and he's never once had a day under eighteen. He sits with the column in the office on the Saturday night and doesn't need Reggie to tell him what it's doing, and Reggie tells him anyway, because Reggie can't not, and it takes four sentences.

"It is falling less steeply than Beta's did in 1953. Not because he is better. Because there's a man on the step."

"I know."

"And it will hold, or it won't, and the only variable anybody can move is whether the man stays there."

"I know, Reggie."

On the Sunday night Pascual takes Nestor home.

He does it himself, in the launch, at eight, because it's the rule he made on the concrete on Thursday and he's not going to be the first man to break it. Nestor's been on that step three nights with an hour off in every one and he's grey to the lips, and he sits in the bow of the launch with Inés's dry towel on his knees and doesn't say a word the whole way across, and at the jetty he says *gracias, doctor* and goes up the hill.

Monday, Chumley takes three pounds.

Nestor's back on the half past five. By the Wednesday it's thirteen, and on Thursday fourteen, and on Friday fourteen again, and it sits there, a third down on anything that animal's ever eaten, and doesn't go lower.

Pascual takes the book up to the office on the Friday and opens it at a page he hasn't looked at in six years and doesn't need to, and Reggie comes and stands beside him, and the two of them look at the two columns side by side.

*Tar, Leslie and Pobil, 1954*, page nineteen. The arrested decline of Specimen Beta on the introduction of a single human attendant.

It's the same curve. Nearly to the week.

"*Ay,*" Pascual says.

"Yes."

"Then he's holding him up."

"He is holding him up," Reggie says, "and we're going to have to ask him whether he will go on doing it, and that is the thing Hester said on the path that nobody has done yet."

---

The letter from the trustees comes on the Tuesday, and on the Friday, by the same route, the broker's quotation.

It's beautifully typed. Somebody in Port of Spain has a good machine and a man who knows how to use it. *Adult female, sound, delivery at the Institute's jetty by the end of April subject to the season. Losses in transit at the supplier's charge.*

Pascual reads it at the office window and reads the last line four times.

*At the supplier's charge.*

It means the Martin won't pay for a dead one. It means the supplier will take as many out of the water as it needs to take in order that one arrives breathing, because the dead ones cost him and not the purchaser, and a man who pays for his own losses doesn't send one boat for one animal. He sends enough.

About one in three.

He puts the letter down on the desk very carefully, because the alternative's to put his fist through the window, and he's broken a window before, in 1941, and it didn't help.

---

He'd been to the bungalow on the Wednesday. He goes back over it on the Friday afternoon with the broker's letter under his hand, because he's been going over it for two days and he still can't find the place where he could have said it better.

The Colonel was asleep on the verandah under a rug in eighty degrees. Hester took Pascual through to the sitting room and gave him tea he didn't want and sat with her hands folded and let him say the whole of it.

"No," she said.

"Mrs Bevan —"

"I told Dr Tar the same in November, and I told him why, and I have not changed my reasons and I shall not change them for you, though I am very glad you came." Her voice didn't move. "The article is not for Gamma and it is not for the trade. It is for that animal. You have his intake column. How long will he last with a man on the step?"

"I don't know. Months. A year." Pascual had his hands on his knees. "Beta was five months into a decline before the boy ever got near him. He's got a man from the first hour. It might hold a long time."

"And without a man on the step?"

He didn't answer.

"Say it, Dr Pobil. I have said worse in this room."

"Beta," Pascual said.

"Yes. Then a second animal is the only thing on this earth that will keep him from being Beta, and I will not suspend the one sentence I have ever written that obtains one."

---

And it was there, on Wednesday, that he lost it.

He's never lost it with Hester Bevan in six years. He's lost it with Reggie more times than either of them has counted, and with a man from Whitley, and with the Navy, and once, gloriously, with a customs officer in Bridgetown about a crate of formalin. Never with her. He'd have said he couldn't.

"You are asking a man in Port of Spain," he said, and he heard it go, and did nothing whatever about it, "to put three animals in a tank on a deck in the sun so that one of them can be dropped in with a widower in April. You have *seen* them die in a box. You've read the tables. You've had my figure since 1955. And you sit there with a cup in your hand and tell me it's for *him* —"

He stopped.

He got hold of it. It took him a moment and she watched it take him a moment, and he rubbed his hand down over his face, hard, and said, "*Perdón, señora.* That was not — I beg your pardon."

"You need not." Hester Bevan hadn't moved at all. "You have not said one thing I did not say to myself in 1946 with the pen in my hand. I wrote it anyway. I would write it again this afternoon." She set the cup down. "You wrote the tenth page, Dr Pobil. You know where the other door is."

"It's in Florida and it's seven years off."

"Yes," said Hester. "It is. I am sorry for that." And then, in exactly the same voice: "Has anybody told Mr Aguilar that he has thirty days?"

"No."

"Then you are deciding it for him by not telling him." She stood, which meant he stood. "That is the one way of deciding it that I will not have in this Institute. I built it so that he should not be a technician to whom things happen. Go and ask him."

---

He tells Reggie on the Friday night at the kitchen table, with the broker's letter between them.

"I'm going up the hill on Sunday."

"I'll come."

"You won't." Pascual turns the letter face down, because he's tired of the typing. "He'll say what he thinks you would like to hear if you're in that kitchen. Not because he is afraid of you. Because you're the Director, and he has been polite to Directors for seven years, and that is the whole of his English. In Spanish, at his own table, he'll say what he thinks."

Reggie considers that for about four seconds and doesn't argue, which is new, and which Pascual notices.

"Then there is one thing I'll say now," Reggie says, "so that it is said before you go, and not after."

"Go on."

"If he says yes, that man will be at the rail every morning for eighteen months, and I will be standing next to him. And I will want exactly what he wants. Every morning." He's looking at the letter, not at Pascual. "I'll tell you each night. I am not asking you to like it. I am telling you it is going to happen so that you don't find out by watching my face."

"I'd find out by watching your face in about a minute and a half."

"I know you would. That is why I'm telling you."

Pascual gets up and goes round the table, and puts his hand on the side of the man's face, and Reggie shuts his eyes and puts his own up to answer it, and they stay like that, in the kitchen, for longer than either of them would've admitted to in 1955.

"It's a dreadful arrangement," Pascual says.

"It's the arrangement," says Reggie.

---

The Aguilars' house is at the very top of the village where the road gives out, and it's blue, and there's a mango tree over the gate that's been there longer than the house.

Pascual's been inside it once before. March of 'fifty-eight, when the youngest had the fever and Nestor sent word he couldn't come, and Pascual walked up after the practice with his bag because the Martin's medical officer is nobody's doctor in the village and a man's child is a man's child. He remembers the kitchen. He remembers a woman who didn't say one word the whole time he was in the house and then walked him to the gate and said *gracias* like a person signing something.

He goes up at four on the Sunday, after the heat, and the eldest girl lets him in.

She's eighteen now. The boy is sent out. The middle girl is sent out and comes back and is sent out again. The youngest — Luisito, eight — is under the table and nobody sends him anywhere, because he has made it clear with his whole body that he isn't going.

Nestor sits at the head of the table with his hands flat on it. Inés brings coffee.

And Pascual — who has known this man seven years, and fed animals beside him every day of them, and sat on the concrete with him on Thursday with his hand on his back — watches Inés Aguilar set the cup down in front of her husband, and then, before she puts her hand on his shoulder, wipe that hand quickly on her apron.

She does it the way a person does a thing they've done ten thousand times. She doesn't look at it. Nestor doesn't look at it.

Pascual looks at it, and puts his own hands round his cup, and understands something about the house he's sitting in that nobody has ever told him, and has the sense not to look at either of them while he understands it.

---

He does it in Spanish, and he's decided on the launch how he'll do it, which is that he'll say it once, all of it, exactly, and then stop talking.

"There are two doors. I am going to describe both of them to you as exactly as I can, and then I am going to stop, and whatever you say I will take down the hill and I will not argue with it.

"The first door. We do nothing. On the eleventh of March a letter goes from Bridgetown to a man in Port of Spain, and his boats go out through the Serpent's Mouth, and they bring back a female. To bring one here alive they will take about three out of the water. If one of them lives, she is put in the pen with him in April or May. He may take her. He may not. Until she comes, he is alone, and you are on the step, and nobody asks you for anything you have not already decided to give.

"The second door. The Englishman sends a cable to London, and for eighteen months nothing is taken out of the water at Port of Spain. Not for us. Not for Florida. Not for anybody. Perhaps forty animals stay in the sea who would have come out. And nothing comes to him. For eighteen months he has you and nobody else, and you are the only thing standing between him and what happened to Beta.

"And the Englishman will be at the rail. Every day, for as long as he likes. He will not ask you to do anything. What he wants to see is whether you will put your hand on that animal's face.

"That is all of it. I have not left anything out."

---

Nobody says anything.

Under the table Luisito is holding his father's ankle.

"Eighteen months," Nestor says eventually. "That's the longest?"

"That's what the suspension runs. A female could come from another pen, if one lost her mate somewhere. There isn't one. The Englishman has looked." Pascual turns his cup round. "He is very thorough."

"And if we don't cable. The three in the boat." Nestor's hands haven't moved on the table. "That's for the old one's paper. The Señora's."

"For Mrs Bevan's article. Yes."

Nestor looks at the table for a while. Then he looks at his wife, who's standing at the stove with her back to all of them.

Inés turns round.

"Doctor," she says. It's the first word Pascual has ever heard her say to him that wasn't *gracias*. "This Englishman. What does he want my husband to do with his hand?"

So he tells her.

He tells her the truth, plainly, in about four sentences: what the hand on the face is; that it's in the stories and the stories are right; that a man who answers it isn't alone again for the rest of his life, not for an hour, and that when the animal dies he's alone in a way that doesn't mend. He doesn't say whose life he's describing. He doesn't need to have seen it himself to describe it; he has sat in a room on the north side of the harbour and heard it for twenty-six minutes.

Inés listens to the whole of it with her arms folded.

Then she looks at her husband and says, "*¿Y lo vas a hacer?*"

And Nestor Aguilar says, "No."

"Then tell the Englishman," says Inés, "that he can stand wherever he likes," and turns back to the stove.

---

Nestor walks him to the gate himself, under the mango tree, and says: "Tell him to come."

"You're sure."

"I'm not sure of anything, Doctor. I've never been sure of anything in my life." He puts his hands in his pockets. "But the young one came out of a boat himself, and I don't want three more going in the water for him now. I'll sit on the step. I'm good at it."

He goes back in.

And Pascual's got as far as the road when he hears the door again, and it's Inés, coming down the path in her apron with her hands wet from the sink, and she stops at the gate, and dries them.

"Doctor."

"Señora."

"Twenty-one years I've been doing this." She holds up the hands, dry, and lets them drop. "You know why?"

"I think so," Pascual says. "Since about twenty minutes ago."

She nods, as though that's a satisfactory answer and not a surprising one.

"Will that animal take him from me?" she says.

It's the question nobody's asked. Not Reggie, not Hester, not Spilsbury in his letter with every item in it. Pascual stands in the road with the light going and finds he hasn't got an answer, and won't give her one he hasn't got.

"I don't know, señora," he says. "There's one person who'd know."

"Then ask her," says Inés Aguilar, and goes back up the path, and shuts the door.

---

The cable goes on the Monday at nine, from the office, in Reggie's hand, read over by Pascual.

*COME. TRANSFERS EXEMPT. POBIL STOP STANDS. TAR.*

The reply's on the Wednesday.

*GAZETTE NOTICE FIRST MARCH. ARRIVING EIGHTH. THERE ARE NONE. S.*

Reggie reads it twice and puts it on the desk and doesn't say anything, and Pascual reads it and says, "*There are none,*" out loud, and they both look at the last three words for a while.

"He checked again," Reggie says. "After our cable. He went through the schedule a second time before he answered, to be sure, and then he put it in a telegram at eightpence a word so that we'd know he had."

"*Ay, Dios.*"

"That is him conceding the exemption. That's what his concessions look like."

---

The notice is gazetted in Port of Spain on the first of March and a copy is in Hester's hands by the second, and Pascual's in the office when she writes to the trustees, because he's come for the weights and she doesn't send him out.

She does it standing up, at the Director's desk, on the Trust's paper, in a hand that hasn't altered in fourteen years.

*Pursuant to article one of the deed, and to the notice in the Trinidad Royal Gazette of the first instant, no specimen is lawfully obtainable, and the Trustees are accordingly released from the obligation under article four for as long as the notice remains in force.*

She signs it. She blots it. She folds it into the envelope with exactly the same care she'd have taken with the other letter, the one to Port of Spain, if it had been that one.

"It was always the second half of the sentence, Dr Pobil," she says, without looking up. "I put it there. I merely did not expect the key to be in that hand."

"Mrs Bevan." He stands with the weight book against his chest. "The Aguilars' wife asked me a question on Sunday, and I told her I didn't know the answer, and I told her there was one person who would."

Hester Bevan's pen stops.

"What was the question?"

"Whether the animal would take him from her."

She seals the envelope. She puts it on the tray. She puts both hands flat on the desk, and looks at them, and after a moment she takes them away again.

"Thank you," she says. "I shall go and see Mrs Aguilar myself. Not this week."

---

Chumley's hand comes up for the first time on the fifth.

Pascual is at the rail and sees it. It's twenty past six. Nestor's on the step with his trousers rolled and his feet in to the shin, and the animal comes up the length of the pen and puts his head against Nestor's knee, the way he's done every morning for three weeks — and then lifts it, and hangs in the water a foot off, face to face with the man, and the right hand comes up out of the water, streaming, and stops about four inches from the side of Nestor's head.

And stays there.

And goes back down.

Nestor doesn't move at all for about a minute after it's gone. Then he puts his hand on the animal's shoulder, the way he has for three weeks, and says his name, and Chumley puts his head back against his knee.

Pascual writes it in the margin of the day book in his own hand, with the time, and doesn't write it anywhere else.

---

Spilsbury comes ashore on the eighth at ten past ten with one case and no camera, and Pascual's on the jetty, which he's decided on, as he decided on it last May, and which he'll be glad of.

"Pobil."

"Dr Spilsbury."

"I gather it was Aguilar who decided." Spilsbury doesn't put the case down. He's fifty-four, and browner than he was, and entirely the same. "Good. I'd rather it was him than either of you. How's the intake?"

"A third down and holding."

"From the first week?"

"From the second."

"Page nineteen, then," Spilsbury says. "To the week. I thought it would be." He looks up the path. "I've brought nothing but a change of shirts and the notice, and Mrs Bevan has the notice already, and I'd like to go straight down, if you've no objection, and if you have one I'd like to hear it."

"I haven't got one."

"Then walk down with me," Spilsbury says, "because I'd rather you were there than not, and I don't suppose you'd let me go alone in any case."

---

They go down together without saying anything else.

Reggie is at the far end of the rail, where he's been since six. He doesn't come over. He looks at Spilsbury for about a second and a half across forty feet of concrete and Spilsbury looks back and nods once, and that's all.

Pascual has put the board up.

He did it on Sunday night, by lamplight, with a hammer: the same board from 1957, the list of signs in his own capitals, and underneath it the rule. *ANY PERSON PRESENT MAY END IT. NO REASON GIVEN. NO REASON ASKED.* There's no session. There's nothing to end. There's a man on a step. He put it up anyway, at the rail, where the Englishman would stand, and he's not going to explain it to anybody.

Spilsbury stops in front of it and reads it.

"You've put the board up," he says.

"Any person present."

"Quite right," says Spilsbury, and puts his case down on the concrete beside it, and turns round to the water.

---

Nestor's on the step with his feet in and his hand on Chumley's shoulder and the animal's head against his knee.

He looks up.

"Good morning, Mr Aguilar," Spilsbury says. "Please don't get up."

"*Buenos días.*"

That's all either of them says.

Spilsbury puts his hands on the rail and watches, and Pascual stands beside him with the board at his elbow and watches Spilsbury, because that's the arrangement he's made with himself, and he's going to keep it for eighteen months if he has to.

For about twenty minutes nothing happens. The animal lies against the man's knee. The generator changes note. Down at the far end Reggie takes his glasses off and puts them back on.

And then Chumley lifts his head.

---

He does it exactly as he did on the fifth. He hangs in the water a foot off, face to face, and the right hand comes up out of the water streaming, and this time it doesn't stop four inches short.

It goes to the side of Nestor Aguilar's face and lies there, flat, the whole palm and the webbing, against his cheek and his jaw and his ear.

Nestor goes completely still.

Pascual can't feel anything; he's never been able to feel anything off those animals and never will, and he's never wanted to be able to as badly as he does in the next four seconds. He can see Nestor's jaw go. He can see the man's throat move. He can see whatever it is go through him, and his eyes close.

Nestor's hand comes off Chumley's shoulder.

Pascual's heart stops.

It comes up out of the water, slowly, the way a hand comes up when a man isn't entirely telling it what to do — and it doesn't go to the animal's face. It goes to his wrist. It closes round the wrist, gently, the fingers not quite meeting, and it holds there for about the time it takes to count to four, and then it brings the animal's hand down, away from his face, into the water, and doesn't let go of it.

And Chumley lets him.

The two of them stay like that, the man on the step with his eyes shut and the animal's wrist in his hand under the water, and neither moves.

---

Pascual turns his head and looks at Spilsbury.

Spilsbury hasn't moved either. His hands are on the rail. His face is perfectly calm, and there's nothing in it that Pascual can name — not triumph, not disappointment, and not, which is the thing, *surprise*. It's the face of a man watching a result come in that he'd put a figure on in advance, and finding the figure right, and writing it down somewhere behind his eyes without a card.

"Once," Spilsbury says, very quietly.

"What?"

"He'll do that once a day for a month. And then more." He doesn't take his eyes off the step. "Where would you like me to stand, Pobil?"

And Pascual, who's been deciding on the answer to that since a kitchen at the top of the village with a boy under the table holding on to his father's ankle, says:

"Wherever you like."

Spilsbury nods, and stays exactly where he is.
