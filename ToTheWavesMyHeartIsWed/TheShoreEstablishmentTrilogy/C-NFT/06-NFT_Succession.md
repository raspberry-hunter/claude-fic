# Succession

The first freshwater pulse ever put into a pen holding an animal of this species goes in at eleven minutes past ten on the ninth of January 1961, and it is four hundred gallons of rain off the store roof, and it has cost the Institute two hundred and eleven pounds, and Reggie has never spent money with more pleasure in his life.

Jean designed it on the *Lady Grace* and sent the drawings back from Port of Spain in December, in a tube, with a covering note that said *build this exactly and do not let Pascual improve it*. It is a header tank on a steel stand above the north wall of the pen, fed from the gutters, with a four-inch pipe and a sluice. Pascual has improved it twice. He has also, in the course of the six weeks it took to build, conducted a campaign against it of such sustained and inventive hostility that the men from the village have begun coming over on their day off to listen.

"It will *crack*," Pascual says, at the rail, at ten past ten, at a volume that carries to the jetty. "That concrete was poured by the United States Navy in 1942 in a hurry, and you're going to put four hundred gallons of cold fresh water on top of it in a single —"

"It's not cold. It's been sitting in a tank in the sun since Thursday."

"— in a single *slug*, onto an animal who's spent her entire life in sea water, on the word of a woman who's presently four hundred miles away and *can't be shouted at* —"

"That's her whole design principle," says Reggie. "Open the sluice, John."

John opens the sluice.

---

The water comes down the pipe with a noise like a drain and goes into the pen at the north end in a brown rope, and spreads across the surface in a skin, the way fresh water does on salt, and for about four seconds nothing whatever happens.

Then Mabel comes up off the bottom of the pen like something fired.

She goes straight up the length of the pen to the north wall and into the fresh water where it is coming down, and turns over in it, and turns over again, on her back, with her arms out, under the pipe, and lets it come down on her face. And Chumley comes after her at a speed that puts water over the rail and gets under the pipe with her, and the two of them go round and round in four hundred gallons of rainwater from the store roof, turning over, and over, and over, and it is the most undignified thing Reggie has ever seen an animal of this species do, and there are eleven people at that rail and every one of them is laughing.

Except Pascual.

Pascual has his reading glasses on and his watch out and is timing it.

"Four minutes," he says, when the pipe runs dry. "Four minutes in the fresh water, and she's taken two fish since, and she took none this morning." He takes the glasses off. "Right. It doesn't crack. I withdraw it. I withdraw all of it and I'll write to that woman and say so and she'll frame it."

"She will," says Reggie.

"*Ay, Dios.*" Pascual puts his face in his hands for a moment and takes it out. "Twice a week. Not more. And I want the salinity at the north wall every hour for a day after, and I want Nestor's hand in the water before and after, and if she goes off her feed for one single day I'm taking that pipe down with my own hands and I'll use it on you."

---

Nestor puts his hand in the water before and after.

He does it at the step, the way he did all of last spring, for about a minute each time, and then he dries it on the towel and goes to the balance. It is the only time now that he sits on the step. He comes on the half past six launch and he goes home on the four o'clock and he sleeps in his own house, and Reggie has not once in five months heard him refer to any of the spring.

"Well?" Pascual says, after the pulse.

Nestor considers it.

"*Contentos*," he says. And then, in English, because it is Reggie standing there and not only Pascual: "They're pleased, Doctor. The rope's tight." He dries his hand. "She likes the rain."

---

The Colonel does not come down for the pulse. He was not able to come down for Christmas, or for the turn of the year, and since about the middle of January he has not been able to come down the two steps from his own verandah without Nestor on one side and Juan's brother on the other.

On the seventh of March, which is a Tuesday, Hester is in Bridgetown for the Trust meeting and will not be back until Thursday, and she sends Reggie a note on the Monday night that says only *Would you read him the drains file tomorrow at four. He will pretend he does not want you to.*

He pretends he does not want Reggie to.

"Tar. What are you doing on my verandah."

"Mrs Bevan asked me to read you the drains file, sir."

"Mrs Bevan is in Bridgetown."

"Yes, sir. She wrote."

"Hm." The Colonel is in the long chair with the rug over his knees that Hester will take off the moment she comes back, and his hands on the rug have got very thin since Christmas. "Culvert first. Then the north drain. Don't read me the appendix. The appendix is a man in Bridgetown covering himself."

Reggie reads him the culvert, and then the north drain, and does not read him the appendix, and it takes twenty minutes, and the Colonel listens to all of it with his eyes shut and says *hm* at two places where the man in Bridgetown has got something wrong, and both of them are real.

---

Afterwards the Colonel does not open his eyes for a while, and Reggie sits with the file on his knee and does not get up, because he has not been told he may.

"Rome," the Colonel says.

"In January, sir. The Commission's constituted in November."

"You'll take it."

"I've taken it. I told Mrs Bevan in the autumn."

"I know you did. She tells me things. Thinks I'm asleep." The Colonel's eyes are still shut. "Pobil going?"

Reggie sits very still on the verandah of the William Martin Institute for Marine Studies with the drains file on his knee.

"Yes, sir," he says. "He is."

"Good." The Colonel shifts under the rug. "Man needs somebody to shout at him. You more than most. I've watched you seven years. Worst-tempered young fellow I ever had on a station and the best, and never once knew what room you were in." He opens his eyes and looks, not at Reggie, but out at the harbour. "Had Hester. Thirty-eight years. She shouted at me by letter, mostly. Very effective."

"Yes, sir."

"Don't *yes sir* me, Tar, I'm dying." He says it without any emphasis whatever. "Pobil's been living in your house for five years and rowing with you about a boat on the jetty where the whole harbour can hear. I was in the Army thirty-one years. I know what two men sound like when they row about a boat." He shuts his eyes again. "Take him to Rome. See he has a decent room with a window. That's all."

Reggie cannot say anything at all.

"Don't let it become a ministry," says the Colonel, and goes to sleep, or pretends to.

---

Colonel Bevan dies at twenty to five in the morning on the fourteenth of March 1961, in his own bed, and does not make a sound.

Reggie does not hear about it until six. Pascual is on the island, because Pascual has been sleeping in the block three nights a week since February for exactly this reason and has not said so, and he goes up the path at a quarter to five when Juan's brother's boy comes for him, and at six he comes to the office, where Reggie has been since half past five because he did not sleep, and stands in the doorway.

He does not have to say anything. His face does it.

"When?"

"Twenty to five. In his sleep. She says it was very quiet." Pascual comes in and shuts the door. "She's dressed. She's in the sitting room with the tea things out and her hat on the chair. She asked me to tell you, and the rest of the station at seven, and she asked for you at nine."

"Is she —"

"She's doing the list," Pascual says. "The Colonial Secretary, the Navy, the Trust, the churchyard, the man in Bridgetown about the appendix. She's got it on a sheet of foolscap in a column." He sits down on the corner of the desk, which is not a thing he does before six in the morning. "*Ay*, Reggie. She's all right. That's the worst of it. I've seen a great many widows at twenty to seven in the morning and she's the only one I've ever seen who's all right in exactly the way she says she is."

---

At nine Hester Bevan receives him in the sitting room with the tea poured, in black, with her hands folded, and it takes Reggie about four seconds to see that Pascual was right and about another four to see why.

"Sit down, Dr Tar. I shall not keep you long. The Navy is sending a Commander from Chaguaramas for Thursday and there will be a great deal to do."

He sits.

"I wanted to tell you two things," she says, "because you have had the rest of it from me in your own house, and it would be ungracious to leave you with the beginning of a story and not the end.

"The first is that this is a different animal. I told Dr Pobil in 1958 that grief is one thing and the other thing is another, and that grief closes. I have had grief. I had it for two brothers and for a daughter. I know its shape. This is that, and it will close, and I am grateful for it, and I am a little ashamed to be grateful." She lifts her cup, and puts it down again without drinking. "He was a very good man, and I have been fond of him every day of thirty-eight years, and he is gone this morning in the ordinary way, and I shall miss him in the ordinary way for the rest of my life. That is all. I wanted you to know that it was only that."

"Yes," Reggie says.

"The second thing is what he said."

---

"He woke at about four," Hester says. "He did not often wake in the night by then. He woke and he did not call me, and I woke because he had stopped breathing in the way he breathes when he is asleep, which I have been listening to since 1923. And he said my name.

"And then he said, *Hester, I hope he was worth it.*"

The fans go round.

"I have been married to that man for thirty-eight years," Hester Bevan says, "and he has never once asked me. Not in 1923 and not in 1946 when he signed a constitution with a clause in it he did not understand, and not in May of 1954 when Mr Cholmondeley was in this room, and not in April of 1958 when I went down to the pens for the first time in a hat at twenty past six in the morning and came back with my sleeve wet. I have known since about 1930 that he knew there was a room he could not go into. I did not know until four o'clock this morning that he knew there was a *he*."

"What did you say?"

"I said yes." She folds her hands. "And he said, *Good. Thought he must have been.* And then he went back to sleep, and at twenty to five he stopped."

---

The funeral is on the sixteenth, at the churchyard on the point, in the heat, with the whole station and half the village and a Commander from Chaguaramas in white who stands at attention in the sun for forty minutes and is visibly unwell by the end of it.

Hester does not cry. Jean is in Port of Spain waiting on a permit and cannot get a boat in time, and sends a cable that says *I AM SO SORRY. HE HAD ME TO DINNER THE FIRST WEEK AND ASKED ME WHAT I WANTED TO WORK ON AND NOBODY ELSE DID FOR FIVE YEARS. J.* — and Reggie reads that on the morning of the funeral and has to go and stand behind the store.

There is one other cable. It comes on the fifteenth, addressed to Mrs Bevan, from Massachusetts, and she hands it to Reggie after the service without a word, and he reads it in the shade of the lych-gate.

*BEVAN WAS THE BEST CHAIRMAN I EVER SAT UNDER AND HAD ME OFF HIS ISLAND IN ONE SENTENCE. I AM SORRY. SPILSBURY.*

It is exactly right. That is the thing about it. It is the only message she has had in two days that has not got a single untrue word in it, and it says the one thing about the Colonel that nobody else was in a position to say, and it costs its sender nothing, and he knew all of that when he wrote it.

Hester takes it back and puts it in her bag.

"I shall keep that," she says.

---

The Council of the Institute meets on the twentieth of April, which is a Thursday, with Hester in the chair, because the Trust deed provides that the Secretary shall take the chair in the Chairman's absence, and she drafted it, and nobody in Bridgetown has the smallest intention of arguing.

Reggie's resignation as Director is minuted in one line, to take effect on the thirty-first of December.

The appointment of his successor is minuted in one line.

He tells John himself, afterwards, in the office, with the door shut, which is the only place he has ever told John anything that mattered and which he is aware John is aware of.

"Me," says John.

"You."

"I'm thirty-one. I *draw*."

"You numbered everything," Reggie says. "Every sheet on this station for eight years, from one, in one sequence, no gaps. The ministry adopted it. Four countries use it. Spilsbury built a study on it and Hester Bevan found out he'd been reading it. There isn't one other person on this island who has understood from the first week that what this place is for is making certain that nothing can go missing without everybody being able to see it has." He puts his hands behind his back. "That's the job. The rest of it's the drains file, and Hester will read it to you."

John goes red from the collar up, which he has not done in front of Reggie in about five years.

"Does Hester know?"

"Hester proposed it. At the Council. In one sentence. Nobody spoke."

John looks at the floor for a while.

"Right," he says. "Two conditions."

"Go on."

"I'm not moving into this office. I'm keeping the lecture hut, with the leak." He lifts his chin. "And that man doesn't go into my pens. Not once. Not for any reason. He can stand at the rail till he's ninety."

"Write it in the day book," Reggie says, "in your own hand, and I'll countersign it."

---

They decide Rome at the kitchen table on the twenty-ninth of May, with the feed book and a bottle, because that is what was promised at that table in November of 1957, and neither of them has forgotten it.

It is not a long conversation. Most of it was had in the autumn. But it was promised, and they have become a pair of people who keep the procedure when it has been promised, even when the decision has been taken, because the procedure is the thing that is promised and not the decision.

"Say what it's for," Pascual says, with his glasses on and his hands flat on the book. "Not what it'll show. What it's for."

"It licenses holdings. Every holding in eleven countries, and any country that wants to buy an animal from any of those eleven. It keeps the returns, and it publishes them. It can send a man into any pen in eleven countries without notice and shut it on the spot. And it can say where an animal may not be taken out of the water, and how many, and by whom." Reggie turns his glass round. "That last one's the one I want. That's the one the Colonial Office did for eighteen months by gazette on a man's cable. The Commission can do it for thirty years by schedule."

"And the man who goes into the pens without notice."

"Is you. If you want it. Chief Veterinary Inspector. It's a real post, it's got a salary and a badge, and I wrote it into the terms, and I've not told the Commission who it's for, and if you say no it goes to Bassani and he'll be very good."

Pascual takes his glasses off.

"A man who can walk into any pen in eleven countries," he says, "and see an animal on its own, and *shut it*."

"Yes."

"With a *badge*."

"It's quite a small badge."

"*Ay, Dios mío.*" Pascual puts both hands over his face, and when he takes them away he is laughing, and his eyes have gone. "I've a great deal of Latin and no Italian at all, and I'm going to be bad-tempered for about a year, and I'm going to shout at a man in Naples in Spanish, and I want it. Yes. I want it. Write it down."

Reggie writes it down.

---

"The house," Pascual says, later, with the bottle down by a third.

"Hester is keeping the lease. On the Trust. For the Chief Veterinary Inspector's use when he is in the region, which he'll be twice a year."

"She did that."

"She did that in April. She did it before I'd asked her, in the same Council meeting, in the line after John." Reggie refills both glasses. "And the boat stays in the garage."

"The boat —"

"Nestor will turn the engine over on the first Sunday of the month."

"You could *sell* the boat. You could sell the boat and put it towards a flat in Rome with a window, which is what the Colonel told you to get me, don't think I didn't hear about that —"

"It's unlucky to sell a boat," says Reggie.

There is a pause on the kitchen side of the table of some considerable quality.

"It is *not* —"

"It's a well-attested principle," Reggie says, and Pascual throws the cork at him, and misses, and they are still arguing about it at eleven o'clock, and it is one of the three or four best evenings of the year.

---

Pascual finds it on the fifteenth of August, at seven in the morning, with his hands on Mabel's flank in four feet of water and Nestor on the step.

He does not say anything for a moment. He stays where he is with both hands flat on her, the way he stood in the water with both hands on the old female's chest in February of last year, and Reggie at the rail watches his face do the thing it does, which is everything, and cannot tell for about three seconds which everything it is.

Then Pascual looks up at Nestor.

And Nestor has taken his hand out of the water and is looking at it, as though there is something on it.

"*Dos*," Nestor says. "*Doctor. Hay dos.*"

"I know," Pascual says. "I know there are. I've got my hands on it."

Then he comes up the ladder soaking wet and walks straight past the rail and past Reggie and past John, who has already got the book open, and goes up the path without a word to find Hester. And Reggie stands at the rail and does not, for the moment, have any arithmetic available to him whatever, and finds he does not want any.

It is the first pregnancy recorded in any animal of this species in any holding in the world.

John writes the date. John writes the number. John draws the whole animal, the boring end as well, and dates it in ink, and his hand is not steady, and he does it anyway.

---

Hester comes down at twenty past six the following Sunday.

Reggie is at the far end of the pen with the balance and his back turned, because Pascual is in Bridgetown about the header tank's second stand and has left instructions, and the instructions are the instructions that were given in April of 1958, and Reggie keeps them.

He does not see her cross the concrete. He hears it: the rail, a pause, eight feet, the edge. He hears a woman of sixty-five sit down, which takes her some while.

He does not hear her put her hand in the water. He would not expect to.

He does not turn round for forty minutes. When he does, she is at the rail again in her hat, with her cuffs straightened, and her sleeve wet to the elbow, and she says, "Dr Tar. The Whitsun roster for next year will want doing before you go," and he says, "You'll have it by Friday," and she goes up the path.

It is the first time since the tenth of February 1960.

---

The letter comes on the nineteenth of October, a Thursday, in the second sack, in Jean's hand, postmarked Port of Spain, and it is addressed to Reggie at the house on the north side and not to the Director at the Institute, which is the first thing about it.

He reads it at the kitchen table at six in the evening. Pascual is at the stove.

It is eleven pages. The first nine are the survey.

She has been in the delta since February on the permit, with a Trinidadian boat and a Venezuelan boatman called Hilario and a boy, and she has done four hundred miles of channel, and it is the finest piece of field description Reggie has read in ten years. The salinity regime. The flood pulse, which is *enormous*, which is the size of a country, exactly as she was told it would be on a road in July. Three places where she has counted animals from the boat, one of them eleven at once. A page on the Venezuelan government's plans for flood control in the western channels, which she has heard about from an engineer in a bar in Tucupita and does not like one bit. And a photograph, numbered and dated, of a stretch of mangrove at dawn with two heads in the water in the middle distance, and on the back in her hand: *Plate 238. Something here.*

Reggie reads the nine pages and puts them down in a stack.

Then he reads the tenth.

---

> *Now the other thing.*
>
> *There is a stretch of the eastern mouths behind Pedernales that Hilario would not take me into. Nor would anybody. I tried four boats out of Cedros and one out of Pedernales itself, and I offered money, and I was told very politely in two languages that the boats don't work that water and haven't for five or six years, and when I asked why, a man at Cedros said a thing I am going to write down exactly.*
>
> *He said: "Because of the drowned man."*
>
> *I did not go in. I want you to know that. I am not going to. I sat on the jetty at Cedros for about an hour and then I went and did the western channels.*
>
> *And here is the reason I'm writing this to the house and not the office.*
>
> *In November your Dr Spilsbury sent me eleven years of the Port of Spain broker's takes by channel for the survey design, which was a real kindness and I have used every line of it. And in the tables the eastern mouths — Pedernales and everything behind it — are aggregated into a single row under "other", with a footnote saying the figures have been combined for reasons of small numbers.*
>
> *Reggie. That man has never in his life aggregated anything for reasons of small numbers.*
>
> *He knows. I don't know how long he's known. He knew by November. He did me a favour and he took the one row out of it that I'd have looked at, and he did it neatly, and he did it so that I would not see it, and I nearly didn't.*
>
> *He'd be thirty-one this year.*
>
> *J.*

---

Reggie reads the tenth page twice and the eleventh, which is only a line about the header tank, once.

Then he gets up and gives the letter to Pascual, all eleven pages, and takes the pan off the stove himself, and stands at the window with his hands on the sill while Pascual reads.

It takes Pascual about six minutes.

At the end he puts the letter down on the table very carefully, the way he put down a broker's quotation in February of last year, and does not say anything at all for a long time; and then he says, "*Ay, Dios mío*," and nothing else, and sits with both hands flat on the pages.

"Say it," Pascual says eventually. "All of it. In order. I can see you have got it in order."

---

Reggie turns round.

"He is alive," he says. "Or he was in 1958, or 1959, or whenever those men stopped working that water. He went into the sea on the twentieth of May 1954 with a chart and a knapsack and an animal who was going home, and he got there. He is in the eastern mouths behind Pedernales, with Beta's people, and he is doing to the broker's boats exactly what any man would do who could feel what comes up off an animal in a net. I do not need to be told that. Nobody on this station would need to be told it.

"Spilsbury has known since at least November of last year. He got it out of the consignment records, which he bought for forty pounds because I sat in that office at one in the morning on the eleventh of December 1956 and said *then get them*. He has had it for eleven months. He has not written to me. He has not written to anybody. He has done a favour for Jean and taken the one row out of it, which means he has decided that nobody else is to have it, which means he intends to have it himself.

"He is on the scientific committee of the Commission I am going to run. The Commission's first returns are due in March. Every holding, every animal, and the place it came out of the water. That is my condition. I wrote it on the back of a spoiled sheet in this house in June of 1955 because I wanted to know where the animals were coming from, and in March it will put the eastern mouths behind Pedernales on a published table with a nought against them, in a column where there used to be two a year, and it will do that on my signature.

"We have not looked for him in seven years because an enquiry is a record and the not-looking was his protection. It is not his protection any more. Somebody is looking. The only question left is who gets there first, and what they are for when they arrive.

"That is all of it."

---

Pascual sits with that.

"Then that's what Rome's for," he says.

"Yes."

"Not all of it. But that." He takes his hands off the letter. "You said at this table in May it can say where an animal can't be taken, and by whom. Then in March you'll put the eastern mouths behind Pedernales on the schedule as closed. No take. No licence. No survey. To anybody. For thirty years."

"And that draws a circle on the chart," Reggie says, "round the exact place he is, in the first act of the Commission, over my name. Spilsbury'll read it in about four seconds."

"Spilsbury's *read* it. He read it in November. The circle's drawn." Pascual's jaw has set. "The only people who don't know where it is are the ones who'd want to go in with a net, and the Commission's the only thing on this earth that can stop them. You close it. And he reads it. And he knows that you know. And then we find out what he wants."

Reggie looks at him for a long moment.

"We already know what he wants," he says.

"*Sí*," Pascual says. "We do."

---

He writes back to Jean at the end of the kitchen table at eleven that night, while Pascual reads the survey pages a second time with the lamp pulled over.

It is one page. It does not ask her a single question.

> Jean —
>
> Thank you. For the nine pages, which are the best field description I have read since you did the ponds, and for not going in, and for the tenth.
>
> Nor will I.
>
> Plate 238 is on the dresser. Pascual has looked at it about eleven times and says to tell you that he withdraws everything he has ever said about header tanks, and that Mabel is carrying, and that you are to come back to this island for it if you have to swim.
>
> R.
