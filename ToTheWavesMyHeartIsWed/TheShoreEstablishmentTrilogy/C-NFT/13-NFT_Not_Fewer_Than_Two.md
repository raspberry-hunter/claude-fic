# Not Fewer Than Two

## June 1969

John names her on the fourteenth of June, which is her third birthday, because she has lived, and he said he wouldn't name another one after anybody until he was sure.

He writes it in the day book at the rail at seven in the morning, in ink, under the date, in his own hand, with Nestor beside him weighing fish, and then he stands and looks at it for a while.

*Epsilon's second calf, female, b. 14.vi.1966. Named HESTER.*

"*Bien*," Nestor says, looking over his shoulder, and nothing else, and goes back to the balance.

Down in the pen the calf who is now called Hester is trying to take the brass hose fitting off her father, who is eleven feet long and has had that fitting since 1957, and who lets her.

John writes the plate number at the top of the next sheet, which is 38,114, and draws it.

## November 1977

The telegram comes to the Viale Aventino at twenty past nine on Tuesday the fifteenth, and Mrs van Wijk brings it in herself and puts it on the desk in front of him and does not go away.

*DR SPILSBURY DIED EARLY MONDAY AT HIS DESK. HEART. HE LEFT WORD THAT YOU ARE TO HAVE THE BOX. I WILL BRING IT MYSELF. ALDRIDGE.*

Reggie reads it twice.

Then he folds it and puts it in the drawer with the Whitley return and the slip of paper with two animals on it, and picks up the third-quarter returns for Bergen, and reads them, all of them, carefully, to the end, with a pencil, and Mrs van Wijk stands in the doorway for about four minutes and then goes and telephones the Chief Veterinary Inspector in Naples.

---

Pascual is home by seven. He should not be home until Thursday.

He comes up the three flights with his bag and his knee and stands in the doorway of the front room, and Reggie is at the table under the window with the Bergen returns, and looks up.

"You came back from Naples," Reggie says.

"Mrs van Wijk telephoned."

"There is nothing to come back from Naples for. He was seventy-two. He died at his desk at five o'clock in the morning with a pencil in his hand, which is the only way on earth he would have consented to do it. I am perfectly all right."

"*Sí*," says Pascual, and puts the bag down, and takes his coat off, and sits down at the table across from him, and does not say anything else at all; and Reggie goes on reading the Bergen returns for another hour with the man sitting there, and does not once ask him to go.

---

Aldridge comes on the twenty-first.

She is sixty now, or nearly. She has been his assistant since 1951 and he called her Aldridge for twenty-six years and she called him Dr Spilsbury, and she comes up the three flights with a leather case and two shoe boxes tied with string, and will not take her coat off, and will not sit down, and puts the boxes on the table under the window.

"He said you'd know what to do with them," she says. "He said it in 1970. He put it in writing in 1974 and again in March." She looks at the boxes and not at Reggie. "He didn't say what you'd do. I asked him once. He said, *Tar will do something I shan't approve of, and that's the point.*"

She takes an envelope out of her handbag.

"This was in his wallet. With his driving licence and a photograph of his mother. He'd had it in there as long as I've known him to carry a wallet." She puts it down on top of the boxes. "I didn't read it. I'd like that understood."

---

He opens the boxes that night, after Aldridge has gone back to her hotel and Pascual has gone to bed, or has gone into the other room and lain down and left the door open, which is not the same thing.

There are one thousand one hundred and six cards. Reggie counts them, because he counts everything.

He does not read them all that night. He reads them over nine nights, in order, from the front of the first box, where there is a card dated 1934 about a man at Cambridge who falsified a set of sodium figures, to the back of the second, where there is a card dated the eleventh of November 1977 about a calf at Naples. They are filed by letter. There are ninety-one under B and nineteen under M, *things decided against*, and there is a card near the front of the second box, under the letter P, that says only *POBIL — asks what it is for —* with the dash he uses when a thing is not solved.

---

There is a cable in the box, clipped to a card.

*BEVAN WAS THE BEST CHAIRMAN I EVER SAT UNDER AND HAD ME OFF HIS ISLAND IN ONE SENTENCE. I AM SORRY. SPILSBURY.* Returned. And the card, in the fast clear hand: *BEVAN — returned by H.B. per her instructions, 1968. Kept on her side table seven years. "The only honest thing I received that week." — Good.*

There are nine cards under H.B. that Reggie reads once each and puts face down on the table.

There are three under A. *AGUILAR — dep. var.?* and *AGUILAR — told cost (by H.B.) — declined. Why does a cost decide it?* and, dated April 1965, *AGUILAR — a heron. — Would not have been given one myself.*

And under R, there are forty-one.

---

He reads the forty-one on the fifth night.

*R.T. — 27 — cannot leave a result alone. Useful.* That is 1946. Then two dozen more, over eleven years, about things Reggie did in the labs that he had half forgotten and that are written down here exactly, dispassionately, with dates. A card from November 1958: *R.T. — took the 4 sentences alone. Out from under. Good.* And under it, stapled to it, a card from May 1962 in a different ink: *Wrong. See above.* And then a card from December 1958 that stops in the middle, the way no other card in either box stops: *R.T. — block, 4 nights —*. Blank after the dash. Filed that way. And underneath, in the ink of November 1962, in a hand that has gone slightly slower than the rest, the sentence finished: *— after my 4 sentences. He paid for them. So did P. I did that.*

*R.T. looks at P. first.* August 1960.

*R.T. — refused C.C. offer. Not alone. — Does not want to be alone. — Why not?* May 1962.

*R.T. — wants to ask the animal about the old female. — So do I. — He has a route. — Pobil will stop him. — Enjoyed this very much.* April 1965.

And the last one under R, which is dated March 1977, eight months ago, and says: *R.T. — 15 years at the Commission. Best appointment I ever made. Did not make it for that reason.*

---

He opens Aldridge's envelope on the ninth night.

There is one card in it. It is soft at the corners and gone a little brown, and it has been in a man's wallet for fifteen years, next to his mother, and Reggie knows before he turns it over where it was written, because he stood beside the man while he wrote it, under a lamp at a cemetery gate on the Via Marmorata, and did not try to read it.

He turns it over.

*R.T. — goes home —*

The dash. Unsolved.

---

He does not feel it that night. He puts the card on the table with the others and turns the lamp off and goes to bed, and Pascual is awake, and puts a hand on his chest in the dark, and Reggie says, "It was the cemetery," and Pascual says, "*Ay*," and that is all.

He feels it on the Tuesday.

He is at the Director-General's desk with the wall outside the window at half past eight in the morning, and the third-quarter returns for the Caribbean holdings have come in, and he is reading them, the way he has read every return that has come to this building for fifteen years. And on the Martin's sheet, in John's column for observations not reducible to figures, there is a line about Hester, the calf, who is eleven now, going off her feed for two days after a storm and then coming back on; and Reggie reads it, and puts a small pencil mark beside it, and thinks, *he'll have seen that*, and reaches —

He reaches. That is the only word for it. Without deciding to, the way a man reaches for a rail on a stair in the dark, some part of him goes out towards Massachusetts: to the one other person on the earth who would have read that line the week it came in, and caught the same thing, and written about it within eleven days, in a fast clear hand, with something in the second paragraph that Reggie would have had to go home and say out loud.

And there is nothing there.

---

It is not the white. Reggie has never had the white. He has only ever had it described, by a boy in a notebook and by a woman in a sitting room and by a man on a step, and he would not insult any of them by saying he had it now.

It is a different thing. It is smaller and it is his.

It is that for thirty-one years there has been a line running out from the middle of his life to a place he could not see, with a man at the other end of it who knew where his head went — who had always known, since a corridor and a plate in 1946; who put things in the places it went, and then stopped, and then stood at a rail and watched him want something and laughed. It was not company. God knows it was not company. It was not a bond and he would put his hand on a stove before he called it one. But it was *there*. It pulled. There was never once in thirty-one years a morning when he read a return and did not know that somebody else was reading it too and would have an opinion he would mind.

And he sits at the desk on the Viale Aventino with his pencil against the paper and reaches out along that line, and there is no pull on it at all. It just goes out, and out, into the place where the man was, and does not come back off anything.

*Call*, he thinks. *No answer.*

---

And then he understands what he wanted, on the seventh of April 1965, at a rail.

He wanted to put a man in the water with an animal who had lain at the shallow end of a pen with his arm over a body, and ask that animal to show him this. The inside of it. In pictures. For a second design. So that Reggie Tar could stand at a rail with his hands in his pockets and find out what it was like, and do the arithmetic.

He sits there.

He sits there for a long time, with the Martin's returns under his hand and a pencil mark beside a line about a calf, and he thinks about a young male going round and round the bottom of a concrete pen in February of 1960, close to the wall, as though on one of these times round there would be a door. And an animal who had never done anything to anybody. And a man at a rail who had wanted, badly, with his whole body, and not one part of him ashamed of it until now, to have that opened up and shown to him.

And the only thing that stopped him was a man at a kitchen table asking what it was for.

Mrs van Wijk comes in at ten with the coffee and finds the Director-General of the International Commission for Captive Sirenians with his forearms flat on the desk and his head down on them, and goes out again, and shuts the door, and telephones nobody, because there is nobody left to telephone who is not already on his way.

---

He goes home at noon. He has never gone home at noon in fifteen years.

He comes up the three flights and Pascual is at the table with the inspection reports, and turns round, and looks at him, and puts the pen down.

"Sit down," Pascual says.

"I'd rather —"

And he stops, because he would not rather stand; and he pulls out the chair across the table and sits down in it, which he has not done at that sentence in twenty-three years, and Pascual watches him do it and his face goes, all of it, at once.

"Go on," Pascual says. "Take your time. I'm not going anywhere."

---

"I have been reading the returns," Reggie says. "And I read a line about Hester, the calf, and I thought *he will have seen that*, and I reached out for him. I did not decide to. I reached out the way you reach for a rail. And there is nobody there.

"It is not what Hester had. It is not what Chumley had. I am not going to call it that. It is smaller, and it is mine, and it was never company, and I would not have wanted it to be. But it was there for thirty-one years, at the other end of something, pulling, and I have reached out along it four times this morning and it goes out and does not come back.

"And in April of 1965 I stood at a rail and wanted to put Nestor in the water and ask that animal to show me what this was. From the inside. For a second design. I told you I wanted it. You asked me what it was for. I said *for me*.

"It was monstrous, Pascual. I did not know it was monstrous. I knew it was a want I ought not to have, and I told you about it because that is the arrangement, and I let you stop me because I trust you, and I was grateful. But I did not know. I did not know what I was asking an animal to open, because I had never had anything that could be opened, and this morning I have, and it is a hundredth of what he had, and I would kill the man who asked me to put it in a picture for him.

"That is all of it. I do not know what to do with it. I do not think there is anything to do."

---

Pascual gets up. It takes him a moment, because of the knee, and Reggie watches him and does not count it.

He comes round the table, and he does not put his hand on Reggie's face. He puts both arms round him, from behind, over the chair, with his chin on the top of Reggie's head, the way he held him once on the floor of this flat under the window in 1962 with a tin on the sill, and holds on.

"No," Pascual says, into his hair. "There isn't anything to do. You have it. That's all. You've got it now, and you know what it is, and you'll never again stand at a rail and not know." His arms tighten. "*Ay*, Reggie. I'm so sorry. I'm so sorry he's gone. I disliked that man for twenty-three years and I'm sorry, and I'm sorrier for you, and I'm not going anywhere."

Reggie puts his hand up and holds on to the man's wrist.

"He wrote *goes home*," he says. "On the card. With the dash. Unsolved. He carried it in his wallet for fifteen years next to his mother."

"I know. I saw it on the table."

"He never had one," says Reggie. "Did he. He never once in his life had anywhere to go home *to*."

"No," Pascual says. "I don't think he did."

---

That evening Reggie takes one card out of the box.

It is the one that was never filed under anything, because there was never a letter for it; it was lying loose at the top of the second box, on its own, where Aldridge said it had stood propped against his desk lamp for seventeen years. *C.C. — Pedernales — alive 1958.* No question mark.

He holds it for a while.

He cannot destroy it. He has never destroyed a record in his life and he is not going to learn how at fifty-nine, and he knows that about himself, and so does the man at the stove.

So he gets up and goes to the stove and holds the card out, without a word.

Pascual looks at the card, and at Reggie's face. Then he takes it, and turns the gas up, and holds the corner of it in the flame, and they both stand there in the kitchen in Testaccio and watch it go, all of it, down to Pascual's fingers, and Pascual drops the last of it in the sink and runs the tap.

"Thank you," Reggie says.

"*For what?*" says Pascual, and wipes his hands, and puts one on the side of Reggie's face, and Reggie puts his own up to answer.

The rest of the box goes to John, in January, to be numbered.

## March 1984

Jean stands up on the gunwale.

She's fifty-nine and Hilario's son has given up telling her not to, the way his father gave up in 1961, and she stands on the edge of the boat at the mouth of the channel below the Caño Mariusa at dawn on the thirteenth of March 1984 with her hat on and her hand up, and counts.

Nine.

Nine heads, in the water, at the edge of the closed water, a hundred yards off. Adults, and two calves.

She counted seven here in 1971, the year they turned up. She'd been counting the Pedernales mouths from the outside for three years by then, from a mile off, and watching the bottles come up saltier every dry season, and the heads get fewer, and then in 1970 there were none. None at all. And the next dry season, sixty miles east, at the mouth of the Mariusa, where the water still comes down big and brown in the rains and goes out slow and salt in the dry, because the Mánamo wall sent half of the western Orinoco this way instead — seven. Out of nowhere. In a place she'd surveyed in 1962 and found empty.

She knew who'd moved them. She sat in the boat that morning in 1971 with her hat off and laughed until Hilario thought she'd been stung. Then she went to Rome and made Reggie close it.

---

There are four more in the tank amidships.

Two pairs. Captive-born, all four of them; two from the Martin, who are Chumley's grandchildren through Leslie, and two from the pens on Vance's old frontage in Florida, which Jean has run since 1974 with a header tank on every one of them. They came across from Port of Spain on a Venezuelan navy landing craft that Caracas lent the Commission, which it wouldn't have done in 1966, and it's done it now because it's 1984. Because there are oryx back in the desert in Oman. Because a woman in Rio has put golden lion tamarins born in the National Zoo into a forest in Brazil. Because the governor of Florida stood up last year and said the word *Everglades* and the word *restore* in the same sentence in front of cameras, and nobody laughed.

Nobody's filling in the canals in Florida. Nobody's taking the wall off the Mánamo. Jean has testified to three committees in Tallahassee and two in Caracas and been thanked beautifully by all of them. It'll be twenty years, if it's ever.

But they're saying *restore*. For the first time in her life, they're saying it out loud. And a Venezuelan navy lieutenant with a clipboard is standing at the rail of a landing craft in the delta at dawn watching four animals that were born in concrete go back into the water, because the Commission asked, and because a paper about a heron and thirty-nine of forty that nobody read in 1966 has been cited eleven times since 1981.

---

Nestor is in the water at the side of the boat.

He's seventy. His hair is white and Inés has come with him as far as Port of Spain and no further, and made him promise, in front of Jean, not to go in above the waist, and he's promised, and he's in to the waist, on a sandbar at the edge of the channel, with his hand on the side of the tank, and his other hand in the river.

"*Ya*," he says. "They're ready, *doctora*."

They open the sluice on the side of the tank.

The four go out one after another into brown water and are gone, straight down, and then they come up thirty yards off, together, all four, and turn round in the water once, and then they go east. Not wandering. Straight. Into the closed water, towards the nine.

"They know," says Nestor, with his hand in the river. His face does the slow thing. "They're being met, *doctora*. The ones out there are calling. It's very loud." He laughs. "*Like a kitchen.*"

---

There's a canoe at the edge of the mangrove.

It's been there since before the light. It's got a young man in it with a Trinidadian father and a Venezuelan mother and an outboard that works about one week in three, and his name is Chago, like his father's, and he has been waiting there with his paddle across his knees since half past four because the doctor who counts asked his father's cousin's grandson at Cedros to ask him.

Jean gets down off the gunwale. She takes the tin out of her jacket.

It's a round tin with a screw cap, gone to rust at the rim. Reggie put it into her hands on the verandah of the house on the north side in February and didn't say a word, and she didn't open it, and she's carried it inside her shirt for three weeks, which Pascual informed her was traditional.

She reaches across the gap between the boat and the canoe and gives it to the boy.

"For the drowned man," she says, in Spanish.

"*Sí, doctora*," says Chago, and puts it inside his shirt, and pushes off, and paddles into the closed water, east, after the four, and is gone round the bend in the mangrove in about a minute.

---

Jean stands in the boat with her hands empty.

Then she says, "Right. *West*," to Hilario's son, and sits down, and doesn't look east again; and the lieutenant makes a note on his clipboard, and Nestor comes up out of the river on to the sandbar and dries his hand on his trousers and looks at it, as though there might be something on it.

---

## May 1984

John opens the safe at nine o'clock in the morning on the twentieth of May 1984, which is a Sunday, because the envelope says the twentieth of May and doesn't say anything about Sundays.

He's fifty-four. He's been Director of the William Martin Institute for twenty-two years and he still works in the lecture hut with the leak, and there are six people in the office, which is too many, and he's told them so. Dr Tar and Dr Pobil, who came back to the house on the north side in 1980 and have been a nuisance to him every day since. Dr Leslie, in from Florida on Thursday. Nestor, in a tie, which Inés put on him. And Inés, who has never once been in the office in thirty-one years, and who came because Nestor asked her to, and is sitting by the door with her hands dry in her lap.

The envelope's buff, and sealed, and on the front of it in a hand John would know anywhere, because he's been reading the Trust's minutes in it for twenty-two years:

*The notebook of Mr C. Cholmondeley. Not to be opened before 20 May 1984, unless its author asks for it. H. B.*

---

He opens it with the paper knife.

It's a black notebook, school size, gone soft and warped with damp at one corner, with a pencil loop on the spine and no pencil in it. John holds it in both hands for a moment and doesn't open it.

Then he takes a label out of the drawer and writes a number on it, the next number in the sequence, 61,208, and sticks it inside the back cover, and writes the number and the date and *notebook, C.C., 1953–54, per H.B.* in the register, because that's what he's for.

And then he does open it, to the first page. Only the first. He's agreed that. Everyone in the room has agreed that.

It says *Specimen B. Nov. '53*, at the top, in a small cramped hand that looks as though an ant has fallen in the ink, and underneath there's a drawing of a fish with blue lines on its face, and a question mark.

John closes it.

"That's all," says Dr Tar, from the window. He says it quite quietly. "Until he answers. Put it back."

---

Dr Pobil turns round in his chair and looks at Dr Tar for a long time.

John has watched those two men look at each other for thirty-one years. He's never once seen Dr Pobil look at Dr Tar like that, which is as if the man had just done something Dr Pobil had privately given up expecting to see before he died.

"You're leaving it," Dr Pobil says.

"I'm leaving it."

"The whole notebook. In the safe. With two hundred and six pages in it that you have wanted to read since the ninth of October 1957."

"Two hundred and nine," says Dr Tar. "And yes."

"*Ay, Dios mío*," says Dr Pobil, and puts his hand over his eyes, and doesn't take it away for some time.

---

And then there's the other thing, which Dr Pobil has told John is his to do, and does.

He takes out his wallet. He takes out of it a sheet of paper, folded in half and in half again, soft as cloth, gone brown at the folds, and he unfolds it on John's desk very carefully with the tips of his fingers, and smooths it out.

It's a drawing. It's John's own drawing. He did it in the last week of May of 1954, afterwards, from what he'd worked out, on the third-last leaf of the plate book, and it went missing out of the plate book at the end of May 1954, and he has known for thirty years exactly who took it, and never once asked.

A man and a mermaid, face to face, in the water, up to the collarbone. Each with a hand on the other's face.

"It's yours," says Dr Pobil. "It belongs in the sequence. It always did. There wasn't an honest paper in the world it could go on in 1954." He takes his hand off it. "There is now."

---

John gets out the register.

He turns back. It takes a while; it's thirty years back, and it's the first volume, and the binding's gone. Plate 209. Plate 210. Plate 211. Plate 212. Plate 213.

*Plate 214 — MISSING.*

In his own hand. In red. He wrote it the week he found it gone, because if something goes missing, everybody can see it has. And there's been a gap in the sequence of the William Martin Institute's plates at 214 for thirty years, a single red line, and every single person in this room has known what was in the gap.

He takes his pen out. He strikes through *MISSING*, once, with a single flat line.

And beside it he writes: *Recovered 20.v.1984. Undated. Drawn from what could be worked out. It was right.*

And then he picks up the drawing, and writes *214* in the corner of it in pencil, very small, and puts it in the sequence, where it goes.

---

## June 1984

Pascual is seventy-one.

He has been seventy-one since March, and he has mentioned it to Reggie on perhaps eleven occasions, most of them at meals, in the tone of a man presenting evidence, because a costing sheet drawn up in a small room with an iron cot in June of 1954 ran for thirty years, and he was forty-one when it was drawn, and he would like it on the record that he has seen the whole of it out.

"You've been a nuisance to me since you were forty-one," says Reggie. "You're not finished. You don't get to be finished on a technicality."

"*Ay*. It was a *term*. Thirty years. You of all people —"

"A term is not a stopping rule."

They have been having this argument, on and off, for three months. Pascual would not give it up for anything.

---

The tin comes on the twelfth of June, a Tuesday, the way everything comes to this house that doesn't come in the post: on the launch, in Nestor's hand, in oilskin, having come across from Cedros on the Sunday from Tito's grandson, who had it from the man at Icacos who talks too much, who had it from a canoe.

Nestor doesn't come up the eleven steps. He's seventy and he says the steps in that house were built by a sadist. He gives it to Pascual at the bottom, on the harbour road, and says "*Doctor*," and goes away without waiting, because it isn't his.

Pascual carries it up the eleven steps inside his shirt on the left, and stops on the ninth, and Reggie at the top says, "Four this week," and Pascual says a thing in Spanish that isn't fit to be repeated.

---

Reggie opens it at the kitchen table.

His hands do it perfectly well. Pascual watches them. He unties the line and unwraps the oilskin and unscrews the cap and takes out a chart, soft as a handkerchief now, gone nearly to rags at one edge, and unrolls it, and there's the reef, in Jean's hand, and there's *Reef opens here*, and *Fresh water*, and *Keep clear — ordnance*.

He turns it over.

On the back, at the top, in a small cramped hand, in pencil gone grey with twenty-two years: *It worked.*

And under it, in Reggie's own hand, small and upright and fast, in ink, from a February afternoon on the verandah:

*So did the other. She lived to be old. He is a grandfather. Mrs Bevan kept your notebook thirty years and died in 1968. We should like to print it, all of it, under your name. Yes or no. — R.*

And under that, in pencil again, in the small cramped hand, fresh:

*Yes. All of it.*

*Tell the one who counts that the reef opens two cables east of where she put it. M. says so.*

*Tell P. my feet are fine.*

*C.*

---

Reggie reads it, and doesn't move.

Pascual reads it upside down across the table, which he has been able to do since 1954, and gets as far as the third line, and makes a noise he has not made since a flat in Rome in 1962, and puts both hands flat on the table.

"*My feet are fine*," he says. "*My feet are fine.* That boy. That — *thirty years*. Thirty years in a delta and he sends *me* —"

"He knew you'd be looking at his feet," says Reggie. His voice has gone. "Twenty-four years old in a sick bay, and he knew you'd be the one worrying about his feet, and he's fifty-four and he's put it on a *chart*."

"*Ay, Dios mío.*" Pascual has his face in his hands. "Telephone Jean."

"Jean's in Florida."

"Then telephone Florida. *The one who counts*, Reggie. He's told her her name. Hester said she'd never hear it and he's written it on the back of her own chart, and told her she drew the reef wrong in the same line, and if you don't telephone her tonight she'll never speak to either of us again."

---

Reggie telephones Florida at ten o'clock that night, from the Institute office, because the house has never had a telephone and never will. Pascual stands beside him in the dark office with his hand flat on the man's back, and hears it, because Jean Leslie at fifty-nine on a long-distance line from Everglades City can be heard from the far side of a room.

She screams. It is not one word. It goes on for some time. There is laughing in it, and something under the laughing, and then a great deal of language, and then, quite clearly, with the line crackling, "*Two cables east?* Two *cables?* I took those soundings in *1954* with a lead line off the back of the Institute launch and he has the *nerve* —"

And then nothing, for a while. And then, very quietly, down four thousand miles of wire:

"Say it again. The first bit. Say what he called me."

"The one who counts," says Reggie.

Pascual can't hear what she says to that. He doesn't try.

---

The review is on the fourteenth of June 1984, at the kitchen table, at eight o'clock in the evening, because that was the date in 1955.

Reggie brings a folder. Of course he does. He has brought a folder to every review for thirty years and Pascual has never once succeeded in stopping him, and the folder has the costing sheet in it, soft at the folds now as a chart, with the cup-ring on the corner and *June 1955 — review* struck through with one flat line and *taken* beside it in Pascual's blunt hand, and above that, underlined twice, a year.

Reggie puts it on the table. And the notebook — the black one, from the safe, which John brought across on the launch that afternoon with a label in the back and a face like a boy's — beside it. Page two hundred and six is marked with a strip of paper. Neither of them has opened it past the first page.

"Item one," says Reggie.

"There's only one item."

"There is only ever one item. It's still item one."

---

Pascual opens the notebook.

He does it himself, because Reggie slides it across the table to him and puts both hands flat on the folder and looks at the window. He turns to the strip of paper, and finds the page, and reads it — the whole page, slowly, the boy's small terrible hand, a day in May of 1954 — and at the bottom of it, the line.

*I do not think he is company. I think he is the place where I am not alone, which is a different thing, and I do not know what I shall be if it ever shuts.*

He reads it twice. Then he turns the notebook round on the table and pushes it back.

Reggie reads it.

Pascual watches his face while he does. It's not a face that shows things; it's never been. It's a face that has to be read, like a column, from the top, and Pascual has been reading it since a pen rail in 1953, and he reads it now, and sees exactly where the line goes in, and what it goes in next to. A card with a dash on it. A reach along a stair in the dark. A morning in Rome. *Call. No answer.*

And then something else, under that. Something that isn't about Massachusetts at all.

Reggie closes the notebook, and puts his hand flat on the cover, and looks across the table.

"That's you," he says. "That's what you are. It isn't company. You're the place." He takes his glasses off. "I have been trying to say that for thirty years and he said it at twenty-four in a sentence, in a hut, about an animal. I would like it on the record that I mind."

"*Noted*," says Pascual, and it comes out in pieces.

---

"The item," Reggie says, when he can.

"The item." Pascual wipes his face with the back of his wrist. "You first. You always go first."

"It is a thirty-year review of an arrangement entered into on the ninth of June 1954 on the basis of a costing sheet, of which the terminal date is —" he taps the year, underlined twice — "now. I have one observation, and one proposal."

"Go on."

"The observation is that it worked." Reggie looks at the chart, which is on the dresser, where Pascual put it, next to a plate numbered 238 with two heads in the water on it. "All of it. Every one of the thirty. I said at the first review that I had been happier than I had any right to be, on a sample of one year, and I am now in a position to say it on a sample of thirty. Some things happened in those years that were not the arrangement's doing. Hester is dead, and the Colonel. The Mánamo has a wall across it, and a company in Florida drained a hundred and seventy square miles of cypress to sell to people in Ohio. And a man in Massachusetts is dead, and I cannot stop reaching for him. None of that is on the costing sheet.

"One thing is. There were nine weeks, from the thirtieth of November 1958 to a Monday night in February, that were the worst of my life, and they were the arrangement's doing — or mine, inside it, which comes to the same entry. They belong on the sheet, and I have put them there." He does not look away from the man across the table. "And I should like it recorded beside them that on every one of those sixty-four days, including the four in the block, I wanted it to go on. That is the only figure on the sheet I have ever been entirely certain of.

"And there are nine in the water below the Mariusa and four more who went to meet them, and a man in the water with them whose feet are fine. There are two hundred and eleven animals in pens in fourteen countries and not one of them came out of the sea. And I have not once in thirty years been in a room by myself." He puts his glasses back on. "That is the observation."

"Sixty-four," says Pascual, and it doesn't come out steady.

"You knew I'd have counted."

"I knew." Pascual wipes his face with the back of his wrist. "Go on. The proposal."

Reggie takes the pencil out of his pocket and holds it out.

---

Pascual takes the pencil.

He looks at the costing sheet for a while. At *1984*, underlined twice, in his own hand, from a night in a small room with an iron cot and a man who couldn't do anything by halves telling him so, and the rain not come yet.

Then he draws one flat line through it.

And beside it, in the same blunt hand, he writes *2014*, and underlines it twice.

"You can't do that," Reggie says, immediately. "You can't extend a prespecified terminal date on the day of the review. The whole point of prespecifying is that you cannot go back and —"

"I can and I have."

"I shall be ninety-five."

"You'll be *unbearable*."

"It's methodologically indefensible —"

"It is," Pascual agrees, "and I have done it, and I'm seventy-one, and I kept my word, and if you say one more thing about prespecification I shall put the lamp out with you still talking."

---

Reggie looks at the paper for a long time.

Then he folds it, and puts it back in the folder, and puts the folder on the counter by the stove, well away from the notebook, and the bottle, and the wet ring the glasses have left on the table; which is a thing Pascual notices him do, and does not comment on, and has been noticing for thirty years.

"Thirty years," Reggie says.

"Thirty years."

"You won't be —"

"*Reggie*."

"I'm only saying the arithmetic —"

"I know what the arithmetic is. I'm a doctor. I've done the arithmetic." Pascual gets up. It takes him a moment. Reggie watches him and does not count, and Pascual sees him not counting, and loves him for it so much that he has to put his hand on the table. "Do you know what I'm going to do about the arithmetic?"

"What?"

"Nothing," says Pascual, and comes round the table, and puts his hand on the side of Reggie's face. "I'm going to be here. And you're going to be here. And every morning, one of us is going to reach out, the way you reach for a rail." His thumb moves. "That's not fewer than two. That's the whole clause. Hester wrote it for us and didn't tell us."

And Reggie Tar, sixty-five years old, at a kitchen table on the north side of a harbour, with his glasses in his hand and his face doing, for once, everything, where anybody could see it, puts his own hand up to answer.
