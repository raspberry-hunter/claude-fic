# The Drowned Man

He sleeps with his arm through the floor.

The floor is split palm laid over four posts, about six feet above the channel at high water and eleven at low, and there's a gap between the third and fourth slats on the side nearest the water where he took a slat out in 1955 and never put it back. He lies on his side on a mat with his right arm through the gap to the shoulder, and when the tide's up his hand is in the water, and when it's down his hand is hanging in the air over the mud, and Monty comes up the channel in the dark and lifts his head until his face is under Charles's palm, and stays there.

That's how he sleeps. He's slept that way for nearly seven years. He could sleep without it. He'd rather not.

*The-fan-lovely-when-there-but-who-disappears-into-its-hole. Here. Here.*

"Here," Charles says, into the mat, in the dark. "Go to sleep."

*Not tired.*

"You're never tired. I'm tired. Some of us have legs."

A picture comes up his arm of a man on a platform with his legs sticking out, very long, looking absurd, and Charles laughs into the mat and goes to sleep.

---

The dry season comes in January and the channel drops and the water goes from brown to the colour of strong tea, and the fish come up the caños from the sea, and it's the best time of year.

He's up at first light. He takes his arm out of the floor and lies on his back for a minute and lets the blood come back into it, which hurts, and which he's stopped minding. Then he goes down the notched post to the mud and into the water up to his chest, and Monty's there, and the whole delta comes up through the skin of him at once.

It used to put him on his knees. It did, the first year — it put him face down in the shallows in June of 1954 on the edge of the mouth, when they got here, when he came into water with thirty-one of them in it at once and all of them *loud*. Monty got under him and held his head up until it stopped. It's never stopped, exactly. It's the way a big house is loud with a family in it: you stop hearing it as noise.

And under it, steady, like a hand flat on the middle of his back, there's Monty. Always. Every hour. Awake, asleep, twelve miles up a caño after fish, it doesn't matter. He's never once been alone since the night he went into the water, and he knows now that he never will be, and some mornings that still takes the breath out of him.

---

He's thirty-one. He worked it out in December from the rains. He had a birthday some time in the wet season and didn't notice, which he thinks is probably the correct way to have them.

He knows what he looks like, roughly, because Chago's got a piece of mirror in the canoe. He's very brown and very thin and his hair's long and he cuts his beard with the knife when it gets in his mouth. His hands are a mess. His feet are worse. There's a scar across the back of his left hand from the first net he cut, in April of 1955, when he did it wrong. He's lost a tooth. He's had fever four times and got through it three times on his own and once with the family at the settlement upstream, who put him in a hammock and gave him something bitter and didn't ask him anything, and whom he'd trust with his life, having done so.

Pascual would be appalled. Pascual would take one look at his feet and shout for about ten minutes.

He thinks that a good deal. It's one of the ways he knows he's all right.

---

Chago comes on Tuesdays in the dry season, more or less.

Chago is a fish trader from Pedernales with a canoe and an outboard that works about one week in three, and a Trinidadian mother, and more cousins than there are channels, and he has come up this caño every week or so since 1956 to trade: dried fish and the places where the fish are, which Charles can find better than anybody on this coast and doesn't explain, for salt and matches and hooks and line and, once a year, a shirt. Chago thinks he's mad. Chago's said so, cheerfully, in two languages, about a hundred times. Chago also thinks he's the drowned man, and told him so in 1957, and laughed until he nearly went over the side, and brought him a bottle of rum the next week as a kind of apology, which Charles gave to the family upstream.

"*Ahogado*," Chago says, on the fourteenth of February, tying up. "You've got visitors."

Charles stops with a string of fish in his hands.

"Not *here*." Chago waves an arm. "West. The western caños. A boat out of Tucupita, Hilario's boat, you know Hilario, the one with the eye. With a gringa."

"A gringa."

"*Una gringa con sombrero.*" Chago mimes a hat, enormous. "She's been in the western caños a fortnight. Counting *sirenas*."

---

He doesn't say anything. He puts the fish in Chago's basket and takes the salt and the matches and doesn't look up.

"She counts them," Chago says. "From the boat. With her finger. *Una, dos, tres.* And she writes in a book. And she takes photographs with a big camera. And she puts bottles in the water on a string and takes them out and looks at them, and puts a thermometer in, and writes that down too." Chago shakes his head. "Hilario says she's crazy. Hilario says she shouts at him in English all day and he doesn't understand one word and he likes her very much."

"Has she come east?"

"No." Chago lights a cigarette. "That's the thing. She went to Cedros first, before Tucupita. At the end of January. She tried to get a boat into the Pedernales mouth. Nobody would take her, because of you." He grins. "Four boats at Cedros. And my cousin at Pedernales. And then the men at Cedros say she sat on the end of the jetty for an hour, by herself, looking at the water. And then she got up and went and got a bus to the ferry and went round to Tucupita and started in the west, and she's never once asked to come east again."

---

He goes to see.

It's a stupid thing to do, and he knows it's a stupid thing to do before he's thought it all the way through, and he does it anyway, because a gringa counting animals in the delta with a book is either the best thing that's happened to this coast in eight years or the worst, and he has to know which.

It's two days. Monty knows the way through the back channels better than any chart; there are ways through the mangrove west that no boat can use. They go at night and lie up in the day. On the second night Charles sleeps in the fork of a tree with his arm down in the water like a man in a bunk, and Monty's face under his hand, and three others of the people round the roots, because they've come too, because they wanted to see what Charlie wanted to see.

At dawn on the third day he's in the water up to his neck in the roots of a red mangrove at the mouth of a western caño with Monty against his side, and there's a boat in the channel about three hundred yards off with its engine cut.

There's a woman standing on the gunwale.

---

Not in the boat. *On* it. On the edge of the gunwale, amidships, in a pair of trousers and a hat the size of a cartwheel, holding on to nothing, with a camera round her neck, which is not where anybody should stand on a boat in a current, and which Hilario is clearly telling her in Spanish at the top of his voice.

And the woman shouts back across three hundred yards of flat brown water at dawn, in English —

"— not the *left* one, Hilario, the *left* — no, that's the right — oh, for *God's* sake —"

And Charles Cholmondeley, in the mangrove roots, up to his neck, with an animal against his side, knows that voice the way he knows the smell of formalin.

---

He goes under.

He doesn't decide to. His body does it. He goes down into the brown water in the roots and stays there with his eyes shut and his hand on Monty, and a great confused rush of pictures comes back to him from Monty and the three others — *the-ones-who-look-like-merfolk-but-are-not*, a boat, a shape standing up on the edge of a boat, and something else, a new picture, one he hasn't had off any of them before: a hand, held up, tapping the air, once, twice, three times, and pointing.

*One who counts.*

He comes up very slowly, only his eyes and nose, in the roots.

She's got her hand up. She's counting. Out in the middle of the caño, a hundred yards past the boat, there are heads in the water — five, six — and she's standing on the gunwale of Hilario's boat with one hand up, pointing at each of them in turn, the way you'd count sheep over a gate, and her lips are moving.

Then she gets down, and writes in a book on her knee, and picks up the camera and takes a photograph, and winds it on, and takes another.

Then Hilario hands her a bottle on a string, and she drops it over the side and counts, and pulls it up, and holds it up to the light and looks at it, and puts a thermometer in it, and writes.

---

And then she does the thing he'll think about for the rest of his life.

She stands up in the boat, properly this time, in the bottom of it, and turns round, and looks east.

Not at the animals. Not at the mangrove where he is. Past it. East, down the long brown reach of the caño towards where it bends, towards the back channels, towards the eastern mouths behind Pedernales, which are two days away and which nobody could possibly see from there. She takes her hat off. She stands in Hilario's boat with her hat in her hand and looks east for a long time, as though she can see something, or as though she's deciding something, or as though she's making quite sure she knows exactly where a thing is so that she'll never, by any accident, go near it.

Then she puts the hat back on and sits down and says, "Right. Hilario. *West*," and points, and Hilario starts the engine, and the boat goes off up the caño away from him, and round the bend, and the noise of it goes on for a long time after he can't see it.

Charles stays in the roots until it's quite gone.

---

He works it out on the platform, over four nights, lying on his side with his arm through the floor.

He does it the way he'd have done it in 1953, in the notebook, if he still had the notebook, which he doesn't, because he left it in the hut. He lays it out in order. He's still good at that. It's one of the things the sea hasn't taken off him; it's just that the problems are different now, and he does the arithmetic in his head at night with Monty's face under his hand.

Jean's in the delta.

Jean went to Cedros *first*. Before she came to the west. Before any of it. She tried five boats to get into the eastern mouths, and they wouldn't go because of the drowned man, and she didn't argue — *Jean* didn't argue, Jean who argued with Reggie about the sodium figures for three days in 1953 — she sat on the end of the jetty for an hour and then went all the way round by bus and ferry to the other end of the delta and never came east again.

So she knew. When they said *the drowned man*, she knew. And then she spent an hour on a jetty making sure she wasn't going to come.

---

And she counts them.

That's the second part, and he lies with it for a whole night.

She isn't hunting. Hunters don't count; hunters take. She counts them, and she photographs them, and she drops bottles in the water to see how salt it is and how warm, up and down the caños, one after another, every day for weeks, writing it all down in a book on her knee.

She's trying to find out what water they need.

He knows it the way he'd know a method from a paper. She's trying to work out what an estuary has to be for them to live in it and have calves — why they're in this caño and not that one, why there were six in one caño and nothing in the next — and she's doing it in the one place in the world where there are still enough of them to count. And there's only one reason on earth for anybody to want to know that, and it isn't to catch them. It's to keep them somewhere. It's to keep them *going*.

Jean Leslie is in the delta trying to work out how to save Monty's people. And she's doing it by standing on the gunwale of a boat in a hat, shouting at Hilario, with her back to him on purpose.

---

And then — about three in the morning on the fourth night, with the tide coming in and the water rising up his arm to the elbow — he goes back further. Because once you've found the method you go back and read the introduction again.

The verandah. After supper. The week before. John had said the thing about Ian Fleming and Reggie had taken him off, and there was that silence, and Jean leaned back in her chair — like Reggie, he'd thought at the time, she leans back like Reggie — and said *Charles, how is Beta doing these days? Stiffness gone?*

And then: *How far do you think he could swim in a day?*

And he'd told her. He'd been *pleased* to tell her. He'd done the arithmetic already, timing Monty's laps; two and a half knots, twenty-five nautical miles. And she'd said, *Ah*, very softly, and *I'm glad he's doing better, Charles*, and Pascual had frowned, and the Colonel and Hester had looked at each other.

She wasn't asking about the stiffness.

She was asking how far he'd get. She was working out, at supper, across a table, in front of everybody, how many days it would take an animal to get from that cay to anywhere his own kind lived. So that she'd know. So that she could draw it.

---

He gets up off the mat in the dark and goes to the roof.

The roof's thatch, palm, over a frame, and there's a place in the ridge above the corner post where he pushed the tin into the thatch in the second week of June 1954, when they got here, and it's been there since. With the knife, most of the time. He takes the tin down and unscrews the cap by feel and takes the chart out and goes to the edge of the platform where the moon's coming down on the water, and unrolls it, soft as a handkerchief now, and holds it up.

*Reef opens here.*

*Fresh water.*

*Keep clear — ordnance.*

He's looked at this chart perhaps a thousand times in seven years. Reggie gave it to him. *Take this. In case.* Reggie thrust it at him in a corridor, and he'd thought — he's always thought — that it was Reggie's.

It's not Reggie's hand. Reggie's hand is small and upright and fast. This is round, with looped *d*'s, and a way of crossing the *t* in *water* that goes too far to the right.

He's seen that hand on two hundred feed sheets. He's seen it on the sodium figures in 1953, in the margin, next to a note that said *Reggie, this is WRONG*.

It's Jean's.

---

He sits down on the edge of the platform with his feet over the water and the chart on his knees, and he laughs.

He doesn't know why. It comes up out of him like the sea did in 1954 — helplessly, in the dark, with the tears running into his beard, for a long time — and Monty comes up out of the channel below him in alarm and puts both hands on the edge of the platform and his head up, and a whole storm of pictures comes up at Charles, *Charlie hurts? Charlie hurts?*

"No," Charles says. "No. It's all right. It's Jean."

*Jean.* A picture comes back — hesitant, hopeful — of a boat, a hat, a hand held up counting.

"Yes. Her." He wipes his face with the back of his wrist. "She knew, Monty. The whole time. The whole week, at supper, drawing the reef. She knew what I was going to do before I did. She drew it before Reggie gave it to me. And she never said one word to me. Not *one*." He's laughing again. "She let me think I'd thought of it."

Monty considers this for a while with his hands on the edge of the platform.

A picture comes. A big fish — a grouper, heavy, slow, sitting in a cave mouth — and a small fish, a wrasse, darting about, cleaning its teeth, and the grouper sitting there with its mouth open, patiently, thinking it's arranged the whole thing.

Charles laughs until he has to lie down on the floor.

---

He doesn't go west again. She doesn't come east.

Chago brings him news all through the rest of the dry season and into the rains, a little at a time, the way Chago brings everything. The gringa did four hundred miles of channel. The gringa counted eleven *sirenas* at once off the mouth of the Caño Macareo and stood up in the boat and screamed so loud Hilario thought she'd been bitten. The gringa photographed everything. The gringa went back to Tucupita in May and drank Hilario under the table, and came out again when the rains came, and was still at it in October.

She never once came east of the bend.

And one other thing, in April, that Chago tells him as a joke.

There are men from the Corporación in Tucupita. Engineers, from Caracas, with a launch and a theodolite and a great many stakes, who've been up and down the Caño Mánamo all through the dry season banging the stakes into the bank and writing numbers on them in red paint. And Chago's cousin in Tucupita, who drives their launch, says the Corporación is going to build a wall right across the Mánamo, from bank to bank, to keep the floods off the islands and make farms.

"*Farms*," says Chago. "In the delta. Rice." He laughs until the canoe rocks. "A wall across the Mánamo. They'll need a bigger wall than God."

Charles laughs too, because Chago's expecting it.

He doesn't laugh afterwards, on the platform, with the tide going out under him. Pedernales sits on the mouth of the Mánamo. Every drop of fresh water that comes past this platform in the wet season comes down that caño first. And a woman on the other side of the delta has spent three months dropping bottles on strings into the water, one after another, to find out exactly how salt it has to be.

He doesn't know what a wall would do. Nobody does; that's the thing about it. He puts it in the roof with the tin, and it's still there in the morning.

---

In June of 1962 the foreigners in Rome close the water.

Chago tells him about it on a Tuesday in the first rains, with enormous indignation, because it's cost two of his cousins their licences for the eastern mouths, not that they'd been using them. Some Commission. A lot of *extranjeros* in Rome. A paper at Cedros with a stamp on it saying nine waters are closed to everyone — *everyone*, even to look — and one of them is this one.

"*Nueve*," says Chago. "Nine. They didn't even say which one was yours."

Charles sits on the platform with that for a long time after Chago's gone.

He doesn't know who's in Rome. He can't. He's never going to ask, because asking is how people find things out. But somebody in Rome closed nine waters so that nobody would notice the one that mattered, and did it without writing its name, and he sits on the edge of his platform over the channel and thinks that he knows only one man in the world who'd hide a result in a table so carefully that nobody could pick it out but himself.

He doesn't let himself finish that thought. He puts it in the roof with the tin.

---

A month later, in July, Chago comes up the caño on a Thursday in the rain, which he never does, with his outboard working, which it never does, and his face grey.

"*Ahogado*. Listen to me."

And tells him.

An Englishman at Cedros. Since Monday. At the end of the jetty every morning at five with a pair of navy binoculars, looking at the Mouth. Asking every boat to take him into the eastern waters. Pleasant. Polite. A linen jacket. Asks questions about *everything* — about the tides, about the *sirenas*, about the drowned man, about how long the story's been told, about when the nets started being cut — and writes nothing down and forgets nothing. Chago's cousin Rafael talked to him for ten minutes on the Tuesday and came away feeling he'd been turned inside out and put back the wrong way.

"Faustino says his name," Chago says. "Faustino's been drinking with him. *Espilsbury*."

---

Charles doesn't move.

He's sitting on the edge of the platform in the rain with his feet in the water up to the ankle and Monty's shoulder against his shin, and he doesn't move at all, and down his leg into the water goes something that makes Monty come up out of the channel all at once with a noise like a bark, and three of the people who were lying up in the next reach of the caño come too.

*Curious*, Reggie said. In the corridor. With his hands shaking. *And impatient. And very fond of knives.*

He's never seen the man. He went into the sea the night before the man came off the boat. And he's been afraid of him, a little, every single day for eight years, in the place under everything else, the way you're afraid of a particular kind of weather.

"He can't get a boat," Charles says, and his voice sounds very far off. "Nobody at Cedros will go in."

"*Faustino* will go in," says Chago. "Faustino owes money. Faustino's taking him at half past four on Saturday morning. Across and down the coast into the eastern mouths as far as the tide goes." He puts his hand on Charles's shoulder, which Chago has never done in six years. "And Faustino's put a net in the boat, *ahogado*. For himself. To sell at Güiria."

---

Charles doesn't sleep on the Friday night.

He sits on the platform in the dark with the knife beside him and his hand on Monty's face, and he makes a plan, because he's always been good at making a plan when there isn't one, and it's a bad plan, and it's the only one there is. He'll take the people back into the back channels, the deep ones, where no boat the size of Faustino's can go. He'll take them tonight. He'll come back himself, alone, before the light, and wait at the mouth in the roots. And if Faustino's boat comes, and the net goes over — well. He's done it before.

He doesn't think about the Englishman. He can't. He thinks about the knife.

He takes the people back into the back channels in the dark, thirty-four of them, and Monty won't leave him, and he can't make Monty leave him, and in the end he gives up and they go back to the mouth together, and lie in the roots, and wait, and the light comes.

No boat comes.

---

Chago comes on the Sunday.

He comes up the caño in the canoe with the outboard not working, paddling, and he's paddling like a man who can't wait to tell somebody something, and he's talking before he's tied up.

A doctor. Not an Englishman. A Spaniard, or a Venezuelan, or something, Chago doesn't know, but a *doctor*, with a small badge in his pocket from the foreigners in Rome, and a technician from some island who was Tito's cousin. And on Saturday morning at twenty past four there were twenty-three men from Cedros on the jetty in the dark, all standing, not one of them saying anything — Chago's cousin Rafael was one — and the Englishman was already in Faustino's boat, and this doctor walked down the jetty between them all and saw the net, and *shouted*.

Chago stands up in the canoe to show him. Chago's never met the man and does a very good impression.

"*Así*," Chago says, with both arms going, at the top of his voice, the canoe rocking. "*Así, ahogado!* In English! For a full minute! Rafael didn't understand a word and says it was the most frightening thing he's ever heard in his life. And then he stopped, all at once, like this" — Chago stops, and puts his hands behind his back — "and he talked. Very quiet. For a long time. And the Englishman got out of the boat."

"And Faustino?"

"Faustino looked at twenty-three men and turned off his engine." Chago sits down. "Rafael says the doctor's name was Pobil."

---

Charles laughs so hard he falls off the platform.

He actually falls. He's sitting on the edge with his feet over and he laughs and loses his balance and goes backwards off the edge of the palm floor and into six feet of brown water at high tide, and Monty's under him before he's touched the bottom, and he comes up still laughing, streaming, with Monty's arm round him and the whole caño full of alarmed *sirenas* and Chago staring down at him from the canoe as if he's finally, definitively, gone mad.

*Pascual.*

Pascual, on a jetty, with a *badge*. Pascual shouting at the man Reggie was afraid of, in front of twenty-three fishermen, in the dark. And then stopping. And putting his hands behind his back.

He knows that voice too. He knows exactly what it sounds like when it stops.

---

That evening he gets the tin down again.

He sits on the platform with it on his knees for a long time with the rain stopped and the frogs going, and he thinks about what he can send. Because he's going to send something. He decided that in the water, laughing, with Monty's arm round him.

Nothing with a name. A name is a record. Nothing that says where. Nothing that could hurt anyone if Faustino got hold of it, or the Englishman, or the Guardia, or the man in Port of Spain. Nothing that anybody but the people it's for could possibly understand.

He's got a stub of pencil Chago gave him in 1959 and never used.

He unrolls the chart and turns it over and looks at the back of it for a while. And he thinks about a corridor in the dark in May of 1954, and Reggie's lips doing the thing they did, and Reggie saying *Colonel Bevan does outrank me. I will try it once, Charles. I shall be quite cross with you if it doesn't work.*

And he'd said *It will work*, and hadn't been sure of anything in his life, and walked out of the corridor and across the concrete and got a tarpaulin.

He writes it in the small cramped hand that Jean used to say looked like an ant had fallen in the ink.

*It worked.*

---

He doesn't know if Gamma lived. He doesn't know if they put the new one in with her. He doesn't know whether Reggie ever tried it, or whether it took, or whether anybody on that cay is alive, or where any of them are. He doesn't know who's in Rome. He's not going to find out. That's the price, and he paid it in the corridor, and so be it.

But it worked. He got here. Monty got home. There are thirty-four of them in the back channels behind Pedernales and not one of them has gone into a net in five years, and a woman is counting them on the other side of the delta with her back turned, and a man with a badge shouted on a jetty.

And whoever gets that tin will know who it's from, and who it's for, and what it means, and nobody else on this earth will.

He rolls the chart up and puts it in the tin and screws the cap on, and wraps it in oilskin, and ties it with line, and on the Monday morning, early, he gives it to Chago.

"For the doctor," he says. "The one who shouted. Through your cousin at Icacos. Nothing written. Just — it's for him."

Chago looks at the packet and at him.

"*Ahogado*," Chago says, "you're mad."

"Yes," says Charles. "Go on."

---

He watches the canoe go down the caño until it's round the bend.

Then he goes down the notched post into the water, which is high and brown and warm, and Monty's there, and the whole delta comes up through the skin of him, loud as a house with a family in it, and under all of it, flat against the middle of his back, steady, the one thing.

*Charlie. Here.*

"Here," Charles says, and puts his hand on the side of Monty's face, and Monty puts his on Charles's. "I'm here. Let's go and get some fish."
