# Writ in Water

Pascual comes up the three flights at ten past seven in the evening on the eighth of August with one bag, four weeks brown, and does not put the bag down.

He stands in the doorway of the front room.

Reggie is at the table under the window with the second quarter's returns, and he looks up, and he has known this man's face since May of 1954, and it is doing a thing he has seen it do perhaps three times, which is nothing at all — not one single thing, held perfectly still, the way a man holds a full glass carried across a room.

"What," Reggie says.

Pascual puts the bag down then. He does it very slowly. And then he unbuttons the second and third buttons of his shirt, standing in the doorway, and takes out a packet in oilskin that has been against his ribs, Reggie will learn, for sixteen days, across the Caribbean and the Atlantic and a night train from Naples, and crosses the room and puts it on the returns in front of him, and steps back.

"It came across," he says. "From the other side. At Cedros. In a canoe. For the doctor who shouted." His voice is not steady. "I don't know what it means. I know it's yours."

---

Reggie unties the fishing line.

His hands do it perfectly well. He notices that, and it is the last thing he notices clearly for some time. He unwraps the oilskin, and there is a round tin with a screw cap, gone to rust at the rim, and he knows it before he has the oilskin off, because he bought it in the chandler's in Bridgetown in 1952 for keeping matches dry in the boat and has not thought about it since the night of the nineteenth of May 1954, when he took the matches out and put a chart in.

He unscrews the cap. He takes the chart out and unrolls it on the returns.

*Reef opens here.*

Jean drew it in the lecture hut with the door shut on the fourteenth of May, from the Admiralty sheet and her own soundings, while he stood at the window and watched the path. He told her it was for the boat. She said *yes, Reggie*, and put in the ordnance, and did not look up.

He turns it over.

---

*It worked.*

He is aware, for about half a second, of the joke — of the whole of the joke, the shape of it, the corridor and the dark and his own voice saying *I shall be quite cross with you if it doesn't work* about an old female and a Navy animal, because he could not say anything else to a boy of twenty-four he was sending into the sea; and the boy's voice, *It will work*, not sure of one single thing on this earth, and going.

And then the half-second is over and he is not aware of anything, because he has put both his forearms flat on the table on either side of the chart and his head down on them, and he is making a noise.

He does not know what the noise is. He has not made it before. It is not crying, exactly, or not only crying; it has laughing in it, and something under both that goes all the way down, and it goes on, and he cannot stop it, and does not try.

Pascual's hand comes down on the back of his neck and stays there.

---

It is some while before he can sit up. When he does the light in the window has gone from gold to red and Pascual has pulled the other chair round and is sitting in it with his knees against Reggie's knees and both hands on Reggie's forearms, and has not said anything, and Signora Bruni has knocked once on the door and gone away again, which Reggie will find out about later and be mortified by for a month.

"Tell me," Pascual says.

So Reggie tells him. The corridor. The chart. *Colonel Bevan does outrank me. I will try it once. I shall be quite cross with you if it doesn't work.* He tells it badly and out of order and has to go back twice, and Pascual listens to all of it.

"Gamma," Pascual says, at the end. "You meant Gamma."

"I meant Gamma. And the Navy animal. And whether Hester's pairing would take. I was talking about the pairing because I could not say anything about *him*." Reggie wipes his face with the heels of both hands. "And he's turned it round. He's taken a thing I said about two animals in a corridor to cover the fact that I was sending him into the sea on a chart drawn by a woman who knew exactly what it was for, and he's sent it back to me eight years later from a delta, on the back of the same chart, in two words, and it means *I got there* and it means *she's home* and it means *you were right to*, and it's a *joke*, Pascual. It's a joke. He's made a joke."

"*Ay, Dios.*" Pascual's eyes have gone. "Of course he has. Of course that boy's made a joke."

"And it worked," Reggie says. "The pairing. Gamma took the Navy animal. Gamma took *Chumley*, and she lived nearly six years with him and died of being old, and Chumley's got a mate and a calf, and I can't tell him. I can't ever tell him that it worked too."

"No," says Pascual. "You can't."

---

Later, in the dark, with the tin on the sill of the window and the chart back inside it, Pascual tells him about the jetty.

He tells all of it, flat on his back, in the voice he uses for owners, which is how Reggie knows it cost him. The net. *Did you know?* and *Yes.* The boiler. And then the rest: the four hours in the rest house, and going through it twice, in order, and putting his shoes on at four, and walking down between twenty-three men and picking up the heaviest thing on the jetty with both hands.

"On purpose," Reggie says.

"On purpose. At the right man. I made myself be sure it was that. I went through it twice." Pascual is quiet. "It worked in four seconds. It always works in four seconds. I watched it land, Reggie. I watched it go in exactly where I put it. And he got out of the boat."

Reggie lies with that.

"You said," he says slowly, "in July of 'fifty-nine, at the table, that you'd have to decide not to reach for it every time for the rest of your life."

"I know what I said."

"And you've kept it. Three years. I've watched you keep it."

"I've kept it. With you. With anybody who didn't deserve it." Pascual's hand finds his in the dark. "I didn't say I'd put it down for good. I said I'd *decide*. That's what deciding is. It means sometimes you decide the other way." A pause. "I hated it. I'm glad I did it. I hated it. I'd do it tomorrow."

Reggie turns on his side and puts his hand flat on the man's ribs, on the left, where the tin was, and leaves it there.

"Thank you," he says.

"*For what?* I shouted at a man on a jetty."

"You went," Reggie says. "When I couldn't. You went, and you asked what it was for, and then you did the worst thing you know how to do, on purpose, to the one man it would stop, and came home with that against your ribs for sixteen days. That's what for."

Pascual does not say anything for a long time. Then he turns over and puts his face against Reggie's shoulder, and his breath goes out, all of it, and after a while it goes slow.

---

The Scientific Committee meets for the second time on the nineteenth and twentieth of November, and its chairman comes from Massachusetts on the Sunday and chairs both days from the head of the long table on the Viale Aventino, perfectly, and moves the second annual schedule in four minutes, and is not warm at Tar once.

Reggie notices. He notices it on the Monday morning and again on the Monday afternoon, and on the Tuesday, over the returns, when Spilsbury looks at the tabulation for about a minute and says *thank you, Tar, that's very clear* and turns the page, he notices it a third time, and does not know what to do with it, and puts it by.

At the end of the Tuesday session, when the room is emptying, Spilsbury gathers his papers and says, without looking up:

"I'd like an hour, Tar. Not the Aventine. Somewhere nearer your flat, if there is somewhere."

---

There is somewhere. It is three hundred yards from the flat, behind the Pyramid, through a gate in a wall: the old cemetery for foreigners, with cypresses and cats and English and German and Scandinavian names on the stones going back two hundred years, where Reggie walks on Sunday mornings when Pascual is asleep, and has never brought anybody.

They go in at a quarter to five with the light going, and walk up the path between the stones, and do not say anything for some time.

At the bottom of the old part, in the grass by the wall, there is a stone without a name on it, and Spilsbury stops in front of it, because it is the kind of thing Spilsbury stops in front of, and reads it.

*Here lies One Whose Name was writ in Water.*

He reads it for a long time. Then he puts his hands in his pockets.

"You chose this," he says.

"I walk here on Sundays. I did not choose the stone."

"No. I don't suppose you did." Spilsbury does not move. "I suppose it's just a coincidence that the one Englishman in Rome with a grave that hasn't got his name on it is three hundred yards from your front door." He turns away from it. "Pobil got home all right."

"He did."

"Good. I'd like you to tell him something from me, and I'd rather you told him than I wrote it, because I'm aware of what a letter is in this business."

"Go on."

"Tell him it was very well put. And that I'd have been ruined. And that I've kept my word; I'll not go back to that jetty, or any jetty on that coast, for as long as I live." Spilsbury walks on up the path. "And tell him I'd have looked. He'll want to know I'd have looked. He'll want it to be true that he was right."

---

They walk.

The cats go ahead of them. Somewhere on the other side of the wall a tram goes by on the Via Marmorata.

"I asked Pobil a question on the jetty," Spilsbury says. "He wouldn't answer it. He said *that's his*. So I'm asking you." He stops under a cypress. "What did you say to him? In the flat, on the Wednesday night in May. Before you came up the hill and refused me."

Reggie stands on the path.

He has thought about this. He thought about it in August, in the dark, with the tin on the sill and his hand on Pascual's ribs; he thought about it every morning of the committee when Spilsbury was not warm at him; he thought about it walking through the gate. He has been waiting since May, he realises, standing there, for Spilsbury to ask him — and knowing that when he did, he would answer.

Because it is the one thing he has that Spilsbury cannot use.

---

"I told him I wanted it," Reggie says.

Spilsbury looks at him.

"That is the arrangement. That is the whole of it. I have been keeping it since February of 1959 and I will keep it until one of us is dead. When I want a thing I ought not to have — a result, a door, a man's warmth across a table, a roof for a boy in a delta — I go home, on the day, and I stand in the doorway, and I say so. Out loud. Going red. I say *I want it*, and I say why, and I say the parts I am ashamed of. And he listens to all of it. And then he asks me what it is for.

"And sometimes he tells me to go. And sometimes he tells me what it costs, and it is somebody else who pays. And once, in November of 'fifty-eight, he told me to do my own arithmetic, and he was right, and it was the cruellest thing anybody has ever said to me.

"On the Wednesday night in May I stood under that window and told him I wanted Charles to have a roof and a doctor and nobody able to move him, and that there was not one piece of the want I was ashamed of, and that it was the worst want I had had in eight years. And he said *He packed a knapsack.* And I stopped.

"That is what I said to Pobil. You may have all of it. It is no use to you whatever."

---

The light is nearly gone. A cat comes and sits on a stone beside them and washes.

Spilsbury does not say anything for a long time.

"That's all," he says at last.

"That's all."

"You go home and tell a man what you want." He says it slowly, as though he is reading it back off a card. "So that he can stop you."

"So that he can ask me what it's for. Which, as it happens, usually stops me. Not always. Not always in time."

"*Why?*"

And there it is: the thing on his face that Reggie saw on the Aventine, and that Pascual says he saw on the jetty, and that Hester Bevan, by her own account, saw at the rail in June of 1960. Not a question a man asks for use. Real, and baffled, and faintly indignant, like a man who has been shown a machine that works and cannot find the motor.

"Why in God's name would a man *arrange* to be stopped?"

"Because I cannot stop by myself," Reggie says. "You know that. You've known it since 1946. You built a department on it. You walked into the lab in 1946 and saw a boy of twenty-seven who could not leave a result alone if the building was on fire, and you gave him results, and he took them, and he was very good. And I'd have gone on taking them. I'd have taken your four sentences. I did take your four sentences, in November of 'fifty-eight, on a verandah, on my own, without going home, and you went up the path pleased, and it was the one night in five years I did not go home and say it, and I paid for it for nine weeks and so did he."

He puts his hands behind his back.

"And I'd have taken Charles. Not the way you'd have taken him. With a roof and a doctor. I'd have been so kind about it. And I'd have got it wrong in exactly the same place."

---

Spilsbury looks at him in the near-dark under the cypress.

"I'd find it intolerable," he says.

"I know. That is the difference between us. It is the only one that has ever mattered."

"I'd rather be wrong on my own."

"I know you would. You always have been. You are very good at it." Reggie hears his own voice, level, not unkind. "That's why you'll never understand it. You looked at the verandah in 'fifty-eight and saw a man come out from under. You were looking at the worst mistake I have made since the corridor, and you went up that path thinking well of me."

---

Spilsbury takes his hands out of his pockets and puts them back in.

"And you put me in charge of the committee," he says, "and yourself in charge of the Commission, and I got the whole field its schedule and its returns and its closed waters, the way I wanted it, and it closed the one water I wanted open against *me*. With my own unanimity clause. And I sat and moved it." He laughs, very short. "And then I went to Cedros, where there was nothing on earth to stop me but a man with a badge I didn't know you'd written into the terms. I thought I'd got the brake off you in 'fifty-eight. I thought the paper was the last thing Pobil would ever be able to put his hand on."

"Yes."

"And it isn't a thing he does to papers."

"No," says Reggie. "It isn't."

---

They walk back down to the gate in the dark.

At the gate, under the lamp, Spilsbury stops and takes out his wallet, and out of the wallet a card, and a pencil; and Reggie watches him write on it in the lamplight, quickly, in the fast clear hand, and does not try to read it.

Spilsbury looks at what he has written for a while.

Then he puts the card back in the wallet, and the wallet back in his coat.

"You've given me something true," he says, "that I can't use."

"Yes."

"That's the second time in my life." He buttons his coat. "The first was a technician at a rail in 1958 saying he was sorry. I've never been able to do anything with that either." He puts out his hand, which he has not done since November of 1957, and Reggie takes it. "Good night, Tar. My regards to Pobil. You needn't tell him the last part."

"I tell him all of it," Reggie says. "That's the arrangement."

"So it is," says Spilsbury, and goes off along the Via Marmorata towards the river, and does not look back.

---

He goes home. It is three hundred yards.

He goes up the three flights and Pascual is at the stove, and turns round, and looks at him, and puts the spoon down.

"Sit down."

"I'd rather stand."

"*Ay.* Then stand." Pascual wipes his hands. "Go on."

"He wasn't warm at me," Reggie says. "Two days. Not once. I noticed it every time. I don't know what that is yet." He is looking at the window, where the tin is on the sill. "And then he asked me what I said to you in May. In the cemetery. By Keats. And I told him."

Pascual goes very still.

"All of it?"

"All of it. That I come home and say I want it. That you ask what it's for." Reggie takes his glasses off. "It's no use to him. He stood under a cypress and asked me why a man would arrange to be stopped, as though I'd shown him a machine with no motor. And he wrote a card at the gate. And he said I'd given him something true he couldn't use, and that it was the second time in his life, and the first was Nestor."

"*Nestor.*"

"At the rail. In 'fifty-eight. Saying he was sorry."

Pascual stands at the stove for a moment. Then he comes round it, and wipes his hands again on the cloth, which he does not need to do, and puts one on the side of Reggie's face.

"You gave him the one thing," he says. "The only thing we've got he can't take off us. You just handed it to him in a graveyard."

"Yes."

"*Why?*"

"Because it was mine to give," says Reggie, "and he's never going to be able to pick it up," and puts his own hand up to answer.
