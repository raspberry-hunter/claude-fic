The room has a wall, and the wall can be found.

He finds it on the second morning, because he is a scientist and because there is nothing else to do. He wades downstream from the mangrove elbow where they have settled, counting paces in the mud, with Monty holding station behind him and doing nothing but exist; and he goes until it stops.

Nine paces. Perhaps ten. Somewhere between the fourth root-clump and the fifth, everything Monty is goes out like a lamp -- not entirely, there is still the wash of him, presence and heat and direction, the thing that carries across any amount of water -- but the pictures stop, cleanly, over the space of about two feet, and Charles stands there in the brown water moving his own head back and forth across the boundary like a man finding the edge of a doorway in the dark.

He goes back and does it upstream, where the river runs fresher. Fourteen paces there. He does it at slack water and again on the flood, when the sea pushes salt up the channel, and gets eight, and understands that the room breathes with the tide.

Then he sits down on a root with his feet in the water and works out what he has just measured, which is a conductivity gradient, and what it means, which is that for six or seven days he and Monty are going to have somewhere to be.

Nine paces. Twenty-seven feet of the world in which the two of them can talk without touching.

It is the only room they will ever have. He is aware of this while it is happening, which is not usual for him, and does not spoil it, which is also not usual.

He measures the pictures and does not measure the floor. It is there in the room and it is there at nine paces and it is there at fourteen and it does not fail anywhere he can wade to, and he decides -- without putting it to himself as a decision -- that a thing which is always on and never changes is not data. He will think about that afternoon for thirty years.

<hr>

The first day he mostly drinks and sleeps.

He drinks about a gallon and a half, in stages, over the first afternoon, and is sick once, and has to make himself go slowly after that. Somewhere in there he stops feeling as though there is a bar of iron across the back of his head. He had not registered that there had been a bar of iron across the back of his head for six days.

He sleeps in the shade at the edge, on a bank of dry leaves above a mangrove root, in the loose foetal shape of a man who has been in a harness. He wakes at about four in the afternoon and does not know where he is, or when, and lies looking up through the leaves at bars of moving light and hears water going past very close, and thinks, with total simplicity, *oh, I am somewhere good* -- and then knows the rest of it, and the knowing does not take the goodness away.

That is new. He turns it over for a while.

Monty is asleep in three feet of water eight feet off, on his side against the bank, out to the world.

<hr>

He takes the harness off on the first night and does not put it on again for six days.

The lines under his arms have gone the colour of raw liver and the one across the breastbone has begun to weep. He does what the woman on Terre-de-Haut told him: soft cloth, two thicknesses, the yellow grease, before and not after. And then he does the thing she could not tell him to do, because it is not available to a man who has to be in the sea by seven in the morning, which is nothing. He stays out of the water for two whole days and lets them dry.

They dry. It is astonishing. On the third morning there is new skin on the breastbone, thin and shiny and stupidly precious, and he sits with his shirt open looking at it like a man reading a letter from home.

The rest of the audit is less good and he does it anyway, because he has always done it, because he is the sort of person who cannot let a thing go unmeasured even when the measuring is only a way of standing next to something intolerable.

He has lost, at a guess, a stone. His hands have not come back and he does not think they are going to; the small muscles between the fingers have simply gone. There is a patch of something fungal starting under his left arm. And the other thing goes on happening, a dozen times a day now, and at night, so that he wakes soaked in a sweat he cannot spare and lies in the dark doing the loss in litres because the arithmetic is easier to hold than the fact.

There is a hospital in Roseau.

It comes to him on the third day, unbidden, while he is filling a gourd: a colonial hospital, on this island, thirty miles down the leeward road, with a dispensary in it. Two days' walk. Perhaps a lorry.

He works it through properly, because it deserves that. A white Englishman with no passport and no papers and no letter, walking into a colonial dispensary in September 1954 and asking a doctor for testosterone ampoules -- what happens next is not a refusal. What happens next is a conversation, and then a form, and then a name in a ledger, and then, in four weeks or seven, a man in Roseau saying to another man over a drink, *funny thing, we had an Englishman in, off a boat apparently* -- and Spilsbury has a chart too, and Spilsbury can read a chart exactly as well as Jean can.

So: no.

He puts the stopper in the gourd. He notices, with a detachment that frightens him slightly, that the decision has cost him almost nothing, and that this is because he made it in June at a chest of drawers and has only now got round to informing himself.

<hr>

In the room, they talk about everything, and none of it can be said.

That is the shape of it and it takes him days to stop fighting it. He has twenty-seven feet and six days and he wants to *use* them, and what he wants to use them for is the thing they cannot do: he wants to explain. He wants to give an account of himself. He wants to say *Reggie sent me and I don't know why*, and *I am not going to be able to eat what you eat*, and *there is a man coming to the island who has a laboratory in England and I have seen his name on a paper about dogs*, and none of that will go, none of it, it is all verbs and names and next weeks, and the channel does not carry next week.

What the channel carries is this.

He learns that Monty can find the pale mud crabs in the bank by holding still, and that they are not worth the trouble, and that Monty eats them anyway, in the manner of a man eating peanuts.

<i>-- the bank at four feet, and in it, at a dozen separate places, a small stupid buried spark that does not know it is a spark; the pale one comes out sideways and is nothing, is a mouthful of shell and water, is not worth the arm --</i>

He learns that the big silver fish that come up on the flood are worth the trouble. He learns what a barracuda looks like from underneath and what one feels like from forty feet:

<i>-- a lit line, laid along the water, holding absolutely still in a place where everything else is moving; and around it, for two body-lengths in every direction, a quietness that is not quiet; and *not now* --</i>

And the storm comes back on the fourth afternoon, with his back against a root and his eyes shut, and this time it comes slowly.

He has had the storm. He had it in the flood, at the wedding, in the middle of nine other things, arriving all at once and none of it separable: the roof of the world turning to noise, the two little ones going down, the surge. What he has never had is the hour it takes. Monty gives it to him piece by piece across a whole afternoon, in order, with the waiting in it -- the way the light changed first, and the way you go down under it and take hold of the bottom and stay there, and how long that is, and how long that is, and how long that is -- and how the little ones went down and he did not, because he wanted to see it.

He still does not learn why he wanted to see it. There is no why available and there never will be. But he receives, quite clearly, that Monty *knows* he wanted to see it, and that this is a thing about himself that Monty has looked at more than once.

And he gives back what he has. His sisters, all three, and the noise of the house. A cold beach in Fife with a grey sea on it that Monty examines with open horror. The smell of the mess hall. Jean at the drawing board with her wrist across the page. Gamma, once, by accident, before he can stop it -- and Monty takes that and turns it over and goes very still, and gives back a thing Charles cannot read at all, and neither of them tries again.

And on the fifth night, lying in eight inches of water at the edge with the fireflies going in the mangrove and his hand on Monty's shoulder because he can, he tries the only enormous thing he has any hope of getting across.

He puts up the picture of this. Exactly this: the elbow of the river, the roots, the fireflies, the two of them. And then, as hard as he can, the sun coming up on it, and then again, and then again, and again, and again --

*Stay.*

He gets it out. He knows he gets it out, because Monty receives it and considers it with the whole of his attention.

And what comes back is not refusal. Charles has braced for refusal and could have borne refusal. What comes back is the brown water again, the heavy silt, the iron and the leaf-mould, the taste of it, and hanging in it a whole scatter of nervous systems at forty and eighty and a hundred and twenty feet, more than Charles has any way of counting, all of them lit and separate and *known*, and one of them is an old woman and one of them is a boy who cannot possibly still be a boy --

and Charles understands that he has just asked a man whether he would like to live in a very nice cupboard.

He takes his hand off. He does that first, before anything can be read off him, which is cowardice and he knows it and does it anyway.

Then he lies on his back in eight inches of warm water and looks at the fireflies and rearranges everything.

He has been thinking of this as their journey. It is not. It has never for one second been that. It is Monty going home, which Monty would be doing if Charles had drowned off Antigua on the first night, which Monty was doing before Charles was born, in the sense that his whole body was built by five million years of going home. Charles is not a fellow traveller. Charles is a thing that is happening to Monty on the way, and that Monty loves, and that Monty would carry to Trinidad on his back and would not, in any world, turn round for.

The right response to this is grief and he cannot find any. What he finds, lying in the shallows, is something closer to relief, and it takes him most of the night to get its shape, and the shape is: *then I don't have to decide anything ever again.*

He puts his hand back. Monty is already asleep. Monty has been asleep for some time, all the way down, the deep half-lit quiet of a creature with somebody watching, and he has been doing it every night since Guadeloupe, and he is doing it now, forty feet from a village, in a river, with a man's hand on him.

<hr>

On the fourth day he pays.

It happens on the beach at the bottom of the bay, in the afternoon, and it is not arranged and nobody calls it anything.

They have been feeding him for three days by then -- Portsmouth knew before he came round the Cabrits; a Saintes boat had gone through on the Tuesday and a Roseau schooner on the Wednesday, and the story has stopped being news and become a fact, which is worse. There are always fish left at the top of the river mouth in the morning. Nobody has asked him for anything.

So he goes down to the beach where the canoes are. There is one up on trestles with its bottom turned to the sun, and four men going over it -- scraping, and a pot of pitch going on a fire of coconut husk that smells like the inside of a chimney, and a great deal of unhurried argument about a plank. Charles sits down on the sand at the end of all this, out of the way, because there is nothing on that hull his hands could do.

After a while one of them says something to him about the weather, and Charles answers, and there is a silence, and then a man of about sixty who has been stirring the pot and has not spoken at all says:

"They keep them in a tank, then."

"Yes," Charles says.

And then he tells them.

He tells them the size of the pen. He tells them it is concrete, and grounded steel, and that the water is pumped in from the bay and pumped out again, so that it is the same sea and it is not the sea at all. He tells them that a merman in a pen cannot feel anything outside the walls of it, and cannot be felt, and that this is not loneliness in the way a man is lonely -- it is nearer to being buried.

He uses the word without thinking about it and then hears himself use it. At the Martin it was *mermaid*, always, in every mouth, in the minutes and the log and the funding letters and out of Reggie, who is the most precise man Charles has ever met: a female English word for a nineteen-foot male animal with a scar on his flank, and seven months of Charles saying the other one in a flat voice at supper until people stopped correcting him and started letting him have it, the way you let a man have a superstition.

Nobody on this beach has to be told. It has not once occurred to any of them to call him anything else. He tells them that they do not sleep in there. Not badly. Not lightly. That they stop, entirely, and go on stopping for five months, and then die of pneumonia, and the record says *no apparent cause*.

He tells them that he kept the log.

He tells them what is in it. *Specimen inactive. No response observed.* In his own hand, in his own capitals, on ruled paper, dated, initialled, for weeks -- while it screamed, continuously, into a dead line, eight feet from where he was standing writing that down.

He tells them the whole of it. Nobody interrupts him. One man gets up in the middle and walks off down the beach and stands with his back to them and comes back.

When he has finished, the sixty-year-old man stirs the pot for a while.

Then he says: "My grandfather could call them."

He does not say it as a marvel; he says it the way a man says his grandfather was a cooper. They drove the fish into the shallows -- the bay is wrong now, there is too much boat traffic, but it was right then -- and the men stood in the water and cast, and there was a share, and the share was not a payment, it was the arrangement. Everyone ate. His grandfather had a knock he did on the side of the canoe. He was shown it once and cannot do it and has never met anyone who can.

"And it stopped," Charles says.

"It stopped."

"Why?"

The man lifts the stick out of the pitch and watches it run off the end of it. "You know why," he says. "You came from where they stopped."

That is the whole of it. That is the entire transaction, and it costs Charles more than the eleven days at sea did, and he sits on the sand for a long time afterwards with his hands hanging between his knees, and no one looks at him, and after a while somebody puts a tin cup of something down beside him in the sand and walks away.

He goes back up the river at dusk and gets into the water and puts his hand on Monty and tries to give him some of it, and it does not go. Of course it does not go. There is no picture of a debt.

What Monty knows -- all that Monty knows, all that Monty will ever know -- is that since the reef the sea has begun, for no reason he can perceive, to put fish where he is going to be.

<hr>

The man from the windward side arrives on the fifth day, having walked.

He comes down out of the track above the river in the early morning with a cutlass and a bag, and he is not from Portsmouth, and the boy who brings him down says a word Charles does not catch and goes away fast. The man is perhaps forty-five. He sits down on a root eight feet from Charles without being asked and looks at the river, and not at the river mouth, and says: "Salybia."

Charles knows what that means. Everybody in the Windwards knows what that means. The other side of the island, the wet windward coast, the last piece of ground in the whole of the Antilles that was never taken off the people who were on it.

"You walked over the mountain," Charles says.

"Since yesterday." He opens the bag and takes out a wrapped parcel of something and puts it on the root between them and does not offer it, and Charles understands that it is not a gift, it is lunch, and that he is being allowed to watch a man eat, which on this island is a considerable act of trust.

They sit for a while. Charles has learned enough by now not to fill it.

"Our people kept it up longest," the man says eventually. "On our side. When it stopped everywhere else, we still had it." He tears the parcel open with his thumb. "Nineteen and thirty-one was the last time anybody saw one of them from our beach. My uncle. He is dead now. He was not a liar and everybody said he was a liar."

"I believe him," Charles says.

"Yes," the man says. "That is why I walked."

He eats. Charles sits. Somewhere behind them in the mangrove a heron goes up.

"When you get there," the man says, "when he goes to his people, and they ask him who took him -- what will he say?"

Charles opens his mouth and finds that there is nothing in it.

Because he has been thinking about the arrival since the reef and every single thing he has thought about it has been about *getting* there. He has thought about the current in the Bocas and the last eighty-five miles and whether his kidneys will do it. He has thought, once or twice, shamefully, about being introduced. He has not thought for one second about the fact that Monty is going to arrive carrying a complete, unfakeable, unarguable account of nine months in a concrete box, and that he cannot lie, and that his people cannot fail to read it, and that among the things he is carrying is Charles.

"I don't know," he says.

"No," the man agrees, unsurprised, and goes back to his lunch.

Later, when he has gone -- and he leaves without any ceremony, up the same track, having accepted nothing and asked for nothing -- Charles will realise that the question was not a challenge. It was a warning delivered by a man whose family kept faith with something for three hundred years and got called liars for it, and who has walked twenty miles over a mountain to tell an Englishman, once, kindly, that he is not the end of this story and had better stop behaving as though he is.

<hr>

They go on the seventh morning.

It is not weather that decides it and it is not the people. It is the fish. Monty has been eating steadily for six days and there is flesh back over the ribs and the shoulders have come up, and on the sixth evening Charles, lying against him in the shallows, feels something he has not felt since the reef, which is Monty being *restless* -- a low continuous hum of orientation, southward, on and on, like a man jingling coins in his pocket.

They have both known the date all week. Neither of them has any way of raising it.

So Charles raises it the only way available: he gets the harness out at dusk on the sixth day and greases it and lays it on the root where Monty can see it, and goes to sleep, and in the morning he puts it on.

The new skin on his breastbone goes in the first ten minutes.

He fills the breaker and both gourds and the canvas bottle. He greases everything of himself that can be greased. There are four fish waiting at the river mouth in a basket that is not his and he takes it and leaves the basket. Nobody is on the beach. Nobody watches them go; the etiquette has reached Dominica ahead of him and by now it is fully assembled and he is grateful for it and it makes his throat ache.

They go down the river on the ebb, which is slack and easy, and the room comes with them the whole way, and Charles talks the entire distance -- out loud, in English, into the air, about nothing at all: the Cabrits, the herons, Jean's handwriting, what he is going to do about his shoes. Monty rides alongside him ten feet off in the brown water, listening to the wash of it and receiving perhaps a third and not caring in the least about the other two thirds.

Then the mangrove opens out and the fan is ahead of them, the great brown pour of the river sitting on the sea with the visible line round the edge of it, and beyond the line the water goes from brown to green to blue in about a hundred yards.

Charles has been ready for this since Tuesday and it turns out that he is not ready for it at all.

At the line he can still hear him at nine paces. Then eight. He knows this coming, he has measured it, he did the work himself with his feet in the mud like an idiot, like a man building his own gallows out of curiosity --

-- six --

-- four --

He gets his arms round Monty's shoulders and his face down against his back before it can finish, and hangs on, and the room shuts behind them like a door in a wind, and he is inside it, and it is two inches wide now and made of skin, and it will be two inches wide for the next three hundred miles.

Behind them the island stands up out of the sea with the cloud dragging off it, wet, green-black, streaming with rivers, going away.
