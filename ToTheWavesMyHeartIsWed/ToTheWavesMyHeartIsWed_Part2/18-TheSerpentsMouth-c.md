There are four months first and he can give no proper account of them.

He is in the water at Carenage for nine days after the mouths, or eleven; he does not count them, and it is the only stretch of his adult life he has not counted. Monty is there for the first part of it and then is not, and then is. Charles drinks, and is sick, and drinks, and stops being sick, and on some morning in the middle of it discovers that the colour has gone out of it and sits down on the concrete plinth with his hands hanging between his knees for rather a long time.

After that he walks, and he never once walks out of sight of the gulf.

He gets to Port of Spain in November and sleeps in a shed at the back of a cocoa warehouse and does work there he never afterwards describes to anybody, and inside a fortnight he has the two facts that will govern the next three years: that a white man with an English voice and no papers is not challenged, and that a white man with an English voice and no papers cannot ask anybody for anything.

Which settles the other question, and settles it in December, on the pavement opposite the colonial hospital, where he stands for the better part of an hour doing the same sum he did on the third day at the Indian River and getting the same answer, and then walks on. He does not fill it again. It goes on arriving on its clock for another six years or seven, and then comes further apart, and then thins, and then stops, in the ordinary way, the way it does; and by the time he notices it has gone he cannot say what month it went.

He goes down the island in January, because the road goes down the island.

<hr>

He gets a job in February, which is later than he would like and sooner than it had any business being.

It is at the far south-west end of the island, past Cedros, where the road stops being a road and the coconut estates run down to a grey beach and the water is so full of the Orinoco that it is brown out to the horizon. Ten miles across is Venezuela. The channel between is called the Serpent's Mouth, which he finds, on the whole, an improvement on the other one.

The job is that he counts fish.

There is a fisheries officer in Port of Spain who has money from London for a survey of the Gulf and no one willing to sit at the bottom end of the island doing the tedious part of it, and there is a man at Icacos with no papers, no boat, an accent, ruined hands and a great deal of biology, who will do it for very little and does not want to be written about. They come to an arrangement of the kind that was possible in 1955 and is not possible now.

So five mornings a week he sits on a box at the landing with a ledger on his knee and writes down what comes out of the sea. Species, count, weight, length, gear, hours fished, where. In his own hand. In his own capitals.

He is aware of what he is doing. He is aware of it the first morning, sitting there with a pencil in fingers that will not properly close on it, looking at a ruled page with a date at the top; and he sits with it for a while, and then rules the columns and starts.

The difference, he decides, is not that this one is kind. It is that this one is going to be true.

<hr>

The arrangement they arrive at is not romantic and takes about six weeks to settle, and once it settles it lasts thirty years.

He lives in one room at the back of a shop with a shutter that props open on a stick. He is in the water every evening and most mornings before the boats, off the point, where the mud shelves out so gently that he can walk three hundred yards and still put his feet down. He never swims out of his depth again as long as he lives. That is not fear; it is arithmetic, and Monty concurs in it, in the way of a man who has seen a thing nearly happen once.

Monty is in the Gulf. That is the whole of the answer and Charles stops needing a better one by about April. He is with his people, forty miles of brown water and a river system the size of a country, and he comes to Icacos, and the intervals are his own business. Four days. Nine. Once, in the first year, nineteen, and Charles goes to work every one of those mornings and rules the columns and does not write anything in the margins.

He is there again on the twentieth day.

Charles counts it, because he counts. Nineteen against the twelve days he was away in the summer, which he can put a figure to because he put a figure to it at the time, lying on a sleeping pad beside the wrong pen with his hands over his face. He sits with the two numbers on the step of the shop for the better part of a week and does not care for either of them.

What he arrives at, eventually, is that the days were never the injury. Twelve days in a concrete box with nothing else inside it is not the same quantity as nineteen days in forty miles of brown water with several hundred people in it, whatever the two figures look like set down side by side; the box was the term, and the days were only the units it happened to be counted in. And on his own side of the nineteen there had been a ledger, and a village, and four letters to write for other people, and the water at the end of every afternoon.

He does not enjoy getting there. Something comes off him in the getting there, and what comes off is the last of being necessary. But he gets there; and some months after that he notices that he has stopped minding, and that it is not resignation, and that somebody who arrives is a better thing to have than somebody who is due.

He keeps his own promise, in the sense that he is in the water every evening for thirty years. It is not much of a keeping. He would be in the water every evening if he had never opened his mouth in that pen, and he has never been in the least certain that any of it reached the other end -- he said it out loud, in English, in July, into a channel he did not then know the first thing about. He does not raise it. There is nothing to raise it with, and nothing to raise.

Monty made no promise and keeps none. What Monty has instead does not require keeping, and is the reason he comes back, and costs him nothing whatever to be nineteen days about.

There is no arrangement about it because there is no way to make one. There is no picture of Thursday. What there is instead is a man who turns up.

<hr>

The water is the point and everything else is what he does between times.

But he does do the everything else, and it surprises him how much of it there is. He learns enough Trinidadian French Creole in two years to argue in it, which he could not manage in French in eleven. He is godfather twice, on paper, in a church, having explained his position and been told it did not signify. He is the man in the village who reads letters for people and writes the replies, which he does badly at first and then well, and it is the only use his hands find that they are good enough for.

And in the evenings, for thirty years, he keeps the other notebooks.

They are not the ledger. The ledger belongs to the Fisheries Division and is dull and honest and full of snapper. The notebooks are his, and he starts the first one in June 1955 with a page ruled into four columns, and at the top of the columns he writes: *What came. Circumstance. What followed. What I supplied.*

That last column is the whole discipline of it and it is the only original methodological contribution he ever makes to anything. He does not trust himself. He knows exactly what he is -- a man who once heard the word *please* in a channel that cannot carry it -- and so every entry has a column for what he put in, and he fills it honestly, and by the fourth notebook the column is doing more work than the other three.

The sounds were never the difficulty. Reggie had the sounds off a tape in a hut on Crabbs Cay and published them, and the paper is not wrong; it is a list of noises with nothing underneath it. What Charles has is the half that cannot be got at with a microphone and that nobody at the Martin was ever in a position to collect, which is what arrived, and what was going on when it arrived, and what happened next.

He gets to forty-one. Not words: he is careful about that, and there is a page at the front of every volume saying so, and by the third volume the page is two pages long and is rather bitter. Forty-one recurrences in eighteen years -- things that came more than once, in circumstances alike enough to be set beside one another -- with the number of times observed and the number of times he was wrong.

He checks them against Reggie's list in 1961, having asked Jean for the offprint and been sent it by return. Item four survives. It is the only one that does, and he sits with the sheet open on the table for some time: a man with his finger on a reel saying *there, that's the interesting one*, and being right, once, about the one that means danger and means nothing else.

Nobody reads them. Nobody is going to read them. He knows that from the beginning and it is why the column is honest.

<hr>

The newspaper comes in September of that first year, a week late, as everything does at Icacos.

A hurricane went over Grenada in the dark on the twenty-second and there is a four-line paragraph about it, and a longer one the following week with a figure in it. Five hundred, they think. Carriacou too.

He reads it sitting on the step of the shop.

He thinks about three men on a beach in the early morning telling him, cheerfully, unprompted, that the storms go north of Grenada, that they always have, that he was not to worry about that at least; and about the six-gallon cask he asked them not to give him; and he puts the paper down on the step and sits there for a long time with his hands hanging between his knees, which is a posture he has been in once before on a beach at Portsmouth.

Then he goes back in and rules the columns, because it is Tuesday.

<hr>

Jean's letter comes in the spring of 1956 and it has taken her a year and a half.

She found him by doing the obvious thing, which is that she had a copy of her own chart. She wrote to the Fisheries Division in Port of Spain in December 1955 asking, in an entirely professional voice, whether anyone in the Gulf survey had any use for a marine illustrator; and got a courteous no; and asked, in the second paragraph, after a former colleague she believed might be in the district.

The letter runs to nine pages and he reads it four times before he can start on a reply.

Spilsbury came, and there was nothing to see. That is the first and best thing in it. He arrived on a Thursday to a station with one merman in it and a junior researcher unaccountably on leave, and by the Saturday everyone concerned had understood that the useful animal was gone and that nobody was going to be able to say where. He interviewed all of them. Jean's account of her own interview is one paragraph and Charles reads it several times and thinks that if he had a hat he would take it off.

Pascual he leaned on hardest, because Pascual was the American, and because it is much easier to lean on a man whose passport you can affect. And Pascual, Jean writes, was magnificent, by which she means that he declined to say a single thing he could not support. He would not say the specimen had been taken. He would not say it had escaped. He would not say what he thought Charles had done, because he did not know what Charles had done, and he said so, repeatedly, in the same even voice, for three days, until Spilsbury gave up in disgust and wrote him down as obstructive. He left in the autumn for a post in San Juan, and is doing well.

Reggie is finished. Not disgraced -- there was nothing to disgrace him with -- but the Navy money went, and the Institute's second grant went, and the name Martin stopped opening doors. He is in England now, teaching, at a school Charles has never heard of and cannot afterwards produce the name of twice running: a small place, by Jean's account, two hundred boys and a leaking chapel, of the sort that takes a man with letters after his name and does not ask why he is going begging.

*He asked after you exactly once*, Jean writes, *in a corridor, in November, with nobody about. He said: "Is there any news of our friend." He did not say which friend and I did not ask him to, and I said no, and he nodded and went into his office and shut the door. That is the whole of it and I have thought about it a great deal.*

John dines out on it. That is the thing Charles laughs at, alone, in a room in Icacos, and then stops laughing at. John tells the story at dinner parties -- the merman, the mad Scot, the night swim -- and tells it well, and has told it for two years, and nobody believes a word of it. He is becoming known for it. He is becoming a man with a story.

*He will be an old man one day*, Jean writes, *telling the truth at a table, and everyone will be very kind to him.*

<hr>

And Gamma is alive.

Hester made Reggie promise it in July and Reggie kept the promise in October, because Reggie keeps promises, and because by then he had nothing left to lose by trying it. He put Gamma and the new one in the same pen.

It worked immediately. Of course it worked immediately.

The feeding came back inside four days, and the weight, and the thing Reggie recorded as *activity* and Charles could give a better name to; and Reggie wrote it up properly and published it in 1956, in a good journal, under a title about the husbandry of captive marine mammals, and it is the most useful paper he ever writes and the only one anybody remembers.

It does not say why he tried it. There is no version of that paper which says why he tried it.

Charles sits with that for a week.

Because it is the best news in the letter and he can already see the other end of it, and so, he suspects, can Reggie, and neither of them will ever write it down. A merman in a pen alone dies in five months and the trade is not worth anybody's while. A merman in a pen with company lives, and can be shown, and can be studied, and can be sold.

They have made it possible to keep them.

*I know what you will think about this*, Jean writes, on the eighth page, having got there without being told, as usual, first, uncredited. *I thought it for a month. I have decided that I would rather Gamma were alive.*

<hr>

He writes back at enormous length and most of it is not worth keeping, and one paragraph is.

He thanks her for the chart.

He tells her that he had the hand from the first night, out past the reef, before he was in any condition to be reading anything; that under a lamp in a shed at Terre-de-Haut he unrolled the whole of it at last and saw how far south it went; that he knew the pencil ring round the north end of Dominica was hers before he had read what was inside it; that the note said *best rest on the whole road* and that it was, and that they had six days there, and that it is the single reason either of them is alive. He tells her he counted, sitting in that shed, and that it was the third time -- the plates, the tables, this -- and that he has been carrying that number about with him ever since and would like to set it down.

*You have never once been thanked in print*, he writes, *and I cannot do it in print, and this is the only place I have.*

She does not answer that part. She answers every other part of the letter, at length, twice a year, for twenty-eight years.

<hr>

The papers come in 1957 in an envelope with a Colonial Office franking on it and no covering letter.

He never establishes what was done or who did it or what it cost, and the one time he asks, obliquely, in a letter, he gets back three lines about the garden. What he can work out is that it required somebody with a Colonel for a husband and a great deal of patience, and that it took two and a half years, and that at no point did anybody write down the interesting reason.

He is, from 1957, a man with a name and a national status and a right to be where he is, and the name is nearly his own.

<hr>

They come out in 1959, after the Colonel retires, on a banana boat, ostensibly to see the island.

Charles meets them at the landing at Cedros and does not recognise her for a moment, because he has been remembering a woman in an office.

She is seventy-one. She gets down the beach on her husband's arm and then declines the arm, and she stands at the edge of the water in a bathing dress that must have taken some arranging and looks out at forty miles of brown water for a while without saying anything at all.

"Well," she says. "It's not a pen, is it."

The Colonel sits up at the top of the beach in a chair with a newspaper and does not come down, and does not watch, and is entirely aware of everything that is happening, and it is the single most tactful act Charles ever sees a man perform.

She walks out. It takes a while. At two hundred yards it is still only up to her waist and Charles is beside her the whole way with a hand out that she does not take.

And then Monty comes up beside her, and she puts her hand on him.

<hr>

She makes a noise Charles never forgets and cannot describe and does not try to write down.

Then she stands there in the Gulf of Paria for eleven minutes with her hand flat on a merman's shoulder and her face working, and Charles stands eight feet off in the brown water and gives her the whole of it and does not come any closer.

He knows what she is getting. He knows precisely what she is getting, because it is what he had for eleven days on the ledge before he ever got in: the wash of somebody, and no pictures in it at all, a hand on a shoulder being the coarsest channel there is. What he has forgotten -- what he has never once had to think about, having only ever had the one -- is that a wash has a pitch to it, and that a woman who spent three years learning one of them by heart can tell them apart across twenty-eight years without any working at all. And he knows what she has not had since 1931.

When she comes out she is perfectly composed. She dries her hands on a towel and sits down on the sand next to him, seventy-one years old, and says:

"His is lower than Tom's."

"Yes?"

"Tom was a click and a hum. Quite high, the hum. This one is nearly under the floor." She looks out at the water. "I should have known that, I suppose. I never had two to compare."

"Neither did I," Charles says. "Until October."

And she nods, and that is the entire conversation about the most extraordinary thing either of them will ever do, and Charles thinks it is probably the only honest length for it.

<hr>

It is on the third day that he tells her about the old woman, and about the word.

He tells it badly. He has never said any of it out loud to anybody -- there has been nobody -- and it comes out in the wrong order, and he gets to the end of it and finds he has stopped somewhere short and cannot get the rest out, and Hester waits, and he says: "She had a word for me. She said it perhaps forty times. I know what it means. I worked it out that afternoon."

"Yes," Hester says. "You would have."

"Do you know it?"

"I know it," she says.

She is quiet for a bit.

"Tom used it about me," she says. "From about 1928. I was forty, and I did not understand it for two years, and then I did. It went on being what he called me, and it was not an insult, and it was not a diminishment, and by the end I was rather proud of it." She turns her hands over in her lap and looks at them. "It means the one who has finished with that and knows things. It is the word for the old women. In their world it is nearly the only word for anybody."

"It is also the word for a woman," Charles says.

"It is the word for a *body*," Hester says. "They only have bodies. They have never needed anything else, and so they have never built anything else, and now here we are." And then, in a completely different voice, one he has not heard from her before: "You will not get out from under it, my dear. Not with them and not here. Not ever. And he will go on saying it for you until he dies, and it will not work, and that is not the same as it being nothing."

Charles does not answer.

"Does he know you know what she called you?"

"No."

"No," says Hester. "Tom never knew I had worked mine out, either. There was a year in which I could have told him, and I did not, and I have never once been able to say why."

<hr>

They go home in April and she writes every month and dies in 1967, and the Colonel, who outlives her by four years and who never once discusses any of it, sends Charles a small parcel afterwards with no letter in it, containing a notebook in Hester's hand covering the years 1926 to 1931 which he has clearly never opened.

<hr>

Jean leaves the Martin in the spring of 1957 and takes a post at a marine station on the Firth of Clyde, and does forty years of good, patient, entirely unglamorous work on the reproductive cycle of an animal nobody has ever made a film about.

From about 1960 she is credited. Her name goes on the plates, under them, in the proper place, in italics, and by 1974 she is the person you write to if you want a decapod drawn correctly, and there is an obituary in 1998 which describes her as one of the finest scientific illustrators of her generation and does not mention that she was also, by some distance, the best marine biologist in any room she ever stood in.

She never publishes the other thing.

She has it. She has had it since a Wednesday afternoon in July 1954, from differential recovery data and a physical examination and something one of the boatmen said once, at the fish store, and would not say twice, and a hand laid flat on a face, and it is the single most important observation ever made about the species by anybody, and it would make her name in a fortnight.

The whole of the evidence for it is a man in Trinidad.

So she keeps it in the back of a drawer for thirty years in the form of eleven pages of notes with no name at the top, and does not discuss it with a living soul, and dies without ever having been asked about it once.

<hr>

She goes down to see Reggie in the autumn of 1960, on her way back from a conference she has no particular business attending, at a minor school in the Midlands where he teaches the lower forms and runs a sailing club.

He is glad to see her. That is the first thing in the letter and Charles reads it twice. He is genuinely, uncomplicatedly glad, and shows her round, and is rather good with the boys, and does not once mention the Martin or the war or anything that happened in either.

She waits until they are in the corridor outside his room, with nobody about, because she has thought about it for four years and has decided that the location matters.

"Is there any news of our friend," Reggie says.

He does not say which friend. He has been carrying the sentence about with him for six years and produces it without any preparation at all, in exactly the same voice, and Jean writes that this was the worst part.

"Yes," she says.

Reggie looks at her.

He does not ask where. He does not ask how, or with whom, or whether it worked, or what became of the animal, or any of the eleven questions that a man who ran that station and built that experiment and knows what the log said would be entitled to ask and would give the rest of his life to have answered. He stands in a school corridor in the Midlands with a bell going somewhere and boys coming past, and he does not ask.

"Good," he says. "Good."

Then he nods, and goes into his room, and shuts the door.

*I stood there for some time*, Jean writes. *I do not think he was in a state to see anybody. I have wondered since whether I ought to have told him more and I have concluded that he would not have let me, and that this was the arrangement he had made with himself, and that it was not mine to alter.*

Charles reads that page in a room at Icacos with the shutter propped open on a stick, and puts it down, and is very still for a while.

He did horrible things and got the result and was not allowed to keep it. He did one decent thing in a room at night with a tin in his hand, and has been given, six years later, in one syllable, the entirety of what it bought: *yes*. Not where. Not how. Not whether it was worth it.

Charles has spent six years assuming that whatever Reggie is, he is not somebody who could have been trusted with more.

He thinks, sitting there, that he may have had that the wrong way round the whole time -- that Reggie knew precisely what he could be trusted with, and asked for exactly that and not one word beyond it, and has been doing the sum himself, at a desk, in the Midlands, since 1954.

<hr>

The rest is thirty years and can be put quite shortly.

The base at Chaguaramas goes in stages and the country becomes a country in 1962 and Charles stands at the back of the crowd for it. The Fisheries Division becomes a Ministry and his ledger becomes a series and then a run, and by 1970 he has the longest unbroken catch record in the southern Caribbean and is regarded, by the four people who know it exists, as a slightly eccentric national asset.

He watches the water go. That is the honest summary of the professional half of his life. He is standing in exactly the right place, with a pencil, for the whole of it: the mangrove going for the estate road, and then for the industrial estate; the run-off; the wells in the gulf; the trawlers; the year the shrimp come and the years afterwards. He writes it all down. He writes it down accurately, in his own capitals, on ruled paper, dated and initialled, month after month, and it does not stop anything at all.

But it is there. That is the difference, and he holds on to it hard, some years harder than others. It is there, and it is honest, and it has a column in it for what he supplied.

He is right about the numbers. He was right about them on a beach at Grenada with a stub of pencil in 1954 and he is right about them in 1975 and he takes no pleasure in it whatever.

<hr>

The letters stop in the spring of 1984.

Jean writes in April and gets nothing, which has happened before and means the post. She writes again in September and gets nothing, which has not happened before and does not mean the post.

She is sixty-two. She flies to Port of Spain in the November, and hires a car with a driver, and goes down the length of the island to a place where the road stops being a road, and gets out at the landing at Icacos, and asks.

And they tell her, straight away, without any difficulty at all, because there is no difficulty in it and never was.

Oh, yes -- the clerk. The one who wrote the letters. He did all our letters, and read them out too, and he was a devil for making you say what you actually meant instead of what you thought you ought to say. He wrote to my son in Toronto for eleven years. He was godfather to two children in this village, and stood up in the church for it, and there is a photograph. He was in the water every evening; he was famous for it; you could set your clock. He was found in the water in the March, in the shallows, off the point, where he always was, and it was his heart, and it would have been quick, and he was fifty-seven.

Jean stands at the landing in the sun with her handbag over her arm and hears the whole of it, and every word of it is true and none of it is the story, and she finds that she is glad about that in a way she had not expected and cannot get to the bottom of standing up.

She had thought she would have to explain him to somebody.

<hr>

A woman lets her into the room behind the shop and gives her a table, and Jean spends four days at it, which is the only work in the world she is qualified to do.

She sorts them. Fourteen ledgers, chronological, complete. Nine notebooks, ruled into four columns, and she reads every page of all nine of them, at a table, with a fan going, in November, and does not hurry.

She does not take them. There is a moment on the third afternoon when she considers it -- she could put them in the case, she could have them in Scotland by Christmas, she knows nine people who would give a year of their lives for them -- and she puts the moment down and does not pick it up again. They are not hers. They are about a place, and they were made in the place, and if they go to Millport they become a curiosity in a cupboard and if they stay here they are a record of the Gulf of Paria kept by a man who lived on it.

She puts them in a cardboard box, in order, ledgers underneath, and asks the woman to keep them, and the woman says of course.

And then, on the last morning, she takes eleven pages out of her own case and puts them in the box under the ledgers.

They have no name on them. They have never had a name on them. They are dated July 1954 and they set out, in a clear hand, with the recovery figures and the examination and the exact date and time she saw a hand laid flat on a face, the only defensible account anybody has ever written of what these animals do to each other and why.

She does not sign them now either. She thinks about it and decides against it, at the table, alone, for reasons she does not write down.

<hr>

Before she goes she walks down to the point at dusk, because she is not going to be in this country again.

The mud shelves out very gently and the water is brown and warm and there is a light on somewhere behind her in the village. She goes to the edge and stops at the edge and stands there with her shoes in her hand.

There is something out there. She cannot see it and would not know what she was looking at, and she watches the flat brown water for a long time and is fairly sure, and is never afterwards certain.

It does not come in.

She puts her shoes on and goes back up to the car.

<hr>

And in 1986, two years after he dies, a doctoral student from St Augustine who has been sent down to Icacos to look for baseline data on something else entirely is given a cardboard box by a woman in the village and opens it in a room with a fan going, and finds fourteen ledgers, and under the ledgers nine notebooks ruled into four columns, and eleven pages in a different hand with nobody's name on them, and reads the page at the front of the first notebook, and sits down.

*I have never had a way to check any of this. Where I have guessed, I have said so in the fourth column. Do not trust the first three without it.*

<hr>

But that is afterwards, and this is not.

This is a Tuesday in about 1971, in the late afternoon, in eighteen inches of warm brown water off Icacos Point, with the boats coming in behind him and somebody's radio going in the village and the light beginning to go orange over Venezuela.

He is forty-four and he looks sixty and his hands have been useless for seventeen years. There is a merman lying along the mud beside him with one hand on the side of his face, and the man is grey at the temples now too, which Charles noticed for the first time last year and has not got used to.

And Monty sings, which he does most times he comes, and which Charles has heard four or five hundred times and has never once got tired of.

It is the same made thing. It has the same shape, and the parts that come back, and the place near the end where it leans and rights itself.

And Charles listens to both of it, because he can, because he is still the only creature in the history of either species who can stand outside the accompaniment and hear the distance between what somebody chose to say and what he could not help saying.

And underneath it, this evening, there is: the mud. The particular fish that are in tonight. A dull ache in the left shoulder that has been there since March. Mild irritation about something that happened in the gulf and that Charles has no way of asking about. The man on the sand -- going grey, breathing badly, aged out of all proportion, forty-four years old and rotten with it -- registering as *there*, and continuing to be there, and having been there on and off for seventeen years, and nothing whatever being made of it.

There is no terror in it anywhere.

Charles lies in the shallows with a hand on his face and gets to the end of the song, and thinks -- as he thinks most Tuesdays, and has never yet found a way to say to anybody, and would not say if he could:

*This is the ordinary part. We got to the ordinary part.*

"Again," he says.
