The rest of it takes about twenty minutes and neither of them does any of the work.

Charles has his glasses on for it. He got them out at the mouth with his hands in the state they are in and put them on his face for the first time since the beach at Grenada, and it was the wrong instinct, and he understands that within about a minute and goes on wearing them anyway.

Because there is a great deal to see and none of it is the thing.

There is an island on the right with buildings on it -- proper buildings, white, a jetty, a row of small houses in a line up a hillside, all of it in beautiful order and completely silent -- and there is a bell on a post and a launch tied up and not a single person visible anywhere on it, and Charles looks at it going past for a full minute before something turns over in him and he understands what an island like that, kept like that, in a place like this, is for.

There is a grey ship at anchor further in, and then two more, and then a long low line of sheds and cranes and hard standing along the shore under the mountains, with a road and a wire fence and a flagpole, and the flag on it is not the one he was expecting, and he has known for a year that Chaguaramas was leased to the Americans in 1941 and it is quite different to come round a corner and find it.

There is a tanker.

There is a smell, under the mangrove smell and the mud smell, which is oil, and it is not a spill and not an accident; it is simply the smell the water has here.

And there is the water itself, which is brown-green and warm and enormous and going out of sight to the south and west with the light on it, forty miles of it, and somewhere down at the far end of it, past the mouth of a river he will never see, there are derricks standing in it and men working on them who have been working on them since before Monty was born.

Charles lies on his back on the floats being carried into the Gulf of Paria at four knots by a tide, and looks at all of it, and thinks: *this is the heartland.*

He has spent thirty-six days and everything he has ever had bringing a man home to a working oil field with a naval station in the entrance and nobody in the whole of it has ever once asked what lives here.

He takes the glasses off and puts them away.

<hr>

He has to drink before anything else can happen and it very nearly does not happen.

Monty takes him along the north shore inside the mouths, past the grey ships at a distance that makes Charles' skin crawl, and puts him into a bay with a village on it where there are pirogues drawn up on the sand and nets on frames and a standpipe on a concrete plinth at the top of the beach. And Charles gets off, and cannot stand, and crawls up out of the water on his hands and his knees like something being born, and gets to the plinth, and cannot work the tap.

A boy of about eleven works it for him and then stands back and watches him drink, out of the pipe, with his mouth on it, for a long time.

Nobody turns round. Nobody arranges themselves. Nobody leaves anything at the tide line, or looks fixedly up the beach at a heap of palm fronds, or hands him a pot of grease and says *before, not after*, and refuses to be thanked. Two men on the sand look up at the harness and the sores and the state of him for about as long as you would look at a fallen bicycle, and go back to the engine they are arguing about.

The story stopped at Grenada. Of course it did. The sloops run the arc and the arc ends, and this island gets its news from Port of Spain, from a newspaper, in English, about federation and the price of cocoa; and there is a naval base in the bay and a refinery down the coast and eleven thousand men on the oilfields, and a wrecked Englishman crawling up a beach at Carenage is not even the strangest thing that has happened here this year.

The boy asks him, without much interest, whether he wants food.

"Yes," Charles says. "Please. I've no money."

The boy shrugs and goes away and comes back with a roti in a piece of newspaper, and Charles eats it sitting on the concrete with the tap running over his feet, and is sick, and eats the rest of it more slowly.

It is the kindest thing anybody has done for him in five weeks and it is the first one that meant nothing at all, and he sits there in the sun understanding that the debt is over, that it ended at Grenada, that he has come out the other side of the only country in the world where he made sense, and that from here he is just a man.

He drinks for an hour. He fills the breaker and both gourds. He greases himself, badly, and puts the cloth under the harness, two thicknesses, and cinches it up.

Then he goes back down the beach and gets into the sea, because that is what he does now, and because there is somebody waiting for him forty yards out who has not eaten in two days and will not leave.

<hr>

The first ones come at dusk and it is the best thing Charles has ever been allowed to watch.

They are out in the middle where the water is brown and warm and there is a long low bank of cloud sitting over Venezuela with lightning going in it silently a very long way off. Charles is holding on with one arm and has been half asleep for an hour. And Monty stops -- and this stop is not any of the stops, and Charles is off him and paddling before he has worked out why.

There are three of them and they are young.

He never does establish how young. But they arrive the way boys arrive, in a rush, from underneath, and there is a great deal of very fast noise in the air -- clicks and that descending buzz and a whooping thing Charles has never heard in his life -- and one of them hits Monty amidships hard enough to put both of them under, and they are gone for perhaps fifteen seconds, all four of them, and Charles is left treading water on his own in the dark with the floats holding him up, alone, with nothing coming in at all.

And he is not frightened, and he does not mind, and he floats there in the middle of the Gulf of Paria grinning like an idiot at a patch of disturbed water.

When Monty comes up he is different in a way Charles has no word for. He is not calmer and he is not happier, exactly. He is *unhurried*. He is a man in a room he knows.

Then he comes to Charles, and takes his face in both hands, out of habit, without thinking about it, in front of them --

-- and the three of them go absolutely still.

<hr>

After that it is not a homecoming. It is an examination, and Charles has seen one before, and he does not enjoy recognising it.

They come out of the dark for two hours. He cannot count them; he cannot see them; what he has is the wash of them and nothing else, coming and going round him in the water at distances he cannot judge, and the secondhand report through his hand on Monty's shoulder, which is like listening to a crowded room from the landing.

And they read him. All of them, in turn, at length, thoroughly, going over him the way you go over a horse.

And Monty holds still for it.

That is the part Charles cannot bear, and it takes him a while to work out why, and then he has it, and it is the ledge of the pen with Pascual saying *if you can keep him this quiet, I'll do a real physical tomorrow*, and himself standing there with his hand on a wet shoulder, taking the compliment.

Monty holds still because holding still is what you do. It is not consent and it is not submission; it is the ordinary courtesy of a creature who has been read by his own people all his life and has no more notion of refusing it than a man has of refusing to be looked at.

That is not the bad part. The bad part takes another minute to arrive.

The courtesy wants somebody on the other end of it. It is a thing you extend, and for nine months there was nobody in that pen to extend it to -- there was a wall, and the pumps, and hands that came over the side and touched him and went away again and were not present in any sense he had a use for -- and it went out into that every day and came back with nothing on it.

And then in the twelfth week something got into the water that was *there*.

He did not hold still for Pascual. He held still because he was being held, in a place where nothing had held anything for three months, and the man doing the holding was the first object in his world since the net that could be addressed at all. Pascual, who is careful, and who will not infer past his evidence, observed exactly and only what was in front of him, which was that the animal was quiet when Charles was in the water, and said so, and asked for nothing further; and by the time it reached the record it had become a temperament.

Charles hangs in the water in the dark with his hand where it always is and grieves, briefly and very hard, over a word in a file.

<hr>

Then they ask him where he has been.

Nobody says it and nothing is said; Charles is not sure, afterwards, that *ask* is the right verb, or that there is a right verb. What happens is that the attention in the water gathers and turns and settles on one thing, and the thing is Monty, and what comes off Monty in answer goes on for a very long time.

Charles gets it secondhand and sideways, at one remove, out of order, and it is the worst hour of his life, and this time it does include the ward and it does include the fourth night out of Dominica.

The concrete. Not as an image -- there is no image of concrete -- but as the absence: a world with the information taken out of it, and no edges, and no distance, and no direction, going on and on. The pumps. The exact place where the wall was and the exact place where the wall still is. Nine months of it, whole, in the order it happened, with nothing left out, because nothing can be left out.

And in it, from the twelfth week onward, a man.

That is the thing Charles is not ready for and could not have been made ready for. He is *in* it. He is not adjacent to it and he is not the end of it. He is one of the things that happened in the box, arriving in the twelfth week and thereafter present, and the box does not stop being the box when he arrives, and there is no picture of *and then someone came who was on my side*, because there is no picture of a side.

They ask -- the water asks, the gathered attention asks -- who took him.

And what comes back is everything, at once, undifferentiated: the net, and the crossing, and the pen, and the pumps, and Reggie in the corridor, and Pascual's hands, and the log, and the reef, and four hundred miles, and Charles, all of it in one piece, because it is one piece, because it happened to one body in one order and that body cannot sort it and would not know what sorting was for.

There is no picture of a difference.

The man from Salybia walked twenty miles over a mountain to tell him this, in the shade, four weeks ago, kindly, and Charles thought he had understood it and had not understood the first thing about it.

He hangs on and takes it. He does not try to explain anything, because there is nobody to explain it to and nothing to explain it with, and because -- and this arrives quite calmly, in the middle of everything else -- it would be a lie. He did keep the log. He wrote *no response observed* in his own hand in his own capitals for weeks while it screamed. Every single thing in the water is true, and it is being read off the only creature in the world who cannot get it wrong, and Charles finds that what he feels is not injustice.

What he feels is that he has finally been reported accurately, by somebody competent, in front of the correct authority, and that it has taken thirty-six days and eighty-five miles to arrange, and that he is glad.

<hr>

They are not angry with him. He is braced for that too and does not get it, and afterwards he thinks the anger would have been easier.

What happens instead is that the attention comes off him almost entirely, quite soon, and goes back to Monty, and stays there; and what is in it, unmistakably, even at one remove, even through a hand, is *sorrow*.

Not for the pen. They have finished with the pen.

For the thing they can feel burning in him and pointing straight up at the surface, at a large warm animal in a rope harness, breathing air.

Charles is a biologist and it takes him about ninety seconds.

*He is twenty-nine*, he thinks, treading water. *They go to seventy or eighty. And I am human, and I have done what I have done to myself, and I will not see sixty.*

Nobody dies of it. He knows that; Monty knows it; it is in the water and it is not what is being grieved. What is being grieved is the other half of the century. They are looking at one of their own who has come home across four hundred miles of open sea married to the one creature in the world guaranteed to leave him inside thirty years, and who will then be bereaved, in the ordinary way, at fifty-nine, and have another twenty of it to get through in a sea that is emptying. A bond that thins out is a thing you can walk away from; they have all seen it done and half of them have done it. A bond that is cut is not the same animal. Every one of them knows somebody it happened to, and knows what they were like afterwards, and knows that they did not go down again.

And they are not reading somebody who did not know. That is the last of it, and it comes through as clearly as anything all night. Every one of them can feel that he has done this before -- can feel the shape of a marriage that ran out quietly in a box while he was inside it, and can feel that he knows exactly what the shape of that is, and did it again anyway, on purpose, on a reef, in the dark, in about the time it takes to change your mind.

And then there is one more thing in the water, and it comes last, and it frightens them more than any of the rest.

It goes round like weather: not that he is bonded, not that he is spent, not that the ribs are standing out of him -- that he is *rested*. That this thin, wrongly-married man who has come four hundred miles down the arc with a full-grown air-breathing animal in a rope harness on his back has slept every night of it, properly, all the way down, better than anybody in this gulf has slept in years.

And every one of them understands, immediately and without being told, exactly what that means about the thing on the surface.

Nothing changes. Nobody comes over. It simply enters the water and stays there, and Charles feels it go through the crowd secondhand, through a hand, without any idea at all what it is or that it is about him, and thinks it is something to do with the tide.

They are gentle with him. It goes on for hours, and it is very gentle, and the gentleness is the part he cannot say a word of to anybody, then or ever.

<hr>

She comes at some point in the night and Charles knows her instantly.

He is asleep, or the thing that is standing in for sleep. What wakes him is Monty going quiet in a particular way, and then -- through the hand, secondhand, a report of a thing rather than the thing, exactly the way the stranger arrived off the Grenadines --

<i>-- violet, the violet at the bottom of a flame, ruffled all round like a fan half closing, and the line of gold laid along the ruffle --</i>

"Oh," Charles says out loud, in the middle of the Gulf of Paria. "Oh, it's you."

And he is *delighted*. That is the thing he will think about for the rest of his life and be unable to get to the bottom of. He is so pleased he could weep. He has been carrying a violet ruffled thing in his head since the pen like a photograph in a wallet, and here she is, and she is real, and she is exactly what he was given, and he has arrived, and he has brought him back, and he has the whole of it down to a sentence.

He gets a hand up out of the water and waves at her, which is the single most useless act in the history of either species.

There is a long pause.

Then something comes back, and Charles knows before he has it that it was meant for him, because it passes through Monty and Monty goes still in a way that has nothing to do with receiving: it is the stillness of a man standing aside in a doorway to let something through. It is brief. Charles cannot read a syllable of it and never will, and what he takes from it, rightly or wrongly, is that it was not unkind.

And there is somebody with her.

Charles registers him as a second wash at her shoulder, close in, staying close, and does not need anybody to tell him what that is.

The whole thing goes out of him in about four seconds, in the dark, with nobody to see it happen.

She is not waiting. She has not been waiting. Every figure in the sum was correct and the sum was of a quantity that does not exist, and the decent thing he got down to a sentence on a beach at Grenada and has been carrying for five days like something in a coat pocket was not, and never had been, in his gift to give. There is no ceremony here. There is no stage, and no seat, and nobody has been keeping one for him, and the largest thing he had left to give away turns out to have been an object he made himself, on his own, out of nothing, on the sand.

He lies on his back on the floats, thirty-six days out of Antigua and three quarters dead, and finds that what he feels, on top of everything else, is embarrassment.

*Good*, he thinks, and means every word of it, and it costs him nothing whatever, which is the worst part. *I'm glad she's got someone.*

<hr>

The old woman comes at first light and she comes for him.

He knows she is old before he knows anything else about her, because Monty's whole bearing changes -- he goes courteous, formally so, in a way Charles has never once seen in him, not for Reggie and not for the Colonel and not for anybody -- and because she does not circle him and does not read him from a distance. She comes straight in, and puts a hand on his shoulder, and the other hand on the side of his head, over the ear, exactly where a hand goes.

And Charles receives another mind directly, for the first and only time in his life, and it is nothing like Monty and it is the loneliest four seconds he has ever spent.

She goes through him. She is thorough and she is quick and she is entirely unsentimental and she has clearly done this ten thousand times, and Charles hangs there in the water in the grey light and lets an old woman he cannot see take him apart, and thinks, with the top of his mind, *she is the library, this is what they have instead of paper* --

And the heat comes up out of his breastbone.

It is the first one since the second night on the wall. It has been almost two days, and it goes off in the middle of a physical examination by the ranking authority in the Gulf of Paria, at dawn, in front of everybody, on schedule.

Forty feet out at the edge of the crowd, something goes through Monty like a shot.

It comes off him hard enough that two or three of the nearest turn towards it. Charles gets it secondhand, through the hand, in the middle of being taken apart by somebody else, and has not the faintest idea what it is, and lets it go.

The clock has started again. It never stopped. There was simply nothing left in the man to make one out of, and for two days the only instrument in the world that could read him had been holding a hand flat on a blank face in the dark and getting nothing back; and nobody in that gulf knows it except the one who did it, and he has no way to say so, and would not think of it as a thing that wanted saying.

Her hand does not move.

Charles feels her get it -- feels the recognition go through her, flat and immediate and completely certain, with no working shown, the way you recognise a smell from childhood.

And then he feels her be *gentle*.

He understands what has happened perhaps two seconds before she says it, and there is nothing whatever he can do in those two seconds, and he does not think there ever would have been.

She says a word.

It is a short one. It is spoken, out loud, in the air, half above the surface, and it has a click at the front and a fall at the end, and Charles has never heard it before and will hear it again perhaps forty times in the next hour and will never learn what it is.

He will work out what it means. He has been given the mechanism to work it out and he works it out lying on his back that afternoon with his eyes shut: that there is exactly one class of body that burns like that on a clock, in her world, and it has burned like that in her own body ten thousand times, and it is the class that has finished with breeding and gone over into the other thing, the thing that comes afterwards, the rank you get for surviving.

She has read him correctly. That is what he cannot get past. She has looked at the animal directly and got the animal right: he is sterile, permanently, surgically, by his own decision, in a nursing home in Sussex in 1947, and he is exactly what she says he is and he has never in his life been so accurately described by anybody.

And there is only one category available, and it is the old women, and it is hers.

<hr>

Monty says he.

He says it the way he said the man's name off the north end of Dominica -- not the sound; the other thing; the whole assembled fact of him -- and he says it to the old woman, and to the four or five nearest, and then, over the next hour, to everyone in the water.

And nobody believes him.

They are not dismissive. That would be something to push against. They receive it, and they consider it, and they set it gently to one side, because the woman with the hands has just read the body herself, at contact, and a body does not lie, and there is not one creature in that gulf who has ever encountered a case in which the reading was correct and the conclusion was wrong. What Monty has is a thing he knows and cannot demonstrate, about an interior he has been inside for five weeks and nobody else has been inside for four seconds, and the channel he would need to prove it does not exist and is not going to.

He has a true fact and no instrument.

Charles floats in the brown water with the sun coming up over the Northern Range and the tankers going down the gulf and works out where he has seen this before, and it takes him a moment, and then it takes him no time at all.

It is not the Petrie. It is a great deal older than the Petrie.

It is a kitchen in Fife, and three sisters, and a child of six or seven saying *you're frightened* to a grown woman who is frightened, and being told, kindly, and every single time, by people who loved him and had every reason and every right not to believe a word of it, that he was mistaken. He was mistaken so often and so patiently that somewhere around his ninth birthday he stopped saying it out loud and started keeping it to himself instead, where it did nobody any harm.

He was right anyway. Not always. Often enough.

He never once had a thing to prove it with.

And then, much later and much smaller, a laboratory on Antigua, and a junior researcher who had never seen one before that year saying *merman* at supper, in a flat voice, repeatedly, in a room full of people who had every reason not to listen and who went on writing *mermaid* in the log and *mermaid* in the minutes and *mermaid* in the funding letters about a nineteen-foot male animal with a scar on his flank, until they stopped correcting him and started letting him have it -- which is not the same as agreeing, and he knew it was not the same at the time, and never once had a thing to prove it with either.

And here is the same room, twice over. And this time he is the one being read, and the reading is correct and the conclusion is wrong, and the only person in the water arguing for him is a man with no words who cannot be believed.

Charles gets his face down against Monty's back, and the room opens, and he does the only thing he has ever had to give, which is to go still inside himself and put his heart down flat and slow and hold it there.

*I know*, he says, into the one channel in the world that will carry it, which carries no words and never will. *I know. Go on.*

Monty does not argue with anybody. He has nothing to argue with. He waits until it goes quiet, and then he says it again, in the flat brown water, to whoever is nearest.

He will say it for fifty years.
