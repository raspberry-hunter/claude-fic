The first thing he does, once the land behind them is only a darker piece of dark, is take off his glasses.

It costs him a hand to do it, and losing one hand nearly loses him the other; he goes under, comes up spitting, and gets his elbow hooked over Monty's shoulder before he can think about it. Then he folds the glasses one-handed and works them down into the knapsack among the coils of line and the tin of zinc paste, into the corner where they cannot slide, and pushes the flap shut over them.

The world goes soft. It is an old softness and not an unfriendly one. He has been half-blind at the edges of his own life since he was nine.

<i>?</i>

Not a word. A turn of attention, the way a man in a dark room turns his face towards a sound.

"I can't see anyway," Charles says out loud, which is useless, and then does the other thing, the thing he has only been able to do for eleven days: he puts his chest flat against Monty's back and his hand on Monty's shoulder and lets it be true.

<i>the-fan-lovely-when-there-but-who-disappears-into-its-hole holds a small hard thing to his face. Without it he does not know where anything is. With it he still does not know where anything is, not the way a person knows.</i>

That is not fair, Charles thinks, and Monty's amusement comes back at him like warmth off a wall, and then, underneath the amusement and not meant for him at all, something enormous and unsteady that Charles has no name for.

<hr>

He had expected to be afraid. He is not, yet, and he thinks this is probably the same mechanism by which men walk out of burning buildings carrying the wrong things.

What he is, is astonished.

Because Monty is *loud*.

He knew about the hand over the mouth. He had it in the flood, at the wedding, along with everything else and no way of sorting any of it: that the first time Monty called back he put Charles on the concrete with his hands over his ears, and that every day after that Monty held it down for him, on purpose, at some cost, the way you keep your voice down for somebody with a headache. Eleven days of a man being careful with him. He has known that and been grateful for it and thought it was the whole account.

What he has not had, and did not know he had not had, is the floor.

That is the only word he can find for it. Out here past the reef, with a hundred fathoms of clean salt under them and no grounded steel within half a mile, there is something running underneath everything Monty does -- low, continuous, not loud in the least, going on when the pictures stop and going on between them. In the pen there was no such thing. Not a quieter version of it. Nothing at all. He would have sworn to that in a witness box; he was in the water with him every day for eleven days and there was no floor under any of it.

So he keeps having to let go for a moment and take hold again, the way you look away from a bright light, and it is not the bright part that does it.

He puts it down to open water, and to a hundred fathoms, and to being frightened, and files it, and is wrong about it for three weeks.

And Monty is not paying attention to him. That is the astonishing part. Monty is paying attention to *everything*.

<i>Small fish in a curtain, thousands, each one a spark, the curtain turning over on itself like smoke -- something long and unhurried far down and to the left, not interested, a slow heavy heartbeat, safe -- the ground falling away, falling, gone --</i>

They stop. Twice in the first hour they simply stop, dead in the water, Charles bobbing at the end of his own arm while Monty hangs there with his head down, drinking it in. The second time Charles feels him make a sound that never becomes a sound, a thing that starts in the chest and is swallowed.

Four hundred miles, Charles thinks. At this rate.

But he cannot bring himself to pull at him, and he doesn't.

Somewhere in there he notices that he cannot hear the generator. He has not been able to hear it for some time -- an hour, perhaps -- and he had not noticed it going. He has slept within earshot of that engine every night since March. It is the loudest thing on the island and he has stopped being able to hear it and did not feel it stop.

He turns his head to look back and there is nothing to look at, only the soft dark, and even with his glasses there would probably be nothing, and he thinks, with a clarity that surprises him: *they will find the tarp on the beach in the morning.*

Well. Let them.

<hr>

The talking, when it starts, is nothing like he had imagined it in the pen, in the weeks when he had imagined it constantly.

He had imagined -- he can admit this now, in the dark, where no one can see his face -- something like a conversation. Sentences. *Where are we going. How long. Are you frightened. I am.* Even after the wedding, even knowing better, even having written in his own log *subject transmits percepts, not propositions* in his own hand in his own careful capitals, some part of him had gone on expecting that once they were alone the door would open the rest of the way.

The door does not open the rest of the way. The door is exactly as wide as it has ever been. What is different is that there is no longer anyone else in the house.

So: he cannot ask *how long*. He can hold the question in his head as an ache and get back the wrong answer to a different question, which is a picture of water that is not this water -- browner, warmer, heavy with silt, tasting (he receives this as taste, and has no idea whether that is Monty's or his own translation) of leaf mould and iron. Home is not a distance. Home is a flavour, and it is on the other side of an amount of sea for which neither of them has a number.

He cannot say *Reggie sent me*. He can put up the image of Reggie in the corridor with the tin in his hand, and Monty takes it, examines it with real attention, and hands him back a version of Reggie that Charles does not recognise at all: a shape that is always in the same place, a heat that never comes closer, something adjacent to the man's whole life and never once in the water with him.

That is not wrong, Charles thinks slowly. That is only what Reggie looked like from in there.

He cannot say *I am sorry*. He tries. It goes out of him and what comes back is Monty turning his head slightly, patient, waiting for the rest of it, the way you wait for a man with a stammer.

But he can say -- he can hold up, and have received -- his hut, and the smell of it, and John's socks on the line, and the particular squeak of the third step of the mess-hall verandah. And Monty takes all of it, every scrap, greedily, because it is Charlie, and Charlie is the only thing in the whole of the world that Monty has ever wanted explained.

Some time after midnight Charles understands that this is going to be his marriage. This exactly. Pictures and heat and no verbs, for as long as he lasts.

He can think *the wedding*, which is a thing that happened to him on an afternoon, and he can think *marriage*, which is a condition, like anaemia. Both of those go down without any trouble whatever. What will not go anywhere near his own name is the other word, the one with a person in it, the one that wants a possessive in front of it; he has tried it twice tonight and both times something has stood up in him and put it away.

He puts his forehead against the back of Monty's neck and hangs on and is, briefly and enormously, happy.

<hr>

The heat comes up out of nowhere, the way it does.

It starts behind his breastbone and goes up through his throat into his scalp, and his face prickles all over, and in eight or nine seconds he is streaming with sweat -- in the sea, at night, in water that is blood-warm and salt and everywhere, he can feel himself sweating, which is an absurd and humiliating thing to be able to feel, and he has been able to feel it since July.

And Monty stops swimming.

Not a slowing. A stop, complete, like a machine cut off at the wall: every muscle in him locked, his whole length gone rigid under Charles' chest, his head coming round.

Charles' first thought is *shark*.

He goes absolutely still. It is the only useful thing he knows how to do and he does it well; he has been doing it since he was eight years old, in a different sort of dark. He makes his hands loose on Monty's shoulder and he brings his heartbeat down deliberately, the old trick, the flat cold slow thing that his mother used to call sulking, and he waits to be bitten in half.

Nothing happens.

Nothing goes on happening. And then Monty moves -- but wrongly. He does not bolt and he does not dive. He *eases off*. He turns himself out from under Charles' chest, gently, unmistakably, and takes Charles' wrist in one webbed hand and holds him out at arm's length, and starts swimming again at half speed, towing him by the wrist like a child who has been sick on someone.

And the channel closes. Not entirely -- he can still feel him, presence and direction and something, a smear, a mood -- but the pictures stop as if a tap has been turned, because there is a foot and a half of conducting seawater between Charles' skin and Monty's, and Charles knows precisely what that foot and a half is worth, he could write out the equation, he has written out the equation.

He hangs there on the end of his own arm in the dark and the heat goes out of him as fast as it came, and then he is cold, and the sweat is still coming, and he thinks:

*He's had enough of me.*

It is not a fair thought and he knows it is not a fair thought. He thinks it anyway. It arrives whole, in his own voice, in the small hours, out of sight of land: eleven days of it and he has had enough, he has smelled what I actually am out here where there's nothing else to look at, and he is being kind about it, and being towed by the wrist is what kind looks like.

His skin lights up all over again with something that is not the flush at all.

Eight or nine minutes later Monty draws him back in, and puts him against his own back, and holds his hand flat there for a moment with a webbed hand over it, and the pictures come back on: the curtain of small fish, and the long thing far below, and nothing whatever about the last eight minutes, because there is no word in the world for the last eight minutes.

Charles decides it was a shark after all. It is the only explanation that lets him keep swimming, so he takes it, and later he will not be able to remember deciding.

It happens twice more before dawn.

<hr>

The dawn is grey and then yellow and then, all at once, enormous.

He can see nothing of it. He can see light, and the fact of a horizon, and a wobbling green-blue field with a lot of glare in it, and he does not put his glasses on, because he has worked out during the night what it will cost to lose them and he is not going to spend it on scenery.

Practical matters, then. He goes through them in order, out loud, because his voice is the only thing out here that belongs to him.

Water. He has a quart in a screw-top and a further half-gallon in the canvas bottle, and it is September, and he is in the tropics, in the sun, on open water, working. He has done this arithmetic before -- for turtles, for a dolphin at Woods Hole, for a paper he never wrote -- and it comes out the same for a man as it does for anything else: three or four pints a day to stand still, more to work. He has a day and a half of water and he needs a lifetime of it.

That is the whole problem, he thinks. That is the entire problem and everything else is decoration. Monty can swim forever. Monty can go where he likes for as long as he likes. Monty is limited by exactly one thing in the whole of the Caribbean, and it is the amount of fresh water that can be carried in a knapsack on a merman's back.

Food. Never mind food. Nobody dies of food this month.

Hands. He looks at his hands. They have been closed on a rope of muscle for eleven hours and they will not open now, and when he makes them open they stay half-shut, curled like a dead bird's feet, and the strength has simply gone out of them. It has not been going. It has gone. He can hold on with the crook of his elbow and with his knees, but the crook of his elbow across a windpipe is not a thing he is prepared to do to Monty for four hundred miles, and he has already noticed the strap of the knapsack biting a line into the meat of Monty's shoulder and has already had to stop himself apologising for it out loud.

He greases his own face and neck with the zinc paste and then, awkwardly, one-handed, works some under the strap where it has rubbed, and Monty tolerates this with the air of a man being fussed over by an aunt.

Then he takes stock of what he actually brought, item by item, and that is how it happens.

Knife. Lines. Hooks. A tin of paste. Six ship's biscuits and a slab of chocolate gone soft and reset into a new shape. A quart and a half-gallon. A folded chart in a tobacco tin. A note, in his own hand, asking whoever finds this animal to be kind to him, which he cannot now read without wanting to be sick.

No ampoules.

He did not forget them. That is what stops him. He has stood at that chest of drawers with the knapsack open, twice, at least twice, and he can see the ampoules in their box in the drawer under his socks as clearly as he can see anything this morning, and he did not take them, and he did not decide not to take them, and the reason he did not take them is that there was nothing to take: he has not filled the prescription since June.

He works it out on his fingers, in the water, with the sun coming up. June. It is the second week of September.

Twelve, thirteen weeks.

He had told himself he was busy. He *was* busy; there was the crisis in July, and the boat was out, and Pascual had the sampling, and it slipped, and then it went on slipping, and every single week of it he had a reason ready and never once had to use the reason, because nobody asked, because a man who intends to go on living in a place keeps his prescriptions current and a man who has already left does not, and he had not let himself hold those two facts in the same hand.

He is not, it turns out, going to have to decide whether to go with Monty. He decided in June, at a chest of drawers, without saying anything to himself about it, in the same voice he has been not-saying things to himself in since he was a child. And it worked, exactly as it has always worked, and the only person in the world he has ever successfully lied to is currently hanging onto a merman in the middle of the Caribbean with thirty-six hours of water.

The heat comes up through him again -- and this is the other end of the same June, this is what June is going to cost him now, every two hours for the next five years -- and Monty stops dead, and eases him off to arm's length, and tows him gently by the wrist.

"It's not you," Charles says, out loud, into the glare, to nobody, in a voice that comes out much worse than he expects. "Monty. It isn't you."

<hr>

The island resolves itself in stages, over four hours, and each stage is a lie the next one corrects.

First it is a discolouration where the sky is thickest. Then it is a shape, low and blue and much too small. Then, quite suddenly, it is *high*, and green, and there is smoke on it, and Charles feels something come up in his chest that he has no business feeling about a place with a customs house.

He gets the glasses out at half a mile, with his back against Monty's shoulder and both of them rocking, and it takes him three tries to unfold them and one attempt to clean them that makes everything worse.

Cane on the slopes. A road going along the top of a cliff with a truck on it. A church tower. A white line of surf and a long grey beach with three boats drawn up, painted red and yellow, and the shape of a man on the beach beside one of them with a net over his knees.

Beyond the surf line, Monty stops, and will not go further in, and Charles does not need pictures to understand it.

"All right," he says.

He unstraps the knapsack, and it takes a long time, because his hands. He is aware, taking it, of exactly what he is doing: he is taking his water off the only thing in the world that can carry it and taking it ashore, where he can drink it, alone.

"I'll come back," he says. "You know that. You know that, don't you."

Monty's hands come up to his face, both of them, and hold him there for a moment in the swell -- and what comes across is not reassurance. It is not anything Charles can use. It is enormous and it is not for him and it has no edges: the coast, the smoke, the road, the hulls, all of it registering as one continuous fact, and against it, very small, one lit thing that must not go into it and is going into it anyway.

Then he lets go, and there is nothing under Charles' hands, and Charles turns and swims for the beach and is knocked over twice by the surf on the way in and crawls the last of it.

<hr>

The man with the net does not look at the water.

That is the first thing Charles notices, on his hands and knees in six inches of foam, and it is so precise and so deliberate that Charles understands it before he understands anything else: the man has seen. The man saw at some distance and has arranged himself since then, and what he has arranged is to be looking steadily up the beach at a heap of drying palm fronds.

Charles gets up. He is aware of how he must look. He says, "Bonjour," and then, in French that is eleven years old and was schoolboy French even then, that he is very sorry, that he needs water, that he can pay.

The man says something to him in Creole, of which Charles catches perhaps three words, and none of the three words are important, and the meaning is not in the words. Then the man says, in French, slowly, the way you speak to a child or a foreigner or a drunk: "There is water at the house."

He does not offer to help Charles up the beach. He walks, and Charles follows, and neither of them looks back at the sea, and it is only when they get to the top and there is a low wall to sit on that the man looks at Charles' hands.

He looks at them for a long time.

Then he goes away and comes back with a gourd of water and stands there while Charles drinks all of it and is sick and drinks half of another one more slowly. He goes away again and comes back with an old woman who has brought bread and a jar of something and who does not look at the sea either, and who tells the man something in a tone Charles knows very well in any language, which is the tone of a woman telling a man what he already knows and is not going to like.

The man goes away a third time. He is gone twenty minutes. He comes back with rope.

He does not explain. He measures it against Charles' chest with his hands, and takes it away, and works at it on the wall with his back half-turned, and what he makes is a bridle -- a loop under the arms, a cross over the chest, a bight left long at the front. He fits it on Charles like a man fitting a harness to a mule, without ceremony, tugging the parts of it that matter and ignoring Charles entirely, and Charles stands there with his arms out and lets him, and understands that he has just been told, with total clarity and without a single word, that his hands are going to fail and that everyone on this beach knows it.

Then the man stops.

He stands back and looks at the harness he has made. He looks at it for much longer than a man looks at his own knotwork, and his face does something that Charles cannot read at all.

He unclips the knife from his own belt -- an old one, black-handled, the blade worn down to a curve by twenty years of stones -- and he puts it into Charles' half-shut hand and folds the fingers round it and holds them there.

He does not say what it is for. He does not need to. Charles stands on a hot wall at the top of a beach in Guadeloupe with a rope round his chest and a stranger's knife in his fist, and works out for himself, in one clean movement, what it is for.

He tries to pay. He has a watch. The man looks at the watch and then at Charles with an expression of such flat, offended incomprehension that Charles puts it back in his pocket and does not try again.

The old woman says something. The man translates it into his slow French, apparently editing as he goes: "She says you will die out there."

"Yes," Charles says. "Probably."

The man nods, as though a small point of fact has been settled between reasonable people, and goes to fill the second gourd.

<hr>

He is on the beach again at four, with a full canvas bottle, two gourds slung on a cord, bread wrapped in leaves, and a rope harness he cannot get out of without a knife.

He asks, at the last, why. He asks it badly, in bad French, meaning something enormous by it. The man considers him for a while, standing in the shallows with the water going over his feet, and then says a sentence Charles will spend the next month failing to reconstruct, of which he understands only the beginning -- *my grandfather* -- and, at the end, quite distinctly, the word for a share of the catch.

Then the man turns his back and walks up the beach, and does not stop, and does not look round, and the old woman has already gone.

Charles stands there a moment with the water round his knees.

He is dry. He is nearly dry, for the first time in a day, and there is bread in him and a pint and a half of water and he can feel his own body being glad of it in a way that is close to grief. Up the hill there is a road with a truck on it, and the truck goes somewhere, and the somewhere has a harbour, and the harbour has a boat that goes to Antigua twice a week.

Out past the surf, forty yards out, something breaks the surface and goes under again, and from here, without his glasses, it is not even a shape. It is a disturbance. He has not been able to hear a single thing from it since he let go this morning, and he will not be able to hear a single thing from it until his skin is on its skin, and that is how it is going to be every day now until one of them dies: silence, and bread, and the walk back down.

He takes off his glasses and folds them and puts them in the knapsack, down in the corner where they cannot slide, and pushes the flap shut over them.

The world goes soft.

He walks down into the sea.
