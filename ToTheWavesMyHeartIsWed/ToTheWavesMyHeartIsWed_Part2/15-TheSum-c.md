They tell him about it at Carriacou, in the evening, in the way of men telling a stranger something everybody has always known: that there is a thing under the sea between here and Grenada that comes up sometimes, and that in thirty-nine it put water in the air higher than the mast of a schooner, and that you go round it, and that this is not advice.

They go round it the next day and he does not need to be told when.

Charles smells it before he understands it -- sulphur, thin and low over the water, in a place with no land in it to make any -- and feels Monty stop, and swing east into the open, wide, wide, at a mile and more. It is not a patch. It is a country.

He would go round in any case. He would go round if it cost them a week.

What he thinks about, that night, is Pointe-à-Pitre: himself hanging on with both arms pushing an image of a crane and a bridge as hard as he could at a creature who could not be made to see it, and saying *no*, and being obeyed, and lying on a beach afterwards knowing he had built a pen out of doors for good reasons. He has been carrying that since Guadeloupe. He has been carrying it, he realises, as a debt, and had rather assumed he would carry it to Trinidad.

And here they are going round something, without discussion, for reasons he understands completely, and Monty will not be talked to for an hour afterwards, and Charles does not try, and there is no debt in it at all.

It is not a reckoning. Nothing is settled. It is only that the same thing has happened in the other direction and it turns out to be perfectly ordinary.

<hr>

The last of it he does at Grenada, on the sand, on the second-to-last night, with a stub of pencil on the back of the chart, because he cannot hold a number that size in his head without writing it down.

He starts with the honest one, which is his own.

Thirty-four days out. Three hundred and fifty-odd miles. Water: he can carry rather more than he can use, because the constraint has never once been the cask. Skin: the lines under his arms have stopped being wounds and become a condition. Food: he has stopped being hungry, which he knows perfectly well is a symptom and not a solution, and he gives it a heading of its own so as not to be able to walk past it. Hands: gone, and not coming back, and the toggle is now the only thing in the world his left hand can reliably do, which is a very great joke and he does not laugh at it. Kidneys: he does not have a number for his kidneys. He has a colour.

Then he does what he has been putting off for a week, which is Monty's schedule against his own, honestly, both columns, no thumb on the scale.

At Monty's rate they reach the Bocas in five days.

At the rate Charles has been fighting for -- a day of rest at every island, a proper lie-up, hunting time, the sensible rate, the rate a competent physiologist would set for a working animal in the tropics -- it is eleven or twelve days, and he goes down the column twice looking for his own error, and there is no error. Eleven days at his current loss is a number he can look at.

Monty is right. Monty has been right since Martinique. He is not being hurried by a frightened creature; he is being carried at the only speed that arrives, by somebody who reached that conclusion without a pencil, five weeks ago, by holding a hand on his chest.

Charles sits on the sand for a long time with the chart on his knees.

Then he tears the corner off, because it is Jean's chart and he is not going to write on the part with her hand in it, and he does the other sum -- the one he has been circling since the Grenadines and has not let himself set down.

Thirty-four days. Three hundred and fifty miles of the only road there is, at the one season of the year when everything in the region is drifting down the plume, in continuous transect, carried by an animal who would register any of his own kind inside a hundred feet in clear water and half as far again in this.

And they have met one.

One, at thirty yards, in the dark, which went round them and away. And the unresolved things at the soft edge of the world, which Monty turned his head to and could not raise, and which may have been anything at all.

He has run line transects. It is his actual profession; he has done it for turtles, off Antigua, in a boat, with Jean at the other end of the tape and John complaining. So he knows precisely what may be done with an *n* of one, which is nothing whatever -- no density, no error, no paper, no sentence a man could stand up in a room and say -- and he sits there with the pencil in his ruined hand and does it anyway, and gets two hundred, and looks at it for a while.

It is not a figure. It is the shape a fear takes in a man who has been trained to put fears into figures. He could not defend a line of it to a referee and does not intend to try. He does not cross it out.

He does not attempt to send any of it. There is no picture of two hundred. And in any case the only person he might be telling it to has already had it, in the only way it can be known, which is a cave with nothing in it.

Then the last piece, which is not a sum at all, and which he has been drafting for four nights in the shallows, in quite good English, and has now got down to about a sentence.

Whatever it is that he and Monty are -- and he has no word for it, and the only word available is one he will not use about himself in his own head at half past two in the morning on a beach -- it is the thing that in every pair-forming species he has ever read about, from the albatross down, has exactly one function and admits of exactly one occupant.

Monty is twenty-nine. Monty is a breeding animal out of a population that, on the only figure Charles has been able to construct and cannot defend, has not got one to spare. And the one occupancy is presently taken up by a short-sighted Englishman with bad kidneys who will be dead inside thirty years and cannot give him a calf under any arrangement whatever.

So: the Bocas.

He will go in with him, because there is no other way in. He will see him met. And then he will stand aside -- no scene, nothing said, there being nothing to say it with -- and simply stop being in the way. He will get himself up onto some beach on the north shore with a road out of it, and be a man again, and let a sea with two hundred people in it do what a sea does.

He turns it over on the sand for a long while and cannot find the flaw in it. He is aware, dimly, of being rather pleased with himself, and decides that this is allowable, on the grounds that it is the only decent thing he has thought of in five weeks and it is going to cost him everything he has.

He does not mention it. He tells himself this is because there is no picture for *standing aside* and no picture for *instead*, which is perfectly true, and is not the reason.

The reason is that he would rather have the five days.

Charles folds the corner of the chart and puts it in his pocket, and does not weep, and goes and lies down in the shallows with his hand on him.

<hr>

They give him the big cask.

It is the last morning and he goes down the beach and asks the old man for the small one, the four-gallon, and gives his reasons, which are drag, and rate, and the arithmetic of a load that costs you the water it carries, and -- though he does not put this part into words for anybody -- that the thing which is going to kill him is not going to be thirst. He gets them out in what he believes is quite good order.

The old man hears him all the way through, and looks at him for a moment with the particular expression Charles has been getting from men on beaches since Guadeloupe, and gives him the six.

Nobody argues. It is not that kind of transaction and never has been. Two of them carry it down and lash it on with the gourds, and there is grease, and a tin of zinc, and soft cloth, two thicknesses, and a fresh line on the toggle, and six fish that Charles does not want and takes.

And they tell him about the weather, because that is the one thing everybody on this island wants to say to him. It is October. There is a great swell running out of the north-east from something that is happening a very long way away and is nobody's business here. And they tell him -- three separate men, unprompted, with total and cheerful confidence -- that the storms go north of Grenada. That they always have. That it is why the boats winter here and why the insurance is cheap, and that in all the years there has never been one, and he is not to worry about that at least.

"Thank you," Charles says. "That's a comfort."

Eleven months later, in September of 1955, a hurricane the wet-season charts have not got a name for yet will come over this island from the east in the dark and take five hundred people off it, and Carriacou with it, and the second man to have told him so this morning will be one of them; and Charles will read about it in a four-line paragraph in a newspaper, somewhere else, alone, and put the paper down and sit for a while.

<hr>

He goes down at three in the morning because Monty comes for him at three in the morning.

The south end of the island is flat and dry and low and the last of the arc is behind him, all of it, every beach, every pot of water, every back turned politely on the sea. Ahead there is nothing. There is not a light, and there will not be a light, and there is no island out there to resolve itself in stages over four hours and correct its own lies as it comes.

Eighty-five miles, and nothing in the middle of it.

He does not count it in strokes. He has counted everything since Martinique -- strokes, breaths, pulses, paces, pints, days, degrees of arc, one merman in thirty-four days -- and this is the first thing since Antigua he declines to measure, and he stands in the shallows being quietly and enormously pleased with himself about it.

Then he takes off his glasses and folds them and works them down into the corner of the knapsack where they cannot slide, and pushes the flap shut over them.

The world goes soft.

He walks down into the sea.
