He stops looking at things.

That is how it announces itself, and it takes Charles two days to notice, because it is an absence and absences are hard to see. There are no more stops. There is no turtle. Nothing is held up and turned over and offered; nothing is examined for its own sake; the whole long wasteful habit of a creature going down a coast reading everything in it has simply been put away, and what is left is a man swimming south at a fixed rate with his head down.

They are off the Pitons when Charles gets it, and once he has it he cannot stop having it.

They go before light now. Monty wakes him -- a hand on the ankle, a shove, once the flat of a palm against his chest hard enough to hurt -- in the grey, an hour earlier every day, and by the third morning it is not grey, it is black. They are eleven and twelve hours in the water. Twice they run through the dark and into the next dawn and Charles is not asked.

He hunts less. That is the arithmetic that frightens Charles most, because he has watched it before, in July, on a chart, in another life: an animal spending capital. Monty takes what comes past him and does not go after anything, and the fish the people give them go into him in the shallows in four minutes with no attention paid to any of it, and the ribs, which had gone away in the river, come back.

<hr>

Charles fights him about it for two days and loses every single time, and the losing is very quiet, because there is no argument to have.

He tries the only things available. He goes limp, which does nothing; a limp man is easier to tow. He hangs back at a landfall, dawdling in the shallows, filling gourds that are already full -- and Monty waits, at the line, without pictures, in a stillness that Charles has learned to read and would rather not. He tries putting up the picture of the room: the elbow of the river, the roots, the fireflies, the two of them, unmoving. It goes out of him perfectly. It comes back to him with the sun already gone across it, and gone again, and gone again, faster and faster until the days are a flicker -- and under it, and quite unmistakable, is a thing that Charles finally understands is *urgency*, and which he can only translate as *there is not enough of it*.

Not enough of what?

There is no answer to that in this channel. There is no *of what*.

But he is not stupid, and on the second evening, lying at the top of a beach under the Pitons with the harness off and his ribs going up and down, he works out what it must be, and it stops him being angry for the rest of his life.

Monty has decided that Charles is dying.

He has decided it correctly, on good evidence, having tested it properly, and the one lever he has is speed, and he is pulling it with both hands.

<hr>

The other change is in the flushes, and it is the one Charles cannot get used to.

They no longer buy him a foot and a half of water. Monty does not lock and ease him off and tow him at arm's length like a child who has been sick on somebody; that finished off Martinique and has not come back. What happens now is the opposite, and it is worse, and it took him a while to see it for what it is.

Now, when the heat comes up out of his breastbone, Monty comes *in*. He gets himself under Charles' chest and puts a hand flat over the middle of it and holds it there, for the whole of it, eight or nine minutes, waiting -- and what he is waiting for is not the skin. The skin has told him everything it is ever going to tell him and it is a liar. What he is waiting for is the other thing, the slow flat cold thing under the hand.

Charles gives it to him. Every time. A dozen times a day, and at night, and in the middle of a channel with the sea standing up in short green walls: he goes still inside himself and puts his heart down where it can be found and holds it there until the hand comes off.

And it is not the sentence he built it to be any more. He built it to say *this is not what it looks like*, and it said that, once, off Martinique, and it worked, and he will be proud of that until he dies. But a thing that has been said and understood does not need saying again. What it is now is a reading. He has taught a creature with no capacity for doubt to take his pulse, and it is being taken, every two hours, waking and sleeping, by somebody who has concluded that the number matters and is checking whether it has changed.

Somewhere off Vieux Fort he thinks, with the top of his mind, quite calmly: *I have spent nineteen years learning to hold this so that nobody could get at me, and I have handed it over as a bedside chart.*

He does not mind. That is the thing he turns over afterwards and cannot get to the bottom of. He does not mind in the least.

<hr>

The water changes north of St Vincent.

He sees it before he understands it: the blue goes out of it. Not dirty, not brown like the Indian River -- but greener, and softer, and there is a haze in it at depth so that the shafts of light stop being shafts and become a general glow, and when he pulls a mouthful in and spits it out it is not as brutal on the tongue as it was at Dominica.

River water. Four hundred miles of it, a fortnight old, coming up out of a delta he has never seen and running north-west through the arc in September the way it has every September since before there were islands, and they have swum into the edge of it.

He puts his face against Monty's back and finds him changed.

Not excited -- Monty has no capacity for looking forward to things, as far as Charles can tell, or none that survives translation. What has changed is the *shape* of his attention. All the way down from Antigua it has been a lamp in a cellar: a bright small circle and a great deal of nothing round it. Now the circle has got bigger and, more than that, softer at the edge, so that the world does not stop, it fades -- and in the faded part there are things that are not fish and not rocks and cannot be resolved, and Monty keeps turning his head to them the whole afternoon and getting nothing, and turning back.

Charles knows exactly what he is watching, because he could write out the equation and once did. Fresher water is poorer water, electrically; a poorer conductor lets a field stand up further before it drains away; a hundred feet becomes a hundred and fifty. It is the least dramatic fact in physics and it is the reason the sea is beginning, very slightly, to have people in it.

<hr>

At Bequia a man looks at the water.

It is the first time it has happened since Guadeloupe and Charles is so unprepared for it that he stands in the shallows with a gourd in each hand for a full ten seconds, doing nothing.

They have come down past St Vincent in the night and this is the first of the Grenadines and the last real harbour before the small cays.

The beach is at the south-west end, under the hill, and the boats are up under a shed with palm thatch over them: two of them, open, double-ended, twenty-six feet, painted, beautifully kept, and hauled well up because it is October and nothing will go out in them before February. The gear is stowed along the wall in the shade. Charles knows what he is looking at before he can account for knowing it, and then he accounts for it: there is a line-tub, and coils of warp, and an iron on the wall with a toggle head on it as long as his forearm, and this is a whaling station.

His left hand has gone up to his own shoulder without being sent, and found the lanyard, and closed on it. He makes it let go.

The man is about fifty and is sitting on a keg mending line, and he watches Monty in the shallows for perhaps a minute and a half, unhurriedly, all the way along, the way a man looks at a horse in a field.

"Twenty feet," he says at last. English, after five weeks of French, which is its own small shock. "Bit under."

"Nineteen," Charles says. His own voice sounds strange to him. "Nineteen and a half."

The man nods. "You would not need the big boat for that."

Charles puts the gourds down. He is aware that something is happening to the back of his neck and he does not want it happening while he is holding things.

"No," the man says, without being asked, still mending. "Not here. Nobody here." He ties off and bites the twine and looks up the beach at the shed and the boats and the iron on the wall, and his face does something complicated and closes again. "My father's father took the share out of the water same as everybody. You do not take an iron to that."

"Some people would."

"Yes," the man says. "Some people would."

He goes back to the line. Charles stands there.

"Why does nobody look?" he says. He has wanted to ask it since Guadeloupe and has never once had a candidate. "The whole way down. Nobody looks at the water. They put the things at the tide line and they turn round. You are the first man in three hundred miles who has looked at him."

The man works at a knot for a while.

"Because if you look," he says, "you have seen it."

And Charles, standing in eight inches of water on a beach in the Grenadines, has the entire thing arrive at once: that the etiquette is not a courtesy and never was, that it is not for him and not for Monty, that the whole length of the arc has been arranging, very carefully and at some inconvenience, to be in a position to say afterwards and under oath that they saw nothing whatever -- and that this is why he is safe, and that it is the same reason nobody will ever be able to say a word for him.

"You looked," he says.

"I looked," the man agrees, and shrugs, and gets up, and goes to see about the water.

<hr>

Something answers on the second night after Bequia.

They are lying up off one of the small cays, a low island with a beach on the leeward side and nothing else on it at all, and Charles is at the waterline half asleep with his hand where it always is, and the whole thing takes perhaps four minutes.

What he gets first is Monty going rigid -- the old lock, the full stop, but with his head up and out and turned away to the south-east, and Charles has his face down against him before he has decided to.

And there is somebody there.

He has no other way to put it, then or afterwards. It comes across the contact second-hand, a report of a thing rather than the thing -- the way you hear a room through a wall -- and it is not a picture and not a sound and not a word. It is a wash. It is presence and heat and direction, the exact quality of the thing Monty is and Charles has been swimming inside for five weeks, and it is arriving from fifty yards out in the dark, and it is *not Monty*, and the difference between them is as flat and obvious as the difference between two voices.

Charles lies in four inches of water with his hand on a man's shoulder and the whole back of his neck goes cold, and what he feels is not fear at all, it is something much closer to what a man feels in a foreign city when he hears his own language behind him in the street.

Monty does not call out. Charles is braced for it -- for the buzz, for anything -- and it does not come, and after a while he understands that it would be redundant. There is nothing to announce. Everything either of them is, is already on the table.

The other one comes in to thirty yards. It stops.

It stays there for a long time. And then it goes round them, wide, at a distance, and away south -- and Charles knows the shape of that manoeuvre in his body, because he was lying along this back off Bouillante when this back made it, going round a patch of the world with the information taken out of it.

<hr>

Monty comes up onto the sand as far as he can get and puts his head down on Charles' thigh, which he has never done, and stays there.

Charles does not ask. He has learned that much. He lies in the dark on a beach in the Grenadines with a merman's head on his leg, doing the one thing he is any good at, which is being still with his heart down, and after perhaps an hour it comes.

It is not grief. He is ready for grief and it is not that. What arrives, when it comes, is a picture of a cave -- rock going up, the light coming in green and low from underneath, a floor of pale sand -- and it is a real place, a specific place, a place with the wear of use on it. And there are shapes in it, five, six, lit and separate and known. And then the same cave, and the same light, and three.

And then the same cave, and the light, and nothing in it at all.

Charles takes a while to be certain, because he has trained himself half to death not to supply meanings, and he can hear himself wanting to supply this one. But it comes twice more and it is the same both times and he did not build any part of it.

"Yes," he says, out loud, into the dark, to a language that cannot carry it. "Yes. I know. We know that too."

<hr>

She is put up the next evening, and it is the only wholly good hour in four hundred miles.

They are in the shallows in the last of the light in one of the small cays where the water goes over sand so pale that even Charles can see the bottom without his glasses. Charles is on his back with his eyes shut. Monty takes his face in both hands, which he has done a hundred times, and holds him -- and then puts her up, deliberately, and presents her, and waits.

<i>-- something small and folded and violet, the violet at the bottom of a flame, ruffled all round the edge like a fan half closed, and along the ruffle a line of gold; and it moves the way a curtain moves in a doorway; and it is a person --</i>

He has had her before. He had her in the pen, with his hand on a wet shoulder and a very great deal else arriving at the same time, and somewhere in the middle of it he had gone hunting for a word and found his cupboards bare; and his brain, which cannot tolerate an unlabelled object for more than about a second and a half, had done what brains do and handed him the nearest thing it had in stock.

*Iris.*

It was not right. It is still not right. It is not going to be right, not tonight and not in thirty years. But it is what there is, and Monty has had five weeks of finding it funny, and here she is again, put up on purpose, in shallow water, at the end of the arc, in a sea she might actually be in.

And because he is pleased with himself, and because being handed a person is not the same as being told about one, he sends it back.

A garden in Fife. A cold wall. A clump of bearded irises with the rain on them, purple going to violet at the fall, and the gold beard running up the middle of each one, standing about so high, in the air, in the wind, in Scotland.

And Monty comes apart.

There is no other way to describe it. The whole length of him goes loose in the water, and the amusement comes off him like heat off a wall and keeps coming -- and the amusement is old, the amusement has been there since the pen, but that was a man who had been told the name of a joke. He has now been shown the picture. And then he makes a sound -- out loud, in air, half above the surface, a rapid descending stutter of clicks that Charles has heard exactly twice before, both times in the pen, both times noted in the log in his own capitals as *vocalisation, function unknown*.

Charles starts laughing without knowing why, which takes about eight seconds to become the hardest laughing he has done since he was a boy, in eight inches of water, in the dark, holding on to a mangrove root so as not to go under.

It is a quarter of an hour before he works out the joke, and when he does he has to lie down again.

He has sent her a picture of a thing that lives in air. He has named a woman after a version of herself that would drown, and has been doing it cheerfully for five weeks.

<hr>

Later, with his cheek against Monty's shoulder and the fireflies going in the scrub above the beach, he asks about her the only way he can, which is to hold the violet thing up in his own head and wait.

And she comes back with two small ones beside her.

He knows who they are. He has known since the pen -- her sister's, no names yet, only the click-and-vocalisation tags that stand in until later -- and he had the storm itself from underneath, for an hour, with his eyes shut, on the fourth afternoon in the room. So there is no new fact in any of it. What is new is where they are being put. Forward. Ahead. In the water they are going to.

He asks, badly, whether she is where they are going.

Yes.

"Good," Charles says. "Good."

And he has had, since the flood, since the wedding, since before he had any business having it, the other half of that: that the thing between the two of them went out during the nine months, gradually, the way a fire does when nobody is in the room, and that Monty called and called into it and got nothing back. Charles has known that for six weeks.

He decides, lying in the shallows on a sand cay in the Grenadines, on no evidence whatever, having read a great deal about albatrosses and nothing at all about this, that a bond which has thinned is not the same animal as a bond which has been cut, and that a thing that went out can be lit.

He is aware that he has decided it rather than found it. He puts that where he puts things.

And he lies on his back looking at the top of the mangrove, and something begins to assemble itself in him, quietly, without having been sent for, the way the good ideas always come.
