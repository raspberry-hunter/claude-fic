The rope begins to eat him on the second morning.

It is not dramatic. That is what he wants to record, if there were anywhere to record it: nothing about the body coming apart is dramatic, it is only steady. A rope under the arms in salt water for nine hours does not cut. It softens the skin and then rubs the softened skin and then the softened skin goes, in lines -- one under each arm and one across the breastbone -- and by the second morning all three are raw and by the third they have a shine on them.

He greases them. He has three-quarters of a tin of zinc paste and a jar of coconut oil from the old woman on the beach at Port-Louis and he works both in with two fingers before he gets in, every time, and it helps for about an hour.

The advantage -- and there is one, an enormous one -- is that he does not have to hold on. He had not understood, until the rope, how much of his mind had been going into his hands. Now the harness takes the load and his hands are free, and by the end of the first day with it he has worked out that he can lie along Monty's back with one arm over and his cheek against the skin between the shoulder blades and simply be carried, and that in this position his whole face and half his chest are in contact, and that the pictures come through so much stronger that he has to shut his eyes. The floor comes up with them. It is there whenever Monty is and it never once does anything, and it is the only thing on this journey he does not think to put a number on.

So on the balance sheet: three open wounds, and the best hour of his life so far.

<hr>

He learns about the sleeping on the first night ashore.

They stop at a beach south of Port-Louis where the coast goes low and dull, cane down to the sand and nobody about. He hauls out at dusk in the last of the light and lies in four inches of warm water at the very top of the swash with the harness still on, because taking it off means putting it on again, and Monty comes in as far as he can, which is not far, and settles in a foot and a half of water two feet away, and puts one hand out and rests it on Charles' ankle.

Charles means to keep watch. He has decided this on the way in, sensibly: one of them ought to.

He is unconscious in under four minutes.

He wakes once, some hours later, with the water at his hip and his mouth full of salt and a cramp coming up his calf, and he lies very still in the dark trying to work out what has changed, because something enormous has changed. It takes him a long time. It takes him so long that he nearly goes under again before he gets there.

It is that Monty is quiet.

Not absent -- the hand is still on his ankle and the contact is live and he can feel him perfectly well. But the thing that has been running underneath everything since the reef, the huge unsteady pressure of an animal watching in all directions at once, has gone down to something slow and enormous and half-lit, like a house with the lights off in most of it.

He has never felt this before. In eleven days in the pen he never once felt this. He had assumed -- everyone had assumed, it is in the record, it is in Pascual's report and in Reggie's summary and in Charles' own hand -- that the specimen rested at night.

The specimen has not rested since January.

Charles lies in the swash on a beach in Guadeloupe with his skin in ribbons and does the arithmetic, and it is very simple and it takes him about four seconds, and then he cries for a while, quietly, in a way he has not since he was a child, because there is nothing else available to do with it.

Then he goes back to sleep. That is the other half of it, and it is worse: he is so tired that he cries and goes straight back to sleep, and the last thing he feels is the hand tightening very slightly on his ankle as he goes down, as if catching him.

<hr>

On the third day they come to the mangrove at the middle of the island, and Charles has to be a jailer again.

The chart is no use to him -- he has looked at it now, ashore, with his glasses on, and it is a proper Admiralty chart of the Leewards with pencil in the margins, and the pencil is the thing he cannot look at for long. But he does not need the chart for this, because he can see it: the two wings of the island almost meeting, and between them a low green gap where the mangrove is, and the water going in through it, brown and still.

The Rivière Salée. Six miles of mangrove channel, cutting straight through the middle of the island, and out the other side.

He knows what it will save them. Round the outside of Basse-Terre is a day and a half: west about the head of the island, and then the whole leeward length of it. Through the middle is an afternoon.

He knows this. Monty knows something else, and gets it into him before Charles can arrange his face.

<i>-- brown water with the light going down into it in rods, roots coming down like a hundred legs, silt, the taste of leaves, and every root and every fish and every crab in it lit and separate and audible at forty feet, at eighty feet, the way water is supposed to be, the way water has not been since --</i>

"No," Charles says. "Monty. No."

Because he has also seen the other thing, and Monty cannot see it and has no apparatus for seeing it: what is at the far end of it. The cranes at Pointe-à-Pitre. The banana boats and the lighters and the customs launch and the whole functioning port of the French Antilles sitting across the mouth they would have to come out of, and a road bridge over the narrows on the way, with a hundred people a day standing on it looking down at the water.

The channel is not a shortcut. It is a funnel with a town at the neck.

He tries to send it. He tries for a full minute, hanging on with both arms, pushing the image of the crane and the bridge and the men on the bridge as hard as he can, and it goes out of him and something comes back and the something is not agreement, it is a kind of patient incomprehension, and under the incomprehension is the mangrove again, the brown water, the rods of light, *please* --

There is no *please*. There is no word for please. He is supplying it. He knows he is supplying it, he can hear himself supplying it, he supplies it anyway.

"We're going round," he says, out loud, in his flattest voice, the one he used to use in the pen. "We're going round, and it's another day and a half, and I'm sorry."

And they go round; Monty turns west when Charles pulls his shoulder, because Monty is not going to leave him on a beach in order to swim up a river. He turns, and they go round, and he does not sulk -- there is no sulking in him, that is a human thing and it requires a story about being wronged -- but for the whole of that afternoon the pictures stop, and Charles is towed in silence along a coast he cannot see, with his cheek against a back that has gone as unreadable as concrete.

He thinks: this is the pen. I have done the pen again, at sea, out of doors, for good reasons.

He thinks: at least in the pen I could tell myself it was Reggie.

Some time before dark the pictures come back on, all at once and without ceremony -- a turtle, of all things, an ordinary hawksbill going about its business twenty feet down, offered up to him like a cup of tea -- and Charles puts his forehead against Monty's back and is undone by it far more thoroughly than he was by the refusal.

<hr>

They know at Deshaies. That is the first sign of it.

He hauls out on a black-sand beach at the north-west corner in the afternoon, meaning to fill the gourds and go, and there is a boy sitting on the wall who does not run away and does not stare, and who gets up when Charles is thirty yards off and walks away up the track without hurrying, and comes back eleven minutes later with a man and a woman and a bucket of fresh water and a pot of something to eat, all of which are set down at the top of the beach, after which all three of them turn round and go and stand facing the road.

Nobody has spoken to him. Nobody has looked at the sea.

He eats standing up, out of the pot, with his hands, and it is fish and breadfruit and he has to make himself slow down, and when he has finished he says thank you to three backs.

The woman says, without turning round, in careful French: "They said you were a big man."

"Who said?"

"The boat from Port-Louis. On Tuesday." A pause; and he can hear her taking her time about it, all six foot of him, the sores and the rope and the rest. "You are not a big man."

"No," Charles agrees, and does not ask what sort of big she means, because he can guess, and because she is right either way.

"You should have gone through the Salée," she says. "It is quicker." And then all three of them walk up the road, and one of the men laughs at something the other one says, and the sound of it goes on for a while after they are out of sight.

Charles sits down on the sand with the empty pot in his hands.

Tuesday. He came ashore at Port-Louis on Monday afternoon. There is a sloop that does the coast, and it does six knots day and night, and Monty does three and stops to look at things.

It has taken the story two days to get ahead of them, and it is not going to fall behind again. Somewhere south of here, at every beach on four hundred miles of island chain, people are already being told; and when he arrives they will already know what he is; and there is no version of the next two months in which he is not, at every landfall, expected.

He should be frightened. He works at it for a while and cannot manage it. What he feels instead, sitting on black sand with a stranger's pot in his hands, is a kind of vertigo, because a thing has got loose in the world that he did not make and cannot stop, and it is going down the islands faster than he is, using the trade winds, and its subject is him.

<hr>

Off Bouillante there is a place where the sea goes hot and dead.

He feels Monty stop -- a real stop, the old kind, four hundred yards of open water short of the shore -- and then edge forward, and then swing wide. The water there is noticeably warm, warmer than the sea has any business being, and it stinks of eggs, and there is a milkiness in it that Charles can see even without his glasses.

Volcanic. The whole spine of Basse-Terre is a volcano and it leaks; there will be springs coming up through the seabed here, sulphurous, mineral, hot.

And salt-heavy water full of dissolved minerals will carry a current the way a copper wire does, which is to say it will carry it straight to earth and nowhere else.

<i>-- nothing. Not silence. Not dark. A patch of the world with the information taken out of it, the shape of a place where things should be and are not --</i>

Monty goes round it at a distance of two hundred yards, and does not want to be talked to for a while afterwards, and Charles, who has just been given the nearest thing to a description of the pen that he will ever receive, does not try.

<hr>

At Les Saintes they are waiting on the beach with the gear laid out.

Terre-de-Haut: a dry brown rock of an island south of Basse-Terre, fifteen hundred people, a great many of them descended from Breton and Norman fishermen who came out three hundred years ago and largely kept to themselves, so that Charles hauls himself up the sand at Anse du Bourg in front of a dozen white men in straw hats speaking a French he can barely follow, all of whom are looking somewhere else.

The gear is at the waterline. He goes along it on his knees, because his legs have stopped being reliable, and it is like reading a letter from someone who knows exactly what is going to happen to him.

A breaker -- a proper ship's-boat cask, hooped, four gallons, with a becket on it. Two calabash gourds, dried and stoppered and lashed on a length of line at the shoulders. A pot of grease, not coconut oil, something heavier and yellow. A roll of soft cloth. And a harness: not a rope bridle like the fisherman's, but a made thing, canvas-backed where it goes under the arms, sewn, with a bight forward and a lanyard running back to a wooden toggle at his left shoulder.

An old man crouches beside him and puts a hand on the toggle and says something. Charles gets perhaps half.

The old man says it again, more slowly. Then he takes hold of Charles' left wrist and puts Charles' hand on the toggle and pulls, and the whole harness comes apart and off Charles like a coat.

"Ah," Charles says.

They make him do it forty times.

That is not a figure of speech; he counts, at first, and gives up somewhere in the thirties. Standing in the shallows. Kneeling. Lying face down in eighteen inches of water with someone's foot in the small of his back. With his eyes shut. With his eyes shut and his arm through the bight the wrong way. With the toggle deliberately fouled under a strap so that he has to find it by feel and clear it first. Over and over, while the light goes orange, until his hand goes to the toggle before he has decided to send it there.

Nobody explains why.

He understands perfectly well why. They are teaching him to be able to let go of Monty in the dark, instantly, without deciding to, in the two seconds available if Monty is taken or dives or panics or is killed. They have thought about it more clearly than he has ever allowed himself to, and they have built the answer into the equipment, and they are drilling him in it on a beach in the evening with children watching from the wall.

Somebody stands in the sea for an hour making a man practise letting go of the thing he came for.

When it is done the old man says one more sentence, in which Charles catches only *maybe* and *quick*, and gets up, and goes.

<hr>

The woman who brings the grease looks at him one second too long.

He is out of the water and half out of his shirt, working the old cloth off the raw places under his arms, and he sees her decide not to look away, and go on not looking away, and take him in properly: the three lines, the shine on them, the shape of him, the pale seam under the left side of his chest that is seven years old and is not a rope burn and could not be mistaken for one by anybody who has ever seen a body.

Then she hands him the pot.

"Every time," she says, in French, tapping the pot. "Before, not after. Before."

"Yes," Charles says.

"Before." She looks at the three raw lines and not at anything else, now, and her face is disgusted in the way of a woman looking at a job someone else has bodged. "And the cloth under. Two thicknesses. You are not a fish."

"No," Charles says.

That is all of it. She goes away up the beach. She will die in about thirty years without having told anybody, and the reason will not be kindness exactly, and Charles will think about her for the rest of his life.

<hr>

The chart comes out that night, under a lamp, in a shed they have put him in.

He has been carrying it for six days and has looked at it twice and both times only for the top corner. Now he unrolls the whole thing and flattens it with the lamp and a stone, and goes down it, island by island, in Jean's handwriting.

*Reef opens here.* *Fresh water.* *Keep clear -- ordnance.* Those he knew.

But there is far more of it than he had thought, and it goes much further south than he had thought, all the way down past Grenada into the corner of the sheet, and it is not annotation, it is preparation. The Saintes passage is marked with an arrow and a note about the set of the current, which the old man told him this afternoon in almost the same words. The Dominica Channel has a figure for the crossing distance. There is a pencil ring round the north end of Dominica and, inside it, in her small upright hand: *river here -- brackish 2 mi up -- best rest on the whole road.*

She did the work. Somebody sat in the drawing office at the Martin with the Admiralty sheets and the pilot and worked out how a man might get down the arc, and wrote it in pencil so that it could be denied, and gave it to Reggie to give to him, and let Reggie hand it over in a tobacco tin without a word about who had made it.

Which is the third time. He counts them, sitting in the shed. The plates for the Alpha paper. The tables in Reggie's report. This.

He gets as far as *Dear Jean* on the back of a sheet of the wrapping paper, and looks at it for a long time, and then thinks about Spilsbury, and what a letter with a Saintes postmark would do, and burns it in the lamp.

<hr>

They give him fish for the crossing.

Two of them come down in the morning with a basket -- four good-sized fish, gutted, fresh, wet -- and he understands the reasoning at once and it makes him feel ill. Monty has not eaten properly since Antigua. He cannot hunt and tow, and he has been towing eleven hours a day for six days, and the ribs are showing now on both sides in a way that they were not showing at the reef, and Charles has been watching it happen and has had no way at all to fix it.

And there is nowhere to carry a basket of fish except on the man.

He says so, in his bad French, and one of them shrugs and says something that means *yes, and?*, and the other one says a longer thing of which Charles gets the word for blood, and the word for smell, and a word he does not know but which everyone on the beach clearly does, because the boy on the wall stops swinging his legs.

"Yes," Charles says. "I know."

They lash the basket to the breaker, which floats, which means the fish tows behind him on a line rather than lying against his skin; that is the concession, and they are pleased with it, and it is a real one. Then they look at the eastern sky for a while and confer.

There is a swell running. It has been running since yesterday: long, slow, glassy, coming up out of the south-east from something that is happening several hundred miles away and is nobody's business here. The wind is nothing. The sky is clear.

The old man says: today, or the third day.

Charles looks at the sky, which tells him nothing, and at the swell, which tells him nothing either, and at the old man, who has been reading this water since before Charles was born.

"Today," he says.

<hr>

They come at the fifth hour, out in the middle where the bottom is nine hundred fathoms and there is nothing to look at but the two islands, one behind and one ahead, and neither of them getting appreciably nearer.

Monty stops. Charles is expecting the old stop, the flush-stop, and he is halfway into being ashamed before he understands that it is not that at all, because Monty says a word.

An actual word. Out loud. In the air, half above the surface: a hard descending buzz with a click at the front of it, and Charles has heard it before -- on a tape, in a hut on Crabbs Cay, in 1954, with Reggie's finger on the reel and Reggie saying *there, that's the interesting one* -- and it is item four in a published paper, and it means:

Danger.

That is all it means. That is the entire content. Not what. Not where, not how many, not how far, not how fast, not whether to run or hold still or get out of the water, because there is no water to get out of, there is nothing for eleven miles in any direction but the sea and the sky and the fish on the line behind him.

"Where?" Charles says. "Monty. *Where?*"

<i>-- danger --</i>

He gets his face down against Monty's back, and the contact opens, and it is worse than the word, because now he can feel Monty's attention going out and round and down like a lamp in a cellar and coming back with nothing he can use: the world, lit; six or seven separate large moving sparks somewhere below and behind, meaning nothing to Charles, meaning everything to Monty, and no way whatever to get the difference across the gap between them. Monty knows the range and the depth and the size and the number and the intention of every one of them, and can say *danger*, and cannot say anything else, and never will be able to.

Charles' hand goes to the toggle. He is not aware of sending it there.

He takes it off the toggle, deliberately, and puts it flat on Monty's back, and says, "All right. All right. Go on, then."

And Monty goes on. That is what happens: nothing happens. They swim for two more hours with the sparks below and behind, and once something bumps the breaker hard enough to knock the whole rig sideways and Charles hears his own voice make a sound he has never made before, and then it is gone, and the fish are still there, and the word is not said again.

Once in those two hours the heat comes up through him and his skin goes over; and this time Monty does something new. He does not lock and ease off. He comes in close, up under Charles' chest, and hangs there for a few seconds -- and then drops back and away and hangs there instead, at the end of Charles' arm, and then comes in again, watching, testing, the way a man will move a lamp about to see which way a shadow falls.

Charles, in the middle of the Dominica Channel with sharks under him and his skin on fire and his hands shaking, reads it as Monty checking whether he is about to die.

It is not that. It is not anything like that. But he will go on thinking it for another fortnight, and by the time he stops thinking it he will have stopped being able to remember when he started.

<hr>

Dominica comes up out of the sea like a wall.

He has seen mountains. He has not seen anything like this: it goes up and up, green-black and vertical, cloud sitting on the top of it and dragging down the ridges in rags, and the whole thing is wet, visibly wet, from a distance of nine miles, in a way no island he has ever seen has been wet.

Water, he thinks. That is what wet means. That is a hundred rivers.

They come in past the two humps at the north end and round into a great open bay, and the light goes off the water and the land takes it, and there are canoes drawn up under the coconuts and smoke going straight up, and a smell coming off the shore that is green and rotten and enormous.

At the southern corner of the bay the mangrove starts, and there is a gap in it, and out of the gap comes a slow brown pour of river water, sitting on top of the sea in a great fan with a visible line round the edge of it.

Monty stops at the line.

Not the locked stop. He simply stops, with his head half out and his mouth a little open, in the way of a man arriving at his own front door six months late.

"Go on," Charles says. His voice has gone. "It's all right. Jean says it's all right."

They cross the line. The water round Charles' chest turns from clear to brown and from cool to warm, and the light in it changes, and there are leaves in it, whole leaves, and a stick, and the roots start on both sides.

And at eighty yards up, in among the mangrove with the birds going up in front of them, in water that is two parts river to one part sea, Monty turns round in the channel, ten feet away, not touching him at all --

<i>-- and says his name.</i>

Not the sound. The other thing: the whole assembled picture of him, the-fan-lovely-when-there-but-who-disappears-into-its-hole, arriving across ten feet of open water as clearly as if there were a hand on his face.

Charles stands up. He can stand up; it is four feet deep here, and the mud comes over his ankles, and he stands in the middle of a mangrove river on the north end of Dominica with a rope harness on and four fish on a line and cannot for a moment do anything at all.

"Say it again," he says.

Monty says it again.
