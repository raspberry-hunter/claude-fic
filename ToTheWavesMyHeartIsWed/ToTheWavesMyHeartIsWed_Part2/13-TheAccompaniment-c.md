The watch stops on the second night out of Dominica.

He notices it in the morning, in the ruins at Saint-Pierre, sitting on a piece of wall that was a house fifty-two years ago: the hands at ten past four, the glass gone milky inside with a bead of water rolling about in it, the whole thing dead as a stone. Salt and six days of immersion. He is surprised only that it lasted this long.

He puts it back in his pocket anyway, because throwing it away would be a decision.

So from Martinique onwards he counts. He counts strokes, and he counts breaths, and when he wants something finer than breaths he counts his own pulse against the inside of his wrist, which is a thing he can do to four or five hundred without losing the place, and always could, and used to do in bed as a child instead of the other thing.

That is how it gets solved. Not by insight. By a man with a broken watch counting his own pulse in the dark because he cannot bear to be inside his own head without a number in it.

<hr>

But first there are four nights, and they are the worst of his life, and that includes the ward in 1947.

It goes like this. It has gone like this since Antigua, but it has been getting worse, and out of Dominica it becomes the entire content of the world, because they are back to open water with a hundred fathoms under them and there is nothing else to attend to.

The heat comes up out of his breastbone. His face and scalp prickle and stream. And Monty locks -- every muscle, dead stop, head coming round -- and then eases him off, gently, unmistakably, and holds him at the end of his own arm and tows him by the wrist.

And the room shuts.

Every three hours. Then every two. Then, on the third night out of Dominica, at an interval he could set a watch by if he had a watch, and it stops being a series of events and becomes the weather.

And what Charles does in the gaps -- in the dark, in the open ocean, at the end of an arm, with the pictures gone and only the wash of him left, presence and heat and no content whatsoever -- is think.

He thinks: he can read me.

He thinks: he has been reading me for eleven days at contact, all night, every night, with his hands on my face and my chest against his back, in a channel that carries body state whole and cannot be lied to. He knows what my heart does. He knows what my skin does. He knows every single thing about the inside of me that I have spent twenty-seven years arranging so that nobody would ever know it, and he has had it, continuously, for a fortnight, without my permission and without the smallest possibility of my preventing it.

And on the fourth night the sentence comes all the way out, the one he has been walking round since Guadeloupe:

*He has found out what I am, and he is being kind about it.*

And of course being towed by the wrist is what kind looks like. Of course it is. It is exactly what Charles himself would do; it is what everybody has always done, the small courteous withdrawal, the extra foot of air, the way the men at the Martin used to make room for him at a table with the most beautiful manners in the world.

His skin lights up all over again, and this time it is not the flush at all, and there is nothing in the world he can do to stop it going out of him at a foot and a half through conducting seawater into a nervous system that reads terror the way a barometer reads weather.

<hr>

And on the other end of the arm, in perfect good faith and with perfect accuracy:

Every two hours, in the middle of a black passage, the lit thing on his back goes off like a torch. Every two hours it screams -- not sound, the other thing, the whole body screaming at once, the way a fish screams when it is taken, the way a calf screams -- and it is screaming at the nearest large animal, which is him.

He has been through the entire repertoire. He has slowed. He has gone still and made himself small, which is what you do for a frightened creature, and which any of his own people would have understood inside a second. He has put distance in, because distance in salt water is mercy, it is the whole point of salt water, it is how you let someone alone.

And every time he gives the man room the man burns worse.

He has no framework for this and no way to get one. He is a creature to whom the interior of another being is simply *available*, and the interior of this one has become a thing that lights up at intervals in terror at the sight of him and cannot be soothed by any of the four things that soothe terror.

So he does the only thing left, which is to keep swimming south and go on being gentle and stop expecting it to work.

<hr>

The counting starts on the fifth night, off the Martinique coast where the land is a black wall with three lights on it, and it starts for no better reason than that Charles is at the end of his arm again with nothing to do.

He counts his pulse. He gets to five hundred and a little over, and Monty draws him back in and the room opens and he loses the thread, and he thinks, idly, with the top of his mind: *eight or nine minutes, about.*

Then, because he is who he is: *eight or nine minutes, and how long since the last one?*

He does not have an answer. He has never once had an answer, because the only data he has ever collected out here has been about Monty. So he builds himself a rule out of the only material available, which is that five hundred beats is one count, and one count is a withdrawal, and a withdrawal is eight or nine minutes. Then he starts fresh from the moment the room opens and holds it all the way through the towing and the dark and the small talk of pictures, bending a finger down at every five hundred, losing the place twice and beginning again -- and comes to the end of his fingers, and is still going, and that is the first news of the night.

Fifteen counts and part of a sixteenth. Something over two hours.

He does it again.

He does it again, and again, and by the fourth repetition his hands have gone cold in the warm sea, because the numbers are wrong. They are wrong in the specific way that has been undoing his own arguments in his own head since he was fifteen. The interval is not Monty's. It has nothing to do with the sea or the depth or the sharks or the distance run or the time of night.

The interval is his.

It is exactly, precisely the interval he has been keeping since July -- the one he has been apologising for to nobody, sweating through in his hut in the dark, and privately, contemptibly, thinking of as his own business.

Monty is not withdrawing from him.

Monty is responding to a hot flush.

<hr>

It takes about ten seconds to go from that to the whole of it, and he spends those ten seconds hanging in the black water making a sound he is not aware of making.

Because he is a physiologist, and he has been one all along, and the mechanism assembles itself in front of him with the horrible tidiness of something that was always going to.

A vasomotor flush is not a mood. It is a whole-body autonomic event: the skin flushes, the eccrine glands dump, the skin's electrical properties go over a cliff in about eight seconds -- and there is exactly one other thing in the entire human repertoire that does the same to the same instrument on the same timescale, and it is terror. The instrument cannot tell them apart. The instrument was never built to tell them apart, because in five million years no merfolk has ever had to distinguish a marine mammal's fright from a menopausal woman's thermoregulation, and there is no reason on God's earth why one should.

He is the only human being alive who knows what his own skin has been saying.

He is also the only human being alive who has no way whatever of saying so.

He tries anyway. He gets his face against Monty's back and he tries for perhaps twenty minutes, and what he has to work with is pictures, and what he needs to convey is a negation and a cause: *it is not fear, it is not you, it is a thing my body does, it is because of a drug I stopped taking in a drawer in June.* There is no picture of *not*. There is no picture of *because*. He puts up a flame and gets back alarm. He puts up himself burning and gets back worse. Every single thing he can build out of images makes it more frightening, because images are all edges and no grammar, and the only grammar in the channel is the body, and his body is a liar that cannot be corrected.

Somewhere off the Anses-d'Arlet, in the small hours, Charles has the experience -- quite calmly, in the middle of everything else -- of understanding that he is going to have to solve this without language, and that he has about eight days, and that the whole of the rest of his life is going to be this exact problem in different costumes.

<hr>

Then he thinks of the one thing he can actually do.

It is not clever. It is the oldest thing he owns.

He cannot stop his skin. But there is one channel in his whole body that answers to him, and he has had it since he was eight years old: the flat, slow, deliberate thing he does to his own heart. He built it in a bed in Fife, listening to somebody coming up the stairs, and he has used it in a headmaster's study, and in a room in 1947 in front of three men who were deciding whether to give him what he had asked for, and in Reggie's office in July, and every human being who has ever met him has looked at the outside of it and concluded that he was cold.

A body in terror has a screaming skin *and* a hammering heart. That is what fear is; the two go together; they have gone together in every frightened animal in the history of the sea.

A screaming skin over a heart at fifty is not a frightened animal. It is not anything. There is no such creature.

He cannot say *I am not afraid of you*. He can, a dozen times a day, produce a physiological sentence that does not parse as fear, and let a calibrating instrument do the rest.

So he starts, off the south end of Martinique, and it is work of a sort he has never had to do before, because the flush is coming, and he can feel it building for about a second and a half before it lands, and what he has to do in that second and a half is go absolutely still inside himself, drop it, hold it -- while burning, while soaked, while frightened for real about the fact that it might not work, and the fear of it not working is itself the thing that will make it not work.

The first four times he loses it.

The fifth time he holds it for nine seconds.

<hr>

Monty does not reason about any of this. Monty has never reasoned about anything in his life and would not know what it was for.

What he does is what his kind have done since before there were islands here: he tests.

He comes in close, right up under the man's chest, contact, during. It does not get worse.

He drops back to three feet, four, the mercy distance. It does not stop.

He goes utterly still, which is the strongest thing he has, the thing that stops fights and settles calves. It comes anyway, at its interval, unbothered.

He hunts on the fourth night and it comes while he is forty feet away and there is nothing anywhere near the man at all.

And it comes when the man is asleep. That is the one that finishes it: the man lying at the waterline at Grande Anse d'Arlet with his hand fallen over Monty's shoulder, absolutely and completely out -- a thing Monty still cannot look at without something turning over in his chest, a whole nervous system going down at once and staying down, undefended, in the open, on purpose -- and the burning arrives on schedule, right through the sleep, into no one, at nothing.

Fear points at something. Every fear that has ever existed points at something.

This does not point at anything.

It takes him five nights and he never once has a thought about it, and at the end of the five nights he has arrived -- by testing, the way a body arrives at a thing -- at the exact and total truth available to him:

*It is not me.*

*Something is burning that man from the inside, on a clock, and it is not the sea and it is not the sharks and it is not me. And it is killing him.*

Which is correct in every particular.

<hr>

He answers off St Lucia.

They are lying up in a bay under the mountains -- black sand, the land going up nearly vertical behind, one lamp somewhere in the trees -- and it is late, and Charles is in the shallows at the edge with the harness off and the sores dressed and his hand where it always is, half asleep.

And Monty comes in and takes Charles' face in both hands, which he has done perhaps a hundred times, and this time does not let go, and holds him there, and sings.

Charles has heard it before. Twice: once in the pen, patchy, and only ever the half of it, before he understood anything at all; and once at the wedding, in pieces, inside the flood. He has never heard the whole of it and he did not know there was a whole of it.

It is a made thing. That is the first fact and it goes through him like cold water. It has a shape and the shape was decided; there are parts that come back, and parts that come back changed, and a place near the end where the whole structure leans and then rights itself; it is not a call and it is not weather, it is a composition, and somebody composed it, and the somebody is holding his head.

And Charles lies in eight inches of water at the edge of the Caribbean and hears it whole, and it is the most beautiful thing that has ever happened to him, and about a third of the way through he stops being able to hear it cleanly, because there is something under it.

It is the floor.

He has been carrying the floor since the first night. He has had it under his cheek for three weeks and filed it, on the reef, in about four seconds, as open water -- as depth, as the noise a sea makes when there is a hundred fathoms of it -- because it never did anything and never stopped, and a thing that does not vary is a thing he has never once in his life been able to keep his attention on.

The sea has nothing to say. It never did.

Because a song comes with its accompaniment. That is the whole of what a song is, in this channel -- one voluntary line laid over an involuntary body, and the body is playing underneath it the entire time and cannot stop. Every merfolk who has ever listened to one has been getting both at once and has never once got them as two, because there has never been anybody in the water whose body was not doing it, and a thing with no silence to be heard against is not heard at all.

And underneath it Monty is terrified.

Not of the man. Charles can hear that clearly now and it is the only mercy in the thing. Of everything else. Of the water ahead. Of the sharks he can feel and cannot name and has one word for. Of the ribs coming up under his own skin. Of some other thing further back, in a cave, that Charles cannot see the shape of and does not think he is meant to. Of arriving. Of arriving *carrying this*. And, threaded through all of it and coming up again and again like the returning part of the tune: the man on his back, going quiet in stages, dying in some manner Monty cannot identify, at a rate Monty can measure exactly and cannot slow.

He has been carrying that for two hundred miles. He has been carrying it since the reef, underneath everything, while being gentle, while towing, while stopping to look at turtles.

And he has been singing over it.

And Charles understands, lying there with a webbed hand on either side of his face, why it is him and nobody else.

It is not the hand over the mouth. That is the part he knew about, and it explains nothing whatever, because a man who keeps his voice down keeps the whole of it down. Monty holding himself back would have handed him both lines faint and together, and he would have had the same song with the same body under it, thinner, and no way on earth of knowing there were two.

It is the wall.

He does the physics on his back in eight inches of water and it comes out clean and he cannot make it come out any other way. The accompaniment is a body: low, and standing, and unstructured, and always on. The song is a made thing: sharp, and changing, and full of edges. Put the pair of them in a box of grounded steel and the box does not turn them down together. It sits on the standing one and takes it to earth, and goes on taking it, all day, every day, for nine months -- and it cannot get its hands on the other one quickly enough. So that what crossed eight feet of pumped seawater into a man sitting on the ledge with his fingers in it was a line with nothing underneath it at all.

He heard the top of a song for weeks and thought he was hearing a song.

And so he is the only creature in the history of either species who has ever had the line without the body under it, and knows what it sounds like on its own, and can hold the one against the other and get a difference.

He is in a position to measure the distance between what somebody chose to say and what he could not help saying, which is precisely, exactly, what a song has always been for, and which no merfolk has ever been able to do for another. It took a concrete box to make it possible.

And he lies absolutely still, and lets his heart go flat and slow, and holds it there.

Because the one thing he must not do -- now, tonight, ever -- is let this creature find out that the accompaniment came through.

So they lie in the shallows under the mountains, the two of them, one singing over his terror and one governing his heart over his grief, each of them competently and deliberately hiding the truth from someone who cannot be lied to; and each of them knows the other is doing it; and neither of them stops; and it is, of everything that either of them will ever have, the closest thing to tenderness.

The song comes back to its beginning and leans and rights itself and ends.

"Again," Charles says, out loud, in the dark, into a language nobody in it can hear. "Please. Again."

And whether it is the word or the wash of him or nothing at all, Monty sings it again.
