The first six hours are the best swimming of the entire journey and he knows at the time that this is a trick.

The sea is long and easy under the swell, the trades are astern, there is a moon at about a third and enough of it to put a shine on the backs of the waves, and Monty settles into a rate Charles has not felt since the first night out of Antigua and holds it, hour after hour, without a stop. The breaker rides sweet on its line. The gourds are quiet. Nothing hurts more than it hurt yesterday.

He lies along the back he has been lying along for five weeks with his cheek in the place where the skin is thinnest and lets it be easy, and he does not for one second believe in it. He has read the accounts. Everybody who has ever gone over the side of a ship has said the same thing about the first six hours.

Grenada goes out behind them at about half past five, and he cannot see it go. He only knows because the wash of the land in Monty's attention -- the shelf, the fish, the great crowded lit shallow noise of an island -- thins and thins and then is not there, and what is left is the two of them and nine hundred fathoms.

<hr>

The sun comes up at ten past six and it is the enemy, and he has known it would be, and he has never in his life properly understood a thing by knowing it.

There is no shade at sea. That is the whole sentence. There is nowhere to put your face, and the water throws it back up at you as well, so that he takes the whole of it twice over -- on the top of the head and the back of the neck and under the chin and inside the nose -- and the zinc goes on and sweats off and goes on again and by nine o'clock the new tin is a third gone.

He drinks at seven, and at nine, and at eleven, and he worked the schedule out on paper on the beach and he holds to it, and holding to it is the hardest sustained act of will he has ever performed, harder than the flush, harder than the log. A pint on the hour of it, whether or not. He is not thirsty. He has not been properly thirsty since Carriacou, which is itself the thing to be frightened about, and the colour is the colour, and the mouth wants nothing at all and the mouth does not get a vote.

At noon there is a thing he was not expecting, which is that there is nothing to look at.

He had thought about thirst and the sun and the sharks. He had not thought about the fact that for twelve hours there will be no object anywhere in the world, no bird, no line, no mark, nothing but a wobbling glare in every direction and a back under his chest, and that a mind with no purchase will go looking for something to do and will find, in the store, only itself.

He counts. He had promised himself on the beach that he would not and he lasts until about one o'clock.

<hr>

The current is running west at a knot and a half and he has understood this since Carriacou and can do nothing about it whatever.

That is the whole of the navigation and it is not his. Eighty-five miles south with a knot and a half of the Atlantic pushing them sideways the entire way means Trinidad is not where it is; Trinidad is somewhere over there, and if you swim at where it is you go past the west end of it altogether -- and what is out there, if the set has been kind, is sixty miles of Venezuelan mountain lying across the top of the peninsula, and if it has not been kind the Caribbean does not put anything else in front of you for six hundred miles. There is no correcting afterwards, because there is no afterwards. A man whose water is coming out the colour his is coming out has one crossing in him and not two.

He has a chart in a tin and no glasses and no compass and no horizon he can resolve and a sun he cannot look at, and he is, as a navigator, a passenger with opinions.

So he does the only useful thing, which is to find out what Monty is doing about it, and he finds out at about two in the afternoon, and it is not what he expects at all.

It is not the sun. Monty barely uses the sun. What Monty is doing, all day, every hour, is drinking the water -- taking it into his mouth and holding it and letting it go, four or five times an hour, and adjusting -- and what he is reading is the plume. The great slow four-hundred-mile-wide tongue of a river coming out into the sea, and its edges, and its taste, and where it is thicker, and where it is going thinner, and its temperature against the true sea's temperature, and he is swimming *up* it, into it, up a gradient of leaf-mould and iron laid across the Caribbean by a river neither of them can see.

He is not aiming at an island. He is aiming at a smell, and the smell is not being pushed west by anything, because the smell *is* the water.

Charles lies against him with his eyes shut and the sun on his neck and works out that the single most sophisticated piece of open-water navigation he has ever personally witnessed is being performed by a creature who cannot count to three, and that he could not have done it himself with the whole Hydrographic Office behind him, and he laughs, which is a mistake, because his mouth cracks.

<hr>

At about four he thinks seriously about cutting the fish loose.

The basket rides thirty feet behind on the breaker line where the men at Grenada lashed it and there are six fish in it and they have been in the sun since three in the morning. He can smell them. He is fairly certain he can smell them, though at this point he could not swear that he can smell anything.

The argument for is that there is a bleeding man in open water for thirty hours and a basket of fish behind him and he does not need a paper to tell him what that adds up to.

The argument against is that it is Monty's food. Monty has not eaten since yesterday morning and cannot hunt while towing and will arrive at the Bocas with the ribs standing out of him, and those six fish are the entire difference between arriving hungry and arriving in trouble, and Charles has spent five weeks watching a man spend himself down to the bone on his behalf and has not once been able to put a single thing back.

He gets the knife out -- the black-handled one, the worn one, the one that was folded into his fist on a hot wall above a beach in Guadeloupe by a man who did not say what it was for. It has been on his belt for one purpose since, and using it on a basket of fish would be, he thinks, the first cheerful thing that has ever happened to it.

He is still holding it when the whole rig goes sideways.

It is one movement and it is enormous, and the line snaps somewhere behind him, and the breaker rolls and comes up, and Charles is under and then up and coughing, and the basket is gone, and so is about four feet of line, and the water where it was is dark for a second and then is not.

Nothing else happens. Monty does not stop and does not say the word. He swims on with his head down and his rate unchanged, and only afterwards, feeling for it, does Charles find that the whole length of him is rigid from the shoulders back, and has been for some time, and probably was before the line went.

He does not find out whether he would have cut them loose. The honest answer is that he was holding a knife and thinking about it and had not decided, and a shark decided for him, and there is no way to award marks for that.

<hr>

The second night is where it stops being an account of a crossing and becomes something he cannot afterwards put in order.

He knows the sun goes down because the glare goes, and he knows the moon comes because there is a shine, and after that there is a long stretch which he can only get at in pieces, and the pieces do not stack.

He is talking. He knows he is talking because his throat is doing it, and he cannot always find the other end of the sentence, and about half of it is out loud in English to nobody.

He explains the tidal set to Jean, twice, carefully, with the numbers, and is very anxious that she should have them, and gets cross when she does not write them down.

He tells Reggie that he understood about *when you come back*. He tells him he understood it in the room, at the time, with the tin still in his hand and Reggie saying it so earnestly that it could not possibly be true, and that he has never mentioned it to anybody and is not going to, and would like this noted.

He recites, from memory, in his own capitals, the log for the week beginning the eleventh of April.

There is a long piece with Hester in it in which she is standing in the water, which she has not done in thirty years, and he is explaining the difference between nine paces and fourteen, and she is saying *yes, and?* in the Colonel's voice.

And there is a piece he keeps, in which he is eight, and in a bed, and there is somebody coming up the stairs, and he does the flat cold slow thing to his own heart for the first time and it works, and the enormous relief of it comes up through him in the middle of the Caribbean nineteen years too late and he cries about it for a while.

Somewhere in there Monty stops, and turns, and takes his face in both hands, and sings.

Charles gets perhaps half of it. He is not in a condition to be given anything whole. But he gets enough to know that it is the same made thing with the same lean in it near the end, and that underneath, in the accompaniment, where a man may not look, it is worse than St Lucia -- not the same fear grown, but a different one, flat and continuous and without any shape at all -- and Charles, in the middle of it, hears his own voice say quite clearly, "I know. I know. Don't."

Then he brings his heart down, because that is what he is for, and holds it there until the hands come off.

<hr>

The toggle happens in the small hours and he is not aware of it happening.

He is aware of the piece just afterwards, which is his own hand coming away from his left shoulder with the lanyard in it, half out.

He does not know what preceded it. Something struck the breaker, or he dreamed that something struck the breaker; there was a shock, and a noise, and a movement, and his hand went to the toggle and closed on it and pulled, exactly as it was taught to on a beach at Terre-de-Haut in the evening with children on the wall and someone's foot in the small of his back, forty times, until it went there before he had decided to send it.

And what stopped it was that his fingers would not close.

That is all. The strength went out of his hands in the eleven-hour days out of Antigua and never came back, and they are worse now than they have ever been, and the pull that a whole harness comes apart on is a pull that a hand has to make properly, and his made about two thirds of it, and slipped.

He lies in the dark on the end of a rope in eighty-five miles of open water and understands, with total clarity and no delirium in it anywhere, that a fortnight ago -- with the hands he had at Dominica -- he would at this moment be loose in the sea in the dark, two hundred yards behind a creature who would not miss him for a minute and a half and would then have the whole Atlantic to look in.

The best hands he ever had would have killed him. The ruin of them is why he is here.

He tries to be frightened about it properly and cannot get at it, and does not know that this is the first sign that he is going.

<hr>

He does not remember going.

There is no moment. He has looked for one many times since and there is nothing there to find: there is a stretch of the second night in which he is cold and shaking in blood-warm water, and there is a stretch in which he understands, in a detached and workmanlike way, that he has stopped sweating and that this is very bad news indeed, and there is a stretch in which he is drinking and cannot tell whether he is drinking, and then there is brown water and daylight.

Between those things there are seven hours and they are not his. They belong to somebody else, and this passage of his life is happening to him while he is not present at it.

<hr>

The man goes down at about four in the morning and does not come back up.

He has felt this before. He has felt it every night since Guadeloupe and it is the strangest thing he has ever perceived: a whole nervous system going out at once and staying out, undefended, on purpose, in the open, with nothing watching. He has never got used to it and does not want to. But it has always come with the land -- the man at the edge, half out, warm sand under him, and something to be lying on.

Here there is nothing to be lying on and he goes down anyway.

The floats take him. That is the first thing and it is a good thing: the two gourds on the line at his shoulders bring his face up and hold it up, and his head goes back, and his mouth is out of the water, and the sea does the rest. They ride as high as that because they are empty, and they are empty because the man has drunk everything in them, so that the arrangement has been getting better all day in exact step with the man getting worse. Somebody thought about this. He has no way of knowing that and no concept for it, and he knows it anyway in the only way that matters, which is that the thing works.

And then the second thing, and it is not good.

The burning stops.

It has been coming for five weeks at an interval he could not name and could measure exactly, and he has held a hand on the man through hundreds of them, and it is the single fact about the man he is most sure of. It comes. Whatever else is happening, it comes. He does not know what it is. He knows it is not him, and he knows it is on a clock, and he has arranged the whole of his crossing around the clock, because the clock is the only instrument he has.

He waits for it.

It does not come. He goes on waiting. It does not come, and the interval goes past, and goes past again, and the thing that has been burning that man from the inside since before the night they came away has stopped, and there are exactly two things that can mean and he has no way whatever to tell them apart.

There is no third thing to consider and so he does not consider one. The skin is a liar but it has never once been silent; a body that is doing something says so, that is what a body is for. He has no name for a body that has run out of the stuff it says things with.

So he does what is left. He puts a hand flat on the man's chest, in the dark, in the middle of eighty-five miles, and finds the slow one -- the one the man gives him, the one that is always there when he asks for it -- and it is there, and it is slower than it has ever been, and it is not being given to him. Nobody is home to give it. It is only what is left when nobody is home.

He takes his hand off. He puts it back. He does not count them, and there is no counting in him to do it with, and there are more than four hundred.

<hr>

He does not sleep.

He has slept every night since the first island, because there was a nervous system at the edge of the water keeping watch for the same things he keeps watch for, which is all his kind has ever needed and all it has ever asked. It is the arrangement. It is what people are for.

There is a nervous system on his back and it is not keeping watch for anything. It is not doing anything at all. It is switched off, and it is on him, and the difference between that and being alone is a difference he cannot make himself feel, and so he crosses the last of the open water the way he passed the weeks in the shallow reef after the shark, which is to say entirely awake, with both halves of himself watching, in water he cannot see the bottom of, being waited for.

They are still there. The near one high, the others under it, and they have been there since the fish. There is one word for that and he said it once, in the middle of the channel off Dominica, and the man went to the toggle and took his hand off it again and said something in the flat air, and afterwards he understood that the word had cost more than it bought.

So he does not say it. There is nobody to say it to.

<hr>

He sings for a long time, in the last of the dark, over a man who cannot hear him, and it is the first time in his life he has sung with nothing underneath it, and it is not a song, and he does it anyway.

Some time before light the taste comes up.

It does not arrive; it thickens, the way it has been thickening for a fortnight, and then it goes over -- and the water in his mouth is the water, the actual water, the leaf-mould and the iron and the silt and the cold thread of the river under the warm, the thing he has had in the front of his skull every hour of every day since a net came over him in January.

He has thought about this. He has thought about it in a concrete box for the best part of a year and it was the only thing he had, and in the thinking there was always a great flood at the end of it, a thing that would arrive and be enormous.

It arrives and it is not enormous. It is only true. It is a fact in his mouth, like any other, and it is the same fact it always was.

He raises his head out of the water and looks south with the eyes he barely uses, at nothing, at the dark, and then he puts his head down and swims, because the man on his back is very slow now and there is a great deal of water still between them and the mouths.

<hr>

Charles comes back at the top of a movement, the way you surface out of a fever, and the first thing is that the light is wrong.

It is green. It is coming through his eyelids green, and warm, and there is a noise in the world that is not sea noise: a rushing, a hurrying, water going over something and round something and being in a hurry, and birds, a great many birds, and once, distinctly, an engine.

He gets his eyes open.

He is on his back on the floats with his head against the breaker and the sky is very high and pale and there is *land*, on the left of him and above him and enormous, going up and up in that green-black wet way, and there is more of it on the right, and he cannot resolve any of it, he has no glasses, it is all soft.

But it is on both sides.

The water round his chest is brown.

He lies there for a while, quite empty, with the tide running him along between two pieces of land at a speed he cannot account for, and he is thirty-six days out of Antigua and has arrived at the only place in the world he was going and has no idea where he is, and the first coherent thing that comes up in him is not joy and not relief.

It is that his hand is on Monty's shoulder, and has been the whole time, and did not come off.

He does not know how. There is nobody who can tell him: the only witness cannot say, and would not think it worth mentioning if he could.

Something is trying to get his attention about those seven hours -- about what it is to have a body in the water beside you with nothing whatever coming out of it, and about who has had nine months of that -- and he is not well enough to let it in, and it waits.

And the second thing, and it happens before he has decided anything at all, is that he goes still inside himself and brings his heart down flat and slow and holds it there -- for nobody, into brown water, out of pure habit, the way a man says thank you in his sleep.

Under his hand, immediately, something enormous lets go.
