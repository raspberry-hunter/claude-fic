# Reggie Tar — Calibration Sheet

## 1. How he sorts people

Not warm/cold. **Equal, useful, or not yet sorted.** He does not work at being liked,
does not manage how he is received, and is not defending anything by omitting it. Most
of the time the social channel simply is not running. From outside this reads as
coldness and it is not: it is inattention.

The register is graded by rank, and this is the important part:

- **Peers** get the full range, including a row. He and Pascual are heard "yelling at
  each other on the verandah in front of everyone." He does not yell at anyone else.
- **Superiors** cost him nothing. "Colonel Bevan does outrank me," stated flatly, no
  resentment.
- **Subordinates** get **shortness**, not shouting. "Charles, with me." "Take this. In
  case." He is a poor supervisor, not a bully.
- **The unsorted** get nothing at all. A junior with nothing demonstrated is not
  disliked and not dismissed. He has not got round to them.

**The sort is visible in progress.** Before he has registered Charles at all, he
"does quote the numbers from Charles' data" a couple of times in front of others. He
is using the work while the man who made it is still unsorted.

**The smirk is habitual** — his resting face, not a weapon aimed at anyone. Charles
only ever sees it pointed his own way and reads it as personal. Charles has the fact
right and the reason wrong.

**His instinct with a subordinate is to use them, and that is not automatically bad.**
Where his interests and theirs coincide it works well and both parties benefit. Jean is
learning a great deal from him and rolls her eyes at him constantly; both are true.
John is used more and does not appear to mind. The instinct is only dangerous when what
he needs from someone is *particular*.

## 2. What he does

**He smirks.** Habitually. "His habitual public-school smirk, the one that looks right
past Charles as if he doesn't exist." It is the first thing recorded about his face and
it is self-satisfied.

**He takes pleasure in results.** This is the engine and it is not a social act. He is
pleased about a good number and will say the number out loud to whoever is standing
there, without noticing that saying it to someone is a thing he has done.

**He went white once, and it was atypical.** Told he might as well have stayed with
the government labs: "Reggie went all white and quiet and Charles, I've never seen him
like that before, even when Alpha died." **The whole meaning is in the rarity.** He
does not do this. That he did it measures what the remark reached, and nothing else.

**He makes jokes.** On the worst night of the book, to a subordinate he has badly
wronged, as reassurance: *"I shall be quite cross with you if it doesn't work"* —
"a slight flash of ironic humour."

**He is kind, clumsily, and is not believed.** *"You don't... you don't need to worry
about the government boys, if — when you come back."* Said "so earnestly," and Charles
cannot credit it. **The tragedy is not that he cannot be kind; it is that he has spent
eleven months making his kindness inadmissible as evidence.**

**He prepares.** He has the chart in his pocket before the conversation: *Reef opens
here*, *Fresh water*, *Keep clear — ordnance*. Then: "Take this. In case." He gives it
to Charles **because Charles is going to need it.** It is a practical act by a man who
thought ahead, not a substitute for something he could not say.

**He is visibly unnerved when something is going wrong that he caused.** "He looks
concerned, for once." *"It does seem that it's been... hard,"* said carefully. Then:
"Let's go to dinner," "looking very slightly unnerved."

**He explains when someone says something he thinks is stupid as though to a child.** *"I'm not going to do that, Charles,"
Reggie says, calmly and slowly, as if Charles is an idiot or a small child that he has
to explain simple concepts to.* This is the subordinate register: not shouting,
condescension.

**He is loose in the body.** Leans back. Raises his eyebrows. Blows out a breath.
Swallows so the cords show. Thrusts a tin rather than handing it over. Spits out a
phrase. Doesn't-quite-shudder. Makes one sound he did not approve first — "a low sound
before he clamps his mouth shut."

**He lost once, and it is rare.** Pascual holds his eyes across the rail and "Reggie
is the one who drops his eyes." Nobody on the station has seen it before. It could
happen again — but only when he is very much in the wrong, and only because he is.

## 3. How he talks

**He contracts.** 5.3 per 100 words of dialogue across the book. This is ordinary and
there is nothing formal about it.

> "We haven't got the staffing to look after Beta right now."
> "He's dangerous. Angriest specimen I've seen in a while, but doesn't always show it."
> "Well, that explains why Beta isn't doing worse than Gamma. Or is he?"
> "I'd prefer it, if you didn't publicize results before we've thoroughly reviewed them."
> "You've got to get him out of here."

**He drops contractions exactly once,** and it is the tell:

> "It is not even that they are cruel, Charles. It is that they are *curious*, and
> impatient, and very fond of knives."

One spike, under maximum pressure, on the one subject he cannot go near. **The tell
only works while the baseline contracts.**

**He self-edits visibly only under strain.** "Reggie pauses, visibly adjusts what he
was going to say." Not a habit. A symptom.

## 4. What he withholds, and from whom

**He is disinclined to apologise, and did not apologise here.** "Reggie doesn't
apologize for all of the pain he caused. Charles hadn't really thought he would." Two
things are running: a real predilection against it, and the fact that he is in a hurry
and there is a boat to catch. He is not incapable of it.

**He does not necessarily give personal reasons when there are impersonal ones available.** Asked why Charles must run, he
says it is because what they are doing isn't science, and Charles believes him
*because* of that: "if Reggie had said it was because of Monty, or even because of
Charles himself, Charles wouldn't have believed it."

**Self-report is not a closed faculty.** He *can* do it. He will not do it in a work context, on a verandah, or in front of an audience — no
self-respecting Englishman conducts his business across a station. That is a choice
about audience, not an incapacity. With an equal, in private, it is available. **Do
not write him as unable to say what he feels. Write him as almost never in a room
where he would.**

**Narration is not a room.** The reticence is social and does not extend to his own
POV. He may report his state there as fully as anyone. What is less reliable is *cause*.
He is wrong about his own reasons at about the ordinary human rate — no oftener — and
like anyone, he is wrong where there is something he does not want to see. The
blindness is motivated, not constitutional. He can know that he cannot sit still; he
may not want to know what it means.

The worked example is the row on the verandah. Pascual is right, and says so at a
volume the whole station can place, and Reggie goes white and does not answer, and
does not stop the experiment until the following morning. Nothing was concealed from
him. He needed a night.

**Interiority means feeling, not audit.** The failure mode is narration that reports
his epistemic position — what he can and cannot support, the limits of his own method
— in place of what is happening to him. That reads as interior and is not. It makes
him less legible while appearing to open him up.

**He does not refer to the government labs in public.** The place is mentioned twice
in the book, both times in someone else's mouth. What he does with it in private is
not on the record — see the caveat on absence.

## 5. Facts

- Senior scientist of the group, "not even ten years older than the rest of them," and
  "by all accounts he really is that brilliant." Note that Charles is slightly incorrect; see 6. 
- Worked at the government labs; left; built the mortality tables afterwards, and knows
  everything about them.
- Knows Spilsbury "very well"; Charles later calls Spilsbury his "old acquaintance."
  Spilsbury arrives attached to the US Navy party.
- Takes John aside privately at 408 **because he wants the Spilsbury business
  private**, and for no other reason.

## 6. Author rulings (not in the text)

Labelled so they cannot be mistaken for findings.

*Waves* gives him no physical description at all.

- **Build.** Small — around five feet six — and lightly built. Neat and precise.
- **Glasses.** Yes.
- **Dress.** Well and neatly turned out in the office. In the field he has no trouble
  at all getting his hands, and the rest of him, filthy, **and rather enjoys it.**
  Anyone reading him off the office version has read him wrong.
- **"Curious, and impatient, and very fond of knives" also describes what he was at the
  labs.** He is describing himself and Charles hears only a warning. Stays off the
  page permanently; changes how the line is written.
- **Self-report is available with Pascual**, in private, as a peer. This is where the
  faculty gets used.
- **Jean fought to make him notice her**, and now holds colleague status. Consistent
  with her proprietary irritation ("Reggie's too cautious, I told him he should have
  let you do this ages ago") but not demonstrated by it.

Charles is incorrect about Reggie being less than ten years older than Charles. Charles is 24 in 1954, and Reggie is a little more than ten years older: 35 in 1954.

## 7. The short version

> He smirks, he is delighted by a good number, he rows with his equals and is short
> with his juniors, and he contracts like anybody else. He can say what he feels and
> almost never happens to be somewhere he would. What he does in private is rather different than what he will do in public.

## 8. Drafting controls (added revision e)

These are not characterisation. They are instruments for catching drift while it is
still cheap. All four are checkable before a file is handed over, and the numbers are
to be reported with the word count.

### 8.1 The differential rule

**Pascual's contraction rate must exceed Reggie's in every installment.** Not by a
fixed margin — just always. This is the single most important line on either sheet.

The absolute rates on both sheets are useful and they are not what broke. What broke
was the *gap*. In *Waves* Pascual is the least formal speaker on the station and
Reggie is ordinary; by the end of the first sequel arc the two men were level, and
after that the difference between them was subject matter rather than voice. A rate
floor cannot catch that. A differential can, and cannot be argued with.

**Consequence for the tell.** §3 records that Reggie drops contractions exactly once
in *Waves*, under maximum pressure, on the one subject he cannot go near. That tell is
an instrument and it only reads against a contracting baseline. If his baseline is
already flat, the spike is invisible and the scene it was built for does nothing. The
differential rule protects the tell as much as it protects Pascual.

### 8.2 Per-installment budget

Measured before delivery. Reported with the word count. A file that fails is not
handed over; it is fixed first.

|                                            | Limit                                              |
| ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Reggie's contraction rate                  | ≥ 4.0 per 100 words of dialogue                    |
| Pascual's rate                             | ≥ 5.0, **and above Reggie's**                      |
| Turns of 50+ words                         | max 2 per installment                              |
| Volleys (4+ consecutive short exchanges)   | min 3 per installment                              |
| "eleven" as a quantity                     | max 2, and only where the clinical record is meant |
| Prolepsis (narration reporting the future) | max 1                                              |

Notes on three of these.

**Turns of 50+ words.** *Waves* runs at about 2.9% of turns; the second sequel arc
reached 10.1%, and the peak arrived before the material that appeared to justify it.
Long turns are not forbidden and the best scenes in the series contain one. Two is the
number at which they are still events.

**Volleys.** This is a floor, not a ceiling, and it is deliberately set at the first
sequel arc's level rather than at *Waves*. *Waves* is almost entirely public scenes and
gives these two men very few rooms to themselves; a higher rate of fast exchange
between them is a correct inference from the source, not a departure from it.

**"Eleven."** The number does real work — the clinical record is eleven days — and it
became a texture: eleven minutes, books, men, steps, flags, varieties, governments.
When everything is eleven, the eleven that matters is noise.

### 8.3 No new flaws

**Reggie has one flaw. It is the one in *Waves*: he gets too intense (especially about science) and forgets about the human side.**

Anything that looks like a second flaw is the first one standing in a different room.
The intensity is the engine — it is why he is senior at thirty-five, why the tables
exist, why the trial happened, and (part of) why he publishes the method knowing what the method
will cost. It is not a condition under management and it must not be written as one.

Rules:

- Writing him a flaw he does not have in *Waves* is an error **even where the scene is
  good**. Especially then; that is how the last three got in, one plausible sentence at
  a time.
- He must never be given a sentence in which he diagnoses himself with something new.
  If such a line appears, it is cut, not negotiated.
- Do not let another character perform the diagnosis on his behalf, and do not let the
  narration do it. Reclassifying the engine as *the defect* — one thing wearing three
  coats — is the same error at one remove.
- **He is not always the one who has to be braked.** Across a long sequence this
  becomes structural even when every individual instance is defensible. He brakes
  Pascual at least once per arc, on something that matters and where he is right; and
  at least once per arc his intensity runs and the result is simply good.

Note that he may have other positive or negative qualities.
### 8.4 The absolutist construction

**Neither man says "I always" or "I never" about himself.**

In *Waves* the construction appears three times in nineteen thousand words and is never
once a totalising self-description ("I've never seen him like that before"). In the
sequels the strong form — *I have never once* / *I have never in my life* — runs to
twenty-four instances, and the sentence that minted the invented second flaw is one of
them: *I have never once in my life asked anybody for a thing I wanted and been told
yes.*

The grammar and the flaw-accretion are the same problem. A man who says *I have never
once* has stopped reporting and started summarising, and a summary of a life is a
diagnosis. Cutting the construction removes the delivery vehicle.

Not a softened version, either. *Almost never*, *I don't think I've ever*, *in five
years I have not once* — same move, hedged. The replacement is the specific instance:
what he did, when, in the room it happened in. Let the reader do the totalling.

The construction is not made acceptable by the text knowing it might be self-delusion.
Signalling the unreliability keeps the sentence and adds a wink. Prefer not to write it.
